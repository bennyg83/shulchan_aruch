One who injures his minor daughter, etc. The Mechaber omits here the law of one who injures a Hebrew servant, which Rambam states in chapter 4 of Hilchot Chovel and the Tur in seif 8 of this siman. If a master injures his own Hebrew servant, he is liable for all categories and pays them to the servant, except for loss of work, which the master retains because the servant's inability to work causes the master the loss. If others injure the servant, Rambam writes that land is purchased with the damages and the master consumes its fruits for as long as the servant remains with him; when the servant goes free, he takes the land itself. Rif agrees, and Beit Yosef rules accordingly. Rabbeinu Chananel explains differently: the major loss of work, namely the value of the severed hand, is used to buy land and the master eats the fruits because through the loss of the hand he loses the difference between the servant's former work and the lesser work of guarding cucumbers once he heals. That is, when the tortfeasor pays bodily damages for the hand, he compensates for the work the servant could have done with that hand, but the servant will still be fit to guard cucumbers once the pain of the amputation heals, as explained in siman 420. Therefore the master eats the fruits while the servant remains with him, paralleling the labor he lost through the amputation, and when the servant goes free he takes the land. The minor loss of work, namely the wage of guarding cucumbers throughout the entire period of illness, belongs entirely to the master. My father Rosh agrees with this. If the injury is of a type that causes the master no loss, such as cutting off the tip of the servant's ear or nose, the damages belong to the servant and the master has no share in them. This is the Tur's wording. See Perishah.
That diminishes her monetary value. Meaning, if he wished to sell her or marry her off, people would give less for her than before the injury. The Tur gives the example that he wounded her face. Even after healing, the mark remains and makes her less desirable. All the more so if he cut off a limb, where the loss of a limb is an obvious and definite reduction in value. See Perishah.
It belongs to the father. Scripture says, "I gave my daughter to this man," showing that the Torah placed her under his authority to give her to whichever man he chooses and to receive her kiddushin money. It also says, "If a man sells his daughter." Since the kiddushin money and sale price belong to him, when an injury lowers that monetary value the father receives the amount of the reduction. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who derives it from "in her youth, in her father's house," on the assumption that all matters of a girl in her youth belong to her father. Tosafos explicitly write that this verse concerns only annulment of vows, and that when the Gemara sometimes mentions it elsewhere it is merely used as a mnemonic. See Perishah.
Some say specifically when she is supported at his table, etc. It appears that if she is not supported at his table, then even if her father wishes to feed her during her illness in order to acquire her loss-of-work payment, he cannot do so merely by paying her maintenance. This differs from where the father himself injured her, where Maggid Mishneh writes that in such a case her loss-of-work payment belongs to him. See there, chapter 4 of Hilchot Chovel, law 19, and examine carefully.
But if she is not supported at his table, it belongs to her. This refers to the loss-of-work payment, from which she then supports herself. There is no distinction, however, regarding bodily damages that reduce her sale value or kiddushin value, as explained above.
If others injured her. Rosh's wording is that with something coming to her from outside sources and through pain, the father does not insist on taking it.
Some say he is exempt. As stated immediately below, meaning in the following seif concerning a father who injures his adult children. This is Rambam's view, and the law is the same for a son and a daughter. Maggid Mishneh also explains Rambam's words as Rama does, that the case specifically concerns one who is not supported at the father's table. See the end of chapter 4 of Hilchot Chovel.
