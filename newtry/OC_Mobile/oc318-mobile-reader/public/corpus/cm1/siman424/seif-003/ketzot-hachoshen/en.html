One who injures his own Canaanite slave is exempt. The master is exempt even from lashes where the injury has monetary value. The reason is not that no monetary liability exists. The slave has a monetary claim, but whatever the slave acquires belongs to the master, so the master immediately recovers the payment. Because the act remains within the category of payment, lashes do not apply. If the injury is worth less than a perutah, there is no monetary liability at all and lashes can apply. A difficulty arises from the case of an orphan girl who is seduced and thereby waives the monetary payments. Tosafot explains that one cannot simply stipulate in advance that Torah damage law shall not apply, because that would be a condition against Torah law. One can, however, waive the money afterward. If the seduced girl merely waives an existing monetary right, the defendant should remain within the category of payment just as the master who injures his own slave is, even though the payment returns to the person who owes it. Why then can lashes become relevant in the seduction case? Shitah Mekubetzet and Rashba explain Rabbi Yehudah's rule that a condition concerning money is effective as meaning, in some contexts, that the party agrees in advance to waive the resulting payment. If that is the mechanism, the difficulty remains. According to what I wrote in siman 209, Rabbi Yehudah allows a monetary condition to prevent the Torah monetary right from arising at all, because the Torah imposed that liability subject to the obligor's consent in monetary matters. On that approach the seduced woman can be understood as having no damage claim from the outset, whereas the master's slave does have a claim that is immediately reacquired by the master. The distinction is then clear.
