At its actuarial value. Bava Kamma asks whether the law that a married woman who injures another has no present means of payment supports the Usha enactment. If she owns usufruct property, why not sell her future interest and pay the victim? The proposal cannot mean simply that the victim should receive that future interest directly. In any event, if the husband later dies first or divorces her, the victim can then collect. The practical benefit would be to sell the future interest now at its discounted actuarial value, perhaps ten zuz for property that could later be worth one hundred. But Baal HaTerumot rules that where beit din appraises property to a creditor and the property later rises in value, redemption follows the earlier lower appraisal. If the victim himself received the future interest now at ten, why would he later gain the full one hundred? The appraisal could be returned at its original low value. The better reading is that the woman's usufruct property should be sold to a third party at its actuarial value and the cash paid to the victim. The outside purchaser can gain from the later full value, because once property appraised to a creditor is sold onward it is no longer subject to the ordinary rule of return to the debtor. This also answers Tosafot's difficulty concerning the Usha enactment. If the future interest were merely transferred directly to the injured party, the special rationale that a purchaser from a married woman knowingly entered a risky transaction would not apply in the same way. But on the present explanation the sale is to an actual third-party purchaser, who assumed precisely that risk. The Gemara's separate suggestion concerning sale of the ketubah directly to the injured party can still work because debt instruments are treated as movable property for these purposes, and the appraisal-return rule does not apply to movables in the same way. A difficulty remains from Baal HaTerumot, who says that if no outsider will buy the usufruct property, the injured party himself takes it at its actuarial value. What does he gain if Baal HaTerumot also holds that appraisal returns at the original low value? Possibly that ruling concerns movable *nichsei tzon barzel*, which are a debt owed by the husband and therefore have the law of movables. The point requires study.
And she pays the injured party. Bava Kamma asks why the woman cannot sell her ketubah at its actuarial value to the person she injured. The Gemara answers that she could then waive the ketubah to her husband, making the sale useless. Ramban and Ritva object that once the injured party acquires her ketubah through Rabbi Natan's lien, she should no longer be able to waive it. The Shach offers an answer and notes that room remained for him to develop the point. I earlier defended his answer against the objections of Tumim. I have now found the substance explicitly in Shitah Mekubetzet in the name of Rashba. The woman's present liability for the injury does not itself allow beit din to collect her future ketubah now, because the ketubah is a contingent right with no presently collectible body. The proposal in the Gemara is therefore that she sell the ketubah at its actuarial value. If she sells it, whether to an outsider or to the injured party himself, the injured party receives it by the law of sale. At the moment of that sale, his prior tort lien is effectively satisfied or replaced by the purchased right. He then stands like any other purchaser of the ketubah, and the woman can waive the ketubah to her husband. One cannot say that beit din should simply collect the actuarial value of the ketubah for the injured party through Rabbi Natan's lien, because there is no present tangible debt body to collect. When the ketubah eventually matures, beit din can then take it from the husband and give it to the injured party. But before that time, only a voluntary sale of the contingent right is possible. Thus Rashba expressly resolves the problem exactly along the line defended by the Shach. His explanation shows why the woman's waiver remains effective after a present actuarial sale to the injured party, despite the general rule that a debtor cannot waive a claim once Rabbi Natan's lien has fully attached.
