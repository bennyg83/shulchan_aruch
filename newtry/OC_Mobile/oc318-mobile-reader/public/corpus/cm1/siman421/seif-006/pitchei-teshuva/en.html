And he is concerned that he will steal from him. See Baer Heitev, which cites Responsa Shvut Yaakov. See what I wrote concerning this above, siman 232, seif 10, seif katan 5.
He may dismiss him. See Responsa Chut HaShani, end of siman 61. It writes that the law did not instead require the employer to administer an oath to the servant that he would not steal from him, which would seem preferable to cancelling the employment relationship, nor did it merely permit him to strike the servant. The reason is that the law does not authorize imposing an oath on a person merely because one fears that he may cause damage when no claim has yet arisen against him, for the other can say, “Guard yourself so that I do not harm you.” Here, however, because this servant constantly lives with him in the same house and it is impossible to protect oneself from him, the employer may dismiss him; see there. According to this reasoning, if the servant voluntarily chose to swear to refrain from such conduct, the employer could no longer dismiss him. The unqualified language of the Rama, however, does not appear to support this conclusion, and the matter requires study.
