If he blinded his eye, he is exempt. The reason is that both parties willingly engaged in wrestling, each intending to throw the other, and each knew that it is impossible to control the encounter so precisely that no injury could occur. It is therefore as though they mutually accepted and waived claims for accidental injury that might arise through the ordinary course of the contest, provided neither intended to injure the other. This resembles two people running in the public domain who injure one another through their running. Each knows that others also have the right to run and that two people running toward one another may collide and cause injury. Although we say that a person is always forewarned, that rule cannot apply literally in every circumstance, because complete unavoidable accidents are exempt. The Rosh gives this reasoning in responsum, cited by the Tur. In siman 378 I further explained that with two people running, exemption does not rest only upon implied waiver but also upon the fact that both actively participated in creating the risk. For that reason the rule can apply even outside the public domain whenever two people run toward one another. The Tur cites the Ramah explicitly this way. In the Derishah there I discussed whether Rashi disagrees and suggested that the positions may be reconciled.
