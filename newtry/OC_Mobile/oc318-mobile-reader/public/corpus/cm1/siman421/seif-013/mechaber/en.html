If two people injure one another, and one injures the other more than the other injured him, he pays full damages for the excess. This applies specifically when they began simultaneously, or when after one injured the other, the second immediately retaliated and injured him. But if one began the attack, the second is exempt for injury reasonably inflicted in self-defense, because he is permitted to injure the attacker in order to save himself. The same principle applies to insults and humiliations: the one who began pays the fine. Hagahot Maimoniyot, Kiddushin. If Reuven struck Shimon and Shimon then came to strike Reuven, and Reuven's wife held Shimon back, and Shimon tore his hands free and struck her, he is exempt. Mahariv, siman 28. See the end of siman 424 regarding a husband and wife who injured one another. Nevertheless, the matter requires assessment. If the defender could have saved himself with a minor injury but instead inflicted a much greater one, he is liable. This applies to the physical injury payments, for which he pays the four additional categories as one who unintentionally injured another, but he is exempt for humiliation because he did not intend to humiliate. Thus, if one person strikes another and the victim responds by calling him a mamzer, the victim is exempt, as reflected in the verse, "when his heart becomes heated," and similarly if one calls another a thief and the other answers, "You lie like a mamzer," or calls him a mamzer, he is exempt for the same reason. Mahariv, simanim 28 and 60. The same law applies to one who sees a Jew striking another Jew and cannot save the victim without striking the attacker. He may strike the attacker in order to separate him from the transgression. Rama: Likewise, one who has authority over another and sees him committing a transgression may strike and discipline him to separate him from the prohibition, and need not first bring him before beit din. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 18.
