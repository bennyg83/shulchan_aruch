He immediately retaliated as well. The Tur also has the word "immediately." In the Perishah I wrote that at first glance it appears to be a scribal error. If the second person retaliates immediately while still inflamed by the first assault, he should be exempt. At the end of siman 402 concerning two oxen, the Tur and Rama do not use the word "immediately," but say that after they separated, the second returned and attacked. Both laws derive from the same Mishnah in HaManiach, and the Rosh there writes "after some time." It is difficult to say that the Tur distinguished people from animals on the ground that a person is always forewarned, so that once the first person stops striking, the second is liable even if he retaliates while still angry because he should have sued in beit din. The wording does not comfortably bear that interpretation, and there is no clear source for such a distinction. The correct text therefore appears to be: if, after some time, the second returned and struck him, both are liable and the excess damage is paid in full; but if the first person began the assault and the second immediately struck him in self defense, the second is exempt. This accords with the Rosh. Rama's comparison to insults also fits this reading. If one calls another a thief and the other immediately responds by calling him a mamzer, the second can be exempt because he reacted to the provocation. See siman 402, where I resolved an apparent inconsistency in the Mechaber's rulings.

In order to separate him from a prohibition. The Tur states another case before this one: if a person sees someone striking his father, son, or brother and strikes the attacker in order to save his relative, he is exempt. In the Perishah I noted that the Tur did not combine the father, son, and brother case with the later general case of one who sees another Jew being struck. He also gives a different reason in each. Concerning relatives he says, "in order to save his relative," while concerning another Jew he says, "in order to separate him from a prohibition." There can be a practical difference. If the rescuer is not generally the kind of person who intervenes to prevent others from sin, and frequently sees one Jew strike another without intervening, then if he suddenly strikes this particular aggressor we may suspect that he acted from hatred rather than to prevent a prohibition. In such a case he may not be entitled to the exemption. But where his father, son, or brother is being struck, he may defend his relative even if he does not ordinarily intervene in other people's disputes. The Mechaber did not distinguish these cases, perhaps because he did not notice this point, but the distinction is reasonable.
