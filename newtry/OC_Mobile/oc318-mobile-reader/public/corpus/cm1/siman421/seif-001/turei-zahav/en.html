(Seif 13: and he too immediately struck back, etc.) The Sema emends the word "immediately" as a scribal error, but his explanation then does not fit the following phrase, "if one began," etc., because the opening case already concerns one who began. According to his view it should have said, "but if he injured him immediately, he is exempt," which would be the true opposite of the opening rule. It appears to me that there is no scribal error. First, the law discusses a case where both began striking at exactly the same time, and then their liabilities are equal. It then states that if one injured the other and finished, and only afterward the second came back and injured him, this is a new act and the second is liable just as the first was, or they pay the full excess damage accordingly. But if both were striking during one continuous incident, with one merely beginning slightly before the other, and while the first was still enraged and actively striking the second struck back, the one who began bears the special liability. When the Shulchan Aruch and Tur say, "he immediately struck back," the novelty is that as soon as the first act ended, the second is liable if he then returns the blow, and all the more so if time elapsed in between. The words are therefore clear in their plain meaning. Regarding striking someone in order to separate people from a transgression, the Sema distinguishes whether this is the person's usual conduct or not. I do not know the basis for that distinction. If the law permits him to save the victim by striking the aggressor, what difference does it make that he personally bears hatred toward the aggressor? He is still performing a mitzvah of rescue. Is the law dependent upon his private thoughts? Even if on other occasions he acted improperly, that does not make this proper act improper. Nevertheless, it appears to me that there is a different distinction. One who strikes an aggressor in order to save an unrelated Jew is exempt only where it is clearly established that rescue could not be accomplished otherwise. By contrast, one rescuing his own relative is not scrutinized so strictly. Even where it is not absolutely clear that no other method was possible, he may strike the aggressor, because his relative is like his own body. Although siman 4 states that one is not exempt for striking where he could save himself otherwise, that concerns striking in order to save property. Here another is striking his relative, which is like striking him personally, and even in a doubtful rescue he may strike the aggressor. This distinction appears precise in the Rosh and Tur. Regarding a relative they write merely, "in order to save his relative," while regarding an unrelated Jew they write, "if he cannot save him in another way." This indicates the distinction I have written, and it is correct.
