I have no claim or dealings. In Ketubot 95a, the Gemara asks what effect such language has, citing a baraita that one who tells his fellow, 'I have no claim or dealings with you,' has said nothing. It answers that a kinyan was made. This comparison is difficult. According to Rashi there, the baraita concerns partners, where the underlying property is actually his. Verbal removal alone certainly cannot transfer his ownership. A husband who writes such language to his wife, however, has no present ownership in her property but merely future rights or liens. Perhaps verbal withdrawal should suffice. Penei Yehoshua already expresses astonishment. The matter can be explained from the Ran at the beginning of Nedarim concerning partners forbidden by vow from benefiting from one another. The Rabbis hold they may not use the jointly owned courtyard. Ran explains that in a courtyard not subject to division, each partner uses the whole, but the body of the property belongs to each only in his half. The reason one uses the other's half is that each partner's half is subjected to the other's use. Thus, even in the partners' case, the right over the other's half is merely a lien or use-right, not full ownership. Nevertheless, the baraita says mere words of removal accomplish nothing. This supports the Gemara's comparison and shows that even a lien cannot necessarily be relinquished by such words alone.
He does not seize it from the second purchaser. The reason is straightforward: the second purchaser can say, 'If you remain silent, fine; otherwise I will return the property to its prior owner.' Sema and Shach ask from seif 2, where a creditor may seize from the first purchaser. Why can that purchaser not make the same argument? The Taz distinguishes: where the second purchaser acquired from the first by an actual sale, he can say, 'Since I can return it, treat me as though I never acquired it.' In seif 2, by contrast, the field belongs to the first purchaser automatically by virtue of his existing lien; it did not come to him through a new purchase from the second person, so he cannot say, 'I will return it to its owner.' This is difficult because the legal argument 'I will return it' does not retroactively annul the purchase. The Tur in siman 119 writes expressly that the purchaser need not return that very field; he could return another field or even give one as a gift. The principle is that anything a person can presently do is treated, for this defense, as though already done. Thus actual purchase is not the decisive point. Perhaps the distinction follows the Sema in siman 107. Shulchan Aruch holds that a later creditor who sells the land to a non-Jew must compensate the earlier creditor under dina d'garmi because he damaged the earlier lien, whereas an heir who sells inherited property is exempt because a creditor collects only prospectively and the heir acted with his own property. A purchaser similarly owns the property until it is actually seized and is no worse than an heir. He can therefore say, 'I will return it,' because even though returning it would be a new transfer and might damage the creditor's lien, he is merely acting with property that is presently his and incurs no damages liability. In seif 2, however, the party who has only a creditor's lien and not full ownership cannot deliberately transfer away the property in a way that damages the earlier creditor, because he would be liable for damaging the lien. He therefore cannot rely on the hypothetical 'I will return it' defense. This resolves the distinction.
