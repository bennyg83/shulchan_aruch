Seif 1: A borrower who sold his properties to two purchasers. Note: See Shach, seif katan 2, who challenges Bach. In my opinion Bach is correct. The rule that a purchaser whose field is seized by an earlier creditor cannot rescind against his seller applies only where the earlier creditor had no designated apotiki. But if the earlier creditor held an apotiki, especially an explicit apotiki stating that he would collect only from that property, the case resembles a sale where the field is later found not to belong to the seller, in which case the purchaser can recover from the seller. The reason ordinarily given for an earlier creditor’s seizure is that the seller could have satisfied him from other assets. Even with an explicit apotiki the debtor can sometimes remove the lien by paying money or movable property, but the cases are not comparable. With an ordinary creditor, the debtor may be able to satisfy him even with other land, so the later transaction was not necessarily mistaken from the outset. With an explicit apotiki, however, the debtor can remove the lien only with money or movables. Who knows whether he will possess such assets when the time comes? It is therefore considered outside his assured power to clear the land, and the later purchaser’s consent can be viewed as mistaken from the beginning. There is an additional reason based on presumed intent. If the second creditor knew that the debtor had already designated this very field as an explicit apotiki to the first creditor, saying, “You shall have payment only from this,” and the debtor then said the same thing to the second creditor about that identical field, the debtor’s scheme is apparent. He is attempting to satisfy or secure multiple debts with one field in a manner that will leave one creditor without recourse. Where the first creditor did not receive an explicit apotiki, the second creditor might still have accepted the field even had he known of the earlier debt, because no deceit is evident. He could assume that the debtor intended to satisfy the earlier creditor from other property and leave this field for him. Moreover, a creditor who failed to investigate whether the debtor had earlier debts knew in general that a debtor might owe many people. By accepting the field anyway, he can be understood as relying on the possibility that earlier creditors would be paid elsewhere. But where the debtor expressly designated the same field exclusively to both creditors, the manipulation is plain. The second creditor would certainly not have agreed had he known. An explicit apotiki of this sort is not a common occurrence, and intentional fraudulent duplication is even less common, so the second creditor was not expected to suspect that the debtor would deliberately act wickedly. We therefore have a strong presumption that his acceptance was based on a mistaken understanding and that the seller must return his money. The authorities often state this rule in the case where a field is discovered not to belong to the seller. That case can actually be viewed as the greater novelty, because the seller’s deceit is less obvious. Perhaps he had originally taken the field from the true owner while his hand was strong and thought the owner would never recover it, or perhaps he expected to buy out the true owner and preserve the sale. Nevertheless, the purchaser is treated as having entered the transaction in error. All the more so here, where the debtor deliberately made incompatible exclusive designations of the same property to different creditors. That is a true mistake induced by his scheme. Examine this carefully.
