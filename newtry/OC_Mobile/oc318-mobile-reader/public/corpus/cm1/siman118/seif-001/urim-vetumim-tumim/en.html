The same applies to a woman with a ketubah. Tosafot hold that the woman does not lose her earlier ketubah claim merely because she later wrote a claim in favor of a second party; see Urim. One should not ask why, according to Tosafot, the Gemara at the beginning of Perek Af Al Pi lists numerous respects in which conditions of a ketubah are treated like the ketubah itself but does not list this rule as well, that assigning or writing to a second party does not cause loss of the first claim although an ordinary creditor may lose his position. That passage lists leniencies or special laws that the Sages established specifically for a ketubah and teaches that the same leniencies extend to conditions of the ketubah. Our rule has a different reason. The woman's later act does not prove she intentionally relinquished her first claim because it is not certain she will ever collect the second. That logic applies equally to the principal ketubah and its conditions without any separate enactment. Indeed, the same reasoning can apply to an ordinary creditor. If a lender and borrower agree that the debt will be lost if the lender dies during the borrower's lifetime, the lender's later transaction that depends on eventual collection likewise does not necessarily show an intentional waiver because collection is uncertain. Thus the doctrine is not specifically a ketubah leniency. Tosafot therefore had no reason to ask why it was omitted from the Gemara's list, unlike their question from the rule that an ordinary creditor takes prospective property at the end of Perek Yesh Nochalin.
