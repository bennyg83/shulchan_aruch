He seizes. The Sema explains that the purchaser cannot use the argument, "If you remain silent," because the creditor can answer, "When you actually return it, then we will discuss it." The Shach asks that the Tur disagrees with the Rambam in a related case. If a creditor wrote to the first purchaser, "I have no claim or dealings against you," and that purchaser then sold to a second purchaser, the Rambam allows the creditor to collect from the second. The Tur objects that the second purchaser should say, "If you remain silent, fine; otherwise I will return it to the first purchaser," and does not accept the answer, "Return it first." In siman 119 the view of Tosafot and the Rosh likewise indicates that we do not merely answer, "When you return it." Perishah notices the contradiction and gives a strained answer. There is in fact no difficulty. In siman 119, when the purchaser says he will return the document to the seller, he has a concrete benefit. At present the creditor may seize superior-quality land from him, but after he returns it the creditor will be limited to inferior-quality land. There is every reason for him to return it, and the law therefore treats the situation as though the return had already occurred. The same is true in the Rambam's case of a second purchaser who bought from the first for money. If he returns the property to the first purchaser, whether by cancelling the sale or by a new conveyance, he receives back the money he paid. If the creditor seizes it from him instead, he loses. He therefore has a real incentive to return it, and the argument is treated as present. Here, however, the first purchaser seized the property from the second purchaser without paying him. If he now cancels his seizure, he loses his own claim. If the earlier creditor takes the property, he likewise loses. Returning it gives him no financial advantage. Why should we pretend he has already done something that only harms him? Nor do we assume a collusive arrangement with the second purchaser. Beit din itself raises the "If you remain silent" argument for purchasers; it cannot invent a possible conspiracy as the basis for the plea. It is therefore better for the parties to compromise now. This distinction is sound, and I am surprised Perishah did not state it. The Shach answers differently: here the seizure comes through the original debtor. His meaning can be explained as follows. The phrase "If you remain silent" is only a shorthand. If taken literally, it could always be used to defeat collection where the creditor lacks an after-acquired clause: "If you remain silent, fine; otherwise I will sell to someone else, and after acquired and sold property will not be collectible." Plainly that is not the rule. The true argument is, "I stand in the rights of the person from whom I came, so why should my position be worse than his?" Thus, in the Rambam's case the second purchaser says, "Why should I be worse than the first purchaser from whom I acquired, against whom you waived your claim?" Likewise below a purchaser may say, "Why should I be worse than if the property were still in the seller's hands, where you could collect only inferior land?" But here the first purchaser who already seized the property does not derive his right from the second purchaser. He asserts that his own lien is earlier and superior. He therefore cannot say, "Why should I be worse than the person from whom I derive?" because he derives nothing from that party. This too resolves the issue, though in my view the Sema's practical distinction is more persuasive.
