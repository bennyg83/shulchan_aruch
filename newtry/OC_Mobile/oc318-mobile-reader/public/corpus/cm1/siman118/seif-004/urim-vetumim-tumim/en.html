To another earlier creditor. The Sema asks why it matters that the other creditor is earlier rather than later. The field is an apotiki in any event. He answers with difficulty. It seems to me that if there are two fields, one made an ordinary apotiki and one not, and an earlier creditor comes to collect, he may choose either field. The holder of the ordinary apotiki cannot tell him, "Collect from the other field, because this one is designated to me." The rule that one does not collect from encumbered property where unencumbered property is available protects purchasers, not a creditor holding only an ordinary apotiki. Such an apotiki gives merely a lien, and the debtor can still remove its holder with money. The earlier creditor therefore says, "My lien preceded yours, and I may collect wherever I choose." This is all the more obvious according to the Sema, who understands the case as an ordinary apotiki. If the apotiki creditor were later, there would be no circular collection requiring compromise. The earlier creditor could seize the first purchaser's field. If there is a second purchaser, the first purchaser can say he does not want the rabbinic protection enacted for his benefit and may recover from the second purchaser, while the later apotiki creditor has no claim against the second field. The chain ends. The baraita therefore specifically says that the apotiki creditor is earlier. If the other creditor first seizes the field designated to the earlier apotiki creditor, that earlier creditor can return and seize it from him, producing the repeated cycle. Thus the wording is exact. The Raavad explains the Gemara's case of "likewise a creditor and two purchasers" in this manner, as cited by the Baal HaTerumot, and it appears Tosafot do as well. Tosafot in Ketubot 95b ask why both clauses are needed and answer that in the first clause one might have thought the first wife can recover from the second because the second suffers less loss, since she may recover from the husband. The Maharsha asks that a creditor and purchaser likewise may recover from the seller because the sale was with warranty, and in all these cases one ordinarily assumes the seller has no remaining assets, otherwise the creditor could say property was left available. According to the above, the answer is clear. The Ramban writes that even where a wife has an explicit apotiki, if that field is swept away she can collect from other assets because of Shimon ben Shetach's enactment that all the husband's property secures the ketubah. Her rights are not weakened merely because one field was designated. Thus the first clause can concern two wives each with designated property, or the three fields traditionally designated for the ketubah. The husband may still own other assets. The second wife takes her apotiki; the first, absent her waiver, would recover from the second; and if the second loses that field she can still recover elsewhere under the general ketubah security. Hence her loss is limited. The clause concerning two purchasers teaches more. If there is no apotiki, the seller must have no remaining property, otherwise the creditor is directed there. The purchaser therefore suffers real loss. If there is an apotiki, seizure of that specifically secured field likewise leaves the purchaser with a genuine loss. The baraita teaches that even where loss exists, the parties can continue to seize from one another until compromise. Tosafot's words are thereby resolved. There is another true case of circular collection. Two creditors lend successively. The first has an after-acquired clause, the second does not. The debtor sells one field to a first purchaser with an after-acquired clause, later acquires and sells another field to a second purchaser, and the first creditor writes to the second purchaser, "I have no claim against you." The first creditor cannot seize the second purchaser because of the waiver, and cannot seize the first purchaser because that purchaser can say, "You left later property subject to your after-acquired lien." The second creditor, who lacks an after-acquired clause, can seize the first purchaser's field. The first purchaser, who does have such a clause, then seizes from the second purchaser. The first creditor can then seize that field from the first purchaser, since his waiver was only to the second purchaser, and the second purchaser can seize again, continuing until compromise. The rule is sound. The Gemara may not have chosen this construction because it would prove that an after-acquired clause is effective, an issue left unresolved in Bava Batra unless one forces the baraita to follow Rabbi Meir.
