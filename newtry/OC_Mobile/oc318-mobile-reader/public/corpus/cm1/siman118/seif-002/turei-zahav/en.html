He does not seize it from the second purchaser because the second may say, “If you remain silent, well and good; otherwise I will return my deed to its owner.” One cannot ask why, in seif 2, when the creditor seizes the field from the first purchaser, the first purchaser cannot similarly say that he will return the field to the second purchaser. Here the second purchaser acquired from the first by sale and may say that if he returns the purchase and receives back his money, it is as though he never bought. His right in the field exists only through the money he paid. In seif 2, by contrast, the first purchaser's right arose automatically from his own lien, while the second purchased that lien from the creditor. It is therefore the first purchaser's own property and not something that came to him by purchase from the second, so “I will return it to its owner” does not apply. Sema writes that there is no benefit to him in returning it. This is very difficult, for the Tur writes in siman 119 that whatever a person is able to do is regarded as though already done. The glossator answers that there the return produces a benefit, since it enables collection from inferior land, whereas here there is no benefit, as Sema writes. He further suggests that here the purchaser cannot say he will return it, because if so, why did he buy it initially, knowing that the creditor would seize it? In the other case he presumably did not know, because a person does not throw away his money. If he knowingly bought, it must have been because he trusted the seller to accept a return of the deed when the creditor came. The same applies to a donee, who trusts the donor not to retain the land after return of the deed, but to retransfer both body and produce. In seif 2, however, return of the deed would give him nothing from the second purchaser, who would permanently keep land that he lawfully bought. This too is correct. See Tosafot, Ketubot 109b, s.v. VeRabbanan.
