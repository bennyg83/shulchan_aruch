We do not listen to the purchaser. The Rosh gives the reason that doing so would cause loss to the borrower, and the Tur writes likewise. This requires explanation. Even if the creditor does not claim that the field is worth more than the appraisal, the purchaser cannot ordinarily remove him unless the creditor's entire debt is paid. Certainly, then, where the creditor says he will take the field in satisfaction of the whole debt, while the purchaser wants the creditor to receive only the appraised value and leave the borrower still owing the balance, the purchaser should have no power to insist. Why is an additional reason of borrower loss needed? The Bach and Derishah address this, but their explanations are not clear. We must therefore understand the case as resembling the Gemara's two-mansion episode. The purchaser bought two fields, each appraised at five hundred, for a total of one thousand. The creditor is owed one thousand. The creditor knows that if both fields are appraised in the ordinary manner at one thousand, the purchaser can pay the entire debt in money and remove him. The creditor prefers one particular field and says, "To me this one field is worth the full thousand; I will take it for my entire debt and leave you the other field." If so, under the rule discussed above in seif 3, the purchaser can recover from the borrower only five hundred, because the court records the seizure according to the field's ordinary value, not the creditor's subjective valuation. The purchaser therefore says: let beit din appraise both fields at one thousand, I will keep them, the creditor will still receive his entire debt, and I will then have a one-thousand claim against the borrower corresponding to the court appraisal of both fields. The Rosh teaches that we do not listen to the purchaser because the borrower would lose. Under the creditor's proposal the purchaser's recourse against the borrower is only five hundred; under the purchaser's proposal it becomes one thousand. The additional rationale of borrower loss is therefore necessary and precise. This also explains the Rosh's later statement, copied by the Tur above in siman 109, that if heirs make the corresponding argument we listen to them because the return-of-appraisal rule operates for their benefit. The scenario may likewise involve two fields. There is an earlier creditor owed one thousand and a later creditor as well. The earlier creditor sets his eye on one field. If both fields are appraised together at one thousand, the heirs could remove him with money. To prevent that, he says the single preferred field is worth one thousand to him and takes only it, leaving the second field available for the later creditor. The heirs say: appraise both fields ordinarily and let the first creditor receive them for his one-thousand debt. The later creditor will then receive nothing. We listen to the heirs because appraisal can later be returned to them. If they later obtain five hundred they can redeem one of the fields from the first creditor. By contrast, if the first creditor is permitted to take one field subjectively at one thousand, the second creditor will seize the other field and the heirs will never be able to recover it through return of appraisal. The first creditor suffers no loss either way because he receives satisfaction of his full debt. This gives content to the heirs' interest. It also helps explain the Beit Yosef's statement in siman 115 that the Tur follows Ramban, who permits a creditor to say that a field is worth the entire debt to him. The Beit Yosef brings support from siman 72. The Shach objects that in siman 109 the Tur and Mechaber rule that we listen to the heirs because appraisal is returnable. According to the present explanation there is no contradiction. In siman 109 the creditor is fully paid in either scenario; the dispute concerns only whether payment comes from one field or two. At first glance, this can also reconcile the Rosh and Rabbenu Yonah, who hold that Rami bar Chama and Rava did not disagree about return of appraisal, because an appraisal is not returned to a purchaser. Their dispute would instead concern an independent right to remove a creditor. With heirs, where everyone agrees an appraisal is returnable, even Rami bar Chama would agree that they can remove the creditor with money, because they could recover the property even after it had been appraised and delivered. If so, however, the Gemara in Bava Kamma and HaMekabel becomes difficult. It says that three parties have improvement appraised to them, including a creditor against a purchaser and against orphans, and asks that the purchaser should say, "Give me land equal to the value of my improvement." The Gemara responds: this works according to the opinion that the purchaser cannot remove the creditor with money, but what shall we say according to the opinion that he can? If the opinion that one cannot remove is Rami bar Chama, the difficulty remains regarding orphans. They certainly can remove the creditor by virtue of return of appraisal. Why, then, is the passage satisfactory according to him? According to the scenario just described, one could answer that the land with its improvement is worth less than half the creditor's total debt, and the creditor says the land is worth the entire debt to him. The heirs cannot invoke return of appraisal unless they pay the whole amount for which the creditor took it. They have no such money; only the improvement amount is theirs and the creditor must pay them that in cash. As to the body of the field they cannot remove him. Thus there is no difficulty.
But this answer is not fully satisfactory. The Gemara's question is that the purchaser should say: "If I had money I could remove you from the whole field; now give me a portion of land equal to my improvement." The Gemara treats a right over the whole as generating a corresponding right to a fraction. If a field worth four hundred plus one hundred of improvement is treated by the creditor as worth his entire one-thousand debt, the purchaser could argue that had he possessed the full thousand he could remove the creditor and would then own the whole field. Since he lacks that money but owns one hundred of improvement, let him retain a corresponding fraction of the field. The same logic should apply to heirs. The fact that the creditor has subjectively valued the field at one thousand should not necessarily destroy the proportional argument. One might respond that the creditor's subjective valuation applies to the bare field because of its location or special value to him, but not to the improvement. Suppose the bare field is appraised at four hundred and the improvement at one hundred, while the creditor says the bare field alone is worth nine hundred to him. If the heirs demand land equal to their one hundred of improvement, they would receive only one ninth of the bare land, which has ordinary market value of roughly forty-four, in exchange for one hundred of improvement. That would itself harm them. This could solve the problem, but it is strained to split the creditor's subjective valuation so sharply between land and improvement. A better resolution is available. The Gemara says a purchaser may demand his share of improvement in land because, since he has a legal right to remove the creditor from the entire field, he has an underlying legal claim in the land itself. If the creditor says, "I do not wish to become your partner," the purchaser can answer that his own land right is no less real than the creditor's. But this applies only where the purchaser's power to remove the creditor is a matter of strict law. If his only ability to recover the property comes from the equitable doctrine of "what is right and good," namely return of appraisal, that doctrine was not enacted to compel a creditor to become co-owner of a field with the borrower or purchaser. It is not "right and good" to force the creditor to retain only part of a field and share it with another. Thus the Gemara says the passage is satisfactory according to Rami bar Chama, who denies an inherent legal right of removal. Even though heirs can later invoke return of appraisal, that equitable doctrine does not allow them to demand a fractional partnership now. According to Rava, however, removal with money is a substantive legal right. The purchaser consequently has a legal claim to a corresponding share of the field and can say, "Give me land equal to my improvement." This also explains the Raamah cited by the Rosh, who establishes the two-mansion case where the creditor sold the first property, so return of appraisal no longer applies. This indicates that he does not follow Rabbenu Yonah's view that an appraisal is returned even to a purchaser. In his framework Rami bar Chama and Rava cannot be disputing the return-of-appraisal rule, because the case was deliberately one where that rule does not apply. The Gemara itself likewise suggests their dispute is not about return of appraisal: Rami bar Chama brings proof from the Mishnah, and Rava answers him on grounds of "what is right and good," a rationale not applicable to ketubat benin dikhrin. According to what we have written, all views fit. A merely equitable return right does not justify forcing the creditor into co-ownership. We now derive an important rule from the preceding seif. Where the creditor will not be paid his entire debt, the purchaser has no strict-law right to remove him. The fact that appraisal may later be returned does not entitle the purchaser to demand a fractional share of the land equal to his improvement. But where the creditor is fully paid, the purchaser can say, as below in siman 115, "Give me land equal to my improvement," because his right to remove the creditor is then a substantive right. Finally, concerning the Beit Yosef's statement that the Tur follows Ramban even though siman 109 appears opposite, it seems the Beit Yosef understood the dispute between Ramban and those who disagree as follows. Their dispute concerns a field that has already undergone appraisal and announcement, after which the creditor says, "I will accept this field for my whole debt." Ramban says that just as any outsider may outbid the announced price, the creditor may also say, "To me it is worth more; I increase the bid," and no one can stop him. The Beit Yosef understands the Tur and Rosh to agree. Siman 109, however, concerns a different case where the creditor refuses public announcement altogether. He does not want the property exposed to the ordinary auction process and says merely that he will accept it at some value which beit din estimates as the highest plausible amount, even though no actual market process has occurred. The Rosh's precise language is, "I will accept it without a court appraisal," and in that case the heirs can object. Ordinarily, where the creditor outbids others within a real appraisal and announcement, return of appraisal remains possible because a fixed collection value has been established. If he takes the land without any genuine appraisal or announcement, return of appraisal may not apply at all, because no formal collection valuation exists. This is worse than a creditor who informally takes property for his debt without troubling beit din, whose appraisal is not later returned. Accordingly, the Mechaber could rule in siman 109 without noting a disagreement by Ramban, because on this reading there is no disagreement there. Many useful principles follow from this analysis, with Hashem's help.
