We do not heed the purchaser. The reason is that doing so would cause a loss to the borrower. If we heeded the purchaser, the borrower would not be discharged from the lender for the excess up to the full amount of the debt. This differs from a lender who comes to collect from heirs, where the Tur and Mechaber write above in siman 109:1 that even if the lender wishes to accept the field left by the deceased borrower in satisfaction of the entire debt, we heed the heirs when they say, "We will give it to you at the court's appraisal." There the borrower has already died and suffers no loss, while the heirs benefit from appraisal because if they later obtain money they can redeem the property from the lender at that appraised value, since appraisal is reversible for them. The Ir Shushan writes here that we do not heed the purchaser because the reason we heed an heir in siman 109 is that the heir needs to know the value in order to redeem it later, since appraisal is reversible for an heir, whereas it is not reversible for a purchaser. His words are very surprising. First, even if appraisal is not reversible after the property has entered the lender's hands, the purchaser here is demanding that it be appraised immediately so that he can pay the lender the appraised value in satisfaction of the claim without ever giving the field to the lender. Why should we not heed him, since a purchaser ordinarily can remove the lender at the outset? Second, no one disputes the present law that we do not heed the purchaser. According to the Ir Shushan's reason, the Rambam, who holds that appraisal is reversible even for a purchaser after the lender has collected, should necessarily disagree here. The correct explanation is therefore as I wrote: the reason is the borrower's loss. The Rosh states this explicitly, as I explained in the Perishah.
