If he wishes. In Urim I cited the Sema's view. Below, seif 6, the law is explicit that if a purchaser merely wishes to add somewhat to the court appraisal in order to keep the land, but the creditor will still not receive his entire debt, we do not listen to the purchaser and the land is given to the creditor. Here, however, the Sema understands that the purchaser may remove the creditor with money even if the entire debt will not thereby be paid. He therefore distinguishes between the present case, before appraisal and public announcement, where the purchaser may remove him even though the land is worth less than the debt, and the later case, after appraisal and announcement, where the purchaser can remove him only if he pays the entire debt. In truth this distinction has no foundation. The source is Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui. A creditor was owed one thousand zuz. The debtor sold two mansions, each worth five hundred. The creditor seized the first for five hundred and then came to seize the second. The purchaser said: either keep the first as payment of the entire thousand, or take one thousand from me and return the first. Rami bar Chama rejected the purchaser's claim, while Rava held that it was a valid claim. The Rosh, citing Ri, writes that if during the days of public announcement the purchaser merely offers more than the court's appraisal, we do not heed him, because he is acting for his own advantage. He says: what do I care if I add to the price and keep the land, since afterward I will recover from the seller. We therefore do not cancel the court appraisal because of his self interested increase. Rav Hai Gaon writes that if the creditor and purchaser each increase the other, the land goes to the one who offers more. The Rosh explains that this may concern a case where one offers enough to satisfy the entire debt, like the Gemara's case. But if the purchaser merely adds somewhat above the appraisal and the creditor will still not be fully paid, we follow the court appraisal. The Tur and Mechaber below copy this rule. The Derishah asks how the Tur could earlier state without qualification that a purchaser may remove the creditor with money. It is strained, he says, to establish that statement only where the entire debt is paid, and he further claims that Perek HaMekabel indicates that even partial removal is possible. He therefore creates the distinction adopted by the Sema, between before appraisal and announcement and afterward. I cannot understand it. The Rosh says explicitly that even within the period of announcement, if an ordinary outsider offers more than the appraisal, the offer is heard, but if the purchaser does so for his own benefit, it is not. Thus the later rule plainly applies before the announcement process is complete. After the period of announcement has fully ended, no ordinary outsider can simply reopen the bidding, or there would never be an end to the process. The Rosh specifically speaks of an offer made within the announcement period. It is therefore impossible to say that seif 6 concerns only after the process has ended. Nor is there sense in saying that before appraisal the purchaser may block the creditor even though the creditor will receive only part of his debt. The creditor can say: let the property be announced publicly. Perhaps bidders will raise the price and I will be paid in full. Why should your private purchase of property already subject to my lien deprive me of that opportunity? One might try to reinterpret the Sema as saying that announcement must still occur, but that if the purchaser declared from the outset that he wished to remove the creditor he may afterward do so even for partial payment, whereas if he remained silent until later he can remove him only by paying the whole debt. This too has no basis in the Sema's words or in reason. Why should an early declaration change the law? There is also a dilemma in the dispute between Rami bar Chama and Rava. If Rami bar Chama held that even before appraisal the purchaser cannot remove the creditor even by paying the whole debt, and Rava disagreed only in that whole debt case, from where could we invent a far broader dispute that Rava permits partial removal? The Rosh explicitly warns not to create a wider dispute than the Gemara establishes. If, alternatively, everyone agrees that before appraisal partial removal is possible, then the Gemara in Perek HaMekabel becomes difficult. It says that three appraise the improvement and asks: this works according to the opinion that the purchaser cannot remove the creditor with money, but according to the opinion that he can, why not remove him? Tosafos and the Rosh identify the opinions as Rami bar Chama and Rava. If everyone agreed before appraisal that the purchaser could remove even partially, Rami bar Chama would face the same question. The Derishah itself wants to place the HaMekabel passage before final appraisal, which only strengthens the objection. Nor do I know where the Derishah sees proof from Perek HaMekabel for partial removal. There the Gemara establishes either that the creditor's debt equals the value of the land itself or the value of the land together with the improvement. In either case, when the purchaser removes him with money the creditor is fully satisfied. Indeed, it appears that the Rosh derived precisely from that passage the distinction between paying the entire debt and paying only part. The order of the questions in Perek HaMekabel confirms this. Ravina asks whether the purchaser has rights in the improvement, since Shmuel says a creditor collects improvement. The Gemara answers by establishing the debt at only the value of the land. It then asks that the purchaser should say, "Give me a portion of land equal to my improvement." That later question depends upon the preceding answer. Before the preceding question we could have assumed that the debt exceeded the value of the land and improvement together. In that case, even if the creditor did not have a substantive claim to the improvement, the court could appraise it and compensate the purchaser in money, because partial payment would still leave the creditor unsatisfied and the purchaser could not remove him. Once the Gemara establishes that the debt is only the value of the land, the creditor can be removed completely, and therefore the purchaser's claim becomes powerful. This sequence is exactly what the Rosh explains. Perhaps the Derishah meant that in HaMekabel the purchaser removes the creditor with money only as to the improvement, which is only part of the land. But that is not partial payment of the creditor's claim. The creditor ultimately receives his entire debt, partly through land and partly through money, and no further claim remains against the debtor. As the Bach explains, this is beneficial because the debtor is then left with one claimant, the purchaser, rather than both the creditor and purchaser. The Rosh's rule addresses a different case in which the land is worth only part of the debt and, after the purchaser's payment, the creditor would still need to pursue the debtor. In that situation partial removal is not permitted. Tosafos in Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui asks why the Gemara in HaMekabel assumes a dispute. Perhaps everyone agrees that when the creditor wants to take the land at the same appraisal price paid by the purchaser, the purchaser cannot remove him, whereas Rava permits removal only when the purchaser increases the valuation. Tosafos answers that the purchaser can always remove him by valuing the land at twice its ordinary price. The Derishah explains that if he can remove when he increases the appraisal, he can remove even without increasing it. This does not fit Tosafos and merely repeats their question. The meaning is straightforward. The creditor is owed only the amount of his debt. If the land is worth exactly that amount, any extra value the purchaser assigns to the land belongs economically to the purchaser after the creditor is paid. The creditor has no interest in whether the purchaser calls it five hundred, one thousand, or more, once the debt is fully satisfied. Therefore the purchaser can say that the court appraisal is too low and that he values the land at double. Since the creditor receives everything owed him and suffers no loss, there is no reason to prevent the purchaser from retaining the land. This is what Tosafos means when they say that he can always remove him by increasing the valuation. One might ask why Tosafos do not distinguish that Rava allows removal only where the debtor gains, as in the two mansions case where treating the first mansion as worth the entire debt saves the debtor five hundred, while in HaMekabel the debtor gains nothing because the creditor is paid in full either way. The language of Rava answers this. He says, "What loss does the creditor suffer?" He does not say, "What gain does the debtor receive?" The controlling point is absence of loss to the creditor, not affirmative benefit to the debtor. There remains a question from Bava Metzia 15, where the Gemara establishes that a creditor takes improvement above expenses when the debt equals only the value of the land, and then asks that the purchaser should demand land equal to the improvement. There, under the initial assumption, the creditor would receive more value by taking the land and improvement than the amount of his loan. If the purchaser paid money instead, the creditor would receive only his debt, so the debtor would clearly benefit because otherwise the purchaser could later recover the extra improvement from him. Why is this not an even stronger case for Rava's rule? The answer is that Tosafos in Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui deliberately base their proof on Perek HaMekabel rather than this earlier passage. In Perek HaMekabel the Gemara has already concluded that outside an apotiki a creditor does not collect more than his debt. Therefore the purchaser's increase in appraisal causes no loss to the creditor, and Tosafos can prove the removal rule cleanly. In Bava Metzia 15, by contrast, the discussion still assumes that the creditor may collect more than his debt through the improvement. There, payment of cash would deprive him of an additional right, so that passage cannot serve as the same proof. The result is clear. If the purchaser does not remove the creditor by paying the entire debt, the creditor cannot be forced to accept a partial cash payment merely because the purchaser wants to retain the land. This is true even before the appraisal and announcement process is complete. The Tur's earlier unqualified language relies upon the later clarification, and the word "remove" itself naturally means that the creditor is fully removed from the debt. The Derishah and Sema's distinction between before and after appraisal is therefore not sustainable.
Now. The Shach understood the Sema's statement that a purchaser may remove the creditor as depending upon the dispute between Ramban and the Tur below, siman 115, concerning a creditor who says that he will accept the property in satisfaction of the entire debt. That is not the case under discussion. The present case is the ordinary one where the creditor accepts the land only according to its court appraised value. The other dispute is separate. Likewise, the Shach misunderstood the Sema's statement that after appraisal and announcement the purchaser can remove the creditor only by paying the entire debt. The Shach says that this differs from the case later where the creditor rejects the court appraisal entirely, whereas here, once the appraisal has been made, why should the purchaser pay more than the appraised value? But the Sema was not referring to the later seif concerning a creditor who rejects the appraisal. He referred to seif 6, which expressly distinguishes between a purchaser who pays the whole debt and one who merely adds somewhat above the appraisal. On that point the Sema's citation is accurate, even though the distinction he derives from it is not.
Appraisal and announcement, etc. The Rama understands Rabbeinu Yonah to mean that once appraisal and public announcement have been completed, the purchaser can no longer remove the creditor, because an appraisal made to a creditor is not reversed for a purchaser. The famous case of the two mansions, where Rava allowed the purchaser to remove the creditor with money, must therefore have occurred before the announcement process was completed. The fact that the first mansion had already been seized does not prove otherwise, because some authorities permit a creditor to take and even sell upon maturity before the formal process is complete. The Sema, however, understands that even after appraisal and announcement the purchaser may still remove the creditor as long as there has not yet been a final award or actual descent into possession. Only once the creditor has been formally placed into the land does the rule that appraisal is not reversed for a purchaser apply. He therefore struggled with the Rama's language and tried to explain it through the partial payment distinction. That is not the Rama's intent. The Shach correctly notes the disagreement. As practical law, though, the Sema's position appears correct. The Rosh writes explicitly that "they placed the creditor into the land" and repeats the requirement of actual placement at the end of the discussion. Ritva in Ketubot, who also follows Rabbeinu Yonah that appraisal does not return for a purchaser, nevertheless writes that a final deed of award to the creditor is required before the purchaser loses the right to remove him. The Tur's phrase "appraisal and announcement" can be understood as shorthand for the completed process, which includes the final award. Likewise, when the Rosh explains that the two mansions case occurred before announcement, he may mean before the entire process was complete, including final placement. There is also a conceptual reason. Under our ruling the creditor does not receive the fruits until the days of announcement are complete and the final award occurs. Until then the land has not fully entered his possession. So long as the award has not become final, a purchaser who offers to satisfy the entire debt should be able to remove him. By contrast, according to Abaye, who holds that the creditor acquires fruits from the time the writ of authorization is signed, one could understand the earlier acquisition as sufficient to block removal. The Rosh's explanation of the two mansions case under Abaye therefore required placing it before any meaningful appraisal stage. But for practical law, where possession is not complete until final placement, the purchaser may remove the creditor until that point.
And all this concerns one purchaser, etc. This clause itself creates difficulty for the Rama's reading. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Ritva, and Ran can say that the right to remove the creditor exists specifically in the property seized from that same purchaser, but not in another purchaser's property, because their version places the case after the announcement period but before final award. At that stage the first purchaser has a special relation to the parcel being taken from him. According to the Rama, however, the case is before completion of the announcement. At that point why should a second purchaser be a stranger to the proceeding? The public announcement is open to everyone. Any person may increase the offer during the announcement period, and there is no reason a second purchaser should have less right than an unrelated outsider. He too should be able to say: accept this land or I will increase the cash offer up to the amount of your debt. This difficulty strongly supports the Sema's practical conclusion that the operative boundary is the final award rather than the mere beginning or completion of announcement.
