If the purchaser wishes to remove him by payment. See Shach seif katan 1 through the words, "he gives him according to the appraisal." The Shach's words are extremely difficult. He writes that according to what he stated in siman 115, the purchaser cannot remove the creditor until he pays the entire debt. But there the creditor says, "To me it is worth the amount of my entire debt," whereas here the case is that the creditor does not receive more than the appraised value. Therefore the Sema correctly writes that the purchaser may say, "Here is money equal to its value," since the debtor gains nothing from the creditor taking the land, for even then the creditor will reduce the debt only by the land's value. The Shach's attempt to reject the Sema on the ground that an appraisal and announcement have already been made is also difficult, because the law is stated explicitly in seif 10 and in the Rosh. The Shach himself writes similarly in siman 116 seif katan 20, that where there is no benefit to the debtor, even a purchaser may remove the creditor. At first glance the two discussions appear unrelated. In my view the position that permits the creditor to say, "To me it is worth my entire debt," applies even where the debtor suffers no loss. In our case the creditor does not bid above the appraisal, but seeks to take the field at the appraised value. There is therefore no benefit to the debtor from the creditor's collection. As the Perishah explains, if the creditor actually offers more than the appraisal, everyone agrees that the purchaser cannot remove him, because the debtor benefits from the higher amount. Indeed, the requirement of public announcement was enacted for the debtor's benefit, and a creditor who bids above appraisal is no worse than any other bidder, to whom the property is awarded at the higher price. The dispute among the poskim about the creditor saying, "To me it is worth my debt," therefore concerns only where he does not add above the appraisal and wants the field at its appraised value. Those poskim nevertheless hold that the purchaser cannot remove him merely by paying that value, because the creditor may say that from the outset the field was encumbered to him for his entire debt, so that if the debtor had no cash the field would stand in place of the whole debt. The statement that it is "worth my entire debt" does not necessarily mean that he is formally accepting it as full satisfaction of the entire debt. Rather, it means that his lien attaches to the land in relation to the whole debt. Accordingly, when the Gemara says that a purchaser may remove a creditor with money, on this view it means only by paying the entire debt, because only then does he remove the creditor from all rights in the field. Paying merely the appraised value leaves the creditor with the residual right that, if the debtor never produces money, the field could answer for the whole debt. The first opinion, however, holds that the land is encumbered only to the extent of its appraisal. Therefore, where there is no benefit to the debtor, the creditor cannot insist that it is worth the whole debt to him. Only if he actually accepts the field in satisfaction of the entire debt do we refuse to hear the purchaser's attempt to remove him, as explained in seif 7, because the debtor benefits. According to this, all of the Shach's comments are coherent. His challenge to the Sema is also sound. If so, even after appraisal and announcement the purchaser should still be able to remove the creditor when the creditor merely seeks the land at its appraised value, because there is no benefit to the debtor. Seif 6, where the creditor adds above the appraisal, is different because the debtor benefits from the creditor's higher bid, while here the creditor adds nothing. See Shach seif katan 2 concerning the Sema. Tumim explains that according to the Sema, once the announcement is fully completed the appraisal no longer reverts to the purchaser even if he offers the entire debt. The Sema himself disagrees and holds that the rule that the appraisal does not revert to a purchaser applies only after the formal transfer of possession, but after appraisal and announcement and before transfer, the purchaser may still remove the creditor by paying the entire debt.
And he collects from him only one hundred. This is specifically where the creditor accepts the field for two hundred. Then the purchaser may recover only one hundred from the seller, because that is all he actually lost. But if the purchaser himself pays two hundred in order to retain the field, he may recover the full two hundred from the seller. The Ritva in Ketubot writes this explicitly. This is certainly so where two fields are involved. If the debtor initially says to the purchaser, "If you want the first field already collected to count for the full two hundred, fine; otherwise take two hundred and remove the creditor," his words "take two hundred" plainly mean two hundred in exchange for preserving both fields, because a purchaser may remove the creditor with money. We cannot say he means to pay two hundred only for the first field. Are we dealing with fools, who would pay two hundred for one field and then be unable to recover from the seller, when by paying the same two hundred for both fields he can retain them and recover the payment from the seller? Since the two hundred are paid to preserve both fields, the purchaser certainly may recover that amount from the seller. Even if there is only one field, bought for one hundred, if the purchaser must pay two hundred to keep it he may recover two hundred from the seller. The creditor himself was willing to take the field for only one hundred, and had the creditor taken it the debtor would still have owed him another one hundred. Thus the purchaser's additional payment creates a direct loss to him corresponding to a benefit to the debtor, and the debtor must reimburse it. By contrast, where the creditor accepts the field as satisfaction of the full two hundred, the debtor suffers no additional liability because of the purchaser and the purchaser's loss arises only through the seller's responsibility clause. That is not considered responsibility for appreciation, because the field itself did not increase in value. Nor can we compare the purchaser who pays the full two hundred to one who voluntarily pays another person's debt, because the purchaser bought the field and values it enough that he is effectively compelled to pay the amount necessary to preserve it. The seller wrote that he would stand and indemnify him against claimants, which is tantamount to authorizing him to satisfy a claimant when doing so does not cause the debtor any added loss. This is also demonstrated by the Rosh, cited by Sema seif katan 17, who writes that the purchaser's motive is obvious: he says to himself, "Why should I care if I add more, since the land will remain mine and afterward I will recover everything from the seller?" This is exactly as explained.
Two purchasers. See Tumim, who asks that if the Rama is speaking before completion of the public announcement, how can it be said that the second purchaser is not a litigant? He has the right to bid higher during the announcement, since the days of announcement are not yet complete, and any person may increase the bid, including the second purchaser. There is no difficulty. According to what I explained above, a single purchaser may say, "Take two hundred and withdraw," meaning from both fields, because a purchaser may remove the creditor with money and later recover from the debtor. But where there are two separate purchasers, the second purchaser cannot say, "Take two hundred and withdraw from both fields," because with respect to the first field, which has already been awarded, he is not a litigant. He can indeed buy that field for two hundred like any outsider who bids during the announcement, but then he cannot recover that payment from the debtor by virtue of his own purchase. The distinction is straightforward.
