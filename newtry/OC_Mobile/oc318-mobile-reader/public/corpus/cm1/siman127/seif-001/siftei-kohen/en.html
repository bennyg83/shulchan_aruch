Some disagree with this. They hold that the rule applies only where the money was concealed when the loan was made, because then the husband may say that he acted merely to induce disclosure of hidden funds. With openly held money, the rule does not apply, and even with concealed money the husband or master must make a definite claim. The related laws are discussed in Even HaEzer 86. In Even HaEzer 85, the Mechaber rules that where money or movable property is found in a wife’s possession and she says that it was given to her as a gift, while the husband claims that it is the product of her labor and belongs to him, she is believed, land is purchased with it, and he enjoys the produce. If she says that it was given on condition that her husband have no control over it, she must prove the condition. The Rama cites views distinguishing between concealed and open money, between a wife who manages the household and one who does not, and between property seen in her possession and property she could deny possessing. The sources, however, do not support the Rama’s distinction here. The distinction between concealed and open money belongs to a case where she purchases from or lends to her husband, because hidden money permits the claim that he sought only to uncover it. Where money is simply found in her possession, the Rashba and those who follow him believe her even if it was concealed. The Maggid Mishneh and Nimukei Yosef also indicate this distinction. Likewise, Maharam’s responsum concerning a wife who says that her husband gave her the objects does not conflict with the Rambam and Mechaber, for they too believe her when she claims that the husband himself gave them to her. They disbelieve her only when she claims that others gave them on condition that her husband have no rights. The Rama’s formulation therefore requires study.
