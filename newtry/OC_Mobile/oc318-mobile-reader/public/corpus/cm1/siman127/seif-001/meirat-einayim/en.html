Because whatever a slave acquires, etc. According to this reason and this opinion, even if the husband or master does not claim with certainty that the money was his, and even if a promissory note was written and delivered to the wife or slave, they have no claim against him. The Maggid Mishneh writes likewise. See the Perishah.
And some disagree in this regard. Meaning that they distinguish. They hold that the first rule applies specifically when the husband or master borrowed concealed money from the wife or slave, because then one can say that he never truly borrowed and wrote the note only in order to induce them to reveal the money in their possession and thereby remove it from them. But if the money was openly in the possession of the woman or slave, we say that it had been given to them on condition that the husband or master have no rights in it. Even with concealed money, this rationale applies only if the husband or master makes a definite claim that the money was really his and that he acted merely to reveal it. The Rambam, who is the author of the first opinion, also distinguishes between concealed and open money where the wife purchases land from her husband or the slave from his master. He distinguishes, however, between a land sale, where the seller does not thereby make himself a servant of the buyer, and a loan. A person does not ordinarily wish to make himself a servant borrower to a creditor, and therefore with a loan we say that he intended merely to uncover and recover the money even if it was not concealed. I discussed this at length in the Derishah. The source is also in Even HaEzer siman 85:6 and below at the beginning of siman 151.
