He should not return it to his children. Every convert is regarded as a newborn person, and therefore his preconversion children are not heirs under Jewish law. The Sages also said that one who returns a lost object to a gentile acts improperly, as the Tur and Mechaber state below in siman 264. Although here the father himself was a convert rather than a gentile, there is still no obligation to return the money to his children, for in this respect they are no better than unrelated persons. According to Rashi's explanation of the phrase, 'the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with him,' meaning that the Sages do not hold him in favor for doing so, the point is even clearer. The Ran explains instead that the conduct itself is not pleasing to the Sages. Some explain the expression as meaning that the spirit of wisdom does not shine forth from one who acts this way.
If their birth was in sanctity, the spirit of the Sages is pleased. Rashi and the Rosh explain this way. According to Ri, the rule is the reverse. If their birth occurred after conversion, the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with returning the property, because doing so may blur their status with born Jews. People might then infer that if one of them betroths his father's former wife the kiddushin do not take effect, or that if he performs yibbum with his brother's widow she was exempt from chalitzah, and similar errors. But if both conception and birth occurred before conversion, the spirit of the Sages is pleased with one who returns the property, because he acts beyond the letter of the law and no comparable error will result. According to the first explanation, difficulty arises from the case of Mari bar Rachel, who was the son of Isur the convert. Isur had left a deposit with Rava, and Rava sought not to return it to Mari. The answer is that Mari's mother was Jewish, and he was conceived from Isur while Isur was still a gentile. In such a case Mari does not inherit his father at all, neither under Jewish law nor under gentile inheritance law. Under Jewish law, the child of a gentile or slave and a Jewish woman follows the mother. Under gentile law, even if both later reverted to gentile status, a son born to a Jewish mother would not inherit that father. This differs from a case where the mother too was a gentile at conception and gave birth after conversion. A convert may inherit his convert father in the circumstances explained below in siman 283. Therefore, when the father is a convert, the spirit of the Sages is pleased with one who returns the property to such a son. See the Derishah for further discussion.
