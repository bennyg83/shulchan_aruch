The borrower. The same applies to one who holds a deposit belonging to a convert. Even though the convert did him no favor, if the children were conceived before conversion and born after conversion, he should return it to them, and the Sages are pleased with him. This is the conclusion of Tosafot, Ran, and Tur, and it is the primary ruling. Shach.
To his children. Every convert is regarded as a newborn child, so his pre-conversion family relationship does not create halachic inheritance. As the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in siman 264 concerning one who returns a lost object to a non-Jew, here too, although the children are now converts rather than non-Jews, they are no better in this respect than unrelated persons. Sema.
Their conception. This means where the mother too was a non-Jew at conception. If the mother was Jewish and conceived by a non-Jew, however, the child does not inherit the father at all, since the child of a non-Jew or slave and a Jewish woman follows the mother. Even if both later returned to non-Jewish status, the child would not inherit the father. Therefore one should not return the property to them. This is clear from the Rosh and Ran. Shach.
In sanctity. He is not obligated to return it, but if he does so the Sages are pleased with him. This appears from the Mishnah at the end of Sheviit, according to Rashi, the Mechaber, and those who agree with them. The Bach's explanation of the Tur, that initially one should not return it and that the dispute between Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam concerns only after the fact, is unconvincing. Rather, the principal reading is that there is a scribal error in the Tur and it should read as the Mechaber writes here. This is explicit in the Rosh's remazim to the first chapter of Kiddushin and is also the Rosh's conclusion. This is the primary ruling. Shach.
