Such as grapes, etc.; and some disagree, etc. The Sema discusses this at length and asks that below, in siman 301, seif 5, the Mechaber rules without qualification that one who entrusts another to guard something attached to the ground is governed by the law of land, even when it stands ready to be harvested. He does not distinguish there, as he does here and in siman 95, between produce that still requires the ground and produce that does not. The Sema therefore explains that ordinary grapes described by the Rambam and Mechaber as ready to be harvested still require the ground somewhat, and are treated as land regarding custodians. Siman 95 likewise concerns custodians, and when it concludes that such produce is like land 'for every matter,' that phrase is not literal, because here the Mechaber rules that for acquisition and overreaching it is movable property; rather, it means every matter of custodianship, such as custodians' oaths and partial admission. His words are incorrect. The Rambam and Mechaber do not speak here of produce that still requires the ground, and 'for every matter' is indeed precise. Here, produce that no longer requires the ground is movable property; there, the case concerns produce that still requires the ground, and it is land for every matter, including acquisition and overreaching. Nor is siman 95 limited to custodianship, for it concerns denial and admission. The matter is simple: the Rambam and Mechaber follow the position of Rabbi Yosef Halevi, as I explain below in siman 95. Therefore, in siman 301, because the produce was delivered for safekeeping, even produce that no longer requires the ground is treated as land. Here and in siman 95, however, it was not delivered for safekeeping, and when it no longer requires the ground it is treated as movable property. This is clearly the Rambam's and Mechaber's view, unlike the Sema. Further objections may be raised against the Sema, but there is no need to elaborate, since I already concluded above, siman 95, seif 2, that the Tur's view is primary. This is also the Rama's view, as indicated by his citation of the dissenting opinion. The Rama did not add that gloss in siman 95 because there one must in any event take a rabbinic oath, so he did not descend to that distinction; here, however, movable property cannot otherwise be acquired at all. The Bach also asks at the end of this siman that the Tur's language here appears not to distinguish at all, treating such produce as land everywhere, whereas in siman 115, below in siman 394, and in siman 257, he rules that fully ripened produce needing no ground is not land, while in siman 301 concerning custodians' oaths he rules the opposite. This presents no difficulty. The Tur writes only that it appears attached regarding acquisition and overreaching because the Sages treated it as attached regarding an oath. In siman 115, a creditor is different, since he does not rely upon produce the purchaser can detach, as explained there in seif katan 18 and in siman 95. Siman 394, concerning one who consumed fully ripened produce, is also different, either because the produce is now detached or because valuation together with land has its own rule, as explained in siman 95. Siman 257 is different because the donor did not intend to include it. But siman 301 concerns an oath; acquisition and overreaching are comparable to an oath, as explained in siman 95. This is the correct ruling. See Responsa Maharshadam, siman 376.
