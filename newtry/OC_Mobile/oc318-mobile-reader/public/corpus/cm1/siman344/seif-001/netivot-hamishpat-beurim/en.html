He cannot change the arrangement through mere words. See Sema, seif katan 3, who cites Mordechai's case where Reuven told Shimon that the ten litrin previously held as a loan should now be treated as iska. Mordechai writes that Shimon remains liable for accidents as before, because the legal act is not altered by an exchange of words. Mordechai brings proof from Bava Kamma 93 concerning one who says "tear my garment." The Gemara distinguishes where the garment had already come into his hand for guarding. Mordechai understands that once it entered as a deposit, a later oral direction does not change the legal framework. He also proves from the end of HaPoalim. The Gemara asks how an unpaid custodian can stipulate to have borrower liability and answers, according to R. Meir, that here he did not originally bind himself. Mordechai infers that once he has already bound himself, he cannot later alter the liability by words alone. Rama therefore rules that after a person has accepted custody, even if the owner later says expressly that he need not pay if theft or accident occurs, the liability is not removed merely by words. These sources are very difficult. In the garment case the Gemara expressly says that if the owner says "on condition that you be exempt," the custodian is exempt. Mordechai elsewhere explains that without such language the words are mere persuasion. That seems to prove the opposite: a complete condition of exemption can work by words alone even after custody began. The proof from HaPoalim is also difficult because the Gemara's reasoning there follows R. Meir. We rule that conditions concerning monetary rights can be valid where the party knows and waives. Moreover, if the problem were a condition contrary to Torah law, even a kinyan should not help, while Mordechai's wording implies that a kinyan does help. We also find in Bava Kamma 109 that one can transform a robbery repayment into a loan or deposit. Why should a loan not be converted into a deposit? To defend Mordechai, one must understand him as rejecting the earlier explanation that "tear my garment" fails merely because of empty persuasion. Rather, he holds that once custodial liability has attached, a later attempt to waive a future contingent liability is different from an immediate waiver of an existing debt. A person can obligate himself by saying "you are my witnesses" even without a kinyan, yet a custodian often needs a kinyan to assume unusual future liability unless the consideration of being trusted supplies the necessary basis. Shach explains that such future liability resembles asmachta because payment arises only if a later loss occurs. The same distinction can work in reverse. A present waiver can operate by words, but an attempted waiver today of a liability that will arise only upon future negligence or accident resembles waiver of something not yet in existence or an asmachta-type contingent waiver. Such a release requires a kinyan. Accordingly, if the lender says after the loan is outstanding, "Let these funds become an iska when you later begin trading with them," the borrower's loan-liability is not immediately discharged. The release is conditioned on a future event. A kinyan is therefore required. The case "tear my garment on condition that you be exempt" is different because the owner's present instruction itself authorizes the destructive act. This invokes guarantor law: one who acts and expends money at another's direction can create or discharge liability through that very act. If one says, "Throw one hundred into the sea and thereby be discharged from the debt you already owe me," the discharge can certainly operate because the debtor performs a present act at the creditor's direction. All the more so, "tear my garment and be exempt from liability for the tearing" can work. But a mere statement that future theft or accident shall no longer be the custodian's responsibility contains no present act by which the owner parts with money. It is a bare waiver of contingent future liability and does not work without kinyan under Mordechai. Mordechai's proof from HaPoalim can also be read from the Gemara's wording. It says the case differs because "from the outset he did not bind himself," implying that the expanded condition is possible only before the original liability has attached. Mordechai treats this as a general rule even according to the authorities who validate monetary conditions. The case where a robber turns the repayment into a loan can be distinguished. There the robber had the money ready to return and the owner accepted it in a new legal capacity. Repayment itself can be effected against the creditor's will in many circumstances. Once the creditor agrees that the identified money is received as payment, the old obligation is discharged immediately and a new deposit or loan begins. Likewise, if the debtor actually brings the loan money for repayment and the creditor says, "Keep this now as iska," the old loan is paid and a new arrangement begins without difficulty. But where the debtor does not present the money for payment and the creditor merely says, "From now on treat the existing debt as an iska," there is no immediate repayment and no new proprietary act. Under Mordechai, a kinyan is needed. This also helps explain Rambam's view that a loan can sometimes acquire in sale but not in kiddushin. When the debtor identifies particular property for payment and the creditor accepts it, that property can transfer as collection of the debt without a separate ordinary purchase kinyan. It is not that the abstract loan functions exactly like cash. Still, Rama appears to limit Mordechai's principle primarily to a borrower. He does not add this gloss to every law of an unpaid or paid custodian where later waiver of responsibility is discussed. A borrower has a proprietary use-right in the body of the object. Borrowing and rental for a day are like sale for that day. Torah's accident liability is not really a duty to guard against accidents, which cannot be guarded against; rather, Torah treats the borrowed object as effectively the borrower's for the term, so if it is destroyed it is his loss. Therefore, after the borrower has acquired that use-right, removing his accident liability can require a new kinyan to change the proprietary structure. An unpaid or paid custodian differs. Their responsibility is directly tied to guarding. If the owner later releases them from a particular level of guarding, that can operate as an immediate waiver of the custodial duty and need not resemble a contingent future release. This, in my view, is the best way to understand Rama's practical distinction.
