The broken pieces were stolen. Once a borrowed item breaks and is no longer fit for the borrowed use, the borrower's full borrower liability ends. He is at most a paid custodian of the remnants because he had earlier benefited from the object. Nimukei Yosef says that if the borrower tells the owner, "Take your broken pieces," and they are later completely lost, the loss is considered to have occurred in the owner's domain. This does not mean that before that statement the borrower remains liable for every accident as a borrower. The original borrowing has already ended because the object is no longer usable for the borrowed purpose. The rule is comparable to depreciation of a damaged carcass. The damaged property belongs to the owner, and the custodian's responsibility for the remnant is no longer the original high-level borrower responsibility. The fact that the custodian may bear the burden of physically dealing with the carcass does not keep him in the status of borrower. Before he tells the owner to take it, he is properly treated as a paid custodian because he benefited at the beginning of the relationship. After he expressly says, "Take your property," he is no longer even an unpaid custodian, like one who tells another to take his money. This also differs from an ordinary loan of money. Money was given to be spent and cannot be restored by pointing to the same coins. Therefore saying "Take your money" does not terminate the debt relationship in the same way. Accordingly, the practical rule is: after the borrowed object breaks, before notice he is at most a paid custodian of the remnants. After notice to take them, he has no custodial status at all. The fact that he may have to perform the physical burden of raising or dealing with the carcass does not change this conclusion.
