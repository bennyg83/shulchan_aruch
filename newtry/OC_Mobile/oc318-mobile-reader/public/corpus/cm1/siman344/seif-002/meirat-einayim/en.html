If the broken pieces were stolen. According to the printed text, the ruling specifically mentions theft. Although the borrowing terminated when the utensil broke and it was no longer fit for the purpose of the borrowing, the borrower must still pay the lender the value of the utensil that broke, and he remains a paid bailee over the broken pieces, as stated in siman 343. Therefore if they are stolen or lost he is liable. But if the fragments are taken from him by an unavoidable accident, he should be exempt because his primary guarding obligation ended. Nimukei Yosef, at the end of chapter HaShoel, writes that later authorities agree with Ramban that if the borrower tells the lender, "Take your broken pieces," and afterward they are destroyed by accident, it is as though the accident occurred in the lender's possession. This implies that if he did not say "take your broken pieces," he remains liable even for accidents. One could answer that Nimukei Yosef speaks where the utensil broke or the animal died during the term of the borrowing. There are then two reasons for stringency: the agreed term has not expired, and even though the utensil broke and is no longer fit for the borrowing, the status of bailee remains until the borrower tells the lender, "Come and take it; I no longer wish to hold the utensil or animal." Rama here could then be speaking where the term already expired. But this is somewhat forced, because ordinary breakage occurs through use and within the term, yet the wording implies liability specifically for theft or loss and exemption for an accident. Nimukei Yosef implies liability more broadly. It is also difficult that Nimukei Yosef's statement that "take it" helps against accidents seems to imply that it does not help against theft or loss, for the reason already stated: even after notifying the lender that he no longer wants the utensil, he remains a paid bailee. Darkei Moshe likewise writes this here. Yet Rama's gloss says that if the broken pieces are stolen or lost he is liable unless he told the lender, "Take yours," implying that this statement also exempts him from theft. This can perhaps be reconciled by distinguishing the form of "take it." Ramban's ruling cited above at the end of siman 120, which the Mechaber follows regarding a loan, says that if the borrower says, "Come and take your money," and the lender refuses, and the money is then stolen or lost, the borrower is exempt. In the Perishah, Derishah, and Sema at the end of siman 120, I explained that "come and take" there refers back to the preceding case: the borrower first offered the actual money, the lender refused to accept it, and the borrower then said, "I will return my money to my house and leave it tied up there; whenever you wish, come and take it." Only in such a case is the borrower exempt even from theft or loss. Similarly, Rama here may mean an equivalent form of "take yours," whereas Nimukei Yosef speaks of a simple statement, "come and take," which exempts from accidents but not from theft or loss, as the Tur likewise writes in siman 198. Still, this too is forced. Rama ordinarily does not state in his glosses a law for which he did not cite a source in Darkei Moshe, while Darkei Moshe here cites Nimukei Yosef precisely for the proposition that "take it" exempts only from accidents, not theft or loss. The gloss, by contrast, does not mention accidents and says that if the fragments are stolen or lost he is liable unless he said, "take yours." It therefore appears that the gloss should be emended to read: "he is liable, and even if they were taken by an unavoidable accident he is liable, unless he said, 'take yours.'" Meaning, once he says "take yours," he is exempt only from accidents. This makes all the sources consistent. Rama's ruling in siman 74, seif 3, that even after saying "take it" a borrower of money remains liable for accidents presents no difficulty, because a monetary loan does not have a point at which the guarding obligation terminates; the borrower remains liable for accidents until the loan money is actually repaid. Examine carefully.
