And he is not considered one who must swear but cannot swear, who therefore pays. The Sema explains that the borrower says, 'I do not know whether I caused any loss beyond this,' and that the lender does not assert a definite claim. I do not know why he required that the lender's claim be uncertain. Even if the lender asserts certainty, once the borrower tenders what he admits, his remaining liability is only for a rabbinic oath that he does not know, and he is not one who must swear but cannot swear. As explained repeatedly in siman 72 and elsewhere, he may shift that oath to the opposing party. Moreover, the case must concern a definite claim by the lender, because otherwise the borrower owes no oath at all, since one does not swear on an uncertain claim. It is also difficult to understand how the lender could swear and collect if he himself is uncertain. The Bach explains the Rosh on the basis that a custodian is not treated as one who must swear but cannot swear where he was not expected to know. That explanation is difficult, because the Rosh limits that principle to goods deposited in sacks, where people commonly do not know the contents. In an ordinary deposit, the Rosh expressly rules that one who says he does not know must pay. Perhaps the distinction applies where the depositor says he does not know the quantity or value, since ordinarily he should know that, whereas here the borrower had no reason to ascertain the value. Alternatively, the Bach may understand the Rosh as speaking only where the custodian mixed the deposited produce with his own. In any event, the Mechaber certainly does not follow the Bach, for in simanim 90 and 298 he rules that even regarding a sack one who cannot swear must pay, as I explained at length in siman 72. It therefore appears that here Shimon says that he caused no loss at all and that the article was already in this condition initially; the words 'and he damaged it' refer to Reuven's allegation.
