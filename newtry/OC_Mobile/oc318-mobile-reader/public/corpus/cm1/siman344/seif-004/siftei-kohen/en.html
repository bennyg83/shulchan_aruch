If there was no evidence there, etc. Ir Shushan writes that the Mechaber's words are confused, and the Sema also labors to reconcile them but leaves them difficult. Their difficulty arose because they assumed that where the lender says, 'I do not know which animal died,' the borrower must take a rabbinic oath. That is incorrect. It is an elementary and universally accepted rule that one does not impose even a rabbinic oath on an uncertain claim, even where the claimant says, 'I do not know whether you repaid me,' and the defendant also answers uncertainly, as explained in siman 75. This is certainly true where the claim is equivalent to, 'I do not know whether you owe me.' The Mechaber himself explains the matter in Bedek HaBayit. The Rambam lists three forms of the case, such as borrowing for half the day and renting for half, borrowing today and renting tomorrow, or borrowing one animal and renting another. If the keeper says he does not know, or the borrower says the rented animal died and the lender says he does not know, or both say they do not know, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Where there is no evidence, the keeper swears that the rented animal died or that he does not know and is exempt. Bedek HaBayit asks how an oath can be imposed on an uncertain claim and answers that since the keeper must take the custodians' oath, the additional matter is included by extension. Thus, where the lender asserts certainty, the keeper must take a rabbinic oath even without extension, for example if the lender accepts that the animal died naturally or witnesses establish this. Where the lender says he does not know, no independent rabbinic oath can be imposed; only if the keeper must swear that it died naturally may the uncertain matter be added by extension. The printed phrase, 'or this one says I do not know,' is plainly a scribal error and should read, 'or this one says the borrowed animal died and the other says I do not know.' This resolves all the Sema's objections. It also follows that Ir Shushan is wrong in saying that the Mechaber does not permit extension where the lender is uncertain. The Rambam expressly rules in his Commentary to the Mishna that extension applies even where the borrower says he does not know, and the Maggid Mishneh and Ritva likewise support this. The Rosh, however, rules that one does not extend an oath to a matter stated as uncertain, whether the lender or the keeper is uncertain, and the Tur follows him. The Tur's language here must therefore be limited to a definite allegation by the lender. Since the primary rules are stated in siman 75, the language here was abbreviated.
When the lender asserts a definite claim, etc. Ir Shushan explains this as a definite allegation that the borrower was negligent and that the animal did not die naturally. He therefore says that if the borrower does not know whether the borrowed or rented animal died, the extension renders him one who must swear but cannot swear. This is incorrect. If the lender definitely alleges negligence, the borrower does not owe the Torah custodians' oath at all. That oath is imposed precisely where the claimant is uncertain whether the custodian was negligent, because the Torah suspected that the custodian might be emboldened when the claimant does not know the facts. The Maggid Mishneh's own language shows that the principal claim is uncertain. Whenever the authorities say that the keeper swears that the animal died naturally, they mean that the lender does not know whether it died naturally or through negligence, as in the ordinary custodians' oath.
And by extension, etc., or that he does not know, etc. One should not object from siman 75, where the Mechaber cites the view that if a person answers 'I do not know' to an extended claim, he must pay because he cannot swear. The Maggid Mishneh, cited in siman 345, explains that here the principal claim itself is uncertain, for the lender does not definitely allege that the animal failed to die naturally. Even though the lender asserts certainty on the extended issue, this is not the ordinary case of one who must swear but cannot swear; the borrower may say he does not know and is exempt. This is the Mechaber's view. The Ramban in Milchamot also appears to agree. The Baal HaMaor limits the Mishna's ruling that the claimant receives the larger item to clothing and not to a slave, relying on the case of two cows where inability to answer the extended claim does not generate payment. The Ramban rejects his distinction regarding the slave but does not reject his reliance on the cows, indicating that he agrees where the principal claim is uncertain. The Rosh, however, rules that no uncertain matter may be added by extension at all, and the Mechaber himself follows that position in siman 94. His rulings there and here therefore require study.
