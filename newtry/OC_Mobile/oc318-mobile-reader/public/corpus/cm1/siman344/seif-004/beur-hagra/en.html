The borrower, etc.; the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. The Mishna follows Sumchus, but the Sages always apply the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Bava Kamma 46a calls this a great principle, applying even to an injured party. This is certainly so where both claims are definite, where some hold that even Sumchus does not disagree. All the more so where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor definite, where everyone agrees Sumchus does not dispute, and likewise where both are uncertain. The categories therefore proceed from an uncertain claimant and definite defendant, to both uncertain, both definite, and finally a definite claimant and uncertain defendant.
And if he did not, etc.; or one says, 'I do not know,' etc. The text contains an error. It should read: 'or one says, it was borrowed, and the other says, I do not know.' The ruling is based on Rambam, although Rambam mentions an oath through extension only when both claims are definite. Beit Yosef concludes that Rambam can be reconciled only according to the Shulchan Aruch's formulation. The Shulchan Aruch holds that the rule, 'because he cannot swear, he must pay,' applies only to the principal oath. If he can swear on the principal claim but cannot answer the extended claim, he does not pay. Nevertheless, he swears through extension that he does not know. When no extended oath exists, he takes a rabbinic oath of denial. See simanim 3 and 88.
And when, etc. Without an extended oath, no oath is imposed on an uncertain claim, whether the renter answers definitely or uncertainly. Through extension, however, he swears as explained above. The same applies in the opening case if an extended oath exists. Shach writes likewise. This follows Rambam's approach. Tosafot, however, holds that an extended oath applies only when both parties plead definitely. If the borrower is uncertain and the lender definite, the borrower takes only a rabbinic oath; if the lender is uncertain, no oath applies. Tur and the Shulchan Aruch therefore require study.
(Supplement) When, etc.; and when, etc. Beit Yosef's interpretation of Rambam is difficult, because Rambam divided the cases into two clauses. His wording indicates Tosafot's view, that where either party is uncertain there is no extended oath. The Shulchan Aruch's ruling therefore requires study. One may answer that this uncertainty differs from ordinary uncertain claims because the lender had no reason to know, and custodial oaths are themselves imposed on uncertain claims. See simanim 7, 8, 50, 75, 88, 94, and 223. (End of supplement.)
