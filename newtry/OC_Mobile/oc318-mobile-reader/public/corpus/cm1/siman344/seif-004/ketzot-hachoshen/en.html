Or he does not know. Nimukei Yosef asks about the Mishnah where it is uncertain whether the animal died during the period of borrowing or renting. If the borrower had first borrowed and then rented the same animal, should this not be like one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," because the stronger borrower liability came first? The answer can be that where the very fact creating the later payment obligation is uncertain and that uncertainty is objectively present in the case, we do not always treat it like an ordinary debtor who knows he once owed and is uncertain whether he repaid. Rashba in Gittin distinguishes ordinary uncertainty about repayment from a case where a genuine doubt was born before us concerning the underlying status. In the latter, the possessor can retain the money. This helps explain Rambam, who exempts in the cases of half day borrowing and half day rental. It also resolves a difficulty in the comparison to Sumchus. Where borrowing and rental occurred successively in one animal, the lender has a presumption of borrower liability, while the borrower has possession of his money. Depending on the configuration there can be competing presumptions. In the cases of two animals, the structure differs because there is no single prior borrower status attaching to the same animal. The essential point is that not every uncertainty following a known earlier obligation is automatically "I do not know whether I repaid." Where the uncertainty concerns which legal status applied at the very moment of loss, and that doubt is inherent in the event itself, the ordinary rule of possession can prevail. This also shows why the doctrine that one who cannot take a custodial oath must pay does not apply to every unresolved question. If the facts are known and only their legal classification is uncertain, the custodian swears to the facts he knows and the monetary doubt remains with the possessor.
