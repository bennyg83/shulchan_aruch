Or he borrowed it today and rented it tomorrow. Nimukei Yosef cites the students of Rashba that the intervening night follows local practice. If borrowers ordinarily keep such animals overnight after the borrowing period, he remains a borrower that night and is liable for accidents. If they are not ordinarily kept overnight, he has only the law of a renter during the night, exempt from unavoidable accidents but liable for theft and loss.
The lender says the borrowed one died, etc. The ruling proceeds in reverse order from the cases previously listed. Where he borrowed one animal and rented another and one died, the lender says the borrowed one died and the surviving animal is the rented one. Where he borrowed today and rented tomorrow, the lender says it died during the borrowing day. Where he borrowed for half a day and rented for half, the lender says it died during the borrowing period. The borrower responds that he does not know when, or which animal, died.
Or the custodian says the rented one died, etc. Here too the clauses correspond in reverse order. If one animal was rented and one borrowed and only one died, the borrower says the dead animal was the rented one and the borrowed animal is the one still alive. The same structure applies to the other cases.
The custodian swears that the rented one died. This means a hesset oath, because he denies the claim entirely. Even if he says he does not know whether the rented one died, it resembles one who says, 'You lent me one hundred and I do not know whether you ever lent it,' rather than 'I know I owed you and do not know whether I repaid.' If the rented animal died, he never became liable for its unavoidable death, while the borrowed animal is returned intact. The Kesef Mishneh likewise explains that these are hesset oaths.
When the lender says, 'I do not know,' the custodian swears the custodians' oath that it died naturally, and by rolling oath swears that the rented one died or that he does not know. The Mechaber follows his own approach. The Rambam and Tur imply that a Torah rolling oath concerning which animal died is imposed specifically when both parties make definite claims. The Rosh states explicitly that where the defendant's response is uncertain, such a Torah rolling oath is not imposed, because rolling oaths are learned from Sotah, where the woman herself makes a definite denial. A claimant's doubtful assertion can support a rolling oath only where there are grounds making the suspicion substantial. Here there is no stronger reason to think the borrowed animal died than the rented one. Yet the Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah appears to impose the rolling oath even when both parties say they do not know. The Mechaber evidently followed that. To reconcile the Mechaber's own wording, the opening clause where only hesset is imposed must speak where the lender accepts that the animal died naturally, so no custodial oath exists to which anything can be rolled. The later clauses speak where he does not accept that and therefore a custodial oath is already required. There is nevertheless difficulty in the printed wording and a possible textual omission.
Or he does not know. Why do we not say that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay? The Maggid Mishneh answers that the principal claim upon which the custodian's oath is imposed is itself doubtful, because the lender is not asserting with certainty that the animal did not die naturally. Although the matter rolled onto the oath may be asserted definitely, we do not apply the rule of payment for inability to swear in this configuration. The Rosh and Tur do not need this answer, because they hold that no rolling oath is imposed where the defendant himself says he does not know, and they also hold generally that inability to swear on a rolled matter does not trigger payment.
