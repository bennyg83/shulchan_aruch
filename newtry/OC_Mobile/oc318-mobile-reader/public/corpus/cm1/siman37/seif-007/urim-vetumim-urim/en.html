He must pay its value. Otherwise we are concerned that he may testify falsely in order to preserve the property for a creditor whose lien predates the gift he gave to his partner. That creditor could seize the property despite the gift because his lien came first, thereby preventing the witness from remaining a "wicked borrower who does not repay." If the debtor possessed other property, the creditor could not seize encumbered property and the concern would not arise. The case therefore concerns one who has no other property, and he prefers not to be deemed a wicked borrower. Toward his partner he is not regarded as wicked, because the gift was given without warranty. But if the adverse claimant takes the property, the creditor will later have nowhere to collect. If, however, he accepts warranty toward his partner, there is no further concern, because if his creditor takes the property from the partner he will then be a wicked borrower toward the partner as well. Sema. See Tumim, where I explain this further and conclude that the Tur means he should not merely accept ordinary warranty toward this partner. Rather, he must undertake to pay him from his own cash and not collect that reimbursement from his encumbered property. See there.
