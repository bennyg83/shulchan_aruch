Reuven who holds a debt document, etc. See Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 14, concerning partners Reuven and Shimon who divided merchandise into two equal lots and separated, while both lots remained in Reuven's possession. They agreed that whichever of them first found a purchaser for his lot would sell it, and the remaining lot would belong to the other partner. One lot was sold on credit, the purchaser took it, and the second lot remained with Reuven. The market price later rose. Reuven claimed that Shimon had sold to the purchaser, so the remaining lot was Reuven's and appreciated while in his ownership. Shimon claimed that Reuven had made the sale, so the remaining lot belonged to Shimon and appreciated in his ownership. Shimon brought two witnesses that Reuven had sold, one of whom was the purchaser himself. The questioner wondered whether the purchaser was an interested witness because he had bought on credit, and perhaps one creditor was easier for him to deal with than the other. He cited Shach, siman 123, seif katan 24, that we do not consider a witness interested merely because one person is easier for him and another harder where the witness himself owes money; that concern applies only when the witness would need to extract money. The questioner objected that Shach's statement seems difficult, because siman 37, seif 11, and siman 66, seif 22, expressly indicate that even where the witness owes payment we consider him interested on the ground that one claimant is easier for him. He therefore wished to disqualify the purchaser from testifying. Noda BiYehuda answered that there is no contradiction. The Shach himself writes there that he has a different concern, concluding that with a guarantor people generally do not call him wicked if he fails to pay. If even a guarantor, whom the borrower himself asked to guarantee the debt, is not popularly called wicked for nonpayment, certainly one is not called wicked for failing to pay someone who merely purchased the debt, who never lent him anything and did not acquire the claim at his request. Thus the Mechaber correctly rules in siman 37 and siman 66 that Shimon may be interested because one creditor is easier: if he fails to pay the later purchaser he will not be viewed as a wicked borrower, whereas if he fails to pay the original lender he will be publicly regarded as a wicked borrower. A practical consequence is this: If Reuven held a debt document against Shimon and transferred it to Levi by writing and delivery before two qualified witnesses, and Levi later transferred it to Yehudah by writing and delivery with Shimon himself serving as one of the witnesses to Levi's transfer, Shimon is not an interested witness. He is no more regarded as a wicked borrower toward Yehudah than toward Levi, since Levi did not lend him the money either. The concern that one is easier and the other harder is not applied when the witness is the debtor, as the Shach states. In the present case, the purchaser owes one of the two partners in any event. If he never pays, he is wicked toward the person to whom he truly owes. What difference does it make whether that is Reuven or Shimon? If he intends to pay, it likewise makes no difference to whom. Although one could imagine minor differences, the Ramban's underlying reasoning cited by Shach is sound: if his concern is not to be wicked, why would he give false testimony? The real concern is how he will be viewed publicly as a wicked borrower. Here each partner claims that he himself did not lend to the purchaser, so according to their own claims the purchaser would not be viewed as a wicked borrower toward either. According to the purchaser's own testimony identifying his seller, if he later fails to pay the person he acknowledged, he will be viewed as wicked. He therefore gains nothing by identifying one rather than the other. The purchaser is thus qualified to testify. See there.
