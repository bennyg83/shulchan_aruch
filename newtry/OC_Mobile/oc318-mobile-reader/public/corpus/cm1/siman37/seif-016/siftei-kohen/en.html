Reuven may testify, etc. Rambam, chapter 16 of Hilchot Edut, writes that this applies only when Shimon recognizes that the cow and garment are Reuven's. The Mechaber makes no such distinction. One cannot explain that Rif and the Rosh ruled like Rav Zevid, because according to Beit Yosef, Rambam also rules like Rav Zevid and Rif and the Rosh do not disagree with him. Rather, Beit Yosef explains that Rambam is discussing an ordinary sale made without an express disclaimer of warranty. In such a case Rav Zevid concedes that the buyer must recognize the animal as the seller's own, the equivalent of recognizing it as the offspring of his donkey. This is a simple point, and all authorities certainly agree. Therefore, when the Mechaber here expressly writes that Reuven sold without warranty, he need not add the condition that the buyer recognizes the item as Reuven's. In my humble opinion, however, Beit Yosef's explanation is not satisfactory. Rambam explicitly discusses land sold without warranty, and then continues with the sale of a cow or garment and distinguishes between land and movable property. This implies that the cow and garment case is analogous to the land case and was likewise sold without warranty. Moreover, the reason that in an ordinary sale of a cow or garment the seller cannot testify is that warranty is presumed even if not written, as Kesef Mishneh states explicitly. This is indeed our ruling even for movable property, as Rambam writes at the beginning of chapter 19 of Hilchot Mechirah and the Tur and Mechaber write below at the beginning of siman 225. If so, why does Rambam write in chapter 16 of Hilchot Edut that where the seller owns land he may not testify because perhaps he pledged the movable property together with land to his creditor and wishes to preserve it for that creditor? Since he remains liable in warranty to the buyer, he would equally have the interest of avoiding the status of a wicked borrower who does not repay his purchaser. Lechem Mishneh raises this difficulty as well. Lechem Mishneh answers that Rambam in chapter 19 of Hilchot Mechirah, law 8, means that the seller explicitly stipulated that there would be no warranty at all, even for theft. This does not appear correct. Rambam's plain language does not indicate such an exceptional stipulation, and it is difficult to say that Rambam rules like Rav Pappa against Rif, whom he generally follows. The Bach's explanation, that Rambam disqualifies the seller as a witness because he does not want to become embroiled in litigation and resentment with the buyer, is also strained. If that were Rambam's reason, the essential basis of the disqualification is missing from his words. Rather, it appears to me that Rambam certainly rules like Rav Zevid, as Maggid Mishneh also writes in chapter 19 of Hilchot Mechirah. Rambam understood from several features of the sugya that Rav Zevid disagrees with Rav Pappa specifically regarding land, but concedes regarding movable property. The Gemara initially challenges the matter straightforwardly from Rav Pappa. After establishing the case as one where the buyer recognizes the animal as the offspring of the seller's donkey, it then says, "Rav Zevid says, etc.," without a conjunction. Rif too cites the establishment concerning recognition at length, which indicates that he regarded it as legally significant, and he changes the Gemara's wording. The Gemara says, "Rav Zevid says: Even if it is found not to be his, he does not return to him," whereas Rif writes, "Even if the field is found not to be his." Why add the word "field"? Rif further writes that Rav Zevid disagrees with Rav Pappa. These details imply that Rav Zevid disagrees only with Rav Pappa's ruling concerning land, perhaps because a person may knowingly buy land even for temporary possession, or for another reason, but does not reject the establishment concerning movable property. Rambam's own language in chapter 19 of Hilchot Mechirah is precise in the same way. He first writes generally that one who sells land, a slave, or other movable property is liable in warranty even if the buyer merely purchased without an express stipulation. He then writes specifically, "One who sells land to his fellow and stipulates that the seller shall bear no warranty, even if it is later definitely discovered that this land was stolen and it is taken from the buyer, the seller owes nothing." Why does he repeat "land" rather than saying "land or movable property" as before, or simply "one who sells"? The repetition indicates that this no warranty rule even for theft applies specifically to land and not to movable property. This is how I understand Rambam. I later found that Gidulei Terumah, gate 43, part 2, page 150, explains Rambam similarly and discusses additional aspects of this issue. The Rosh, however, omits the word "land" in citing Rav Zevid and does not say that Rav Zevid disagrees with Rav Pappa. His language implies that Rav Zevid disagrees also regarding a cow and garment. The Tur accordingly rules that even for a cow or garment Reuven may testify if he sold without warranty. Rashbam explicitly explains so, Nimukei Yosef writes likewise, and Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 16 of Hilchot Edut, says the same in the name of Avi Asaf. In any event, all these authorities teach that mere concern over a purchaser's resentment does not render the seller an interested witness, contrary to the Sema, seif katan 36. Nowadays, etc. The Mechaber writes this on the basis of Bedek HaBayit in the name of the Rosh and Tur below at the beginning of siman 60. I already expressed surprise at the Mechaber there, siman 60, seif katan 4. This enactment mentioned by the Rosh and Tur is not cited by the other authorities. On the contrary, other poskim repeatedly rule in actual cases that creditors collect from movable property. The Rosh and Tur themselves wrote their rule only according to the known custom of the places in which they lived. Here too Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri write that nowadays it is common for a person to admit that he owns land, and therefore the seller cannot testify, which shows that even in their era creditors did seize movable property. Ramban's novellae also imply this. Nevertheless, the Mechaber's rule is correct in a locale where the custom is that creditors do not collect from movable property that the debtor has sold.
