Regarding the claim that he purchased it. See the Sema, who writes that even if he claims that Reuven himself stole it, he is not considered interested merely because he wishes not to be called a robber, because with movable property he is not called a robber for that reason. The Taz asks from the first chapter of Bava Metzia 16a concerning a robber who afterward purchased the property from its original owners. The Gemara says that what the first seller sold to the second buyer included every right that would later come into the seller's hand. Why? Mar Zutra says that the robber wishes to maintain his credibility; Rav Ashi says that he wishes not to be called a robber. What practical difference is there between them? If movable property were different, the Gemara should have said that the practical difference is movable property. This would show that even regarding movable property the concern not to be called a robber applies. The matter can be resolved, for with movable property the relevant case cannot arise at all. The Gemara there asks until when he wishes to maintain his credibility and answers, until the matter has been brought to judgment. We rule that when a robber took property and the owners did not despair, neither party can consecrate it: the robber because it is not his, and the owner because it is not in his possession. As we explained in siman 354, this is not limited to outright robbery. Even a deposit that requires litigation for recovery, because the possessor has some claim concerning it, cannot be consecrated or sold by the owner until after judgment. Only a deposit that the custodian does not deny can be consecrated, because wherever it is located it remains regarded as being in the owner's domain and the owner may take it whenever he wishes. Where judicial enforcement is required because the possessor asserts some claim, however, the owner cannot consecrate it. The case in Bava Metzia concerns a buyer who did not know the item was stolen, as Nimukei Yosef explains. In such a case the purchaser cannot have bought merely in reliance on the robber's credibility, and because he did not know it was stolen, litigation is certainly required to establish the theft. It is therefore unlike an undisputed deposit that the owner can retrieve at will. Consequently, the robber cannot afterward purchase the movable item from the original owner, because the owner is trying to transfer something not presently in his legal possession. The case of the robber who later purchases from the original owner therefore exists only with land, which the original owner can sell even while another person holds it. This is why the Gemara did not state movable property as the practical difference between the explanations.
