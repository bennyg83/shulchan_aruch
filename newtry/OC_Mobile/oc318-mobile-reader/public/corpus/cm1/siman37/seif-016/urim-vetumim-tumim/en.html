That he purchased it from a robber, meaning... Sema understands the seller, Reuven, to have been the robber himself, with Shimon purchasing from him. Perishah supports this from Ramban, cited by Tur. Ramban says the case must be one where takanat hashuk does not apply, or where Reuven himself bought the object from a robber. If Reuven himself had stolen it, he would have to reimburse because of takanat hashuk. Perishah asks: why is that additional monetary reason needed? Even without it, Reuven should be unable to testify because he does not want to be called a robber. One cannot answer that the moral concern is irrelevant for movables. On the contrary, a person shown to have stolen or misappropriated a cow or garment can be disqualified from testimony. The stigma is serious. Bach therefore says Ramban does not accept Tur's broader concern about avoiding the title of wicked person. That is too difficult, since Tur cites Ramban without indicating such a disagreement. There is no contradiction. Ramban could have used the concern of being called a robber in an ordinary case. But consider one who had previously been publicly established as a robber and disqualified by beit din, and later repented and was restored to qualification. Now a claimant says that during the old period of robbery he stole this cow or garment and sold it. The seller no longer has a meaningful interest in avoiding the old label. He was already publicly known then as a robber and has since repented. Nevertheless, takanat hashuk can still create a direct monetary liability. If the purchaser must be reimbursed, the seller may bear the loss. Thus Ramban gives the monetary reason because it applies even where the reputational reason does not. Likewise, if the claimant says the purchaser acquired the object from the son of a robber and the son inherited assets carrying his father's obligations, the son may have no personal concern that his father be called a robber. But he can still bear monetary liability under takanat hashuk and therefore be interested. Accordingly, Ramban and Tur need not disagree.
Reuven can, etc. Gidulei Terumah, Shach, and before them Perishah understand Rambam to hold that with movables, unlike land, even a sale without warranty is rescinded if the object is found not to belong to the seller. They follow Rav Pappa: a person may sometimes buy land for one day even without warranty, but not ordinary movables. The conclusion is correct, but Shach's proofs are not compelling. Shach says the Gemara asks simply from Rav Pappa's rule without saying "this works only according to Rav Pappa," implying that Rav Pappa's view is accepted. He also infers from the wording "and R. Zevid said" that R. Zevid's statement is connected to Rav Pappa rather than an independent disagreement. These deductions are weak. Ramban explains that where takanat hashuk applies, the Gemara's question does not work in the ordinary way. If the claimant says the purchaser himself stole the object, and the seller testifies that the purchaser bought it from him, then absent the seller's testimony there may be no proof the purchaser ever acquired it from the seller. If the claimant says the seller bought it from a robber, then under takanat hashuk the claimant reimburses the purchaser and receives the object. The purchaser may therefore have no claim against the seller. Thus the Gemara's question, "even without warranty, if it is found not to be his he returns against the seller," is not naturally directed to the movable baraita at all. It is better read as directed to Shmuel's land case. If so, the subsequent answers of Rav Pappa and R. Zevid are likewise discussing land. Shach's linguistic proofs therefore fall away. Still, Rambam's distinction can be derived differently. Since the entire Talmudic give-and-take is about land, we need only say Rav Pappa and R. Zevid disagree there. There is no reason to extend R. Zevid's leniency to movables. Accordingly, all can agree that where an ordinary movable is found not to belong to the seller, the purchaser returns against him even without an express warranty. I suggested elsewhere an additional proof from the structure of the movable baraita. Suppose the claimant seeks a cow or garment as stolen property and Reuven plus one other witness testify that the object belongs to the purchaser. If the purchaser himself knows clearly that he bought it from Reuven and that the claimant's witnesses are false, then even if the claimant first succeeds and receives the object under takanat hashuk, the purchaser's own knowledge can combine with the independent witness who testified alongside Reuven to protect Reuven from a reimbursement claim. After the purchaser has already received his takanat hashuk money, he is no longer interested and can testify concerning the earlier sale. Thus the seller can sometimes avoid monetary exposure if the purchaser recognizes the truth. But if the purchaser does not recognize the object's provenance, then the seller's testimony is plainly interested because, according to Rav Pappa's rule for movables, the purchaser can return against him if the object is found not to be his. This suggests the movable baraita's permission must involve a case in which the purchaser recognizes the seller's title, or an equivalent circumstance. It also explains Tosafot's concern that a purchaser may harbor resentment. Recognition does not necessarily mean the purchaser has legally conclusive evidence. He may personally trust the seller and believe the challenger false, yet lack admissible proof. If the claimant later prevails, the purchaser may still have a claim unless he had previously acknowledged the seller's title in a manner binding himself. Ramban states explicitly that recognition of the seller's title should occur before the dispute arises. Otherwise the purchaser might admit title merely in order to qualify the seller as a witness. That distinction is important. A later acknowledgment motivated by litigation can itself create suspicion. One might still ask whether the seller remains interested because the purchaser owes others. If the seller's testimony preserves the object in the purchaser's hands, perhaps the purchaser's creditors can reach it, whereas if the claimant takes it they may seek reimbursement from the seller through R. Natan's rule. According to Tosafot, who do not ordinarily fear unknown creditors, this is not a concern. According to Rashbam and Nimukei Yosef, who do fear creditors, one can answer that where the purchaser had previously acknowledged the seller's title, that acknowledgment can operate like waiver toward derivative creditors to the extent allowed below in simanim 47 and 86. In any event, Rambam's substantive distinction remains sound. The practical rule is: For land, a purchaser may sometimes knowingly accept the risk of title failure and buy "for one day," so no-warranty language can prevent recourse. For ordinary movables, a purchaser does not ordinarily throw away his money that way. If the object is found not to belong to the seller, he generally returns against the seller even without express warranty. Therefore a seller of movables is ordinarily interested and cannot testify unless an independent circumstance removes that recourse, such as a prior binding recognition of title or the operation of takanat hashuk in a way that fully satisfies the purchaser. This gives Rambam a strong basis without relying on Shach's linguistic proofs. There is also a subtle point in Ramban's treatment of takanat hashuk. If the claimant says that the purchaser himself stole the object, while Reuven now says the purchaser bought it from him, Reuven's testimony does more than establish the seller-purchaser transaction. It directly defeats the claimant's accusation that the purchaser was the thief. If Reuven remained silent, the purchaser might have no admissible proof that Reuven ever sold it to him. The buyer's later attempt to recover from Reuven could therefore fail for lack of proof even if the claimant wins. This is another reason the Gemara's simple question from Rav Pappa cannot be directed automatically to every movable case. Conversely, where there are independent witnesses that the purchaser bought from Reuven, then if the claimant wins by proving the object was stolen property, the purchaser's recourse against Reuven becomes direct under Rambam's movable rule. In that case Reuven is plainly interested and cannot testify. Thus the factual posture of the movable claim matters greatly. This explains why Ramban supplies several alternate configurations rather than a single universal rule. One can also understand Rambam from commercial expectation. Land purchases are often speculative. A buyer may knowingly accept questionable title or limited-duration enjoyment and discount the price accordingly. Hence Rav Zevid can say a person may buy land "for a day" even without recourse. Ordinary movable purchases are different. A person buying a cow, garment, or tool ordinarily expects ownership of the object, not merely temporary use until a superior owner appears. Unless the contract says otherwise expressly, finding that the seller had no title is a fundamental failure of the sale. This commercial distinction supports Rambam's rule independent of the exact wording of the Gemara. It also explains why no-warranty language can operate differently in land and movables. In land, "without warranty" can reflect an understood allocation of title risk. In movables, ordinary expectations remain that the seller is selling his own object unless the buyer unmistakably accepted otherwise. The concern about a purchaser's creditors can also be narrowed. Where the purchaser has already acknowledged before witnesses, prior to litigation, that the object was truly the seller's and that the present claimant is wrong, that acknowledgment may bind the purchaser personally. If the purchaser later loses to the claimant, his own prior acknowledgment can defeat his reimbursement claim against the seller. A derivative creditor under R. Natan ordinarily cannot stand in a better position than the debtor whose claim he seeks to enforce, subject to the laws of prejudicing third parties. Thus, at least in many configurations, the prior acknowledgment removes not only the purchaser's own interest but also the indirect creditor concern. This is another reason Ramban insists the recognition occur before the dispute. If it is made only afterward, it may not prejudice existing creditors and may be viewed as strategic. There remains a difficulty from the rule concerning an agent whose oath is waived, discussed below in siman 121. There, even if the debtor releases the agent from an oath, Sema says the agent can remain interested because he receives the benefit of that release and may testify falsely in gratitude. Why not say the same here? The purchaser's recognition of the seller's title relieves the seller of possible reimbursement liability. Perhaps the seller will now testify falsely because of that benefit. The cases differ. In the agency case, without the waiver the agent faces a present oath obligation. The debtor confers a new concrete benefit upon him solely in the context of litigation. Here, if the purchaser genuinely recognized the seller's title before any challenge arose, the recognition is not a new favor designed to obtain testimony. It reflects the original commercial understanding of the sale. We do not suspect collusion from an ordinary prior acknowledgment merely because it later proves beneficial. This distinction is supported by Ramban's explicit requirement that the recognition precede the challenge. Another distinction is possible. Where a person simply says, "I trust this agent" without an objective reason, a litigation-related waiver can be suspicious. Where he gives a substantive basis for recognition, such as long personal knowledge of the seller's ownership, the acknowledgment is grounded in fact rather than favor. This is analogous to the distinction in siman 121 between unsupported trust and reliance based on concrete knowledge. Accordingly, the requirement of prior recognition is not a technical formality. It ensures that the removal of interest is genuine and not manufactured for testimony. With these distinctions, Rambam's rule becomes coherent in both the Talmudic and commercial framework.
Where the claimant, etc. A difficulty remains. If the claimant says the seller himself merely bought the object from a robber, then even though takanat hashuk requires the claimant to reimburse the purchaser, the purchaser will already receive his money from the claimant. Why then does the opening of the seif emphasize that the seller sold without warranty? Even if there was warranty, the seller should be able to testify because the purchaser suffers no loss and has no reason to return against him. The same question exists in Tosafot, who say Shmuel's land rule applies all the more where the seller gave warranty, but the cow-and-garment baraita permits testimony specifically without warranty. One cannot answer by simply establishing everything with warranty and saying takanat hashuk protects the purchaser of movables, because then the land case would teach almost nothing: where warranty exists and land is taken, the seller is obviously a direct litigant. Nor would the contrast between land and movables be formulated naturally. The correct distinction is that takanat hashuk generally reimburses the purchaser only what he paid. Suppose the purchaser bought the cow for twenty and by the time of the claim it is worth thirty. The true owner pays the twenty under takanat hashuk and recovers the animal. If the seller gave warranty, however, he may be liable for the value at the time of dispossession, namely thirty, as discussed below in siman 116. The seller therefore still has ten of direct exposure. He is interested in preserving the animal in the purchaser's hands and cannot testify. If there was no warranty, the purchaser receives his purchase price from the claimant and has no further claim against the seller. Then the seller may testify. Accordingly, the no-warranty limitation is meaningful even where takanat hashuk applies. If the object has not appreciated and the purchaser receives from the claimant everything the seller would otherwise have owed under warranty, the seller may indeed have no remaining monetary interest even though a warranty was written. This also explains why warranty clauses remain important generally even where Rav Pappa says a purchaser can return when title fails. Rav Pappa's rescission gives back the purchase price; an express warranty can cover additional loss, including appreciation where the law recognizes it. Thus Tosafot and Mechaber's wording is precise. The appreciation distinction also clarifies why the Gemara and poskim continue to care about express warranty even though some forms of title failure produce rescission automatically. Automatic rescission generally restores the transaction to its starting point: the buyer returns the object and recovers the price paid. Warranty is broader. It can obligate the seller for losses arising from later dispossession according to the value and legal rules at the time of taking. Therefore, even where Rav Pappa says "if it is found not to be his, he returns against him," this does not make a warranty clause redundant. The no-warranty sale can still differ materially from a warranty sale when the asset appreciates, when expenses are recoverable, or when additional encumbrance consequences arise. This is why Tosafot can say Shmuel's land rule applies all the more with warranty while the movable baraita still specifies no warranty. The two bodies of law are addressing different layers of seller liability. There is also a reason the Gemara may prefer the no-warranty formulation even where the object has not actually appreciated. A legal rule should be stated in a form free of financial interest in all ordinary realizations of the case. If warranty could create interest whenever the object appreciates, the witness is not categorically qualified merely because in the particular case the current market value happens to equal the purchase price. The baraita therefore uses the cleaner no-warranty case. In an actual litigation, however, if the court knows there is warranty but also knows with certainty that takanat hashuk fully reimburses the purchaser for every amount recoverable from the seller and no additional liability remains, then the seller's interest may indeed disappear. The law of witness interest follows actual benefit, not labels alone. Thus the conceptual distinction and the practical rule are consistent. There is also another way to understand why the Gemara did not simply establish the entire baraita with warranty. If the land case were merely one of warranty, the seller's disqualification would be obvious because he is directly liable if the land is taken. The baraita's contrast between land and movables would teach very little. By speaking of a no-warranty sale, the Gemara teaches the stronger point that land can still create testimonial interest through indirect lien and creditor consequences even where the seller has no ordinary contractual warranty obligation. Movables, by contrast, can be different because takanat hashuk may reimburse the purchaser and remove the seller's exposure. This is a meaningful contrast. It also explains why the Gemara's answer of immediate purchase and resale matters. Where the movable was bought and sold immediately, there may have been no opportunity for ordinary liens or creditor claims to attach. The only remaining question is whether title failure itself returns against the seller and how takanat hashuk affects that claim. Land has a richer lien structure and therefore can create interest even without express warranty. Accordingly, one should not read "without warranty" as saying that warranty is irrelevant. It identifies the baseline case in which the seller is free of direct contractual recourse, allowing the Gemara to isolate the indirect sources of interest. Where warranty exists, the seller's disqualification is even more readily understood unless the court can establish that the purchaser will be fully reimbursed from another source and no appreciation or additional loss remains. Thus the formulation of the baraita and Tosafot's language are both precise.
And Yehudah can, etc. Ramban gives several cases in which takanat hashuk does not apply; see Tur. Another simple case is this. The claimant brings two witnesses that the object is his, but one of those witnesses is related to Reuven. Their testimony cannot obligate Reuven to reimburse money because, regarding him, one of the witnesses is disqualified by kinship. Reuven and another witness then come to disqualify the claimant's second witness, who is unrelated to Reuven. If they succeed, only one valid witness remains for the claimant and the entire claim fails. In that configuration Reuven has no direct monetary interest and can testify.
