And concerning charitable endowments. See Chatam Sofer responsa, Choshen Mishpat, siman 38, concerning a man from the town of Semri who died in the town of Kat. Two groups of witnesses testified that he had directed that one thousand gold pieces from his estate be placed in a permanent fund whose profits would support Torah study for poor children. The witnesses disagreed over the destination: the Semri witnesses said he directed that it be given to Semri, while the Kat witnesses said he directed it to Kat. Chatam Sofer writes that at first glance all the testimony appears void because each group is interested, as Bava Batra 43a states that if one says, "Give a maneh to the poor of my city," the matter is not judged by local judges and proof is not brought from local residents, even where each resident's share is fixed. Although Beit Yosef at the end of siman 37 cites Rosh and Rashba that communal witnesses testify about enactments and endowments despite being interested because that is the accepted custom, it appears that this applies only against members of their own city. For example, if a city resident dedicated a vessel to the synagogue, local residents are believed to extract it from him or his heirs, because the townspeople associated with one another on the understanding that they would trust one another despite personal interest, analogous to accepting an otherwise disqualified person as trustworthy. But to testify against another city they are plainly disqualified. Shev Yaakov, siman 3, writes the same and proves it from the Rosh. I cited this above in siman 7, seif 12, seif katan 26, and see what I wrote above in seif 19, seif katan 11. In the present case, Kat residents are disqualified from testifying in favor of their city against Semri, and vice versa. Since they are interested in part of the testimony, all of their testimony is void, following Mordechai in chapter Zeh Borer as cited and accepted by Shach in siman 34, seif katan 28, that where the defect is personal interest we do not divide the witness's statement. Consequently, if both towns possess an existing Torah-study fund, the testimony would be void and there would be no enforceable endowment. If, however, neither town currently has such a fund, the witnesses are not personally interested and their testimony is valid, as explained in Nimukei Yosef and Beit Yosef at the end of siman 37. As for the contradiction between the two groups regarding the destination, both groups agree that he established a permanent fund and disagree only where it should be given. This resembles Rabbi Nachman's rule in Bava Batra 31b, also codified below in siman 146, seif 23, that contradictory witnesses concerning ancestry do not necessarily contradict the underlying ownership, and Ketubot 22b where both groups testify that the woman was married. Here too both groups testify to the permanent fund. Therefore one thousand gold pieces must be separated from the estate for that purpose. As to where it should be given, Chatam Sofer rules that it should go to Semri. First, as to the location there are two witnesses against two, so the money remains with the default destination of Semri. Yoreh De'ah 258:5 rules that one who says "Give a maneh to the poor" is presumed to mean the poor of his own city. There is also a strong estimate of intent: a person does not ordinarily abandon the poor of his own city to give to another; see Shach, Yoreh De'ah 251, seif katan 8. Second, from the questioner's letter it appears that the two groups were not present together with the dying man but came at different times, and the Semri residents were the later group. If so there is no contradiction at all. He could first have directed that the money go to Kat and later retracted and redirected it to Semri, since a deathly ill person may retract his gifts. Chatam Sofer continues: Once we have established that the testimony need not be treated as contradictory, then even if both places do have Torah-study funds, so that Kat residents are disqualified because of personal interest, the Semri residents need not thereby be disqualified from testifying about the deceased, because he was a member of their own town and their communal association was formed on the understanding that they would be trusted against him in matters not affecting another community. In other words, once the Kat testimony has already been invalidated for interest, the Semri testimony no longer harms Kat. It follows that if Kat residents were qualified because Kat had no Torah-study fund but Semri residents alone were interested, Semri's testimony that they were the later witnesses and that the dying man retracted would not help, because then their testimony would affect another city. The word "even" in Chatam Sofer's formulation is therefore not entirely precise. His intent is that not only where everyone is qualified, and certainly where Kat witnesses are disqualified while Semri witnesses are qualified, but even where Semri witnesses too would ordinarily be considered interested, once Kat witnesses are disqualified their testimony may be accepted for Semri. See there. Chatam Sofer nevertheless concludes that as a matter of prudent practice an administrator should be appointed for the minor heirs and all testimony should be submitted to him, so that he can consider whether there is any argument to advance on their behalf. If he has none, the law returns to the conclusion stated above. See there for the reason.
