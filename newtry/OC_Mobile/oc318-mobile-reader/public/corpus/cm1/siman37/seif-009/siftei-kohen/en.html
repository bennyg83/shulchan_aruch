He writes that even absent ordinary personal interest there may be kinship interest, because the ruling gives the treasurer great benefit and enables him to distribute the charity as he wishes, even to dependent children or relatives. The Ramah inferred that according to the view that discretionary benefit is not money, testimony is valid. This requires study. The rule that discretionary benefit is not money concerns matters requiring monetary value, such as double payment, kiddushin, or an oath. Disqualification for interest requires no direct monetary gain. Any benefit may suffice, such as preserving property for a creditor, avoiding the reputation of a wicked borrower, preferring one litigant to another, or desiring enrichment of the poor. The Rambam and Tur rule that even a remote indirect benefit disqualifies. Certainly the treasurer's ability to distribute as he wishes creates interest. If he is expected to distribute to relatives, the benefit is plainly monetary to them, even according to the view that discretionary benefit is not money. The Ramah's words therefore require analysis.
