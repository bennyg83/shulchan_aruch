Even though the treasurer distributes it, etc. Shach, seif katan 10, objects at length to Rama. He argues that even if the right to choose a recipient is not considered money, the treasurer still derives a benefit from that choice. Any possible personal benefit creates financial interest, and we rule that even a remote interest prevents testimony. Shach explains Ritva's statement, made according to the view that such discretionary benefit is money, as merely stating the truth according to that opinion. In reality, Shach says, even the view that it is not money should still disqualify the treasurer because of interest. There may be force to this argument regarding the treasurer himself. But I do not understand Shach regarding the treasurer's relatives. Relatives are not disqualified because we suspect them of lying. Their disqualification is a Torah decree. If the discretionary benefit is legally money, then the treasurer has a monetary right and his relatives fall within the decree concerning relatives. But if the discretionary benefit is not money, there is no monetary right or liability belonging to their relative. The treasurer himself may still be interested because he enjoys the power of distribution and could therefore be suspected of biased testimony. His relatives, however, are not suspected of lying merely because their relative has a non-monetary interest. Thus Rama is correct that the relatives may testify. As to the treasurer himself, Shach's doubt has substance. If the testimony concerns something from which the treasurer derives a full personal benefit, Ritva holds that the dedication itself can still take effect and the property may be delivered to another treasurer, because we divide the statement. See below, siman 51, for further discussion.
