The benefit of discretion. The Shach argues that the statement, "the benefit of discretion is not money," was said only for matters like double payment or kiddushin. For interested testimony we do not require a monetary interest. Any real benefit can disqualify, as shown by concern that a debtor testifies in order not to be called a wicked borrower. According to Maharit, however, only financial benefit creates the legal status of an interested witness. Rambam's phrase that even a remote and extraordinary interest disqualifies still refers to monetary benefit, however indirect. Mahari ben Lev sought proof from the wicked borrower case that nonmonetary interest suffices. Maharit answers that this too has a monetary side: whenever the matter later reaches his hands, he will otherwise have to take money out of his own pocket in order not to remain a wicked borrower who fails to pay. Thus the interest ultimately involves money. Maharit supports his position from several sources. Fathers of a groom and bride may testify for one another despite the strongest emotional closeness. Mordechai allows testimony in matters touching a blood redeemer even though the redeemer has a personal right. It follows that nonmonetary affinity alone is insufficient. Therefore, if the mere benefit of choosing which poor person receives charity has no status as money, it should not create disqualifying interest under the ordinary rule. One might even think that the charity treasurer himself could testify. Maharit, however, discussing Rosh's responsum concerning relatives of a murder victim and relatives of the blood redeemer, explains that even when the benefit is not monetary it can suffice to disqualify the person who personally holds the special right, while not extending the disqualification to his relatives. On that basis the charity treasurer himself may still be disqualified even though the benefit of discretion is not money, while his relatives remain qualified because there is no monetary benefit attaching to them. This fits Rama's language, which specifically permits relatives of the treasurer but does not permit the treasurer himself.
