All the more so their relatives are valid to testify. Ritva discusses a case where the brother of a charity treasurer testified with another witness that someone had dedicated property to the poor. They later testified that the donor instructed the treasurer to distribute the funds to whomever he wished. The treasurer then gave some of the money to the witness's daughter. Ritva ruled that there was no initial interest, because at the time of the first testimony no identifiable benefit to the witness existed. The later gift to his daughter does not invalidate the testimony retroactively. Nevertheless, there may be a different problem: the treasurer himself, the witness's brother, benefits from having discretion to distribute the money, and according to authorities who hold that discretionary benefit is considered money, the testimony concerning his authority is interested testimony. Even so, the dedication itself remains established. We can divide the testimony, accepting that the property was dedicated while rejecting the part that grants the treasurer discretion. Moreover, the witnesses initially testified simply that the donor dedicated the property. Once that first testimony was given, the later statement about the treasurer's discretion cannot undo it. Rama's wording here suggests that even where the treasurer's discretionary authority is known from the outset, the relatives may still testify. That is correct only according to the view that discretionary benefit is not money, and where it was not initially apparent that the treasurer would give to the witness's relative. According to the view that such benefit is money, testimony regarding the treasurer's discretion is not valid, although the dedication itself may remain established under the distinctions above.
