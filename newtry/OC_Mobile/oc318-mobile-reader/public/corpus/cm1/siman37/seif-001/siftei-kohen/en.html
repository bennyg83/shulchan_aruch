Any testimony in which a person has a benefit is disqualified. The Ir Shushan explains that this is not because we suspect lying, but because a person is considered related to himself. The Sema questions the source. The difficulty prompting that explanation is understandable: why suspect a person of false testimony, a grave sin, merely to avoid being called a wicked borrower who does not pay? Maharam of Lublin answers that false testimony may remain concealed. He also suggests that any personal interest gives the law of kinship. This latter explanation is not primary. An interested witness may testify against his own interest, whereas a relative is disqualified both for and against. Nimukei Yosef states this explicitly. The correct answer is the first one: the witness may lie to avoid public disgrace, because people would call him a borrower who does not repay, even though the lie itself may not be publicly known. The Ramban similarly explains that he seeks to satisfy public opinion.

This cannot mean merely that he shall pay and thereby satisfy the creditor, for if he has other assets the creditor must collect from them before reaching property transferred to another. Rather, he must accept responsibility to compensate the partner if the creditor later collects the gifted property. This is the Gemara's case of one who accepted liability. The Bach's interpretation led him to unnecessary distinctions, such as dispensing with the stipulation when the debtor's assets and creditors are known. That is untenable, because hidden creditors, an apotiki designation, or uncertainty whether the money is truly his may remain. In every case, he must expressly undertake to pay the partner.
