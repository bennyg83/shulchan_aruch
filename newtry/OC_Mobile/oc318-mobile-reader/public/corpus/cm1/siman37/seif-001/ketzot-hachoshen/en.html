He has an interest in it. Ir Shushan writes that an interested witness is not disqualified because we suspect deliberate falsehood, but because a person is legally his own relative. He explains this from the rule that a person who testifies in order not to remain a wicked borrower who does not pay is considered interested. It would be absurd to say he lies in order not to be called wicked, because there is no greater wickedness than false testimony. Therefore the defect must be legal closeness to oneself, not factual suspicion. The Shach objects: if interest is relationship, why is a person believed against himself, when relatives are disqualified both for benefit and detriment? He cites Ramban in the name of Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, who explains the "wicked borrower" case as a person who wants to satisfy people and avoid failing his obligations, not as one literally preferring one sin over another. See what I wrote in siman 34, seif katan 3. The reason a person is believed concerning himself, despite being his own relative, is a separate Torah rule. The Torah entrusted every person with credibility about himself, even though he possesses every ordinary disqualification, whether slave, woman, or single witness. The verse requiring two witnesses governs testimony about others, not one's own admission. Therefore there is no difficulty in saying that an interested witness is disqualified because he is legally close to himself, while his adverse admission is accepted under the separate rule of personal credibility. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi himself, in his work Get Pashut on Bava Batra, also speaks in language close to Ir Shushan. Ramban cites only the alternative explanation because in that particular passage the conceptual novelty of interest as relationship was not the central issue. This will be discussed more fully below.
Disqualified to testify. Whether disqualifying interest requires specifically financial benefit or includes other benefit is discussed by Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, responsa 80 and 97. He concludes that financial benefit is specifically required.
The partner's share. Taz understands the Sema as saying that after partners divided property, if a claimant comes and removes only one partner's share as stolen property, the other partner cannot testify for him because if the claimant succeeds the first partner may later tell him that their division was mistaken and demand redivision. Taz argues that in siman 171 he will prove that division is undone only among brothers, because brothers are treated as heirs standing in their father's place. The reason is not ordinary mistake. He quotes his discussion there: the Gemara and decisors frame the law specifically as brothers who divided, not two purchasers or ordinary partners. When a creditor takes one brother's portion, the other must share because heirs are their father's extension and both must bear the father's debts equally. Ordinary partners who bought jointly and divided do not have that obligation. In my view both Sema and Taz are correct, because they address different forms of removal. Taz is right where a creditor comes and takes one partner's portion. At the time of division the division was valid. The creditor could legally have chosen either share. Without the special principle that heirs stand in their father's place and jointly bear his debt, there would be no reason for one brother to compensate the other. They resemble purchasers without warranty. But where the property is removed as stolen property belonging to a third person, the division was mistaken from its inception. There can be no greater mistake than dividing property that partly belonged to someone else. When a third brother later appears, the original division is void because the parties divided on a false assumption concerning the estate's size. The same principle applies where a third party proves that part of the property never belonged to the partners. Nimukei Yosef, citing Ritva, writes that stolen property that cannot be discharged with money is retroactively revealed never to have been included in the sale and is treated as a physical deficiency in the property. Therefore the Sema, who discusses stolen property, correctly treats the division as mistaken. His language is precise: he compares it to the case where a third brother appears, not to the case where a creditor takes one brother's share. Thus ordinary partners do not redivide merely because a creditor collects from one, but they do redivide where title to part of the divided property is shown never to have belonged to them.
