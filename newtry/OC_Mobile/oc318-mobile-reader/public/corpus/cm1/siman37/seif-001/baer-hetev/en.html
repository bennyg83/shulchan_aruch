Benefit. The Sema writes that the reason for disqualification is that we suspect him where the testimony may bring him a benefit. Therefore Nimukei Yosef writes that one who is interested in the testimony may testify against his own interest, unlike one disqualified by kinship. We do not apply the principle that a person is considered related to himself except where his testimony would render himself wicked, not merely where a monetary benefit is involved. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who explains the matter based on the principle that a person is related to himself. See the Shach.
The partner. This applies so long as they have not completely divided their partnership. Once they have fully divided, however, one may testify for the other, because he can no longer say that their division was mistaken, as explained in siman 171 regarding brothers who divided and then a third brother arrived from overseas, and similarly in simanim 176 and 177. The present case is different because the witness himself disqualifies the challenger's witnesses. Although he is not believed regarding his own portion, we tell him that his own bad fortune caused his loss and his field suffered the consequence. Thus the Sema. See the Taz's objection to this.
And they made a kinyan. The Sema writes that the kinyan must explicitly state that he removes himself from ownership in his actual share of the jointly owned field. Otherwise one could still say that he withdrew only from claims and arguments, but not from ownership of the land itself.
It is effective. We do not suspect collusion as we do regarding a promissory note that was found after being lost, at the beginning of siman 65. There, the circumstance of the loss itself creates a defect in credibility, whereas here there is no such defect before us. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that according to some poskim, removing one's interest is ineffective unless he did so before witnessing the event, because testimony must begin and end while the witness is qualified.
For him. Meaning for the recipient of the gift. Otherwise we would be concerned that the witness testifies in order to preserve the property for a creditor to whom he himself owes money and has no other property from which to pay. He would prefer to give false testimony, which is hidden from others, rather than be publicly called a wicked borrower who does not pay. If, however, he accepted liability toward the partner, this concern disappears. If the property is taken by his creditor he will then remain liable to the partner under the warranty and will be called wicked by him, so he gains nothing by one course over the other. Sema.
To stipulate. The Sema writes that this condition is necessary only where he accepted general warranty. If he said specifically, "If my creditor comes and takes it from you, I will reimburse you," one can say that he accepted liability only for a creditor arising through himself. There is no basis to infer liability for unrelated challenges. The Sema adds that he wrote in Perishah that even if he stipulates merely that he will not reimburse the recipient for the warranty arising from this particular challenger, he may testify for him.
