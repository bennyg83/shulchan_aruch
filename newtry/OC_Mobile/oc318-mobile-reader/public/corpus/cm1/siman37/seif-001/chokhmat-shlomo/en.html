Seif 1. In Shach, seif katan 1: The Shach discusses whether one who has a personal interest in the matter is nevertheless competent to testify to his own detriment. I saw that Tzemach Tzedek, responsum 37, discusses this at length and cites the views of the Rishonim. He writes there, in the passage beginning “However, in the responsum of the Rosh,” that otherwise the Rosh's words would be difficult to understand, since the Rosh himself ruled that removal of one's interest is effective when he derives no benefit from the testimony. With due respect, this is not precise. The two matters are not comparable. So long as he remains interested, if he would be disqualified from testifying in his own favor, he is likewise disqualified from testifying to his detriment, under the rule that testimony partly invalidated is entirely invalidated. Once he removes his interest, however, he is competent both for and against himself, and therefore removal is effective. The principal matter may be resolved by distinguishing among the authorities rather than treating them as contradictory. It is known that a litigant is not categorized merely as a disqualified witness, as Urim VeTumim writes in seif katan 1. Where removal of the interest is possible, such as one who shares in payment of a communal tax, he does not have the full status of a litigant, since he can remove his interest. Nevertheless, so long as he has not done so he remains a disqualified witness because of the concern for personal interest. There the rule that testimony partly invalidated is entirely invalidated applies, and he is not believed even to his detriment. But where removal is impossible, so that he can never testify in his own favor, he is treated as a litigant and not as a witness at all. The rule of partial invalidation of testimony therefore does not apply, and he may testify to his own detriment. Urim VeTumim, seif katan 21, cites the responsum of the Rosh but not the Tzemach Tzedek's rejection of its proof and alternative explanation, and Netivot, seif katan 1, likewise does not cite it. This is what appears correct to me. As to the Shach's basic question whether an interested person may testify to his own detriment, I once brought a proof from the verse in Parashat Vayelech, “I will call heaven and earth to witness against them.” In the first chapter of Avodah Zarah the Gemara says that heaven and earth are interested witnesses regarding testimony in Israel's favor, yet Scripture employs them as witnesses to Israel's detriment. In my Torah commentary to Parashat Vayelech from 5611, however, I rejected this proof; see there. There, in seif 1: The Gemara in Bava Batra 43 asks why the witnesses are considered interested, and Tosafot, in the passage beginning “because he establishes it before his creditor,” add that the case may even be one in which the purchaser recognizes that the property truly belonged to the seller, so that there is no grievance. According to this answer I find the Gemara's later question difficult. It asks whose warranty is involved and says that if it is general warranty, it is all the more advantageous to the seller. But Choshen Mishpat 225 rules that even when a seller accepted warranty, he is liable only when the property is taken from the purchaser through a Jewish beit din. If it is taken by coercion through a non-Jewish tribunal, he need not reimburse him, because that tribunal may have ruled falsely. Certainly, if the purchaser himself knows that the witnesses are false, the seller should not have to reimburse him, for there can be no greater coercion than that. Why then is it necessarily more advantageous to the seller? If the purchaser does not recognize the property, the witness is already disqualified because of the purchaser's grievance. If the purchaser does recognize it, he must have admitted beforehand that he recognizes it as the seller's, and then even general warranty should not obligate the seller to reimburse him. Perhaps Tosafot's second answer does not interpret the Gemara like Rashbam. “General warranty” may mean liability even where the property is taken by coercion, while “warranty arising from himself” means liability only where it truly was stolen property or was subject to the seller's creditor. On that reading the passage is resolved. There, seif 1: A new question arises concerning an interested witness whose interest was unknown and who testified falsely. Does he violate the prohibition of withholding testimony? Likewise, if such a person is placed under oath to come and testify, does the oath take effect, so that a false oath is punishable? It appears clear to me that the oath does take effect because he has the ability to remove his interest. Likewise, if he testifies falsely he violates the prohibition concerning testimony. A proof may be brought from Shevuot 31, which says that the oath of testimony applies only to those fit to testify. Why does the Gemara not say that this excludes an interested witness? Necessarily, the exclusion concerns one inherently unfit for testimony, such as a king, a gambler, or a relative, who cannot remove his disqualification. An interested witness, however, can remove the interest and therefore is subject to the oath of testimony and, consequently, to the prohibition if he gives false testimony. A further proof is from Shevuot 32b, concerning two sets of witnesses. The Gemara asks why the first set should be liable when the second set is available. It could have answered that the second set were interested at the time and later removed their interest. The fact that it does not do so shows that because they could remove the interest, they are treated as available witnesses, and if the litigant failed to place them under oath he caused his own loss. This is a clear proof. I record it because I heard that a certain scholar wrote the opposite in a matter involving the slaughterer in Krisnipoli. See also my fourth edition to Choshen Mishpat 171, where I discuss several distinctions concerning communal treasurers as witnesses, and cite Knesset HaGedolah that townspeople may testify for persons of their own town; the concern that they are interested applies when the litigation concerns persons of another town.
