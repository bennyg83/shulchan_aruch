Any testimony from which a person derives benefit, etc. Sema and Shach conclude that an interested witness is disqualified because of suspicion that he may lie, not merely because he is like a relative. A practical difference is where he signed a document while still disinterested and only later became interested. If the defect were only like kinship, others could authenticate his signature as with one who became related only afterward. But if the defect is suspicion of falsehood, it is like one who later became known as a robber, and others may not authenticate the signature. Still, there can be cases where no real suspicion of lying exists, such as the interest of avoiding the stigma of being a wicked borrower who does not pay. In such a case the element of kinship may still explain disqualification. Urim VeTumim sought to prove that even testimony against one's own interest is invalid, from townspeople who testified that someone was exempt from communal tax even though this increased their own burden. In my view the broader rule is that when the witness testifies concerning property that is legally his own, he does not have the status of a witness at all, even when the immediate statement harms him. This is like a lender who says a document in his possession was paid: if that admission harms others, he cannot combine as a witness merely because he speaks against his own financial interest. A person is effectively close to his own property. This also resolves the question from the case where a person says, 'So-and-so violated my ox.' His testimony can be accepted regarding the perpetrator, and once the perpetrator is established the ox's legal consequence follows, without treating him as a witness directly against his own property.

Even regarding the partner's share. Sema explains that after partners divide, one cannot later say that the division was mistaken merely because one portion is taken by a creditor. Taz disagrees in certain stolen-property cases. The correct distinction is that ordinary creditor foreclosure does not automatically render the original division mistaken. But where the portion turns out to have been stolen and must be returned together with its produce, there is stronger basis to treat the division as subject to responsibility. From Sema one can infer that if partners divided and one share was later destroyed by natural disaster, a creditor of that partner cannot foreclose from the other partner's share merely because heirs who divide are sometimes treated like purchasers. The rule of no retroactive clarification creates only a doubt whether each received his own preexisting share; a creditor cannot extract from the second partner on that doubt. But where the first share is taken because it was stolen property, the creditor can argue that the division was mistaken. Also, Mechaber's disqualification of a partner from testifying does not mean that he can testify to disqualify the challenger's witnesses as robbers. Such testimony affects his own share as well and is plainly interested.
