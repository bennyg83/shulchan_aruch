Benefit, etc. See Urim regarding Sema's discussion of Ir Shushan, who writes that an interested person cannot testify because a person is related to himself. Shach says Ir Shushan means to answer a difficulty: why suspect a borrower will give false testimony merely to avoid being called wicked for not paying, when false testimony itself would also make him wicked? The answer is that he is related to himself. But in truth Ramban already answers differently: failure to pay makes him visibly wicked in the eyes of people, whereas the falsehood of his testimony is known only before Hashem. I do not understand Sema and Shach's objection. Ir Shushan's words follow the authorities cited by Ramban and Rivash, whose view I discussed above in siman 36. They explain that an interested person need not have been qualified both at the beginning and end because, as a litigant, he is not within the category of witness testimony at all. This is like the person involved in the act or the victim, who is not counted among those who "establish the matter" and therefore does not invalidate the remaining witnesses. That is also Raavad's explanation of the rule that a person is related to himself. The point is not merely suspicion of lying. He is a litigant and not part of the testimonial unit. The same applies to financial interest. One who is interested is treated as a litigant. If the sole reason were suspicion of falsehood, he would resemble a robber suspected of lying and would be an ordinary disqualified witness. Thus Ir Shushan's language is exactly the view of those authorities. Ramban does not use this answer because he does not accept their conceptual approach. I am therefore surprised that Shach, who in siman 36 agrees with Rivash and questions Rama on precisely this ground, did not recognize that Ir Shushan is saying the same thing here. See also what I wrote above in siman 34.
He shall pay its value, etc. See Urim, where I cited Sema's explanation, drawn from the Gemara, that after this payment there is no longer a concern of financial interest. One might ask according to Ramban and Baal HaTerumot, who hold that if a borrower designated a field as an explicit apotiki and it is later found not to have belonged to him, the creditor does not return to the borrower for other property. Perhaps the field about which the witness testifies had been designated as an apotiki to his creditor. He later sold another field without warranty. If the disputed field remains with the partner and the apotiki creditor later takes it, the partner, who received warranty, could collect from the later purchaser without warranty. No one would be called a wicked borrower. But if the challenger takes the disputed field first, the apotiki creditor will have nowhere to collect and, according to Ramban, cannot pursue the later purchaser because his claim was confined to that field. The witness would therefore have an interest in keeping the field with the partner. A similar question arises according to Tosafot, who hold that if the apotiki is found not to belong to the borrower the creditor may return to him. Where an earlier creditor later takes the apotiki, everyone agrees the apotiki creditor cannot simply collect from unrelated assets in the same way. The answer is that just as a purchaser without warranty knowingly accepts the risk and therefore does not make the seller a "wicked borrower" when he loses, so too an explicit apotiki creditor knowingly accepted this field alone. If the field is later found not to belong to the borrower, and certainly if it is taken by an earlier creditor, the borrower is not called wicked toward the apotiki creditor. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 43, part 2, says so explicitly. This is unlike Gidulei Terumah's understanding that Tur also follows Ramban concerning an apotiki found not to belong to the borrower, yet still assumes the borrower wishes to avoid the title of wicked borrower. If so, the difficulty above remains. The better conclusion is that such a borrower is not called wicked in relation to the apotiki creditor. There is nevertheless another possible financial interest. Suppose the witness accepted warranty toward his partner and included after-acquired property. Perhaps he had an earlier creditor without an after-acquired-property clause. He later acquired another field and sold it without warranty. If the disputed field remains with the partner, the earlier creditor can take it, and the partner can then collect the later-acquired field because his warranty included after-acquired property. But if the challenger takes the disputed field first, the earlier creditor cannot collect the later field because his lien did not include after-acquired property. The witness would then become a wicked borrower toward his partner. We must therefore say that the warranty given to the partner did not include after-acquired property. Even this does not fully solve the problem. Perhaps the witness acquired a field after the first creditor's loan but before accepting warranty toward the partner, and sold it only afterward. The partner's warranty would attach to that field even without an after-acquired-property clause, while the earlier creditor could not collect it. Again, the witness could have an interest in which claimant takes the disputed field. It is difficult to dismiss this as too remote on the ground that sales without warranty are uncommon. We hold that even a remote and unusual financial interest can disqualify testimony. I therefore believe Tur's wording addresses precisely this problem. Tur writes that the witness must obligate himself toward the partner that if the field is taken, he will satisfy the partner in cash. Commentators struggled to explain why Tur specifies cash. The reason is clear. He does not accept ordinary full warranty allowing the partner to collect from encumbered property. He personally undertakes to pay the partner money from his own assets. Then no future purchaser or property lien can create the indirect interest described above. If he has cash, he can pay whichever creditor must be paid. If he lacks it, he remains equally obligated personally and does not gain from one land-recovery sequence over another. Thus Tur's language is precise and removes every possible financial interest.
Only the partner's share, etc. Beit Yosef cites Rashbam that if partners divided the property and each selected his own share, one may thereafter testify concerning the other's share. Sema cites this above. I find the rule difficult. It resembles witnesses who are related to a guarantor but unrelated to the borrower. They cannot testify, because testimony void in part is void in full. The authorities ask why we do not divide the statement. One answer is that in the classic case of "so-and-so sodomized..." we can linguistically divide the statement, but where no such division is possible, as in "I committed adultery with your wife," the entire testimony is void. The present case appears similar. Just as the witness's testimony is void regarding his own former share or interest, why should it remain valid regarding his partner's portion? According to Raavad and Rosh, who treat a litigant's own portion as outside the category of testimony rather than as invalid testimony, Rashbam's rule is easier. But those same authorities explain that in "so-and-so sodomized my ox" we do not say a person is formally related to his property, and the monetary interest can still cause the entire testimony to fail. This suggests financial interest is not always treated as mere litigant status outside testimony. It is difficult to distinguish by saying that favorable testimony for one's own property makes him a litigant, whereas testimony harmful to his property does not. I have not found such a distinction in Rosh. Accordingly, at least according to the first approach, which is the view of Tosafot and Raavan discussed above in simanim 33 and 34, Rashbam's ruling is difficult. Perhaps Rashbam holds that where testimony concerns two distinct people, one related or interested and one unrelated, we can divide the testimony between the two persons. But below, siman 51, our practical ruling does not generally accept such division between two persons. Therefore Rashbam's law requires study.
