Who stole, etc. See Urim regarding the dispute between Rabbenu Yonah and Ri. According to Ri, the case is one in which we already know that the claimant has witnesses through whom he can establish title to the field. At first glance this is difficult. If beit din has already heard those witnesses, how can the Gemara say that when Levi possesses the field and the claimant wants to take it from Yehudah, the claimant prefers Yehudah because "the second is easier for me and the first harder"? If his witnesses are legally sufficient to remove the field even from Yehudah, all the more they should suffice to prevent beit din from transferring the field from Levi to Yehudah. The claimant could simply appear in court and object. Once the judges know he has valid proof, why is his testimony in the later dispute interested? It is strained to say the judges merely heard about the witnesses indirectly and cannot rule on that information because the witnesses themselves have gone overseas. That would reduce the matter to rumor, which is essentially Rabbenu Yonah's approach. The answer must be that Ri holds testimony received outside the litigant's presence cannot be used to decide against that litigant. Beit din may already have received the claimant's witnesses when neither Levi nor Yehudah was present. That testimony gives the claimant a real evidentiary position, but cannot presently stop collection in favor of Yehudah because it was not received in Yehudah's presence. The witnesses have since gone overseas and cannot now repeat it before him. Thus the claimant still has an interest in who holds the property: he believes he can later recover from Levi through those witnesses, but may find Yehudah harder to recover from. This interpretation yields a further proof. It shows that testimony originally received outside the litigant's presence can be repeated later in the litigant's presence. We do not say the witnesses are barred because they may merely reinforce their earlier statements. Otherwise the original testimony would be useless permanently, and the claimant would have no reason to regard it as a future means of recovery. This supports the authorities discussed above in siman 28 and siman 33, seif 8. However, according to Rama, who rules that testimony received outside the litigant's presence can be effective after the fact, the original difficulty returns. The previously received testimony would already suffice to block beit din from transferring the field to Yehudah. Accordingly, under Rama's position we must explain the Gemara like Rabbenu Yonah rather than Ri: the claimant has only a report or expectation of favorable witnesses, not testimony already legally received.
Because it is possible that this one is more convenient for him, etc. The Gemara asks: with the same testimony by which Shimon can remove the field from Levi, let him remove it from Yehudah. The Gemara answers that the second holder is more convenient for him and the first is harder for him. Alternatively, it establishes the case where each side has witnesses, so the land remains where it presently stands. There is a practical difference between the two answers. If Shimon's witnesses are sufficient to remove the field even from Yehudah, for example because Yehudah too claims through Reuven the robber and Shimon has witnesses that Reuven stole it, then under the second answer Shimon may testify, because those witnesses will help him against Yehudah as well. Under the first answer, he may still not testify, because even with equally effective witnesses he can prefer one opponent over another. Tur rules according to the first answer and therefore says he may not testify in any such case. Perishah argues that the reverse difference should also apply. If it is known that Yehudah is unusually cooperative while Levi is violent or difficult, the reason "the second is more convenient for me" should disappear, whereas the second answer would still apply. Sema says that even this is included in "the second is more convenient." Shimon may have some private leverage over Levi, or fear Yehudah for some undisclosed reason, so these are matters entrusted to the heart. Ramban in his novellae to Bava Batra supports this. He writes that the holder is presently in possession while the claimant is the robbed party, and nevertheless we still suspect that the witness prefers one opponent. Many struggled with Ramban's wording, but the point is clear. The apparent facts may suggest Levi is the stronger or more violent party, yet we still do not assume that Shimon's private preferences follow appearances. This rejects Rabbenu Chananel's view that we need actual knowledge that Yehudah is a violent person. Rambam's wording, however, suggests that ordinarily we say the second holder is more convenient only where neither party is established as violent. When the field is still with Reuven the robber, Rambam gives a different reason for disqualifying Shimon's testimony: perhaps his witnesses can remove it from Reuven but not from Yehudah. He does not say "the second is more convenient," presumably because Reuven is himself the robber and plainly not the easier opponent. Once Reuven sells to Levi, the preference concern arises. Mechaber follows Tur and Ramban, and that appears correct. Even if one opponent is outwardly difficult, we cannot know the witness's private calculations. I suggested in my novellae another indication that the first answer is primary. The Gemara later abandons Rav Sheshet's original explanation of the cow-and-garment baraita and adopts another interpretation, even though Tosafot and Rosh say despair can apply even to land and the distinction is not absolute. One could have preserved Rav Sheshet's explanation as follows. Despair operates like ownerless status. Where a person owes others, he cannot simply dispose of property in a manner that harms their rights. Likewise his despair should not prejudice an existing creditor whose lien remains upon the land. Thus with land, even if the robbed owner despaired, the witness may still wish to preserve the field before his creditor, for the creditor does not lose through the owner's despair. Movables, by contrast, are not ordinarily subject to a creditor's lien. Despair can therefore fully remove the owner's interest, and he may testify. This would fit the wording of "warranty upon it" and "no warranty upon it" quite well. Why then did the Gemara need a different explanation? The answer may be that the Gemara treats the first answer, "the second is more convenient for me," as primary. That concern applies when the witness himself hopes to recover. But if he merely wishes to leave the field available to his creditor, what difference does it make whether the creditor's eventual collection from one holder is easier or harder? The witness himself has no remaining hand in the property. Thus the first answer cannot support the creditor-based land distinction in the manner just suggested. This is evidence that the Gemara did not want to rely on that construction. One may object that Shmuel's separate rule, that the seller wants to leave the field before his creditor, still requires the usual case where the seller recognizes title and does not expect personal recovery. That is true, but there the concern is not that a different possessor is more convenient. It is simply that the creditor should have an asset from which to collect. The "second is more convenient" rationale is narrower and applies where Shimon himself still contemplates future litigation. There is also a difficulty concerning takanat hashuk. If the cow or garment is subject to despair and change of possession, but under our law the purchaser may still have to pay the robbed owner where takanat hashuk does not apply, then the original owner can still have a monetary interest in who holds the object. Shach below in siman 353 raises this and suggests, somewhat reluctantly, that the case is one where the object is known not to be worth more than the amount already paid by the purchaser, so takanat hashuk leaves the owner with no additional claim. This is not especially strained. Ordinarily one assumes the object has not appreciated beyond its purchase price. But the Gemara itself suggests establishing the case with an heir, according to the view that an heir's domain is like that of a purchaser. There takanat hashuk does not apply, because there was no market purchase to protect. Why then can the heir testify if he must pay money to the robbed owner? This is difficult according to Rama, Sema, and Bach. I believe Rambam's system resolves it. In Bava Kamma 111b, the Mishnah says that a robber who died leaving the stolen item to his sons leaves them exempt from payment. Rabbah bar bar Chanah derives that an heir's domain is like a purchaser's. One might ask: even if there is a change of domain, why are the heirs exempt from paying the value? Rambam appears to derive his distinction from the law of the customs officer who took one person's donkey and gave him another. Where the recipient took the substitute while it was still forbidden property in the hands of the transferor, mere despair plus change of possession does not necessarily remove the obligation to pay. The reason is that the transfer came through another person's intentional conveyance while the object was still unlawfully held. Since the transferor could not convey clean title at that moment, the recipient's acquisition is tainted and value remains payable. Inheritance is different. No human transferor acts at the moment of acquisition. The heir acquires automatically at death, by operation of law. The change of domain and acquisition occur simultaneously, without the property first entering his hands through an unlawful act of conveyance. Therefore even Rambam can agree that the heir owes no payment to the robbed owner. This resolves both the Bava Kamma passage and the Gemara's suggestion here to establish the case with an heir. It also yields a possible defense of Rashbam. Rashbam writes that Levi bought the cow or garment without warranty. Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef understand this to mean that if there had been warranty, there would be no complete change of domain because the seller's responsibility remains attached. Perhaps Rashbam means something else. Rav holds that where the thief is known, takanat hashuk does not require the robbed owner to reimburse the buyer because the buyer can return against the thief. In our case Levi plainly knows from whom he bought. If so, why can Reuven testify? The purchaser may have to pay the owner and then return against Reuven, giving Reuven a direct interest. Rashbam therefore specifies that the sale was without warranty. The buyer then has no recourse against the seller, and takanat hashuk can protect him. This reading avoids attributing to Rashbam the novel rule that warranty prevents a change of possession altogether, a rule not clearly adopted by the early authorities. It is true that Tosafot could still read the case differently and say warranty prevents complete change of domain. But this need not be Rashbam's final halachic view. Indeed, the Gemara's conclusion after Abaye's objection suggests that the earlier conceptual link between warranty and change of domain was not accepted as practical law. This would explain why later poskim do not record it. The distinction between land and movables can then be understood without inventing that new doctrine. There is another way to see why the heir case is different for Rambam. Where a buyer receives stolen property through another person's conveyance, it comes to him through an act of transfer that is itself still legally defective before the acquisition is completed. He therefore acquires the object but may remain liable for its value. Where an heir acquires automatically, there is no such intervening act. The moment the father's ownership ends, the heir's ownership begins. It never reaches him through an unlawful transaction. Accordingly, the heir is fully protected by the change of domain itself. With this, the Gemara's heir suggestion is coherent even though ordinary takanat hashuk does not apply to inheritance. I considered whether the Gemara could instead be read as depending on the opinion that the heir also pays value, but that would make its suggestion incomplete and would require identifying a Tanna who rejects Rav's rule. The Gemara gives no such indication. Thus Rambam's distinction is preferable. All this also explains why the poskim did not preserve Rashbam's supposed rule that a warranty sale can never count as change of domain. The statement belongs to the preliminary discussion or can be understood as directed to the takanat hashuk and recourse issue, not as a general law of acquisition. The practical conclusion remains that Tur correctly follows the first answer, "the second is more convenient for me." A person can remain interested even where his witnesses are legally sufficient against either opponent, because private ease of litigation itself is a benefit. The second answer, concerning two sets of witnesses and leaving the land where it stands, explains additional cases but does not limit the broader concern. Accordingly, whenever a witness stands to gain by having a disputed asset remain with one of two possible holders, and the gain can arise from ease of future recovery rather than from formal title alone, he is disqualified. But where the witness's own interest has truly disappeared and only a creditor's abstract rights remain, the "second is more convenient" rationale does not automatically apply. There is another indication from the Gemara's own rejection of Rav Sheshet's first explanation. If the distinction between land and movables rested only on despair and change of possession, the land case could still have been preserved where we know the owner despaired. Tosafot and Rosh both hold that despair can apply to land, although it is less common. Why then did the Gemara abandon that line entirely? One reason is the creditor problem just discussed. Even if the owner personally despaired, a creditor whose lien already attached does not lose merely because the debtor mentally despaired. A debtor cannot surrender or abandon property in a way that prejudices a creditor. Despair is conceptually similar to abandonment in this respect. Thus, regarding land, the witness could still be interested because he wishes the field to remain available to his creditor. Movables are different because ordinary creditors do not collect from movables already transferred to another person's possession in the same manner. The witness's personal despair can therefore remove the practical interest. This would have supplied an attractive explanation of the baraita. Nevertheless, the Gemara did not settle there, which suggests that the "second is more convenient" rationale remains central. That rationale cannot simply be transferred to a creditor. The witness may care whether Levi or Yehudah is easier for him personally, but once he has despaired and only the creditor's collection remains at stake, the creditor's convenience is not ordinarily the witness's benefit. This helps explain the structure of the sugya and why the alternative answer cannot replace the first one in every case. There is also a practical point concerning the identity of the stolen movable. Suppose Shimon has witnesses overseas who can testify that Reuven robbed him of a certain cow or garment. Reuven sold it to Levi, and Yehudah now claims the item. Even if Levi acquired through despair and change of possession, Shimon may still need the physical object preserved until his witnesses return, because they must identify that exact animal or garment as the one stolen from him. If Levi is a cooperative holder, Shimon trusts that he will not slaughter the cow, consume it, or wear out the garment before the witnesses arrive. If Yehudah is less convenient, he may fear the identifying object will disappear. This is another concrete expression of "the second is more convenient for me." It also explains why Shimon's interest can persist even when he no longer expects to recover the object itself. His monetary claim against the robber can depend upon proving that this is the very item originally stolen. If the object disappears before the witnesses arrive, the robber may claim he returned the original object and that the one sold to Levi was a different item. Since a robber who stole in the presence of witnesses need not necessarily return in the presence of witnesses, the evidentiary problem can be serious. Accordingly, the holder's cooperation can affect Shimon's ability to prove his later monetary claim. This gives added force to Tur's adoption of the first answer. One should not reduce "the second is more convenient" to mere physical strength or violence. It includes any private circumstance that makes one holder more useful to the witness's anticipated proof or recovery. This is also why Ramban says we do not need actual evidence that Yehudah is a violent person. The witness's private preference can concern evidence, access, negotiation, or other matters unknown to beit din. Once a realistic benefit can exist, the witness is treated as interested. The same reasoning explains why we do not permit testimony merely because, in formal legal theory, his evidence could work against either possessor. Practical ease of enforcing the right is itself a benefit. Accordingly, the first answer is broader than the second. The second answer, involving two sets of witnesses and leaving the land where it stands, shows one particular structure of interest. The first establishes the general principle that the identity of the opposing possessor can itself create testimonial interest. That is the rule Tur codifies.
And he despaired, etc. Shach cites Rashbam that if the object was sold with warranty, there is no true change of possession. Tosafot quote this without dissent. Bach asks why the poskim do not record such an important rule. In truth Rashbam's statement appears limited to the Gemara's initial approach, before it established that the robber had died. At that stage, if the seller remains alive and the purchaser can return against him, one can say that the seller's domain remains legally entangled with the object through warranty. The purchaser's possession is therefore not entirely independent. But under the conclusion, the case is one where the robber died and left no assets. The purchaser then has no one against whom to exercise warranty. The seller's responsibility has ended in practice, and the change of possession is complete. Thus Rashbam's point has no practical consequence in our present case. Still, one might expect the poskim in siman 353, in the general laws of stolen property, to record the principle that a warranty sale does not count as change of possession. Their silence suggests that Rashbam did not mean to state such a final rule at all. I believe Rashbam was only explaining Rav Sheshet's preliminary interpretation and the tension with Abaye's wording. Rav Sheshet had to explain why the baraita distinguishes between land, "whose warranty is upon the seller," and a cow or garment, "whose warranty is not upon the seller." Rashbam therefore says that where warranty remains, the seller's legal responsibility is still attached to the object and one can conceptualize the transfer as incomplete for purposes of that preliminary answer. But Abaye's objection shows that this conceptualization is not retained. This also answers Tosafot's question that despair can apply to land as well. Under Rav Sheshet's initial framework, land remains attached to the seller through the recourse rights of the purchaser in a way that ordinary movables subject to takanat hashuk do not. Abaye rejects the framework, forcing a different reading of the baraita. Thus there is no reason for later authorities to codify Rashbam's statement as halachah. One must also distinguish Rav Pappa's rule. Even where the sale says "without warranty," Rav Pappa says that if the property turns out not to belong to the seller, the purchaser can return against him. This itself leaves the seller's responsibility attached. But with movables subject to takanat hashuk, the buyer may recover his purchase price from the robbed owner and therefore need not return against the seller. Any excess value beyond the purchase price may remain outside the seller's obligation absent express warranty. Land is different because takanat hashuk does not apply. There the purchaser's entire purchase price remains exposed, so even a no-warranty sale can leave the seller's responsibility engaged under Rav Pappa. This neatly explains the language of the baraita under the preliminary discussion. A further problem arises according to Rambam and Mechaber below in siman 353, who hold that where there is despair plus change of possession but takanat hashuk does not apply, the purchaser can still be required to pay the value to the robbed owner. If so, even after a valid change of possession the original owner may retain a monetary claim against the purchaser. How then can the original owner testify here? He has a direct interest in keeping that payment obligation available. Shach notes this and suggests that the case is one where the object is known not to be worth more than the amount paid by the purchaser. Since the buyer receives reimbursement under takanat hashuk, nothing additional is owed. This is not overly strained. The ordinary assumption is that the object has not appreciated materially beyond its purchase price. The stronger problem is the Gemara's proposal to establish the case with an heir according to the opinion that an heir's domain is like that of a purchaser. An heir does not receive takanat hashuk. If the heir owes the object's value to the robbed owner, how can the owner testify without interest? The answer is the distinction stated above. Rambam's rule requiring the transferee to pay value after despair and change of possession applies where the change came through another person's act of conveyance while the object was still unlawfully held. The transfer begins in prohibition and only afterward is perfected through change of possession. Inheritance is different. It occurs automatically. No transferor conveys the object unlawfully. At the father's death, the heir's domain begins immediately and the change of possession is complete by operation of law. Therefore Rambam can agree that the heir owes no value. This also explains the Mishnah that a robber who dies leaving the stolen object to his children leaves them exempt, from which the Gemara derives that an heir's domain can count like a purchaser's. Accordingly, the heir suggestion here is fully consistent with Rambam. This framework also offers another explanation of Rashbam's words "without warranty." Rav holds that where the thief is known, the purchaser does not receive takanat hashuk because he can return against the thief. In our case the buyer obviously knows the seller. If Rashbam's sale had warranty, the buyer would return against the seller and takanat hashuk would not protect him. The seller would therefore be financially interested and could not testify. By specifying no warranty, Rashbam removes that recourse and allows takanat hashuk to operate. This reading is preferable to saying warranty destroys change of possession as a matter of acquisition. The latter is a major novel rule with no clear Talmudic foundation and no codified support. The former follows directly from Rav's takanat hashuk rule and the purchaser's recourse. One may object that the Gemara's question, "he despaired of the object, but did he despair of the money?" could then be read as a question about the purchaser's payment obligation rather than the robber's liability. That is strained. If so, the Gemara should have framed the challenge according to the dispute between Rav and R. Yochanan about a known thief. Also, when the Gemara later asks to establish the case with an heir, the whole point is that the robber has died. If the earlier question were about the purchaser's direct payment, the heir suggestion would not naturally follow. The better reading is that the Gemara's question concerns the robbed owner's monetary claim against the robber's estate, not the purchaser. This again supports interpreting Rashbam's no-warranty wording as a local procedural point rather than a broad doctrine. Accordingly, the practical halachah is: Despair plus a genuine change of possession can operate even where the original sale had warranty. Where warranty or statutory recourse creates an actual monetary claim against the seller, the seller may still be interested for purposes of testimony. But that is a question of financial interest, not of whether a legal change of possession occurred. Once the seller has died without assets and the buyer has no recourse, the change of possession stands regardless of the original warranty language. This explains why the poskim did not codify Rashbam's statement as a general rule. There is also a difficulty from the wording of the Gemara's question: "Granted he despaired of the object, did he despair of the money?" If the problem were that a purchaser who received the item with warranty has no true change of possession, the Gemara could have focused directly on the legal acquisition. Instead it asks about the robbed owner's monetary claim. This supports the explanation that the decisive issue is financial interest, not whether the buyer acquired title at all. The object may indeed pass through despair and change of possession, yet the robbed owner can retain a monetary claim somewhere in the chain. If that monetary claim is affected by the testimony, he remains interested. Once the robber has died without assets, that claim disappears, and the owner can testify. Thus the Gemara's final establishment removes the monetary interest rather than repairing the mechanics of change of possession. This also explains why the distinction between a buyer and an heir matters. A purchaser receives the object through a human act of transfer and may participate in a legal structure that leaves recourse against the seller. An heir receives automatically and has no seller against whom recourse can exist. The heir's acquisition therefore presents the clearest case of change of domain without an attached warranty chain. If the law nevertheless required the heir to pay the robbed owner, the Gemara's suggestion would fail. The fact that it makes the suggestion supports the conclusion that Rambam does not impose that payment in inheritance. The same distinction appears conceptually in the customs-officer case. Where one person wrongfully hands another person's donkey to a recipient, the recipient's acquisition comes through the wrongful possessor's act. The transfer itself begins while the object remains under a legal cloud. Rambam can therefore require value despite change of possession. Inheritance is not a transfer by the wrongdoer in the same sense. The law itself places the property in the heir's domain upon death. There is no new unlawful conveyance. For that reason the heir may acquire more cleanly. This resolves what otherwise would be a contradiction between Rambam's rule in siman 353 and the Mishnah that the robber's sons are exempt. It also lessens the force of the view attributed to Rashbam that warranty negates change of possession. If the real distinction throughout the sugya is whether the transferee's acquisition remains connected to a recoverable monetary claim, then "without warranty" can simply describe the absence of that claim. There is no need to redefine the acquisition itself. This is especially compelling because the poskim routinely recognize a legal change of possession in transactions even though separate contractual rights survive between buyer and seller. Warranty normally creates a personal claim for reimbursement; it does not mean the seller retained ownership in the sold object. To say that warranty prevents change of possession would therefore be a major departure from ordinary property law. Absent an explicit Talmudic source, one should avoid attributing such a novelty to Rashbam. Accordingly, his words are better read through the local takanat hashuk and recourse structure, or as part of the preliminary rejected explanation. A final practical consequence follows. Where the purchaser bought with warranty but has already irrevocably waived every claim against the seller before the dispute arose, there is no reason to say the change of possession fails. The seller may still be qualified if no other interest remains. If warranty itself destroyed the change of possession, later waiver would not repair the acquisition retroactively. The fact that ordinary monetary removal can qualify testimony strongly favors the view that warranty creates interest rather than a defective change of domain. This, in my view, is the correct practical understanding.
But if he comes to testify that it belongs to Reuven, etc. One might ask that the witness is still interested because he may wish to leave the property before his creditor. A creditor could say that the witness's admission does not bind him where it prejudices others. The answer is that once the witness admitted in beit din that the property belongs to Levi, if a creditor of Shimon later takes it from Levi, Shimon becomes responsible to Levi for the loss caused by his own admission. Levi would have been deprived of his field through Shimon's creditor even though, according to Shimon's testimony, the field was never Shimon's property. Thus the Gemara's original question returns. If Shimon has other land, the creditor can collect there. If he has no other land, what does he gain? He remains a wicked borrower toward Levi, because his creditor took Levi's field in satisfaction of Shimon's debt while Shimon had no right to expose that field. The principal explanation is therefore that this part follows the answer, "the second is more convenient for me." That rationale does not apply to preserving property before a creditor, because Shimon does not care whether one holder or another is easier for the creditor. His only concern is that the creditor have something to collect. Tosafot support this reading. They say the Gemara could simply have established the case as one where Reuven sold the property to Levi. At first glance that helps only under the "second is more convenient" answer and not under the creditor concern. If the issue were leaving property before a creditor, Shimon could still be interested despite the sale to Levi. Thus Tosafot's formulation shows that the relevant discussion is tied to the "second is more convenient" answer. This also clarifies the Gemara's suggestion, "let us establish it with a robber." If we read the passage through the creditor concern, the distinction between land and movables can be straightforward. Land can be preserved for a creditor, whereas ordinary movables are not subject to the same collection. But under the "second is more convenient" approach, different problems arise and the Gemara needs a more elaborate establishment. This may explain why the sugya appears to shift between several possible factual structures. A further question arises where each party brings witnesses. Suppose Yehudah first brought witnesses and Reuven succeeded in having them disqualified in beit din before the judges knew Reuven was interested. Later Reuven brings his own witnesses. Yehudah now argues that his original witnesses should be restored, because it has become clear that Reuven's earlier attack on them served his own interest. Rashbam in Bava Batra 44a gives two possibilities whether, once witnesses were disqualified, they remain disqualified for this matter or can be brought again after the interest becomes known. In that case there is a practical benefit to Reuven's original successful disqualification. Even if Yehudah's witnesses later return to qualification, while the property is already in Reuven's preferred holder's possession the rule of leaving land where it stands can help Reuven. This supports the Gemara's need to establish the case as one where each side has witnesses. Tosafot ask about the phrase "with this right that he removes it from Levi, let him remove it from Yehudah," but do not answer by saying that Reuven's witnesses are effective only to disqualify Yehudah's witnesses. The reason may be that if Yehudah's witnesses later return to qualification when Reuven's interest is exposed, the original disqualification alone gives Reuven no lasting victory. Ramban therefore prefers a different establishment, such as where Reuven has witnesses of theft but not witnesses of possession, or where Yehudah's witnesses are related to one party and unrelated to another. Still, once we recognize the first answer, "the second is more convenient," as primary, these complications are less decisive. Reuven's interest can exist even without a guaranteed final victory against Yehudah. There is also a point concerning despair of money in the robber case. If Reuven has witnesses that the object was stolen but those witnesses are now overseas, he may prefer Levi because he trusts Levi not to consume the cow or wear out the garment before the witnesses return. If Yehudah is less cooperative, the object may disappear or lose identifying features before the witnesses can recognize it. Even after despair and change of possession, Reuven may still need the witnesses to identify the specific stolen object in order to establish his monetary claim against the robber. Thus "the second is more convenient" can apply very concretely to movables as well. This explains why the Gemara sometimes needs the robber to have died before permitting testimony. If the robber remains alive and Reuven still hopes to prove the original theft, the identity and preservation of the object can affect his ability to recover money from the robber. Only when the robber has died without assets does that concern disappear. Accordingly, the apparently technical distinctions in the sugya all turn on whether Reuven retains some real future advantage from the asset being in one person's hands rather than another's. Where the advantage survives, he is interested. Where his own rights have been completely extinguished, he may testify.
In every case he may testify, etc. Nimukei Yosef derives from this that an interested person may testify to his own detriment. Here, although the witness would ordinarily be interested in saying the field does not belong to Yehudah, once he testifies that it belongs to Levi, that statement is accepted from him. This seems to show that financial interest does not remove him entirely from the category of witness where his statement harms himself. Sema and Shach likewise state this rule as though undisputed. But there are major dissenting authorities. Rosh, responsum klal 6, siman 20, rules that if townspeople testify that one resident is exempt from a tax, they cannot testify either for or against him because they themselves are among the taxpayers. Their own willingness to forgo part of the tax can bind their own shares through admission, but it does not make them valid witnesses regarding the remaining townspeople. This shows that an interested person can obligate himself through the rule that a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses, but he does not thereby become a witness whose testimony operates against others. Rosh's ruling follows his broader position and reflects a major dispute among the Rishonim. In Zevachim 71a, Rashi explains why an ox that killed or was sodomized according to its owner's own statement is not stoned: the owner is admitting to a fine. This implies that, absent the rule exempting one who admits a fine, the owner's statement could operate against the ox even though he has a monetary interest in it. Tosafot reject Rashi's exact reasoning, but their discussion too implies that, where another witness joins him, the owner's own interest does not necessarily prevent the substantive testimony from operating. This supports Nimukei Yosef's approach that a person can testify to his own detriment. Raavad and Rosh, however, take a different view. They explain the case "So-and-so sodomized my ox" by saying that because the owner's statement is invalid regarding the ox, it can be invalid regarding the person as well under the rule that testimony void in part is void in full. Why is his statement invalid regarding the ox? Not merely because of admission to a fine. If that were the only issue, there would be no "void testimony" to spread to the other portion. The problem must be that he is financially interested and therefore not within the category of testimony at all regarding the ox. This is exactly Rosh's position in the tax responsum. Thus the dispute is conceptual. One view says financial interest creates a suspicion of falsehood. When the person testifies clearly against his own interest, that suspicion disappears and the statement can function as testimony. The other view says an interested person is a litigant, not a witness. His personal admission can bind him, but he cannot become a witness for purposes of affecting another person's rights merely because the statement is adverse to himself. Rosh and Raavad follow the second view. In my opinion, even Rashi may not disagree. When the owner says the ox was sodomized, his statement can bind him personally like admission. Rashi therefore needs the rule that admission does not obligate a fine, because otherwise his admission would be enough to destroy his monetary ownership. But this does not necessarily mean he becomes a "witness." When the same statement also concerns another person, one might then say that because the words function as testimony regarding that person, we do not divide them and the entire statement takes on testimonial form. That can explain Rashi without establishing a broad rule that an interested litigant is always a valid witness against himself. There is an apparent proof for Rosh from Kiddushin 62a. The Mishnah says an ox sodomized by one witness or by its owner is prohibited to the altar. The Gemara asks what "by its owner" means. If it means the owner admitted it, that is simply the case "by the owner." A stronger question would seem available: if the owner's admission could combine with one valid witness to execute the ox, why does the Mishnah treat the owner's statement as merely creating sacrificial disqualification rather than capital liability? The implication is that the owner cannot function as a witness even when his statement harms his own property. Rambam's position is not fully decisive. He rules in Hilchot Edut that in some divided-testimony cases the ox can be stoned together with the human offender, but in Hilchot Issurei Mizbe'ach his wording concerning owner testimony is broader. This can be reconciled either way depending on whether one views the accepted portion as admission or as divided testimony. From pure logic as well, Rosh's position is compelling. If an interested person becomes a valid witness simply because the statement harms him, why should his brother not be able to testify against him financially? We know relatives are disqualified by decree even where there is no reason to suspect falsehood. Financial interest can likewise be a formal disqualification rather than a mere credibility concern. This supports Ir Shushan's view, discussed above, that an interested person is disqualified as a litigant, not merely because we suspect lying. Nimukei Yosef's proof from our sugya can be rejected. Here the financial interest is only a remote concern. Once the witness first states in beit din that the field belongs to Levi, the possible interest falls away. It is revealed retroactively that the concern was unfounded, and at the moment he gives the rest of his testimony he is no longer interested. Rashbam expressly explains that he first testifies that the field belongs to Levi and then that it does not belong to Yehudah. Since monetary disqualification does not require qualification both at the beginning and the end according to the relevant authorities, the later testimony can be valid. Thus the case need not prove that an interested person can testify while still interested merely because the statement harms him. Accordingly, the law is genuinely disputed. Rosh and Raavad hold that an interested person cannot function as a witness even to his own detriment, though his admission can bind himself. Nimukei Yosef and the authorities following him permit adverse testimony where the interest is thereby removed. This is a substantial unresolved dispute among the poskim and should not be treated as entirely simple.
