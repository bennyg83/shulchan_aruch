Once he admitted. Nimukei Yosef derives from this discussion that an interested person can testify when his testimony is to his own detriment. Tumim therefore suggests that an interested witness can testify for others whenever the testimony harms him as well, and points to the rule concerning a partner who makes a statement detrimental both to himself and his fellow. In my view the Rosh's responsum is primary. The Rosh rules that townspeople who are jointly liable for a tax cannot testify either for or against one of their number, because they are participants in the obligation. Their willingness to waive part of their own interest through an admission does not make them witnesses for others. This follows what I explained in siman 34: an interested witness is disqualified as one legally testifying about himself, not merely because we suspect falsehood. He is therefore disqualified both for benefit and detriment when the testimony also concerns others. A person's own adverse admission is accepted only because Torah law grants special credibility to a person concerning himself. It does not convert him into a witness for other people. The case in Nimukei Yosef is different. If the person admits the land belongs to Levi, he has thereby removed himself entirely from any legal relation to the land. Even if he had witnesses that the land was his, his own admission would defeat them, because a litigant's admission is stronger than witnesses concerning himself. Once he is no longer legally connected to the land, he is not an interested witness at all and may testify between the remaining parties. This is no worse than formally removing an interest. But townspeople who remain participants in the tax remain legally interested, even if they admit something adverse to themselves. The same principle is evident from the law that a debtor's purported testimony does not assist purchasers where there is concern of collusion. If adverse interest itself made him an ordinary witness, his statement should at least count as one supporting witness. It does not, because an interested litigant does not become a witness merely by speaking to his own detriment.
