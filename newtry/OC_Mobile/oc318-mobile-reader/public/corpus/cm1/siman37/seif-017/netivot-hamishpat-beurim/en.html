Because it may be convenient for him, etc. Urim VeTumim suggested that a creditor can collect even from property the debtor despaired of recovering, because despair is like abandonment and a creditor can sometimes collect from abandoned property. In my view this is incorrect. Despair is not ordinary abandonment. Its legal force specifically applies to property outside the owner's control and is a scriptural removal from his ownership. Indeed despair does not operate in the same way when the item remains within his control. Once the Torah itself removes the item through despair, the creditor's lien does not allow him to recover it from the subsequent possessor. Otherwise a creditor whose deed encumbers movables together with land could routinely collect found objects after the debtor had despaired, a rule never stated by the authorities.

But if he comes to testify that it belongs to Reuven, etc. Urim VeTumim asks that perhaps Shimon wishes to preserve the field for his own creditor, because his admission that it belongs to Reuven cannot prejudice that creditor. The answer is not that Reuven would have to reimburse Shimon if the creditor later takes the field. Rather, the whole concern for interest here is that Shimon wishes to avoid appearing before people as a wicked borrower who does not pay. Once Shimon admits the field belongs to Reuven, if Shimon's creditor later seizes it from Reuven, Shimon is not viewed as having paid his debt. The public instead sees his creditor as taking another person's property because Shimon failed to pay. That does not remove the stigma; it may even increase it. Therefore he has no such interest in the testimony.
