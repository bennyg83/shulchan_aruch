Likewise, in a case of this kind we do not say that absence of observation is no proof, etc. At the beginning of my Siftei Kohen on Yoreh Deah I used this principle to defend the Agur, who wrote that women should not be permitted to perform shechitah because the accepted custom was that they did not do so. The Beit Yosef objected that 'we have not seen it done' is not proof. I wrote that with respect to an established custom, the fact that a practice was never observed is itself evidence, as Maharik and the Rama rule here. Some challenged me, arguing that Maharik and the Rama speak only where an opportunity arose and people deliberately refrained because of the custom. Shiltei Giborim indeed limits Maharik in this manner. In my view, however, Shiltei Giborim did not understand Maharik correctly. Maharik's words plainly distinguish ordinary factual testimony, where one group says it saw an event and another merely says it did not see it, from testimony concerning communal practice, where no one testifies that earlier generations ever acted otherwise. In the latter setting, universal nonobservance establishes the custom. The same follows from Maharik's examples, such as communities that do not recite Shehecheyanu or certain blessings, or do not complete a fast on Friday. One need not prove that each generation faced the precise case and consciously abstained. A stable and public absence of practice is sufficient when the matter is one that would ordinarily have occurred and become known. This is also the force of the Rama's ruling here. Accordingly, the Agur's statement concerning women and shechitah is sound: had women customarily slaughtered, the practice would have been known. Since it was never observed, the nonobservance is evidence of the custom. This applies only to a settled public practice and not to a chance occurrence that may simply never have arisen. 7100160
