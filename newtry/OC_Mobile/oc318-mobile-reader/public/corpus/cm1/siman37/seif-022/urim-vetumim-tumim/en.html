"We have not seen" is not proof. Shach writes at length to defend the Agur's statement at the beginning of the laws of shechitah that women did not customarily slaughter because "we have never seen women slaughtering." Beit Yosef answered that absence of observation is not proof. Shach argues from our law that regarding established custom, "we have not seen" can be proof. I do not understand the defense. Where it is already known that practices differ among communities, or the poskim record alternative customs, lack of observation can establish which custom a particular place follows. For example, concerning gifts sent before kiddushin, some places treat them as evidence of kiddushin and others do not. If reliable people say they never saw this community treat such gifts as creating concern, that absence can establish the local custom. Likewise, where the poskim record competing practices concerning an eruv or similar matter, the fact that one practice was never observed can identify which opinion the community follows. But regarding women slaughtering, there was no prior known dispute of local custom establishing that some communities prohibit it and others permit it. The Agur argues merely: I never happened to see a woman slaughter, therefore a prohibitory custom exists. That is not proof. One cannot create a new custom solely from absence of observation where no independent basis shows that the practice was intentionally avoided. Beit Yosef's objection is therefore correct.
