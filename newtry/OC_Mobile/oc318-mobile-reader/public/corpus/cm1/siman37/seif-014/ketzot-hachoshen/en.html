To the second purchaser. Bava Batra teaches that a first purchaser may testify for a second purchaser provided the seller has other land. Rashbam explains that the first purchaser fears the seller's creditor may seize his field. If the second purchaser has other land, the first can say to the creditor, "I left you a place from which to collect." Tosafot rejects this explanation, because one would still have to fear an especially large creditor. Tosafot instead explains that the first purchaser fears the field he himself bought may later be found stolen. He therefore wants the seller's remaining assets preserved so that he will have property from which to collect his warranty claim. Once the seller or the second purchaser has sufficient land to satisfy that warranty, the first purchaser is no longer interested. A practical difference follows where the first purchaser bought expressly without warranty. According to Rashbam he may still be interested because of an ordinary creditor. According to Tosafot, since he has no warranty claim at all, there is no remaining interest. Ramban agrees with Tosafot.
That a field remain with the seller. Tosafot asks why a purchaser should not be able to testify for the seller when the seller owns other land. According to Tosafot's rationale, the other land exists to preserve the purchaser's warranty claim. The answer is that the purchaser's warranty against the seller is not fixed to a determinate amount. The standard warranty formula obligates the seller for the purchase price, exertion, and improvements. The value of future improvements can rise without limit. Even if the seller presently owns many fields, the purchaser may still prefer that the seller retain more assets so that his eventual claim for improvements will be secured. Therefore one cannot define a point at which the purchaser has no remaining interest in the seller's property. By contrast, when the first purchaser testifies for a second purchaser, his potential claim against the second purchaser is limited to the principal amount, because improvement is not collected from encumbered property. Once sufficient land remains for that fixed amount, the first purchaser is no longer interested. This resolves Tosafot's difficulty.
