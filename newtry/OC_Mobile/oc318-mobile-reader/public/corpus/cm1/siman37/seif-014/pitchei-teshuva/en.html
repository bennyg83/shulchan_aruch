Nowadays the custom is, etc. Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 8, writes that this applies specifically to communal enactments where there is an established local custom, but not automatically to a communal claim against individuals. Even if there is an established custom concerning communal enactments, that does not by itself extend to suits by the community against individuals unless such a custom is independently established. Otherwise the Rosh's responsa would contradict one another. The present rule that "nowadays the custom is" derives from the Rosh, klal 5, ruling 4, whereas in klal 6 he ruled in favor of Rabbi Menachem because the witnesses from the community who had signed the conditions were interested. Rather, the relaxation applies only where there is a fixed custom or enactment, as Shulchan Aruch, siman 7, states regarding judges: "if they enacted" or "if there is a custom." Maharam Lublin, siman 42, likewise ruled regarding a Torah scroll that testimony of townspeople was invalid because the entire city was interested. Nachalat Shivah, siman 27, similarly distinguishes an established custom from a practice that developed informally. People cannot simply declare such a practice an established custom unless it is known with certainty to be ancient. Although he offers another distinction that is not convincing, this first distinction is sound. Thus, according to all views, where no fixed custom exists, townspeople who are interested are disqualified. In the case discussed there, the community therefore had to bring valid proof to remove property from the heirs' possession; testimony of communal members did not suffice because all were interested. Until valid proof was produced, the widow remained entitled to possession upon swearing that she knew her claim to be true. However, Shev Yaakov appears to contradict himself in siman 4. There he discusses four members of the Mantova community who argued that the community lacked authority to impose a levy on assets outside the country according to an amount it had assessed, and who wished to submit to a beit din of another city because all judges of their own city were interested. He replied that although their claim initially appears correct from Bava Batra 43, nevertheless they must litigate in their own city because of the communal custom cited by Rashba that for public matters there is no need to bring qualified witnesses from outside, and the same applies to judges. This indicates that even where no independent proof of the custom is known, it is treated as established by virtue of the fixed custom recorded by Shulchan Aruch in the name of the Rosh and Rashba. His siman 3 likewise indicates this. Nachalat Shivah, siman 27, cites Divrei Rivot, siman 197, who writes that because the rule rests on custom rather than strict law, only a fixed and universally known city custom that communal members are believed can override the halakha. An uncertain or non-universal custom cannot displace a settled rule. Rama responsa, siman 73, also somewhat imply this, since in the case there he found it necessary to say that they had a custom to adjudicate all public matters in the major city, despite already citing Rashba's responsum that public affairs are customarily adjudicated by local judges. Maharam Padua in siman 51 there similarly speaks this way, though perhaps those statements were merely added for greater strength. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef note 37, cites Rabbi Shmuel Yafeh from a manuscript responsum that in ordinary places we do not rely on this custom, and Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 66, writes that in practice the custom did not spread. In my opinion, however, none of this proves the point for our times. All these great authorities lived before the Shulchan Aruch had become universally established. Nowadays, since this practice has been fixed in the Shulchan Aruch itself, it has the status of a standing custom for generations. Shev Yaakov's proof from Maharam Lublin is also not decisive, because as Shev Yaakov himself writes in siman 3, the rule may differ when the defendant is from another city. Maharam Lublin's case may have involved such an outsider. Nor is the wording of Shulchan Aruch in siman 7, "if they enacted" or "if there is a custom," decisive. There the Mechaber may simply have copied Tur to teach that enactment or custom is effective, while waiting until the conclusion of the laws of judges and testimony to cite the Rosh and Rashba that such a custom has in fact become accepted. We find analogous drafting in Rama, siman 155, seif 35, as Taz explains there. Chatam Sofer responsa, siman 38, cited below in seif katan 16, also support the formulation of Shev Yaakov in simanim 3 and 4. The matter requires study. See Birkei Yosef on this subject.
