Provided that land remains with the seller, etc. Most authorities explain that the claimant seeking to remove the field from the second purchaser claims that it is stolen property. If he were merely a creditor, the second purchaser could answer that free property remained with the seller and direct him there. Raavad instead explains that a creditor seeks the field from the second purchaser because the field was designated as an explicit apotiki, so the purchaser cannot redirect him elsewhere. Shach questions why Kesef Mishneh did not explain Raavad's words explicitly. Raavad's reason, however, is understandable. Tosafot hold that we do not ordinarily suspect undisclosed creditors. They therefore must explain the first purchaser's possible interest through a concern of stolen property. But if the present claimant himself comes on a theft claim and removes the second purchaser's field, there is no reason to suspect a second theft as well. We do not ordinarily fear two separate stolen fields. Thus, if that were the case, the first purchaser could testify even where no other land remains. Raavad therefore explains that the present claimant is an apotiki creditor, not a theft claimant. The first purchaser can then still fear that his own field is stolen. Tosafot's approach points in the same direction, although they do not mention apotiki expressly. Another possible reading is that the "other land" remains not with the seller, but with the second purchaser, who bought two fields. The claimant takes one for his debt and another field remains with the second purchaser equal in value to the first purchaser's claim. Then the first purchaser has no concern: if his field is later taken, his recourse remains against the second purchaser's remaining property. This avoids the need to say the field was an apotiki. Still, if the other field remains with the seller, a further concern arises. The first purchaser might fear loss of the increase in value. If the second purchaser's field is taken now, the second purchaser may return upon the seller's free field. Later, if the first purchaser's own field is discovered stolen, he may have recourse only against property already transferred to the second purchaser and may lose recovery of the appreciation, since appreciation is not ordinarily collected from encumbered property. If the additional field remains with the second purchaser from the outset, this concern is weaker. One might say the first purchaser would not lie merely because of a contingent claim to appreciation. The seller could sell the field at any moment, so the future benefit is uncertain. But then Tosafot's question why the first purchaser may testify for the second purchaser but not for the seller becomes difficult, since that very uncertainty would explain the distinction. Perhaps where the second purchaser acquired property through collection after his original field was taken, the first purchaser can collect appreciation from him because the later acquisition was not a voluntary purchase and the usual public-policy limitation on collecting appreciation from encumbered property does not apply. This requires study. There is another explanation of Tosafot. Tosafot's statement that we do not suspect creditors may mean specifically written creditors. A lender normally investigates before lending, including whether earlier written debts exist, because such debts can defeat him. A purchaser, however, does not investigate earlier oral debts, since oral debts do not collect from encumbered property. Accordingly, if a field is removed from the second purchaser and free property remains with the seller, the first purchaser may still fear an earlier oral creditor who will collect the seller's free property. If the first purchaser's own field is later found stolen, the free property may then be gone. But if the remaining property is itself already in the second purchaser's hands, an oral creditor cannot collect it. Written creditors are not feared because purchasers normally investigate them. This gives a practical difference between the approaches. Under Rashbam, if a theft claimant attacks the second purchaser, the first purchaser cannot testify unless other property remains, because he fears a creditor. Under Tosafot and Raavad, since ordinary creditors are not feared, the first purchaser could testify even without other land if the present challenge is theft, because we do not fear a second theft. Tosafot's view appears stronger because Rosh and Hagahot HaRosh follow it. Nimukei Yosef follows Rashbam. Still, it is surprising that the poskim do not record the practical difference. Perhaps Tosafot also fear two thefts after all, once the present claimant's theft allegation is excluded by the first purchaser's own testimony. The present claim is then not counted as a true theft concern, leaving room to fear one real theft concerning the first purchaser's field. Yet Tosafot explicitly speak of concern for one theft, which implies not two. Accordingly, Raavad's apotiki explanation remains the best fit for Tosafot. Another practical difference arises if the first purchaser bought without warranty. According to Rashbam, he can still be interested because an earlier creditor may redirect himself to the first purchaser if the second field disappears. According to Tosafot, who do not fear creditors and where there is no warranty, his concern may vanish entirely. Rashbam's system can also be defended from the distinction between one and two layers of creditor concern. We fear one plausible creditor, but not a chain requiring both the seller and the first purchaser to have separate unknown creditors and both relevant fields to be encumbered. That becomes too remote. After considerable examination, the most plausible reading of Rashbam is this. The first purchaser fears an existing creditor. Why has the creditor delayed collecting? Because he knows the second purchaser's field is subject to a theft claim. If he collects it now, he may later be forced to begin collection proceedings again from the first purchaser after the true owner takes it. He therefore waits until the theft issue is resolved. The first purchaser thus has reason to preserve the second purchaser's field for that creditor. By contrast, when a theft claimant attacks the first purchaser's own field, the second purchaser need not fear such a creditor. If a creditor truly existed, he would already have come against the second purchaser, because the first purchaser's field is itself the allegedly stolen one and does not provide the same reason for delay. Accordingly, where additional land remains, the second purchaser may testify. This is, in my view, the best reading of Rashbam, though his language is still compressed. A further consequence follows. If an explicit apotiki creditor attacks the first purchaser, the second purchaser should not testify where the remaining field may itself be stolen, unless one adopts the broader view stated above that Rashbam does not fear theft at all in that configuration. There is also a possible way to defend Tosafot without reading the claimant as an apotiki creditor. If one follows Ir Shushan's underlying theory that an interested person is disqualified because he stands as a litigant rather than because we suspect deliberate lying, then even if the present claimant's theft allegation is known by the first purchaser to be false, that does not eliminate the witness's status as a litigant regarding his own possible field. The present false theft claim is not itself counted among the feared future defects. One may therefore still fear one genuine theft concerning the first purchaser's field without saying we fear "two thefts." This would allow the claimant against the second purchaser to be a theft claimant even under Tosafot. Nevertheless, this explanation depends on the broader theory that financial interest is a formal litigant-status disqualification and not ordinary suspicion of falsehood. I discussed that dispute above, and it cannot be assumed universally. The distinction between property remaining with the seller and property remaining with the second purchaser also affects the appreciation claim. Suppose the second purchaser's field is taken and he receives the seller's remaining free field in satisfaction of his warranty. If the first purchaser later loses his own field, his claim against the second purchaser may be only against encumbered property. Ordinarily a purchaser does not collect appreciation from encumbered property because of tikkun olam. But perhaps where the second purchaser received the seller's field not through voluntary purchase but through collection after dispossession, the reason of tikkun olam does not apply. He did not voluntarily buy property and then improve it while relying on public title. He merely received it as satisfaction of an existing warranty claim. If so, the first purchaser may recover appreciation as well, and the supposed interest disappears. This point itself requires study. There is a further way to explain why Tosafot do not fear ordinary creditors while Rashbam does. A lender who is about to lend investigates both written and oral claims against the borrower, because even an earlier oral debt can defeat his position against free property. A purchaser, however, need investigate only written liens that can follow the land. An earlier oral creditor cannot collect from encumbered property once the purchaser buys. Thus when the seller still retains free land, the first purchaser may fear an earlier oral creditor that he never investigated. That creditor can take the free land. If the first purchaser later loses his own field, his warranty source will be gone. This concern does not exist if the additional field is already in the second purchaser's possession, because an oral creditor cannot collect from it. This neatly explains why "other land" may need to be land already held by the second purchaser rather than merely land left with the seller. It also explains why the same concern does not arise for a lender. Before making the loan, the lender investigates earlier debts, including oral obligations to the extent relevant, because his own recovery depends on the borrower's free property. Accordingly, when the Gemara elsewhere allows a lender to testify once other property remains, we need not fear unknown oral creditors to the same degree. This line of reasoning produces another difference between the approaches of Tosafot and Rashbam. If the present claimant is an ordinary creditor rather than an apotiki creditor, and the second purchaser can answer, "I left free property with the seller," then the creditor should collect there. The first purchaser's possible interest lies only in the possibility that some prior oral creditor will consume that free property. Under Rashbam this possibility can matter; under Tosafot it is normally too remote. If, however, the claimant is an explicit apotiki creditor, he can ignore the free field and take the designated property directly. Then the first purchaser's concern is much more immediate, and even Tosafot can understand the disqualification. This is why Raavad's reading has particular force. I also considered whether Rashbam might hold that concern for a creditor is limited to one creditor but not two. Suppose the first purchaser fears one creditor of the seller. That is plausible. But to create an interest in the reverse case we might need both a creditor of the seller and a separate creditor of the first purchaser, with each field encumbered in the precise order necessary to cause loss. That is already a compound chain of remote possibilities. We do not invalidate testimony for such compounded speculation. This provides another reason the reverse testimony can be valid when an additional field remains. Still, none of these explanations fits every phrase of Rashbam perfectly. His language is simple: the first purchaser would collect from the second purchaser. He does not explicitly introduce a creditor of the first purchaser, an oral creditor, appreciation, or a special apotiki arrangement. That is why I ultimately prefer the delay explanation stated above. An earlier creditor's failure to appear is itself explained by the instability of the second purchaser's field. He waits until the theft controversy is resolved, because otherwise his first collection may be undone. The first purchaser therefore has a realistic present reason to preserve the second purchaser's title. In the reverse case, that reason for creditor delay is absent. This interpretation uses only one creditor and one presently disputed field, and is therefore less strained than the alternatives. It also explains why we do not say every buyer in the world is always interested because perhaps some unknown creditor exists. The concern becomes relevant only where the circumstances themselves explain why such a creditor would rationally have delayed appearing until now. That is an important limitation. Accordingly, under Rashbam we do not generally fear all hidden debts. We fear one where the litigation structure supplies a reason for the creditor's silence. This is the most satisfactory reconciliation of his ruling with the general rules of testimonial interest.
Likewise, the second purchaser, etc. Rashbam writes that if the first purchaser bought without warranty, the second purchaser may testify even where no additional land remains. According to Tosafot, who do not ordinarily fear unknown creditors, this is straightforward. Once the first purchaser bears no warranty claim, the second purchaser has no indirect interest. But according to Rashbam, who does fear creditors, the case should be distinguished. If the first purchaser's field was taken for an ordinary debt, or through an apotiki claim, the second purchaser can testify because we do not fear two separate creditor claims. But if the first purchaser's field was taken as stolen property, the second purchaser may still be interested. Perhaps the seller owes another creditor and had designated the first purchaser's field as an apotiki. If that field remains validly with the first purchaser, the apotiki creditor has no claim against the second purchaser. But if the first purchaser loses the field as stolen property, then the apotiki was never validly the seller's. Under the law below in siman 114, the apotiki creditor can return against the seller's other assets, including property now held by the second purchaser. The second purchaser would therefore lose. Rashbam does not make this distinction, perhaps because he follows Ramban's view that even where an apotiki is found not to belong to the debtor, the creditor still does not return against other property. But according to our ruling in siman 114 that he does return, the distinction stated above appears correct.
Unless other property remains, etc. Ir Shushan asks: what does it help that another field remains? Perhaps that field too is stolen, in which case the first purchaser could later return against the second purchaser. His answer is that we do not fear two separate thefts. Sema calls this reason remote; Shach defends it. According to Tosafot, who do not fear creditors and explain the concern only through theft, Ir Shushan's answer fits well. Once one field has already been removed as stolen, we do not additionally fear that the remaining field is also stolen. Still, if the first field was removed by an apotiki creditor rather than by a theft claimant, one might still fear that the remaining field is stolen. One can answer that sale of an apotiki is itself analogous to wrongful taking: the creditor has a special proprietary claim against that field, and selling it is treated like disposing of another's designated property. Two such independent defects are not ordinarily feared. This works for Tosafot. Rashbam remains harder because he also fears ordinary creditors. If the remaining field is with the seller, perhaps an earlier creditor will take it. Then, if the first purchaser later loses his own field, he will have no free property left from which to collect. Why is he not interested? Perishah and Bach answer similarly: the first purchaser needs the second purchaser's field to remain as a source of warranty, whereas the second purchaser does not regard the first purchaser's field as part of his own warranty position. This is difficult. Even if the second purchaser bought without warranty, when the first purchaser's field disappears he may become exposed to an earlier creditor who can now reach him. His legal position is plainly weakened. Maharsha uses this asymmetry as proof for Rashbam: the baraita says the first purchaser can testify for the second only if other land remains, whereas the reverse testimony is allowed more broadly. But this is exactly what is difficult to explain. For Rav Pappa, who holds that even a sale without warranty can be rescinded if the property is found not to belong to the seller, the difficulty is even stronger. Why should the reverse case be simpler? At first I thought to explain Tur differently. Many of these concerns are remote. Perhaps ordinarily they would not be enough to establish suspicion of false testimony. But where the witness has an existing property lien over the very assets in question, he resembles a litigant regarding his own encumbered property. Then even a remote financial concern is enough to prevent testimony. By contrast, the second purchaser has no lien on the first purchaser's property. If another free field remains, any further danger is only speculative and unrelated to property presently securing him. Therefore he may testify. This explains Tur better, but not Rashbam, who says even a first purchaser without warranty may still be unable to testify. There is then no warranty lien at all. The best explanation of Rashbam appears to be based on the timing of unknown creditors. Tosafot do not fear an unknown written creditor because if such a creditor existed, he would normally have appeared earlier. Rashbam does fear one creditor, but not a chain of multiple hidden obligations and multiple encumbered fields. Why might the creditor have delayed? Because he knows the second purchaser's field is subject to a theft claim. If he collects it now and the true owner later removes it, he will have to begin new collection proceedings against the first purchaser. Therefore he waits for resolution of the theft claim. This gives the first purchaser a present interest in supporting the second purchaser's title. But in the reverse case, where a claimant attacks the first purchaser's field, the second purchaser cannot use the same concern. A creditor of the seller who could collect from the second purchaser has no comparable reason to wait: the first purchaser's field is the very field under attack and is not a secure alternate source. Thus, if other land remains, the second purchaser can testify. If no other land remains, his interest is direct because the first purchaser may immediately return against him. This is the most satisfactory reading of Rashbam I have found. One more consequence follows. If an explicit apotiki creditor attacks the first purchaser rather than a theft claimant, then under Rashbam the second purchaser should not testify where the remaining field may be defective. The delay rationale no longer applies in the same way. This point is not clearly stated by the poskim, but follows from the structure above. The point about an existing lien deserves further clarification. Where a purchaser bought with warranty, his right against the seller's later property is not a mere future possibility. It is already a legal lien securing him. In a broad sense, he has a present right in the seller's assets. For this reason, even a remote chain of events affecting those assets can make his testimony resemble testimony concerning his own property. That is unlike a person whose only possible benefit depends on becoming wealthy someday or on some future voluntary transaction. There, Tosafot say we do not suspect false testimony because the benefit is not presently anchored in a legal right. This may explain why Tur is stringent in the first-purchaser case even where the specific future loss is uncertain. The witness does not merely hope for a future benefit. He already owns a warranty lien that may be weakened by the present judgment. Still, Rashbam's allowance where the first purchaser bought without warranty remains difficult on this approach, because there is then no lien. One might answer that even without formal warranty, according to Rav Pappa a sale found not to belong to the seller can be rescinded. The first purchaser therefore still has a restitution claim against the seller. But R. Zevid disagrees regarding land, and our practical law in some contexts follows his distinction. Rashbam's ruling cannot comfortably depend on a disputed restitution theory without saying so. This is why Maharsha's proof from the asymmetry remains incomplete. Maharsha says: if Tosafot were correct that hidden creditors are not feared, why does the baraita not simply state the reverse case, that the second purchaser testifies for the first? Rashbam's very choice of direction shows that creditor concern remains. But for Rav Pappa the reverse case can fail for a different reason, because the second purchaser may retain a direct restitution claim if the first purchaser's title fails. Thus the baraita's wording alone cannot prove Rashbam's creditor theory. The distinction between a theft claimant and an apotiki creditor is also crucial. If the first purchaser's field is taken by an ordinary theft claimant, then according to Ir Shushan we have already used up the normal "one theft" concern. To fear that the remaining field is also stolen would be a second theft and too remote. But if the field is taken by a creditor on an explicit apotiki, the loss is not the same kind of defect. One can still fear that the remaining field is stolen, because the first loss came from debt, not theft. I suggested above that sale of an apotiki resembles wrongful taking enough to count as the same kind of defect. That is possible, but not obvious. Indeed, the law that an apotiki creditor may collect appreciation beyond principal rests on the creditor saying, "My land itself was improved," which gives the designated field a quasi-proprietary status. That makes its unauthorized sale resemble disposition of property already specially allocated to another. If so, apotiki plus theft may be treated as two variants of the same underlying title defect. But ordinary debt collection certainly cannot be treated that way. Therefore the precise ground on which the first field left the purchaser matters. This yields a practical distinction not stated explicitly by the early authorities and therefore should be applied cautiously. There is another possible resolution based on the rule "the second is easier for me and the first harder." A litigant may be interested not only in whether he has some legal recourse, but also in which person he will have to litigate against. If one purchaser is known to be difficult and another cooperative, the identity of the person holding the property can itself create interest. Thus even where an alternative field technically remains, the witness may prefer one configuration because his future collection would be easier. This principle appears elsewhere in the Gemara and is part of the law of testimonial interest. However, we cannot use it without limitation, because then almost every witness with any remote commercial connection would be disqualified. The concern must be concrete and tied to an existing claim. In the present case, the warranty relation supplies that connection. The reverse case is weaker because the second purchaser may have no claim at all against the first purchaser. This again supports the general asymmetry, even if the technical creditor analysis remains difficult. After considering all these possibilities, I return to the interpretation stated at the end of the first discussion. Rashbam fears a creditor only where there is a rational explanation for the creditor's delay. The pending theft claim against the second purchaser supplies that explanation. The creditor waits because immediate collection may be undone, requiring repeated court proceedings. The first purchaser therefore wants the second purchaser's field preserved until the creditor collects there. If the claimant instead attacks the first purchaser's field, the second purchaser cannot posit the same reason for creditor delay. A creditor who can reach the second purchaser would have no reason to wait for resolution of a theft claim concerning the first purchaser's separate field. Once another field remains, any additional chain of creditor concerns becomes too remote. This reading also explains why the Gemara can state the law generally without cataloging all hypothetical liens and hidden debts. The disqualifying interest arises from the ordinary and immediate structure of the case, not from every conceivable future loss. Accordingly, where the facts depart from that structure, especially with an apotiki creditor or known competing liens, the testimony must be reassessed according to the actual interest present.
