The first purchaser, etc. This applies where another field remains in the possession of the second purchaser. The Raavad, in his glosses to chapter 15 of Hilchot Edut, writes that this is specifically where the creditor designated the field as an apotiki. The Kesef Mishneh cites him without comment. At first sight the Raavad is difficult. It appears that he understands the case as one in which a creditor seeks to seize the field from the second purchaser for his debt, while the first purchaser wishes to testify for the second that the promissory note is invalid, so that the field will remain with the second purchaser. An apotiki is therefore required, for otherwise the creditor cannot collect from the second purchaser at all, since the latter may say, 'I left unencumbered property in the seller's possession for you to collect.' Where the field was made an apotiki, however, he cannot make that argument, as explained below in seif katan 17. The Rambam and the other authorities, by contrast, understand the case as one in which the dispossessed original owner seeks to recover the field. In that setting there is no distinction between an apotiki and an ordinary sale, for the concept of apotiki is altogether irrelevant.
