Shach writes that many disagree. Maharam explains the Gemara's question, "let two of them remove themselves and judge," as referring specifically to judges who become disinterested before judging. If they had already seen the event while interested and now wished to testify, he would regard the testimony as having begun in disqualification. Rosh, responsum klal 55, proves from the law of partners that Maharam is wrong: a partner can remove his interest and then testify. Shach rejects Rosh's proof. He notes that Tosafot ask the beginning-in-disqualification question specifically regarding the townspeople who are asked to remove themselves, not regarding the partner. Perhaps the partner's case simply means he observed the relevant evidence before becoming interested. This is strained. If the partner had already left the partnership before the dispute arose, why call him a partner and why is there any novelty in Shmuel's rule that partners can testify for one another after removal? It is more natural that he was a partner when the dispute arose and only then removes himself to qualify as a witness. One can answer that he observed the disqualifying facts concerning the opposing witnesses many years earlier, before the partnership existed. The present issue is not testimony about the property itself but testimony that the other witnesses are robbers or otherwise disqualified. In that sense his "beginning" can still have been in qualification. This explains why Tosafot did not necessarily ask their question from the partner case. Still, Rosh's responsum contains a stronger proof. The Gemara challenges Shmuel's rule about a partner from the baraita that townspeople may not judge or testify concerning communal property. It asks why two residents cannot remove themselves. If the issue were merely collusion, there would be no real contradiction. We could say Shmuel does not fear collusion, while the baraita does. Rather, the Gemara assumes Shmuel's novelty is that removal works even though the person was initially interested. It therefore asks why the same should not work for townspeople. The answer is that a Torah scroll belongs in a special way to everyone and cannot be fully abandoned from communal use. This shows that, for ordinary monetary interest, removal cures the disqualification even where the person was initially interested. The same reasoning applies to judges. One might have thought removal is less effective for judges because judging depends on mental impartiality. Even if they surrender their monetary share, their minds may remain inclined toward the community. Indeed, in Ketubot judges who refused a gift still declined to judge the giver because the attempted benefit had already drawn their hearts closer. This concern is stronger for judges than for witnesses, because a friend or enemy can testify but may not judge. Thus, if the Gemara still asks that townspeople remove themselves and judge, it must assume that ordinary prior monetary interest can be cured completely by removal. This strengthens Rosh. The wording "let two of them remove themselves" also fits witnesses better than judges. According to Shmuel, even one judge can technically decide, though doing so may be improper. If the Gemara referred only to judges, why specify two rather than the number needed for a court under the applicable procedural rule? By contrast, two is exactly the required number of witnesses. Also, there are usually many possible judges elsewhere. The practical need to remove local residents is more obvious for witnesses, because witnesses to a local event may not exist outside the town. This again supports Rosh's interpretation. There is an additional proof from the earlier passage. The Gemara asks: once the witness testifies that the field belongs to Levi, how can he then remove it from Levi? At the moment he originally saw the evidence that would defeat Yehudah's claim, he may still have been interested, because he had not yet made the admission that removed his possible right. If beginning in qualification were required for monetary interest, his later admission could not cure the defect. Yet the Gemara assumes that once he has removed his interest, the testimony can operate. This is close to a direct proof that monetary interest does not require qualification at the beginning. One can attempt to avoid it by saying the witness's testimony concerns the personal disqualification of other witnesses, such as their being known robbers. He may have observed that misconduct at a time when he had no connection to this later property dispute. But the Gemara does not limit the case to that unusual form. Rashbam explicitly allows other testimony. It is difficult to force the entire sugya into a narrow witness-disqualification case merely to preserve the requirement of beginning in qualification. Similarly, the cow-and-garment baraita says generally that the person may testify after his interest disappears. If the relevant facts were known to him while he was still the robbed owner or otherwise interested, beginning-in-disqualification would again seem to block him. The broad formulation strongly suggests that monetary interest differs from intrinsic witness disqualification. There is also support from the rule, cited by Mordechai and accepted by Shach above in siman 34, concerning one who says, "He lent to me with interest." If the witness has no remaining claim to return of the interest, he may disqualify the lender. But at the time of the interest transaction he plainly had a monetary stake in the matter. If prior interest permanently disqualified the testimony, later waiver or removal would never help. Likewise, two robbed parties can sometimes testify after the property issue is resolved even though at the time of the robbery each had an interest in recovering his own property. All this favors the rule that ordinary financial interest is curable by later removal. The conceptual reason is the one stated by Ramban and Rivash. An interested person is not intrinsically a disqualified witness like a relative or robber. His defect is that, while the interest exists, he stands as a litigant in the matter. Once the interest disappears, he enters the category of witness. Because the earlier period was not a period of "invalid witness testimony" but a period in which he was not yet functioning as a witness at all, the ordinary rule requiring beginning and end in qualification does not apply in the same way. This reasoning can extend even to close relatives of the interested person only according to some authorities. Ramban explicitly distinguishes. If removal helps because monetary disqualification itself does not require qualification at the beginning, then perhaps the relative of an interested person can also testify after the interest is removed. But if removal helps because the interested person is merely a litigant outside testimony, his relative remains an ordinary relative of that litigant at the beginning and may retain the usual beginning-in-disqualification problem. Rosh and Raavad support the stricter view in some contexts, while Ramban and other authorities distinguish as above. Therefore, although the principal rule is that the interested person himself can testify after complete removal, the status of his relative is more doubtful. Given the division among the authorities, one should not extend the leniency unnecessarily to relatives. Maharam's broad dissent also remains relevant. At minimum, where no clear Talmudic proof supports the relative's qualification, the possessor can invoke the stricter authorities. Accordingly, the practical conclusion is: For the interested person himself, the majority of major authorities, including Ramban, Rivash, Tosafot in several places, Rosh, and the plain Gemara, support that complete removal cures the monetary interest even if the relevant facts were known earlier. For the relative of the interested person, the law is more doubtful and may depend on the conceptual basis for the removal rule. In that case, one can claim kim li according to the stricter view.
