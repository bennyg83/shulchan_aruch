That if he becomes wealthy. See Baer Heitev and the gloss of Rabbi Moshe, author of Tiferet LeMoshe. He explains a practical case as follows. Suppose a seller sold land with warranty for the principal but not for appreciation and produce. The seller is not interested merely because he wishes to avoid being viewed as a wicked borrower, since in any event he will be regarded that way toward the purchaser if he fails to pay; assume it is known that his obligation equals the full purchase price. One might nevertheless have thought him interested because he prefers a creditor to seize the land for the entire debt, for example one thousand gold pieces, its present value, leaving him liable to the purchaser only for the five hundred gold pieces actually paid. When he later becomes wealthy he would then need to pay only five hundred, since he did not accept warranty for appreciation. The law therefore teaches that this is not sufficient to render him interested. Alternatively, suppose he expressly designated the field as an apotiki for a debt of one thousand gold pieces, and the field then deteriorated and became worth only one hundred. He sold it with warranty, and another person challenged title claiming the field was stolen, while the seller had not accepted ordinary warranty or it is otherwise known that the field is truly his. One might think the seller seeks to preserve the field for the creditor because if he later becomes wealthy he will need to reimburse the purchaser only one hundred, and the creditor cannot pursue him further because the field was an express apotiki. If the title claimant takes the land as stolen, however, he would later owe the creditor the full thousand. See below, siman 117. The ruling teaches that this too does not make him interested. Tumim gives several additional applications. See Ketzot HaChoshen, Netivot HaMishpat, and Rabbi Moshe Rothenberg responsa, Choshen Mishpat, siman 7.
