A kablan guarantor, etc. Rashbam explains the Gemara's case of a kablan as one who received the money from the lender and handed it to the borrower. Shach objects: if so, the borrower should be completely discharged from direct liability to the lender. The kablan who received the lender's money is the primary borrower, and the lender should collect only from him. Shach even doubts that Rashbam wrote this. Mordechai in Perek Get Pashut already raised the same difficulty. There is a parallel discussion in Gittin 49b. The Gemara asks regarding a ketubah: why is the guarantor liable at all, when an ordinary ketubah guarantor is not obligated? It answers that the case involves a kablan. Rashi explains that the husband delivered movable property to his wife for her ketubah, she gave it to the husband's father in the form of kablan responsibility, and he returned it to his son. Tosafot object that this seems to be the case of one who receives and transfers the money directly. In Perek Get Pashut, where a guarantor handled the funds personally, the lender has no claim against the original borrower. Many explanations have been offered. Maharam of Lublin distinguishes a case where the wife originally had a legal relationship with her husband and the kablan was later inserted, from a case where the borrower had no dealings at all with the lender except through the kablan. According to that reading, Rashbam is not difficult. Here the loan was originally arranged between borrower and lender, and only afterward did the kablan receive the funds. I do not think that is the plain explanation. In the ketubah case, the husband had already written and obligated himself in the ketubah. The property later delivered to the wife was not final payment because payment was not yet due. It was security. She therefore held the property as a bailee over collateral, while the husband's ketubah obligation remained intact. When she gave that collateral to his father and the father returned it to the son, this was not an ordinary case of one bailee wrongfully transferring to another. Even while in the father's hands, the collateral could still be treated as standing for her benefit. The husband therefore remains liable on the ketubah. The father, however, can also be liable because she can say: I delivered my collateral to you and you failed in its safekeeping by returning it to your son. This does not resemble an ordinary loan. Where the borrower never received the lender's money and the kablan took it from the lender and transferred it onward, the borrower may indeed have no direct liability to the lender except through the structure of the guarantee. Thus Shach's difficulty against Rashbam remains. I believe Rashi and Rashbam follow Ramah, cited by Tur in siman 129. Ramah rules that the statement "where one received and transferred the funds, the lender has no claim against the borrower" applies specifically to a kablan. But with an ordinary guarantor, even if he physically handled the money, the borrower is not discharged, because by using language of guarantee he also intended to preserve the borrower's liability. This is explicit. The guarantor who received the money is still the primary obligor because he received it from the lender. But by speaking in the language of guarantee, he additionally intended that the borrower also remain obligated. That explains Rashi's ketubah case. The father received the property and is himself responsible, but because the undertaking was expressed as guarantee, the wife also retains her claim against the husband. Likewise it explains Rashbam. Rashbam was troubled that the baraita says an "ordinary guarantor" may testify. How then can the alternate opinion say a kablan may not testify? Also, according to Tosafot and Rashba, cited by Maggid Mishneh, if the borrower has only inferior land, even a kablan need not pay from medium-quality land. His liability follows the borrower's available property. If so, what financial interest would the kablan have? Rashbam therefore explains that the case actually involves one who received and transferred the money, but who used language of guarantee. The baraita can still call him a guarantor because that is the wording he used. Yet because he physically received the money from the lender, he is the primary obligor. The borrower remains additionally liable because of the guarantee language. Accordingly, if the lender turns to him, he may have to pay according to his own primary obligation rather than merely following the borrower's inferior land. That creates the financial interest required by the Gemara. This is precisely the structure reflected in Ramah's wording: because he used the language of guarantee, he intended also to obligate the borrower. The word "also" shows that the guarantor himself remains primary and the borrower is added secondarily. Thus both Rashi and Rashbam can be read consistently, and Shach's objection is resolved.
