And Yehudah comes to contest, etc. The Tur writes: if Yehudah contests the field and says that Reuven stole it from him, it is obvious that Reuven may not testify for Shimon, because he prefers not to be called a robber. Even if Yehudah claims that Reuven purchased it from a robber, Reuven still may not testify, etc.
Even though he bears no warranty, etc. The Tur adds: even if Shimon, the purchaser, recognizes that this field always belonged to Reuven and his ancestors, so that if Reuven remains silent Shimon has neither a monetary claim against him because the sale was without warranty nor even a grievance because he knows the contestant is falsely testifying, nevertheless Reuven may not testify for him.
So that Reuven's creditor may come, etc. Even if Reuven still owns another field equal in value to this one, we are concerned that Reuven the seller knows he owes more than the value of that remaining field. See what I wrote immediately above and in the Tur.
And collect it for his debt. The Tur writes that even if Reuven bought this field and immediately sold it, without borrowing anything in between, we are still concerned that he borrowed before acquiring it and wrote to the lender a clause encumbering future-acquired property.
And he will not be a wicked borrower, etc. The Tur says that toward Shimon the purchaser he is not considered a wicked borrower, because he sold the field without warranty.
Then the seller may testify for him. The reason is that in this case Reuven's creditor can collect the field from the contestant just as he could have collected had it remained with Reuven or with Shimon. So writes the Tur.
