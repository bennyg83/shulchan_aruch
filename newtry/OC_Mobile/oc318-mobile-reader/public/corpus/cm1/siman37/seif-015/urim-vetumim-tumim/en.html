Without warranty, etc. There are variant readings in Tur. Sema and Perishah read: even though he sold without warranty, the purchaser must nevertheless recognize that the field belonged to Reuven and his ancestors. According to this text, two conditions are required: no warranty, and recognition of the seller's title. Otherwise the seller may still testify falsely in order to avoid the purchaser's grievance against him. Bach and Shach read: whether he sold without warranty, or sold with warranty but the purchaser recognizes that the field was unquestionably the seller's. According to that text, where there is no warranty, recognition is unnecessary because mere resentment does not make a person suspected of false testimony. Recognition is needed only where there is warranty, to remove the seller's monetary exposure. These are essentially the two answers of Tosafot in Bava Batra 43a. Sema and Perishah correctly preserve the first reading in Tur. Tur later explains that the seller cannot testify because he wishes to leave the property available for his creditor and avoid being a wicked borrower who does not repay. That rationale fits the no-warranty case. But if Tur were also speaking of a warranty sale in which the purchaser recognizes the seller's title, the explanation becomes difficult. If the field is taken by a creditor, the seller is also a wicked borrower toward the purchaser because he must reimburse him under the warranty. Why would he gain morally by preferring the creditor? If Tur had a separate reason for the warranty case, he should have stated it. Bach senses this and says "warranty" there means only ordinary external warranty and not warranty against defects arising from the seller himself, but that is very strained. In Tur, an unqualified reference to warranty does not normally bear that unusual meaning. Thus Sema's version is preferable. A question remains from Tur's rule concerning a cow or garment. There he says that even if the purchaser does not know the seller's title, the seller may testify where there is no warranty. Why is there not at least resentment? The question is really on Tosafot themselves. The answer lies in the distinction between land and movables. Chazal enacted takanat hashuk for stolen movables because purchasers cannot reasonably investigate title every time they buy an ordinary movable object. Even with investigation, reliable ownership history is often impossible to establish. Land is different. A purchaser is expected to investigate the chain of title. If he fails to do so and later discovers the land was stolen, he can reasonably complain to the seller: I relied on you to have investigated title before selling. Therefore a land purchaser may harbor a grievance even without formal warranty, whereas a purchaser of ordinary movables generally cannot complain in the same way. This also explains why the Gemara did not simply establish the movable case as one in which the purchaser recognizes title. The whole policy of takanat hashuk reflects that ordinary movable purchasers are not expected to investigate ownership as land purchasers are. There remains a difficulty from Tosafot elsewhere, who say that Shmuel's rule that one who sold land without warranty may not testify because he wishes to leave it for his creditor applies all the more with warranty. This seems inconsistent with the answer that the purchaser's recognition of title removes the seller's direct monetary interest. At first this suggests Tosafot retracted the grievance explanation. But the matter can be reconciled through the indirect creditor concern discussed above. Suppose the seller has an earlier creditor without an after-acquired-property clause. After that debt, he acquires another field and later sells it without warranty. He also sold the present disputed field with warranty. If the present field remains with the purchaser, the earlier creditor can collect it. The purchaser can then pursue the later field under his warranty, because his own warranty reaches it. If the present field is instead removed by a theft claimant, the earlier creditor cannot collect the later-acquired field because his lien did not extend to after-acquired property. The seller then becomes unable to satisfy everyone and has an interest in preserving the present field even though the purchaser recognized title. This is why Tosafot can say the concern is all the stronger with warranty. The same framework also explains several apparent inconsistencies in the Gemara concerning rapid purchase and resale of movables and whether after-acquired-property clauses or apotiki arrangements are plausible. For movables sold immediately after acquisition, an apotiki concern is generally implausible. One cannot realistically designate as an exclusive apotiki an asset not yet acquired and unknown in advance. Thus the Gemara's discussion can differ between land and movables without contradiction. The practical result is that there is little difference between the two textual versions. Where there is no warranty but the purchaser does not recognize title, the seller may still be disqualified because of the purchaser's grievance in the land context. Where there is warranty and the purchaser recognizes title, the seller can still be disqualified because of indirect creditor concerns. Accordingly, under either version the seller often remains unable to testify. The difference between land and movables also answers another difficulty in Tosafot. If mere grievance were enough to disqualify the seller in the land case, why does Shmuel himself say the reason is that the seller wishes to leave the field before his creditor rather than simply saying he wishes to avoid the buyer's grievance? The answer can be that Shmuel speaks in a case where the buyer already recognizes the seller's title well enough that ordinary resentment is weak, yet there remains the creditor concern. Alternatively, Shmuel gives the more robust reason that applies even where no personal grievance remains. This does not prove Tosafot abandoned the grievance explanation. The creditor rationale can coexist with it and be necessary for broader cases. One should also distinguish "recognizes that it belonged to the seller" from legally conclusive proof that the claimant is lying. A buyer may personally know the seller possessed the property for years, trust his account, and therefore have no complaint against him if a third party later wins through technical title evidence. Yet that personal recognition may not suffice to defeat the claimant in beit din. Thus recognition can remove the buyer's grievance without necessarily removing every legal consequence of dispossession. This is one reason the seller can still have an indirect creditor interest even where the buyer recognizes title. Ramban's requirement, cited below, that such recognition precede the litigation reinforces this distinction. If the buyer makes the acknowledgment only after the challenge arises, we suspect it was made strategically to qualify the seller as a witness. A prior acknowledgment reflects genuine acceptance of title risk and can eliminate complaint. A later one may be merely litigation behavior. These points further support Sema's text in Tur and explain why the practical outcome of the variant readings can converge even though their conceptual paths differ.
So that he should not be a wicked borrower, etc. Tosafot write that the Gemara could have established the case where the debtor designated the field as an apotiki to an earlier creditor. Then even if other land remains, if the disputed field stays with the purchaser the apotiki creditor takes it and the purchaser who bought without warranty loses nothing from the seller. But if the claimant removes the disputed field as stolen property, the apotiki disappears and the creditor may then seek other property from the debtor. Tur nevertheless says explicitly that even where we know the seller owns much other property, the witness can still be interested because perhaps this field was designated as an apotiki. Why then does Tur need the additional rationale that he wishes not to be called a wicked borrower? The apotiki concern seems sufficient. The answer is that Tur states the law generally, even where the seller acquired and resold the property on the same day. In such a case there may have been no interval in which an ordinary earlier creditor could obtain a lien against the field. One could suggest that the creditor had an after-acquired-property clause, but that works only for ordinary liens. It is very difficult to speak of an explicit apotiki over property not yet acquired, since neither party knows whether such property will ever come into existence. No one lends money on such a precarious exclusive security arrangement. Therefore the apotiki possibility cannot explain every case. Tur adds the broader reason of avoiding the status of a wicked borrower who fails to repay, which can apply even where no apotiki arrangement is plausible. Tosafot's question is still sound because the Gemara need not have been speaking of the unusual same-day purchase and resale case. It could have established an ordinary case with an apotiki. Tur, however, is stating the practical rule for all cases and therefore needs the more general rationale.
