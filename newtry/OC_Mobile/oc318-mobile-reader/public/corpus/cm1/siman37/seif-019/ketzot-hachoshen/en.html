That was stolen from them. Bava Batra asks why, when a communal Torah scroll is stolen, two townspeople cannot simply remove their interests and then serve as judges. Rashbam says they should write that they have no claim or dealings in the Torah scroll and make a kinyan. My brother Rabbi Yehudah raised a powerful difficulty. Stolen movable property that remains unrecovered cannot be sold, consecrated, or abandoned by its owner before despair, because it is not in his possession. How then can the townspeople transfer away their rights in the stolen Torah scroll? The problem appears even with land: if the owner has no evidence and needs his own testimony to recover it, perhaps it too is not effectively within his control. With land, however, Rashba's principle can help. Where ownership and acquisition arise simultaneously, the act can be effective. If the person transfers his interest to his partner, the very transfer provides the partner with the witness needed to recover the land, so recovery and acquisition come together. This resembles consecration that becomes effective simultaneously with the acquirer's change of status. With stolen movables, however, even one who could recover them through beit din still cannot ordinarily transfer them while they remain outside his possession. Therefore Rashbam's language remains difficult. Perhaps the Gemara means that the townspeople transfer their share to the thief himself. A transfer to the present possessor can be effective even though transfer to another cannot. Yet Rashbam's language of making a kinyan does not sound like this, and the thief would hardly consent to a gift that creates witnesses against him. The matter therefore requires study.
