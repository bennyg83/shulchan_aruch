For at present he is not interested. The early and later authorities often state that where a witness has no present financial interest, we do not disqualify him merely because he may later become wealthy and acquire an interest. This rule, however, must not be applied indiscriminately. Tosafot in Ketubot discusses witnesses who validate a creditor's document and are relatives of Reuven but not of Shimon. Even if Reuven presently owns nothing, they cannot testify where the effect of their testimony may later fall upon Reuven. If one were to say that no concern exists because he is presently poor, absurd consequences would follow, such as allowing a debtor's relatives to testify that his debt was paid merely because he presently has no property. The correct distinction follows the principle that an interested witness is not disqualified because we assume he will lie, but because a person is legally his own relative. I explained in siman 34 that one who is paid to testify is biblically valid, because monetary temptation alone does not create Torah disqualification. Thus there are two kinds of interest. First, where the witness is legally testifying about his own obligation or right, he is biblically disqualified as one testifying about himself. In such a case it makes no difference that he presently has no assets, because the testimony still concerns his own legal position and may affect him later. Second, where he testifies only for another person but may receive some collateral financial benefit from the result, this resembles paid testimony and is only rabbinically disqualified. In that second category, if the benefit is remote because he presently has no assets and may never acquire any, the Sages did not extend the disqualification. This explains Tosafot. It also explains the Sema in siman 123, who permits a borrower with no assets to testify that another person is his guarantor. The borrower is not thereby testifying to reduce his own debt, which remains the same whether or not a guarantor exists. His only benefit is that the creditor may collect from the guarantor. That is collateral benefit, comparable to paid testimony, and where the benefit is not presently before him the Sages did not disqualify him. By contrast, where two people borrowed together and one admits while the other denies, the admitting borrower cannot testify to obligate his fellow merely because he personally has no assets. His testimony directly reduces or reallocates his own legal liability, and therefore he is biblically interested. According to the Shulchan Aruch, each is a guarantor for the other's share, and according to the Rosh and Tur each is treated as borrower for the whole and lender to the other for half. In either model he is testifying about his own obligation. Thus the distinction is clear: direct self testimony is always disqualified, whether or not an immediate financial benefit exists; collateral financial advantage is only rabbinically disqualifying and can fall away when the benefit is remote. This also resolves the question why the desire not to be viewed publicly as a wicked borrower can count as benefit. Since this latter category is not the biblical defect of self testimony, the concern is not that he prefers one sin over another. Rather, as Rabbi Yosef HaLevi explains, he wishes to avoid public disgrace and the status of a person who does not pay his debts.
