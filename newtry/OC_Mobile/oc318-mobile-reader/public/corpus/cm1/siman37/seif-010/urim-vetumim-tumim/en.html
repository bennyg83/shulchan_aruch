Wherever, etc. This is based on Tosafot's answer to the Gemara in Perek Chezkas HaBatim. The Gemara asks: if the witness has land, how can he establish this field before his creditor, since the creditor can ultimately return upon him? And if he has no land, what difference does the testimony make to him? Tosafot ask that there is still a future difference: perhaps he will later become wealthy, in which case the creditor could collect from him, whereas if the creditor now takes the field that he sold without warranty, no one could later return against him. Tosafot answer that because there is no present benefit, we do not suspect him of giving false testimony merely because a remote benefit may arise if he later becomes wealthy. This is the law Mechaber records. Sema asks that according to the Gemara's answer, that a person prefers not to be called a wicked borrower who does not repay, this law seems to have no practical case. Sema answers that it applies where we know the person does not care about being called wicked. Shach correctly objects. Who can know that? A person may disregard one kind of stigma and care deeply about another. This is a matter of the heart. Shach proposes instead a case where the only obligation is damages rather than a loan. Then, he says, the consideration of "a borrower who does not repay is wicked" does not apply. This is even more difficult. First, we generally do not know the hidden debts or obligations of a person. If such facts could simply be known, the Gemara could have answered that we know he has no debt. Second, one who owes damages imposed by Torah and refuses to pay is no less wicked than an unpaid borrower. Torah is especially stringent about damages by requiring payment from superior-quality land. A better practical case is as follows. A person owes two creditors and owns only one field. He voluntarily allowed one creditor to collect it. The second then claims that his own debt is earlier and the dispute turns on witness testimony establishing priority. Perhaps one of the potential witnesses is the debtor himself, or the dispute concerns whether a note was signed before the witness became a robber, or the second creditor claims that the first waived his priority in the presence of witnesses. At present the debtor has no financial difference between the two outcomes. He owes both, and only one can collect the available field. There can nevertheless be a remote future difference. If the creditor who voluntarily received the field keeps it, the rule of returning an appraisal may not apply in the same manner as where another creditor later takes it through beit din. That distinction matters only if the debtor later becomes wealthy enough to redeem the appraisal. Since he is presently poor, this future possibility does not make him interested under Tosafot's rule. Another example is where a woman received a field through beit din and later married. She claims that her husband expressly agreed not to inherit her property, while he denies it. A creditor may be a witness concerning that condition. The creditor could have a future difference because an appraisal in the hands of a woman can be returned, whereas after marriage certain rules may prevent its return. But again, the difference matters only if the debtor later acquires money with which to redeem the field. Since there is no present benefit, he may testify. A further example follows the original Talmudic law that a ketubah is not collected from movable property, while an ordinary creditor can collect movables. Suppose a debtor has only one field and a former wife and a creditor dispute priority. The debtor wishes to testify that the creditor is earlier. At present he gains nothing whichever one takes the field. But if the woman collects the field, the creditor may later collect the debtor's movables should he become wealthy. If the creditor collects the field, the divorced woman, under the original law, cannot later collect movables. Thus there is a possible future difference if he becomes wealthy. Nevertheless, Tosafot's principle allows the testimony because there is no present interest. Even after the Geonic enactment allowing broader collection from movables, there remain practical differences between original Talmudic law and the enactment, as discussed elsewhere, and parties may also have stipulated to follow the original law. Many analogous cases can be constructed. The governing rule is that a merely contingent benefit depending on future enrichment does not create present testimonial interest.
