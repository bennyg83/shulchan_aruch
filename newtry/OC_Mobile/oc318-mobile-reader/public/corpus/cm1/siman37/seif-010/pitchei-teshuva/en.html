Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 45, discusses a will challenged because one witness's wife was related in the second degree to the husband of a woman who was bequeathed seventy-five gold pieces. A further concern was that the will donated a garment to the synagogue to make a wedding canopy, and the witness lived in that town, seemingly giving him an interest. Regarding the canopy, Noda BiYehuda writes that if the town already has another canopy of equal quality, the witness has no interest at all. A canopy is analogous to a Torah scroll: if the community has another Torah scroll, a member is not considered interested, as explained in siman 7, seif 12, and Sema there writes that the judges may even read from that same scroll. Since no removal of interest is required at all, there is no distinction between a witness and a judge. Where removal is required, authorities dispute whether removal helps a witness because a witness must have been qualified from the outset. But where no removal is required, he was qualified from the outset. Thus if the town has another comparable canopy he is not interested. We do not suspect the remote possibility that two weddings will occur on the same day and one will involve a relative of the witness, since he is not presently interested. For additional assurance the witness and his relatives may formally refrain from using this canopy, and most authorities hold that removal of interest is effective even for a witness. The kinship issue, however, is genuine: since the witness's wife is related to the husband of the beneficiary, he is disqualified, because the husband has rights to the fruits of the money. Even granting the challenger everything, so that one witness is disqualified because of the canopy and the other because of kinship, that does not weaken the will with respect to the independent gifts made to other people. They acquire everything individually bequeathed to them, and the rule that testimony partially void is entirely void does not apply. Had all beneficiaries been joined in one disposition, for example, "So-and-so and so-and-so shall divide the estate," and the witnesses were related to one, the will would indeed be invalid, as stated in Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 51, seif 6. Even there, if the witnesses remembered the testimony they could testify orally. In the present case, however, the deceased did not group the beneficiaries together. Each was separately told "give" or "he shall take," and the like. Shulchan Aruch explicitly distinguishes a document stating, "I gave such-and-such courtyard to Reuven, and I gave such-and-such to Shimon." See there.
