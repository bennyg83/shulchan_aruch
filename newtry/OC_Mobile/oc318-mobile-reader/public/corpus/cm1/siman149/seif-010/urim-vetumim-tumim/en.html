One who possessed. Seifim 21 and 22, concerning one who consumed for the years of chazakah, are the Rambam's words. The Mechaber added only the phrase referring to "the type of report discussed immediately above," and nothing more. The Sema and Shach discuss these rulings at length. Their positions can be summarized as follows. The Sema understands the report that "the land belongs to orphans," which according to the Rambam and Mechaber weakens the possessor and causes the land to leave his hands without payment, as a report that emerged before the completion of the three years, not afterward. The Mechaber's added phrase, "in the type of report discussed immediately above," refers to the prior seif, where the report had to emerge before the years of chazakah were complete. In seif 22, where the possessor is believed by migo of purchase to say that he has a debt secured by the land, the Sema explains that no report emerged at all. If there were a report, he would not have a useful migo of purchase, because, as explained below in siman 150, a person prefers a claim that does not directly contradict a public report. The Shach, in seifim katanim 18 and 22, correctly rejects the Sema's first point. Here the possessor did not complete three years during the father's lifetime at all. His possession continued after the father's death, and one cannot establish a chazakah against a minor. His entire argument rests on a migo that the field had never belonged to the father. Once a report says that it did belong to the orphans' father, that migo is weakened whenever the report appears. There is no completed chazakah whose timing relative to the report matters. On this point the Sema plainly erred. The Shach also rejects the Sema's explanation of seif 22 as a case with no report. If the presence of a report also invalidated the latter case, the Rambam could have combined the two clauses and said simply that whether the possessor relies on migo that the property never belonged to the father, or on a migo arising from three years of possession during the father's lifetime, the report defeats him. The Shach's textual objection can perhaps be answered according to his own reading of the text, which says in the first case that the possessor collects with an oath where there was enhancement, because the cases would then differ regarding the oath. Still, the Raavad and Maggid Mishneh's discussion strongly indicates that in the latter case the possessor can be believed even though a report exists. The Rambam's ruling is built on the interpretation of his teacher, Ri Migash, concerning Rabbah bar Sharshom. Ri Migash understands that it was not otherwise known that the land had belonged to the orphans' father. The possessor relied on a migo to deny the father's title. If, as the Sema says, the Rambam's distinction were merely whether a report existed, why abandon the simpler interpretation accepted by the other commentators, namely that the possessor had already consumed for three years in the father's lifetime while holding the land as a mashkon, and ordinarily would have had a migo of purchase, but the public report weakened him? The Rambam's entire reformulation indicates that the two clauses operate differently. Thus the Sema's explanation cannot be primary. The Shach explains instead that the report did not say the field still belonged to the orphans, only that it had originally belonged to their father. This, he says, is also the report in the episode of Rabbah bar Sharshom. Such a report defeats only the migo that the land was never the father's, because a person does not prefer a claim directly contradicting a public report. But where the possessor has three years of valid chazakah and could claim purchase, the report that the land once belonged to the father is not inconsistent with his claim. The father may well have later sold or mortgaged it. Thus the possessor can say that he has a debt against the property by migo that he bought it, because both claims coexist with the report. By contrast, below in siman 150 the public report says expressly that the land is held as a mashkon from the orphans' father. A claim of outright purchase would directly contradict that report, so no migo of purchase is recognized there. This is the essence of the Shach's interpretation, and it is logically attractive. Nevertheless, it conflicts with the Tur. The Tur writes that the Rambam agrees with Rashbam that if a report emerges, the possessor cannot claim purchase even after three years of chazakah. According to the Shach, the Rambam provides no proof for either Rashbam or Tosafot, because the report in the Rambam's case does not contradict a later purchase at all. The Rambam would therefore be neutral on the central dispute. It therefore appears that the Tur understood the Rambam differently. The report truly contradicts the possessor's asserted ownership: the public report says that the land belongs to the orphans, not merely that it once belonged to their father. This is the natural language of the Rambam, "it belonged to the orphans," and of the Gemara, "a report went out that this was orphans' land." The report does not by itself have enough legal force to destroy an already completed three-year chazakah. Where the possessor has no completed chazakah and relies only on migo that the land never belonged to the father, the report defeats the migo. But where a three-year chazakah was completed, the chazakah is stronger than a mere report. This is how the Tur understood the Rambam. If the report emerged only after the possessor had already completed three years, the chazakah had already acquired its full strength and a later report cannot undo it. But if the report emerged before completion, the possessor cannot say that the eventual chazakah overcomes it, because at the time the report arose the chazakah was incomplete. If a report is strong enough to defeat the migo that the land was never the father's, all the more so it can prevent an incomplete possession from becoming a chazakah, functioning like a protest. The Tur therefore understands the Rambam as agreeing with Rashbam, as Rabbenu Yonah explains, that a report arising during the three years can prevent the chazakah. This also explains why the Beit Yosef, and likewise the Kesef Mishneh, cite the Tur's understanding of the Rambam without noting any contradiction. They clearly understood the Rambam in the manner just described.
Accordingly, however, the Sema's question returns. In seif 22, where the possessor says that the father owed him money and relies on a migo of purchase after three years, why is that migo effective? Would he not prefer a claim that avoids contradicting the public report? I therefore propose the reverse of the Shach's formulation concerning the content of the report. The report says that the land belongs to the orphans in the sense that the possessor is consuming their land without right, not merely that it once belonged to their father. This appears to be the report in the Rabbah bar Sharshom episode: a public report arose that he was consuming orphans' land. If the report had merely said that the field was held as a mashkon, no one could yet say that he was consuming the orphans' property wrongfully, because who knew whether the mortgage term had expired? The report must have alleged that his possession was wrongful. On that understanding, both of the possessor's possible claims, outright purchase and a valid debt secured by the land, contradict the report equally. Since he completed three years, we no longer give dispositive weight to the report. He may therefore rely on a migo of purchase to support his debt claim: whichever claim he makes, he is contradicting the same report. By contrast, in siman 150 the report specifically says that the land is held as a mashkon. There, a claim that it is a mashkon conforms to the report, while a claim of outright purchase contradicts it. A person naturally prefers the non-contradictory claim, so there is no migo from purchase. This reading is also necessary to sustain the Tur's assertion that the Rambam agrees with Rashbam. If the report merely established earlier ownership by the father, the Rambam's refusal to accept migo that the land was never the father's would reflect only the general Tosafot principle that we do not use migo to contradict a public report. It would not prove that the report functions as a protest against a purchase claim. But if the report says the orphans presently own the land and that the possessor is holding it wrongfully, then its force against an uncompleted chazakah is readily understood. It appears further that such a report must have arisen during the father's lifetime. A report that first emerges only after the father's death may not be a sufficiently substantive report. Maharit writes that the report must have real substance. Perhaps this is what the Mechaber intended by adding, "in the type of report discussed immediately above," namely the preceding seif's report that arose while the father was alive. This also resolves a difficulty according to the Rambam and Tosafot, who hold that a completed chazakah can support a purchase claim despite a report while migo alone may fail. The Mishnah in Ketubot says that if there are witnesses that the field belonged to the father, the possessor is not believed. Why not teach a greater novelty, that even if there is only a public report that it belonged to the father, then although the possessor would have been believed had he flatly denied prior ownership, he is not believed when he instead says, "I bought it," because migo does not contradict a report? The answer is that the Mishnah is used to teach an additional point: even if the possessor consumed two years in the father's lifetime and one in the son's, there is no chazakah against a minor. If the Mishnah spoke merely of a public report, the failure of the chazakah could be attributed to the report itself functioning as a protest, rather than to the special rule protecting a minor. By stating that there are witnesses, not merely a report, the Mishnah isolates the minor-possession rule. This also creates a difficulty for the Shach's understanding of what a general report "that the field belongs to so-and-so" means. If such a report merely means that it once belonged to him and does not contradict a later purchase, the Mishnah could have been framed with a report that it belonged to the father. The report would not function as a protest against a purchase claim according to the Shach, yet the law would still teach that possession against the minor fails. The fact that the Mishnah instead uses witnesses supports the view that an ordinary report of ownership means continuing present ownership and therefore would itself interfere with the chazakah. Consider this carefully.
