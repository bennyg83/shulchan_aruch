The Shach in seif katan 17 writes that this migo is accepted even though the alternative claim is more brazen, and Tosafot say this in several places. Tosafot understand the heir's claim, "it belonged to my father," as a definite claim, while the claim, "your father did not sell it to you," may be uncertain. Therefore, if the heir instead claimed, "it never belonged to your father," he would be speaking more brazenly, whereas the claim of purchase from the father is less confrontational. The Gemara's question, why the Mishnah did not teach instead, "this field was yours and I purchased it from you," can likewise be understood where the original owner says with certainty that the field was his but does not know whether he sold it to the possessor. The possessor would be believed by migo. If there are witnesses of the original ownership, however, the possessor is not believed merely because the seller says, "I do not know whether I sold it," because the burden of proof rests on the one seeking to alter established ownership and land remains in its prior chazakah. This is like the Mishnah concerning one who exchanges a cow for a donkey, and like the rule below in siman 223 where the purchaser says, "I bought the larger field," while the seller says, "I do not know"; the purchaser does not receive the larger field because the burden of proof is on him. Thus even in this formulation the migo is one involving brazenness, and Tosafot teach that such a migo may nevertheless apply. The Gemara's answer, that if he lacks a chazakah the result is obvious, is likewise understandable even where the seller is uncertain whether he sold. The general rule remains that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. This also avoids the difficulty that otherwise the Gemara should have said that the proposed Mishnah works only according to Rabbah, who treats the possessor's physical holding of land as sufficient to prevent his migo from being classified as extracting, and not according to Rav Yosef, who, in the view of Riva and other authorities, rejects even such a migo regarding land. According to Ri, Rav Yosef may accept migo in general and object there only because the claimant had previously lied, but according to the other approach the difficulty remains. The answer is that where one side has a definite claim and the other an uncertain one, all agree that a migo can support the possessor even in circumstances resembling extraction, as the Rosh and Mordechai write in their discussions of migo. Thus the Mishnah's clause concerning "your father's field" can be understood consistently according to all. This also answers the Bach's question on the Gemara. The Gemara explains why Rav Yosef disagrees in the first clause but not the latter by distinguishing a case of a slaughtered ox already before us. Why does it not simply say that Rav Yosef rejects a migo used to extract in a definite-versus-uncertain case, while in the latter clause the claimant is physically holding the land? Because if that were the distinction, one would still have to explain Rav Yosef's position regarding land where he himself holds that migo does not overcome prior title. The better conclusion is that where the opponent's claim is uncertain, all agree that migo can prevail. Likewise, the Shach's earlier question in the laws of migo, siman 82, is resolved. He asks why the Rosh needs the reason of definite versus uncertain where the claimant also holds a ketubah, and we generally allow migo to extract where there is a document. The answer is that the Rosh needed a reason that also covers the Mishnah's land case according to Rav Yosef, where there is no document. Therefore he states the broader principle that in definite versus uncertain, the migo is accepted according to all. Tosafot in the passage beginning "and let it teach" interpret the case as definite claim against definite claim. According to them, the Gemara's question may indeed follow our ruling like Rabbah regarding land. They must understand that the Mishnah's proposed novelty would be greater by teaching that migo works even in a definite-versus-definite dispute and even where it resembles extraction, thereby excluding Rav Yosef's position. By comparison, teaching that a migo involving brazenness works would be less novel, because the Gemara nowhere records a dispute about migo solely on that ground. The Gemara thus had good reason to prefer the stronger novelty involving migo and land.
And as to the Bach's question: what migo does the possessor have according to the Rosh, who considers the claim "it was never your father's" weak because prior ownership can readily be proved by witnesses, as the Rosh writes above regarding one who says, "I bought it from so-and-so"? There is no difficulty. In that earlier case the purchaser relies on his seller's statement that the seller truly bought the field from the challenger. Therefore the purchaser does not wish to deny prior ownership, because he assumes that the challenger's title is genuine and easily provable. By contrast, the fact of an unobserved sale is not easily disproved, so he prefers the purchase claim. Here, however, the possessor himself knowingly claims, perhaps falsely, that he purchased the field. He does not rely on another person's account. If he is lying, both alternatives can be disproved in comparable ways. As the Pilpula Charifta writes, even the supposed purchase could be disproved by witnesses who establish the circumstances in which no sale could have occurred. Thus the possessor has no particular reason to prefer one false claim over the other. The migo therefore remains effective and the Bach's objection is answered.
