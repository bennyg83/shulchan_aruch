He dug pits in the land. Rashbam in Bava Batra writes that this creates chazakah immediately. Ramban, however, explains that a three year chazakah is required, because without three years there remains the weakness that he should have preserved his deed. Yet this appears difficult. The ruling seems to imply that even where produce consumption itself is not proof, once he physically exercised dominion over the land and held it for three years, so that the missing-deed weakness disappears, chazakah arises. If so, the poskim should have stated this as a general law of land chazakah: if Reuven digs pits in Shimon's field and then holds it for three years, he acquires chazakah even without consuming produce at all. It therefore appears that the present case concerns a husband who needed the pits in order to obtain produce from them, as Taz explains. Since the land was not intended for pits, the wife's land was never subjected to the husband's use in that form. His right of produce extends only to the land as it stands. Consequently, when he consumes produce generated through the pits for three years, that is a genuine chazakah against her. One cannot answer that the husband is anyway entitled to the produce, because he is not entitled to produce obtained from pits created by changing the land into a use to which it was not subjected for him. By contrast, if he merely dug the pits and damaged the land, then left them for three years, while also consuming the ordinary produce of the land according to his marital rights, there is no chazakah. Once the case concerns three years of consuming the special produce of the pits, there was no need to state the law separately among ordinary rules of land chazakah, because its effectiveness is obvious. One may nevertheless learn from here that if a person rents land for its produce and later digs pits to obtain a form of produce not included in the rental, he can establish chazakah by consuming that produce, just like a husband who uses the wife's land in a manner to which his marital right did not extend. According to this, Tur and Mechaber follow Ramban. First, they did not say that chazakah arises immediately. Second, if digging itself created immediate chazakah, they should have stated the rule generally for all land chazakot. According to those who say that digging pits establishes immediate chazakah, it would seem that this is like other servitude chazakot between neighbors and would indeed be effective immediately. Still, Taz's explanation appears primary. If the act is merely destructive, there is certainly no chazakah, no better than continuously planting in a place where fields are customarily left fallow. Conversely, if the act is entirely beneficial, such as digging in land naturally suited for pits, there is likewise no chazakah, because the claimant can say, "I did not object because I assumed that you entered in order to improve the property for me," like one who descends into another's field without permission. Siman 141 states similarly regarding one who rebuilt another's ruins. Chazakah through digging therefore exists only where the act benefits the possessor while damaging the owner's normal use, as Taz explains here. The husband needs the pits, but the land is not normally used that way, so the act is a deterioration from the wife's standpoint. The same type of act would establish chazakah even between unrelated people. Rashba explicitly rules that one who digs a pit in another's property and opens it toward his own property establishes chazakah. This also answers Sema's question in seif katan 11 from the rule that building and then demolishing does not establish chazakah. Demolition is a loss to everyone and rebuilding is a benefit to everyone, so neither act is the sort of adverse, self-serving use that proves a claim against the owner.
