Even though he stipulated with her that he has no rights to the produce of her property, and even though while she was betrothed he stipulated that he would not inherit her, etc., their consumption is not proof of ownership. The Mechaber follows the Rambam, whose position differs from the Rosh and Tur, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 92. Because the Tur and Mechaber abbreviated here and relied upon what they wrote there, I will state the framework. According to the Rambam and Maggid Mishneh, after marriage a husband can validly remove himself from rights to the produce if he says, even without writing, "I have no claim or dealing in your property or its produce," and a kinyan is made from him. The force of the kinyan removes his rights to the produce and validates her later sale or gift. If he made this stipulation while she was only betrothed, no kinyan is needed, and the verbal disclaimer alone suffices. Moreover, while she is still betrothed, even a stipulation that he will not inherit her is effective by mere verbal disclaimer, without kinyan. After marriage, however, even a kinyan does not enable him to remove himself from inheritance rights, because those rights have a Torah aspect and the Sages strengthened them. Before marriage, by contrast, the rule of Rav Kahana applies: one may stipulate away an inheritance that comes to him from another source before it has vested. This differs from partners, where the property is already partly his and a mere disclaimer is ineffective without a kinyan. The Rosh and Tur agree that while she is betrothed a verbal disclaimer without kinyan suffices, even regarding inheritance, but they disagree with the Rambam after marriage and hold that even a kinyan is ineffective to remove his rights, even merely to the produce. With this introduction the language here is clear. When the Tur says that he wrote to her while she was betrothed that he had no claim to her property or produce, "wrote" is not literal. The same rule applies if he merely said it. The Tur deliberately specifies that she was still betrothed, because according to his view a postmarriage disclaimer is ineffective even with a kinyan. The Rambam and Mechaber, however, mention betrothal only regarding the inheritance clause, because as to produce they hold that a postmarriage disclaimer can be effective through kinyan. Thus the two uses of "stipulated" in the Mechaber are not identical: the first, concerning produce after marriage, requires kinyan, while the second, concerning inheritance while betrothed, does not. In any event, even where these stipulations are legally effective, the husband's consumption of the property does not establish a presumption of ownership, because a wife ordinarily does not object to her husband using her property.
And he built and demolished. The Maggid Mishneh infers from this that the Rambam holds that even if the husband dug pits, ditches, and caves in his wife's fields, he still does not thereby acquire a presumption of ownership, as the Tur writes in the name of the Rif and the Rosh. If so, Rama's gloss here, "specifically where the substance of the land was not damaged, but if he dug, etc.," is difficult. This is the view of the Ramah and the Tur, and Rama should have introduced it as "some say," rather than writing "specifically," as though he were merely explaining the Mechaber, when in fact the authorities disagree. This requires study.
But if he dug pits in the land, etc. Since Rama states this without qualification, it appears that in his view such digging establishes possession even if the husband did not previously write to his wife, "I have no claim or dealing in your property or its produce." This follows the Tur and excludes the qualification stated by the Ramah in the Tur. See seif 10. In Even HaEzer siman 87 Rama did not mention this distinction concerning digging pits, because that is not the primary location for the laws of presumptive possession. He therefore abbreviated there and did not undertake to decide a disputed point, especially since the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh do not accept this distinction.
Likewise, presumptive rights regarding nuisances, etc. The Tur cites Ri Migash, who explains that the husband is not acquiring the body of his wife's land. He merely seeks an intermittent use of it, or to look into it from his window. In such nuisance easements the Sages elsewhere were lenient and did not require either a claim of purchase or three years of possession. Once the owner sees the use and remains silent without protesting, we say that he waived the objection immediately. Therefore, according to this view, such rights may be acquired immediately against a married woman's property, whether by her husband or by another person, once she saw, for example, that a window was opened toward her courtyard and remained silent. Ri disagrees and requires a claim and three years even for such nuisance rights. According to him, neither the husband nor another person can establish such possession against a married woman merely by three years of use, just as ordinary consumption of her property's produce does not establish possession. This differs from digging pits according to those who say that such conduct does establish possession, because there the very substance of the land is damaged and she would not normally remain silent. I established this explanation in the Perishah and Derishah. Rama therefore cites only the opinion that nuisance possession is effective and does not also cite Ri's contrary view, because Rama's own halachic inclination follows the Rosh that these easements do not require three years and a claim, as he rules in siman 155:35. For the same reason Rama rules without qualification in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 87 that nuisance possession is effective. See what I wrote in siman 153:16 for the general framework.
