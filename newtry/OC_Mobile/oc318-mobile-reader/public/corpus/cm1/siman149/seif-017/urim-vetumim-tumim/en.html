Specifically where the treasurers have a share in that consecrated property, etc. “So that the poor should not suffer loss, etc.” Sema in seif katan 48 connects this rule to the dispute above in seif 24 whether one can establish chazakah over property of orphans who have guardians. He therefore understands the Mechaber, who above recognized a chazakah, to mean here that the treasurers need not themselves have a share. In truth, however, we have not found that Rashba holds that where there are guardians no chazakah can be established. What Sema cites from Responsa Rashba, siman 642, where Rashba writes that the poor should not suffer because the treasurer failed to protest, is no proof. In that responsum the possessor did not claim that he purchased the property from the treasurer. He claimed that he had acquired it before the donor consecrated it. Rashba therefore properly writes, according to all views, that the treasurer’s silence creates no chazakah. The same is true of orphans. If one claims that he purchased the property from the father of minor orphans, one cannot establish chazakah in their property even if they have a guardian. Chazakah was recognized only where he claims that he purchased it from the guardian himself, not from the orphans’ father. In truth, there is room to distinguish between the rule above concerning a guardian and the present rule concerning treasurers. Even according to the view that one cannot establish chazakah against a guardian, there the reason is that the possessor should preserve his deed until the orphans reach adulthood, as Sema explains above. Here, however, must he preserve his deed forever? Consecrated property always lacks a specific owner and remains continually under treasurers. Conversely, even according to the view that one may establish chazakah against a guardian, perhaps no chazakah can be established here, for the reason given by the Tur: although the seven representatives of the town have authority to sell, had they sold the property the sale would have become publicly known. The same should apply to treasurers of consecrated property. What difference is there between treasurers and the seven representatives of the town? It is difficult to distinguish between them with respect to publicity. It therefore appears that Rama requires the treasurers themselves to have a share in the property according to all views, and that this ruling is not dependent on the dispute as Sema understands it. Yet whatever the reason, I do not understand how the treasurers’ ownership of a share helps. Does possession of the treasurers’ share somehow establish possession of the poor people’s share? Even ordinary partners provide a counterexample. If one establishes chazakah against one partner who did not protest, he does not thereby establish chazakah against the other partner who was not in the country and could not protest, as explained above in seif 1 in Rama, citing Responsa Rosh. The present case is similar, for the poor are like persons absent from the country. Who is there to protest on their behalf? If we say that they rely upon the treasurers as orphans rely upon guardians, then chazakah should be effective even when the treasurers have no personal share. In truth, were it not for Rama’s wording, I would say that Rashba’s requirement in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef, that the treasurers have a share, means the following. Otherwise, whichever way the matter turns, we can ask the treasurers. If they admit the sale, the possessor plainly has chazakah, as the Tur writes. If they deny it, they themselves are witnesses, because a treasurer who has no personal benefit from the consecrated property may testify. Nor do we demand an accounting from treasurers, so a treasurer may say, “I received the money and spent it for the consecrated property,” without an oath. They therefore qualify as witnesses, like the rule that the same persons may be his witnesses and his agents. The Tur must likewise be understood this way when he writes concerning the seven representatives of a town that if the possessor says he purchased from them and they admit it, he has chazakah, but if he claims that they have departed, etc. The Tur does not state what happens if the seven representatives deny the sale, because it is obvious that they are believed as witnesses when they have no personal share in the consecrated property. If he claims that they departed, the Tur already writes that this is a weak claim and is not accepted. Rashba therefore required that the treasurers have a share, because then they are disqualified as witnesses, as Rashba writes in Responsum 642, and the possessor may properly claim, “I purchased it from you,” while their testimony that they did not sell is ineffective. However, Rama’s wording does not indicate this explanation. We must therefore say that Rama reasons as follows. Since the Tur’s principle that a sale would become publicly known is itself weak, to the extent that even from the Tur’s words it appears that he did not rely upon that reason alone but joined to it the additional consideration that perhaps the possessor entered the property for the benefit of the poor, when there is an indication that a sale occurred, namely that the treasurers themselves own a share and would not have remained silent concerning their own property, we presume that a sale was indeed made. This seems a possible explanation of Rama. Knesset HaGedolah likewise cites his teacher Maharit to this effect. See there.
