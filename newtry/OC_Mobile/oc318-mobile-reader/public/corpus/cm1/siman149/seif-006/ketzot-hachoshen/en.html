Maharit ruled that where the parties were relatives and the owner had once protested only against a particularly severe intrusion, he could no longer later explain his silence regarding the lesser use by saying that family affection made him tolerant. He compared this to the household of the Exilarch, where once a person demonstrates he is not afraid by protesting once, he cannot later plead fear for continued silence. I disagree. Fear and affection are different. If a person protests once against a powerful party, that very act proves he was not afraid. But relatives often tolerate one thing out of affection and object to another when the burden becomes great. One moment of anger does not remove the entire relationship of closeness. In Maharit's case the protruding metal lattice caused a serious injury while the underlying window caused a smaller one. An owner may object to the greater injury yet tolerate the lesser because of family affection. Even where the two harms are similar, tolerance of one and objection to another does not by itself prove waiver. Therefore, according to authorities who require an actual sale, gift, or waiver to establish a damage right and do not treat mere endurance as enough, selective protest does not establish waiver.
