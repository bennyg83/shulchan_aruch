According to the judges' assessment. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov III, siman 160, concerning Reuven, who owned a seat in the women's synagogue upon which his daughter-in-law sat for several years. After Reuven died, his son claimed that he had purchased the seat from his father and possessed a deed and evidence of possession, while the other heirs argued that the seat remained in their father's ownership. When beit din instructed him to produce his deed, he went home and returned saying that despite searching he could not find it and that it had been lost. Shevut Yaakov rules that before the court adjudicates the claim of possession, the son must swear that the deed was in fact lost, as explained by the Bach in siman 140. As to possession itself, the Gemara teaches that a father and son do not establish possession against one another when they are supported from the same household. The poskim add that the matter depends upon the judges' assessment: where relatives customarily do not object to one another's use, they do not acquire a prescriptive right against one another. Nevertheless, if the father had other sons and daughters-in-law but seated only this daughter-in-law in this particular place, her husband's claim is plausible, since ordinarily he would not have distinguished her from the other children and daughters-in-law by placing her in his own seat unless she had acquired it from him. See also Responsa Maharit II, Choshen Mishpat, siman 2.
