His consumption is no proof. See Sema, seif katan 22, who gives the reason that the owner may fear protesting against the non Jew, and likewise against the Jew who comes through him, as with a purchaser from a robber or a robber's son. This is very difficult. If the owner also fears protesting against a Jew who bought from the non Jew, how can Sema later, in seif katan 26, say that even where it is known that the property came to the Jew from the non Jew, the Jew is believed to say that he later repurchased it from the original owner? In a place where protest is impossible because of fear, there is no chazakah. This is according to Sema's own approach. See siman 236, where I wrote that the primary rule follows Rashba in Gittin 58, who says that a purchaser from a robber cannot establish chazakah because the owner is afraid to protest against him. Here, however, the owner is certainly not afraid to protest against the Jew who purchased from the non Jew. The reason there is nevertheless no chazakah is different. Even when a Jew purchases from another Jew, beit din does not advance a title claim for the purchaser unless the seller lived there for at least one day, for only then do we treat the property as having been in the seller's ownership and advance the seller's claim for the purchaser. Residence by a non Jew is not treated as such a one-day chazakah from which beit din advances a Jewish law title claim. Therefore, the purchaser from a non Jew does not obtain the usual benefit merely from the non Jew's prior residence. Sema further writes that if the non Jew or a Jew deriving title from him held the property for forty years, that establishes chazakah. In my view this requires study. Rashbam in Bava Batra 55 explicitly requires that the non Jew himself have lived there for forty years before selling to the Jew. This implies that if the non Jew held it for less than forty years and the Jew then held it until the combined period reached forty years, it does not help. Raavad, cited by Nimukei Yosef, implies the same. The reason appears to be that when the non Jew held it for forty years, he acquired under dina d'malchuta and the original owner despaired because he could no longer recover under the governing law. Such despair can be effective even with land, as explained in siman 236, seif 9. The Jew then receives the property permissibly, like despair followed by change of domain, because it came to him after the owner's legal right had already been lost. But where the non Jew did not hold it forty years, the owner did not yet despair while it was in the non Jew's hands. Even if he later despaired after the Jew held it long enough to complete forty years, the property had already come to the Jew while the owner's right remained, and despair afterward does not help. Nor can dina d'malchuta be invoked to abolish Torah law, as Shach writes in siman 73, seif katan 39. Moreover, why should the owner despair once the property is in a Jew's possession and he can sue the Jew in beit din? One might suggest that the Jew can say, "If you remain silent, well and good; if not, I will return the deed to the non Jew." Mishneh LaMelech writes a similar idea in Hilchot To'en. But such an argument appears applicable only where the claimant's right had already been lost while the property was in the seller's hands. The purchaser can then say that he acquired whatever the first seller validly possessed. Where the seller himself never acquired a legal right in the field, the purchaser cannot say, "What the first sold to the second," because there was no valid title to transmit. The Jew can therefore be sued and the original owner can say, "I never despaired of my right against you."
