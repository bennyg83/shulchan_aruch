Land known to belong to a Jew. See Rambam, chapter 14 of Hilchot To'en. In this law he does not use the word 'known' either regarding the non-Jew's possession or regarding the Jew who purchased from him. The Tur does not use it regarding the non-Jew, but does use it regarding the Jew who bought from him. The Mechaber uses it in both places, correctly. The Tur's word 'known' regarding the Jewish purchaser does not mean that ownership was known to everyone, such that the purchaser necessarily knew the property belonged to a Jew and therefore should not have bought it on the uncertainty whether the non-Jew had acquired it. Rather, even if it was not publicly known, but the Jewish claimant now proves before beit din that it was his, he can recover it from the Jewish possessor because an ordinary non-Jew is presumed capable of robbery, and wherever a person finds his property he takes it back, as I explained in the Perishah. Accordingly, when seeking to recover directly from the non-Jew, the Jew likewise must prove by witnesses that it was his. Rambam therefore omitted the word 'known' in both places because it is obvious that without witnesses he cannot recover either from the Jew or from the non-Jew.
His consumption does not constitute proof, etc. We may say that the Jew was afraid to protest against the non-Jew, and likewise against the Jew who comes through the non-Jew, just as with one who purchases from a robber or from a robber's son. See below siman 152. Raavad, Mordechai, and Ran write that if the non-Jew, or the Jew who comes through him, held the property for forty years, that does constitute chazakah, as stated in chapter Chazakat HaBatim concerning the Persian sharecroppers whose period was forty years.
The field returns to its owner without any oath. Meaning, the Jew who takes it from the non-Jew need not swear either a severe oath or a hesset oath. Ramban concludes with the reason that hesset was enacted only between Jews. Maggid Mishneh writes that this is clear, for the non-Jew's claim is not a claim that can require an oath. He further writes that Rambam appears to hold that even where a Jew purchased from the non-Jew, the Jewish claimant recovers from him without an oath, since Rambam simply says that a Jew who comes through the non-Jew has the same law as the non-Jew. The reason is that hesset is not administered on an uncertain claim, and this Jewish purchaser does not know with certainty that the non-Jew acquired the land. Raavad disagrees and holds that when the property is in the hands of a Jew who comes through the non-Jew, the claimant must swear hesset. His reason is that he treats the claim, 'My father told me so,' as a definite claim. See the end of siman 75 for this dispute. Maggid Mishneh notes that even according to those who treat a father's statement as definite, the same should not follow merely because a non-Jew told him something. How can that be regarded as a definite claim?
