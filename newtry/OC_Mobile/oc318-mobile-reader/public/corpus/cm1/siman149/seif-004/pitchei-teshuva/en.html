It is forbidden to change it. This is also stated in Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim siman 153, seif 17. See Responsa Shev Yaakov, siman 8, concerning Reuven, who obtained from the king a privilege and license to maintain a permanent public synagogue in his house. After fifteen years the king ordered the community to build a large synagogue and later ordered the old synagogues closed. Because Reuven petitioned the king to preserve his right, the king ordered the community to pay Reuven two hundred Reichsthaler for taking away his privilege. After Reuven's death the community demanded that his widow return the money, claiming that it had been paid unlawfully. The widow answered that her husband's original synagogue license had been obtained with the community's consent, whereas the later royal order closing the old synagogue resulted from the community's lobbying and from a large payment they made to the royal treasury. The parties presented further claims and responses. Shev Yaakov ruled that if the community was legally entitled to abolish Reuven's synagogue, the widow would indeed have no right to retain money that her husband received from community funds merely because of the royal order, even if the order had not resulted from his request, as explained in Shulchan Aruch siman 369, seif 8. But according to the widow's claim that her husband's synagogue and royal license were originally established with the community's consent, the community had no right to abolish the synagogue from his house. Orach Chaim siman 153, seif 17, states that where a person had a synagogue in his house for many years, the congregation may not transfer it. Although the Levush distinguishes and says this applies only to transferring it to another individual's house, whereas they may build a permanent synagogue for everyone, that distinction cannot justify abolishing the old synagogue. The source is Maharik, shoresh 113, where no such distinction appears. The Levush's rule is correct only insofar as the community may establish an additional permanent public synagogue while leaving the old synagogue available to whoever wishes to enter it. To build the new synagogue while suppressing the old one is certainly forbidden, as is also explained in Responsa Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi, siman 45. The Magen Avraham at the end of siman 154 cites Knesset HaGedolah that a congregation can decree that ten men not pray outside the synagogue. That concerns prayer at a place that is not an established synagogue. It cannot justify prohibiting prayer in an established synagogue that existed earlier with communal consent. Consequently, in this case the community acted unlawfully by paying the royal treasury to compel everyone to enter the new synagogue, and they thereby caused the king to order Reuven's synagogue closed. They therefore have no claim to the two hundred Reichsthaler. On the contrary, the community is liable for Reuven's loss under the law of garmi. Their claim that the step was necessary so that seats in the new synagogue could be sold to help fund the building is no defense to abolishing Reuven's synagogue. This follows as well from the Rama in siman 162 concerning a wealthy person who owned many synagogue seats and objected when the congregation sought to add benches. See there further.
