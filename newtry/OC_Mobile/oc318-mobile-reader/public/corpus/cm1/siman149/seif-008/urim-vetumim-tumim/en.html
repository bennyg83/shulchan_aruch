Until he consumes for three consecutive years after the minor reaches majority, etc. The Raavad objects and writes that the Gemara proves that even if the possessor continues for three years after the minor reaches majority, he still has no chazakah. See Sema, seif katan 27. The Shach in seif katan 15 likewise says that the chapter HaMafkid proves the Raavad's view, following the Ra'ah's inference in Ketubot: Rav Huna says that a relative is not placed over a minor's property, implying that a non-relative may be appointed because one cannot acquire a chazakah against a minor. According to the Rambam, however, why may a stranger be appointed? We should fear that he will remain for three years after the child reaches majority and then claim that he purchased the land. I find an even stronger difficulty from Ketubot 17b. The Gemara establishes the Mishnah's clause, "if there are witnesses that it belonged to his father, he is not believed," as where the possessor consumed two years in the father's lifetime and one in the son's. Otherwise the rule would be obvious. The Gemara asks whether Rav Huna merely came to teach a Mishnah, and answers that Rav Huna teaches the additional rule, "even if he reached majority." If, as the Rambam understands, this means only that the possessor continued one year after majority rather than a full three, the difficulty remains. The Mishnah itself must already refer to two years in the father's lifetime and one year after the son reached majority. If the third year were while the child was still a minor, what novelty would the Mishnah teach? Obviously there is no chazakah against a minor, because who can be expected to protest? The Mishnah does not separately teach that there is no chazakah against a deaf person or mentally incompetent person because that is self-evident. The same is true of a minor. Thus the Mishnah already teaches that one year after majority does not help, and Rav Huna's statement would add nothing. The Raavad's reading is therefore compelling: Rav Huna teaches that even if the possessor consumes all three years after majority, there is still no chazakah. To reconcile the Rambam, I believe one must pay close attention to his formulation. He states Rav Huna's law as a case where one consumed before the minor for one or two years and one year after the minor became an adult, but does not frame it as two years in the father's lifetime and one year in the son's. The reason is that we rule that a person may not sell inherited ancestral property until age twenty. According to the Rambam, this restriction applies to inherited property generally, though a gift may be made earlier. In chapter 29 of Hilchot Mekhirah, the Rambam explains that a gift is uncommon and the Sages did not enact special restrictions for an uncommon case. Accordingly, when an unrelated guardian or manager is placed over a minor's inherited property, everyone knows the land came from the minor's ancestors. We need not fear that after the child reaches ordinary majority the possessor will remain three years and claim purchase. If he claims that the minor gave it to him as a gift, that is an uncommon event. People would regard such a claim as highly implausible, for who would believe that a young heir gave away ancestral land? The Sages did not legislate against an uncommon possibility, and a migo based on such an uncommon claim is ineffective. If the possessor claims purchase, the minor cannot legally sell inherited ancestral property until age twenty. Nor need we fear that the guardian will remain from the child's thirteenth year until three years after age twenty, a full decade after ordinary majority. It is unreasonable to suppose the land would remain under such management for so long. Without Rav Huna's teaching, however, one could still fear that the possessor would remain for a year or two after the child's ordinary majority and then claim, "I purchased it from your father." That claim does not depend on the child's ability to sell inherited land. Rav Huna teaches that such a claim is ineffective because the child's failure to protest after majority does not validate possession that began while the child was incapable of understanding his father's affairs. This also explains why the Rambam does not formulate the rule in terms of the father. Where the possessor claims purchase from the father of ancestral property, even three full years after the child reaches ordinary majority need not establish a chazakah, because the possessor lacks the alternative migo that he bought from the child. The child could not sell such inherited property until twenty, and a gift is too uncommon to create a useful migo. The Rambam's statement that after three years of adult possession the possessor can claim purchase therefore concerns property that truly belongs to the child independently, such as property given to him as a healthy person's gift, property purchased by his guardian with his money, or property collected for a debt owed to his father. But as to ancestral land inherited from the father, he cannot plausibly claim purchase from the child during this period, and a claim of purchase from the father is likewise unsupported by migo. The Bach also notes that the Rambam's words somewhat imply that if the possessor claims he bought from the father, even after the child reaches majority he is not believed, but the Bach does not explain why. According to what I have written, the reason is clear: ancestral property cannot yet be sold by the child, so the possessor has no useful migo. Nor is he believed by a migo that the child gave it as a gift, because such a gift is uncommon, and an uncommon migo is not accepted. Rav Huna's statement in Ketubot now teaches a substantial novelty even according to the Rambam. Even where the possessor continues for three years after majority regarding inherited ancestral land, he cannot say, "I bought it from your father," by migo that the son gave it to him, because that alternative is uncommon. The Rambam's position is therefore internally sound. I later saw Maharit, Choshen Mishpat responsum 84, who writes regarding property inherited by orphans that one in possession is not believed to say, "I bought it from the widow when she sold it for her ketubah," by migo that he bought it from the orphans, because inherited ancestral property cannot be sold by them until twenty. Maharit discusses the issue according to the Rosh and Rambam and does not suggest the migo that the orphans gave it to him as a gift. Evidently, such a migo is indeed considered weak and uncommon according to all, which supports the explanation above. This may also explain the Tur's statement that the Rosh agreed with the Raavad. Where does the Tur find this? In his rulings the Rosh merely copies the Gemara's phrase that one cannot establish a chazakah against a minor, even if the minor later reaches majority, without explaining whether "reaches majority" means one year, as the Rambam, or three years, as the Raavad. According to what I have written, the Rambam's entire reconciliation depends on the rule that inherited ancestral property cannot be sold until twenty. The Rosh disagrees with that rule, as explained below in siman 235, holding that a minor who understands commerce may sell from age thirteen. The Rambam's reconciliation is therefore unavailable to the Rosh, so the original Gemara difficulty returns and the Rosh must follow the Raavad. I later saw Rosh responsa, klal 99, rule 2, which indeed appears explicitly like the Raavad: even after majority and many years of possession, there is no chazakah in the circumstances there. One should not ask according to the Rambam why the Mishnah did not state the law directly concerning the child's own field: "this field was yours and I purchased it from you," and teach that even if there are witnesses of the child's title and the possessor consumed one or two years after majority, he is not believed, thereby teaching that there is no chazakah against a minor. That would be self-evident if the full three years were not consumed after majority. Years consumed during minority are as though they were not consumed for purposes of protest. The novelty in the father's case is that the possessor may have consumed two valid years in the father's lifetime, then one year during the son's minority, and another year after majority. One might think that the year during minority merely fails to help but also does not interrupt the sequence, because the possessor did continue consuming, and that the two years in the father's lifetime plus the later year after majority should establish a three-year chazakah. The Mishnah teaches that it does not, because when the father died while the son was a minor, the son did not know of the prior possession and therefore did not understand the need to protest when he later became an adult. According to the Raavad, however, why did the Mishnah not teach the stronger law directly concerning the child's own field, that even if the possessor consumed during minority and then for several years after majority, there is no chazakah? The answer is that if the possessor initially entered during the child's minority and later claims purchase after majority, one must ask when the purchase supposedly occurred. If during minority, the child had no capacity to sell. If after majority, the possessor thereby admits that his earlier occupation was not under a purchase. According to many authorities, one who initially entered land under a non-purchase right cannot later establish a chazakah merely by continued possession while claiming a later purchase. See what I wrote above in siman 147 and the Sema in siman 150. Such a case would therefore not isolate the special law of a minor. By contrast, where the possessor entered during the father's lifetime, remained after the father's death while the heir was a minor, and then remained three years after majority, he claims consistently that he purchased from the father and that his possession was under purchase from the very beginning. One might then think the later three years establish his claim. The Mishnah teaches that even so he is not believed, because the child was a minor when the father died and did not know that the inheritance belonged to his father or that the possession required protest. Even many years after majority may therefore fail to create a chazakah. The Ra'ah's concise words in Ketubot may intend this distinction, which is intrinsically sound. It also follows that according to the Raavad's approach, even if the possessor claims, "I bought it from you after you reached majority," he is not believed merely by the later possession, as Rashi writes explicitly in Bava Metzia, chapter HaMafkid, and as appears from the Raavad's objection to the Rambam. The Rambam's formulation there does not mention the father at all, so the Raavad's objection necessarily concerns even a direct purchase claim from the child. The Tur and Rashi in Ketubot mention the claim, "I bought it from your father," only to teach the stronger point that even where the possessor does not admit any period of non-purchase occupation, he still is not believed. All the more so where he admits that he initially occupied without purchase. The Bach's assertion that Rashi in Bava Metzia contradicts Rashi in Ketubot is therefore incorrect. The Tur's emphasis on purchase from the father likewise does not prove a different rule. We should not multiply disputes. The Rosh, Raavad, and Rashi can all be understood consistently as stating the same law. The Sema was therefore correct that according to the second view, the Raavad's, even a later claim of direct purchase from the child is not believed merely because of possession after majority. The Bach's objection to the Sema has no force.
