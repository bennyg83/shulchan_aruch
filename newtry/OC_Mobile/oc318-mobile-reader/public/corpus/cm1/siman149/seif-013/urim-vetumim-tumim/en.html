The craftsmen, etc. See Beit Yosef, who writes that the Tur and Rambam interpret the Mishnah of “craftsmen have no chazakah” as referring to land, not to other property, because the Mishnah there is stated regarding land. See also Chiddushei Ramban, who likewise writes that the principal subject of the Mishnah is land. This requires analysis. If so, what is the Gemara’s challenge to Rabbah, who said that the rule applies only where the item was delivered in the presence of witnesses, from the Mishnah’s statement that a craftsman has no chazakah, implying that another person does have chazakah? If there are witnesses, why should another person have chazakah? The Mishnah deals with land, where he held it for three years. A craftsman has no chazakah, whereas another does. Surely with land Rabbah would not require witnesses, since everyone knows that the land belonged to the claimant, and Rabbah’s entire rationale is the migo that he could have said he purchased it from another, as the Rashbam and Tosafot write. That does not apply to land, since another person also has no chazakah until he has possessed it for three years. Rabbah can therefore agree that a craftsman has no chazakah while another person does have a three year chazakah. It may be said that the migo mentioned there is not literal. According to Rabbah, even with land we require witnesses who know that he entered the land in the capacity of a craftsman to repair it. But if there are no such witnesses, and the claimant says, “You entered to repair it,” while he says, “I purchased it,” he is believed. Therefore the Gemara asks: if so, another person should likewise have no chazakah. This follows the view that when there are witnesses that he entered the property other than by way of purchase, he is no longer believed to say, “I purchased it.” That is the view of the Rashbam and Rambam, unlike the Baal HaMaor and those who agree with him. This also explains why the Gemara does not conclude, “If there are witnesses and he saw it,” because both are required. See Tosafot and Ramban there, who discuss this point. Rabbenu Tam sought to derive from this inference a novel rule which the sages of his generation did not accept. See there. According to this, it is well understood that the discussion refers to the Mishnah, which concerns land, and therefore witnesses of seeing are unnecessary, since the land is before us. What is required are witnesses as to whether he entered it as a craftsman or the like, or by way of purchase. Accordingly, perhaps the Rashbam, who explains that Rabbah requires witnesses to delivery because otherwise the craftsman has a migo that he purchased it from another, found the above difficulty, namely what the Gemara asks from the Mishnah’s distinction between a craftsman and another person. He therefore explains explicitly that “craftsman” there does not refer to land at all, but to movable property, as Beit Yosef cites in his name. The Gemara’s question is then entirely proper according to his approach. One could nevertheless answer with difficulty that the Gemara’s question is not based on the Mishnah alone, which deals with land, but also relies on the baraita stating, “If one saw his garment in the hands of a launderer,” implying that specifically the launderer lacks chazakah, while another person has chazakah. But this conflicts with Ramban’s explanation that the question, “another person does have chazakah,” applies even to the initial understanding of Rabbah, where witnesses suffice without seeing, and therefore the Gemara did not mention seeing. At that stage it had not yet derived the rule from the baraita concerning the launderer, since it is from that baraita that the Gemara later retracts and requires seeing. From what we have written we may learn that according to the conclusion, where we do not follow Rabbah and distinguish between a craftsman and another person, even if there are no witnesses that he entered specifically as a craftsman, the craftsman nevertheless has no chazakah, because we presume that he entered the field in the course of his craft. The claimant must, however, bring witnesses that the property originally belonged to him. Otherwise, even if the craftsman admits that it once belonged to him, the craftsman is believed by a migo that he could have said, “It was never yours,” just as with a garment where there are neither witnesses nor seeing. This is obvious.
