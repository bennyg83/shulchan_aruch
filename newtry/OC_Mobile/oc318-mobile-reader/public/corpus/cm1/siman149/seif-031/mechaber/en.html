One who possesses property consecrated to the poor or to a synagogue does not establish presumptive ownership. If appointed treasurers administer it, however, possession can establish ownership. {Rama: This applies only when the treasurers have a share in the consecrated property. Otherwise, no ownership is established, because the poor should not suffer from the treasurer's failure to protest when he has no share in it, as stated in Responsa Rashba 641. Some say one who possesses communal property cannot establish ownership, and the community likewise cannot establish ownership of another's property. It appears to me that communal property has the same law as property consecrated by an individual. If a person owns a Sefer Torah and the congregation continually reads from his Sefer Torah, then if the congregation owned another Sefer Torah when it read from his, his established right is effective and they may not change the arrangement. If they had no other Sefer Torah, he established no right. If a person held an established mitzvah, such as gelilah or the honor connected with a Sefer Torah, and an unavoidable circumstance occurred, so the communities gave the mitzvah to another, the first person resumes it when the unavoidable circumstance ends. But if he gave the mitzvah to another without duress, his established right is canceled. See Orach Chaim, siman 153. A house that has long served as a synagogue may not be changed to another location. If Reuven and Shimon inherited from their father the exclusive authority to permit construction of a synagogue, they cannot sell that authority to others. Permission was granted to them and not to another, and no position of authority may be sold. See responsum 182 and note 10.}
