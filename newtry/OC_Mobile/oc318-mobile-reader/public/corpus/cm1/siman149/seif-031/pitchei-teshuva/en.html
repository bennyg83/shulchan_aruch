A person who had established possession of a mitzvah. Shaar Mishpat cites Sefer Chasidim, simanim 762 and 763, that where a Torah scholar had customarily obtained a mitzvah by contributing a certain sum to charity, and persons of lesser character then offered more charity for the mitzvah, they take precedence because the increased contribution benefits the communal charity. Even if their intention is not for the sake of Heaven but for honor, they should not be prevented from purchasing the mitzvah for the higher amount. All the more so, if one had held the mitzvah without payment, a person who offers money to charity for it takes precedence. Responsa Mahari HaLevi, brother of the Taz, siman 21, likewise rules this way.
It is forbidden to change it. This is also stated in Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim 153:17. Responsa Shav Yaakov, siman 8, discusses Reuven, who obtained royal authorization to maintain a permanent public synagogue in his home. Fifteen years later the king ordered the community to build a large synagogue and then ordered the older synagogues closed. After Reuven petitioned the king to preserve his right, the king ordered the community to pay him two hundred Reichsthalers for the loss of his privilege. After Reuven died, the community demanded that his widow return the money, claiming it had been paid improperly. The widow answered that her husband's synagogue and royal license had originally been established with the community's consent, whereas the later order closing it resulted from the community's own lobbying and expenditures to the royal treasury. Shav Yaakov rules that had the community been entitled by halacha to abolish Reuven's synagogue, the widow could not retain money paid from communal funds merely because the sovereign ordered the payment. But if, as she claimed, the synagogue was established with communal consent, the community had no right to abolish it. A synagogue long maintained in a private home with communal approval may not simply be displaced. Even the Levush, who permits establishing a new central synagogue, does not permit abolishing the old one; rather, the new synagogue may be built while the original remains available to those wishing to pray there. Thus the community acted improperly by paying the sovereign to compel everyone to use the new synagogue and thereby causing Reuven's synagogue to be closed. Consequently they have no claim to recover the two hundred Reichsthalers. On the contrary, under the law of direct causation they are liable for the loss they caused him. Their argument that closure was necessary so that seats in the new synagogue would sell and help defray construction costs does not justify depriving Reuven of his established right, as is also evident from Rama in siman 162 regarding an individual who may object when communal alterations impair his established synagogue places.
