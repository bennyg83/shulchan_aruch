Consecrated property. The Tur writes that people do not protest on behalf of consecrated property, because if someone enters it, the public assumes that he entered to collect the produce for the poor and will hold it until it is needed. If he claims, 'I purchased it from the seven trustees of the city, and they have gone overseas,' they answer that such a purchase should have become publicly known. See there. The Tur concludes that if the seven trustees admit that they sold it, he has a chazakah. This is difficult, for why would chazakah be needed? It appears that they admit only that they sold it but do not know to whom, and his possession identifies him as the purchaser. Their admission is effective even though there was no public report. One cannot compare this to the end of siman 222, where a seller says, 'I do not know to whom I sold it,' and a person who says, 'I purchased it,' is believed. Here the case is weaker because a purchase from the community ordinarily becomes public. Taz. See Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 183; Responsa Maharit, siman 132; and Responsa Maharshdam, simanim 226, 262, and 383.
He protested. The Sema writes that this resembles the Rama's ruling in seif 24 regarding a guardian who failed to protest. The Mechaber states the ruling here without qualification according to his own view, cited there as a dissenting opinion, that possession does establish a chazakah. See Responsa Raanach, who discusses these laws at length, and Responsa Rama, siman 32.
Like. But if communal trustees possess property belonging to others, their possession is effective. See the Bach. Shach.
Gave. Meaning, the individual himself gave it voluntarily to another person. The community itself lacks authority simply to give it to someone else. This is derived from the preceding law concerning a Sefer Torah: when they had no other Sefer Torah, they were forbidden to divert it to another use. Sema.
