The trustees have a share. Tumim asks that at most they are partners, and one partner cannot establish chazakah against the share of another partner who was out of the country and unable to protest. What difference should it make whether the trustees themselves have a share? Tumim appears to understand the phrase as meaning that some separate portion belonged to the trustees personally. Rashba's responsum, cited by Beit Yosef, has a different meaning. Since the entire responsum clarifies the issue, its reasoning should be set out. Rashba was asked whether one who establishes possession over property consecrated for a charitable association or for burial funds can obtain chazakah. He answers that it is reasonable that he can. Although one who encroaches upon property of the public cannot normally obtain chazakah, because there is no single person capable of waiving the public's right, Rashba distinguishes property with identifiable owners. Public property dedicated to an association has specific administrators responsible for its needs. If funds are lacking, the association members replenish them, and if there is excess the members' future contributions are reduced. The association is therefore treated as the owner of the fund. Its representatives can compromise or waive rights even though the group is numerous. This differs from a public road dedicated to everyone, where there is no defined body of owners capable of settling the public's right. That is why Tur in siman 155, concerning a dovecote, rules that where the relevant neighbors consented, the use is effective against both an individual and a group. The local property owners are identifiable and are treated like a single defined ownership group. The Gemara itself calls such a dovecote case one involving an individual for this reason. Rashba then says that there are trustworthy trustees who have a share in those dedicated funds and who buy and sell on behalf of the association. By saying that they "have a share," he means that they themselves are members of the association. This completely answers Tumim's question from ordinary partners. An ordinary partner has no authority to protest concerning his fellow's independent share, and his protest concerning that other share is legally irrelevant. He may also sell his own share without the other's. The trustees here are different. They are members of the association and thus have a personal share, but at the same time they alone are empowered to buy, sell, and manage the association's property. No other member can act without them. The association has effectively withdrawn from direct ownership powers and vested them in the trustees. Even ordinary guardians are treated by many authorities as owners for purposes of protest and chazakah, as explained later in seif 54. Those who disagree do so because a guardian without a personal share can say, "You appointed me only to improve, not to impair." He is merely an agent. These trustees, however, have a personal share. If money is lost they must themselves contribute their portion to replenish the fund. They are full owners and may act both to improve and, within their authority, to impair. A partner generally has that power, as Shach writes in siman 77. All the more so here, where the association has made the trustees the exclusive acting owners and invalidated action by anyone else. They resemble the seven good men of the city, against whom chazakah can be established. This also explains Tur. Tur first says that there is no chazakah because there is no one to protest, for the public may assume that the possessor entered for the benefit of the poor. He then says that if the possessor claims to have purchased from the seven good men of the city, we answer that a sale by them would have had publicity. At first glance, why are two reasons necessary? And why should the public assume that he entered for the poor if the seven good men know all communal affairs and should have protested? Tumim answers with difficulty. In my view, Tur does not accept Rashba's broad rule that ordinary trustees who have a share can establish or defeat chazakah. Tur makes the matter depend specifically on the seven good men of the city. If the seven good men appoint administrators to supervise communal business and even give them authority to sell, a sale from those administrators need not have a public voice. Tur nevertheless holds that their failure to protest cannot itself establish chazakah, because they cannot lose the association's property by silence. He therefore gives the first reason: unlike the dovecote case, where the neighboring owners know that the other person's use is adverse and should protest, here the public may not realize any adverse claim at all, because they assume that he entered for the poor's benefit. But if the possessor claims that he purchased directly from the seven good men themselves, those men are the actual communal principals and should indeed have protested. Tur therefore gives the second answer: such a sale would itself have generated public knowledge. Since no such report exists, the possessor is not believed. The dovecote case is different because it concerns a limited group of identified neighboring owners who can consent without formal action of the seven good men, and such consent need not become public. Thus some authorities hold that one who possesses communal property has no chazakah, similar to the head of the exile. The dovecote is no contradiction, because there the parties are identifiable owners rather than an undifferentiated public.
