In my view, everyone agrees here that he collects even from future produce. Only an ordinary migo is sometimes not used to transfer from one form of property to another or to extract property. Here, however, it is not known independently that the property belonged to the claimant's father. The possessor's own admission creates that fact, so this is peh she'asar, as explained at the beginning of Ketubot, Perek HaIshah Shenit'armelah. Peh she'asar is stronger than an ordinary migo, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 72, s.v. ein lecha bo, and can be effective even against witnesses.
Like the type of rumor we wrote about immediately above. See Sema, seif katan 37. The Shach disagrees, and he is correct that with a minor there is no difference whether the rumor arose before or after three years, because one does not establish chazakah in a minor's property. Nevertheless, the Shach's explanation that the phrase means a rumor arising before the possessor was legally established, and that even after three years he remains like one possessing against a minor, is very strained. His substantive rule is also difficult. He says that once there is a rumor the property belonged to the heirs' father, the possessor loses the migo that he could have said, "It was never yours," because he cannot brazenly contradict a public rumor. Yet the Mishnah at the beginning of Perek HaIshah Shenit'armelah expressly requires witnesses that it belonged to the father. According to the Shach it should have taught the stronger rule that even a mere rumor suffices. Tumim raises this question as well. In my view, a mere rumor that the property belonged to their father is certainly insufficient, as the Gemara proves conclusively. Nor does the rumor destroy the possessor's position. Even if he cannot say, "It never belonged to your father," because the rumor contradicts him, he does not actually need to make a definite ownership claim at all. Even a chazakah unsupported by a claim need not be surrendered so long as the challenger has no proof of prior ownership, as explained in siman 146, seif 9. A rumor is not proof. The possessor can therefore say that he purchased it from some other person who died or went overseas and had lived there. So long as the challenger brings no proof, the possessor need not answer him. The Mechaber therefore correctly requires a rumor of the same kind mentioned in seif 20, namely a rumor that the property came into the possessor's hands specifically as a mortgage from the father. Such a rumor concerns the possessor's own receipt of the property from the father and therefore implies that the possessor himself knows it belonged to the father. He then lacks even the alternative of saying that he does not know whether it belonged to the father, because such a denial would be brazen against the rumor. He must therefore return it. This does not conflict with the Mishnah, because witnesses who merely establish that it belonged to the father suffice, whereas a rumor does not suffice unless it has this more specific content. The legal quality of the rumor should resemble the standards for a rumor of kiddushin in Even HaEzer, siman 66.
