One who established possession of a minor's property, etc. The Sema discusses this at great length, but his analysis does not appear correct to me. He interprets the Mechaber's words, 'But if a report emerged that it belonged to the orphans, in the manner of the report mentioned above,' as a report that arose before the possessor completed the years of chazakah and that the property had come to him as collateral. He further reads seif 22, which says that the possessor consumed the produce for the years of chazakah during the father's lifetime, as a case in which no such report emerged, because siman 150, seif 5, says that after a report one cannot assert a different debt by virtue of a migo based on a claim of purchase. He therefore rejects Raavad's reading of Rambam in seif 22 as applying even after a report. In my view, the Mechaber in seif 21 means that even if the report arose only after three years or more, the possessor is not believed. Once a report has established that the land belonged to the orphans, the years during which he possessed it while they were minors are legally ineffective, since no chazakah is established against a minor. His only possible underlying claim would be that it had never belonged to their father, and the report renders that alternative claim unattractive. The timing of the report does not cure the defective chazakah. By contrast, the authorities who distinguish above, in seif 20, between a report before or after three years concern possession during the father's lifetime. There, a report that the property came as collateral functions as a protest and requires the possessor to preserve his document. A report arising only after three years is therefore ineffective. Here, however, the possession occurred against a minor and never matured into chazakah at all. The phrase, 'in the manner of the report mentioned above,' means before he acquired a valid three-year chazakah, excluding the later case where he held for three years during the father's lifetime, or, according to Rambam and the Mechaber in seif 19, three years after the minor reached majority, and only afterward did a report arise. Gidulei Terumah likewise explains that the Tur and Beit Yosef distinguish between a report in the father's lifetime, where its timing determines whether it serves as a protest, and possession against orphans, which can be nullified even by a later report because there was no possible protest during minority. The Sema also asks how seif 22 can allow the possessor to say, 'The father owed me,' by virtue of a migo that the property was purchased, when siman 150, seif 5, rejects a different debt claim where a report existed. He therefore forces seif 22 into a case with no report. That cannot be correct. Had Rambam and the Mechaber intended no report, they should have combined the case with seif 21 rather than formulate a separate clause. The wording plainly continues the preceding discussion and teaches that if he completed the years of chazakah during the father's lifetime, he may assert the debt even though a report later emerged. The Beit Yosef's question in siman 150 concerns a different matter. Rabbi Yonah there speaks of a case with no report at all and nevertheless denies retention of future produce by virtue of a migo from one asset to another. The Beit Yosef asks whether this follows Raavad's position that Rambam's case permits exemption only for produce already consumed. It does not show that the Tur rejected Raavad's understanding of the report. The simple reading is therefore correct: Rambam's case in seif 22 applies even where a report existed. Nor is siman 150 contradictory. There the report was that the property had entered his possession as collateral, so a claim of purchase is itself weakened and he prefers to assert another debt. Here the report merely says that the property had belonged to the orphans' father. That is entirely consistent with a subsequent purchase. Indeed, even witnesses that it had belonged to the father would not prevent a possessor who held for three years during the father's lifetime from saying, 'The witnesses speak truth, but I purchased it from him.' Such a claim of purchase is therefore strong, and he may assert that the father owed him by virtue of that migo. Tosafot in Perek Chazakat HaBatim and the Agudah similarly distinguish between a report that the land presently belongs to the orphans and one that it had formerly belonged to their father. They explain that the words, 'You cannot say it is purchased in my hands,' mean that because of the report he is reluctant to make the brazen claim, but if he actually made the claim he would be believed. Although they understand the Gemara as involving three years of possession and Rambam as involving no three-year chazakah where the father's ownership was not otherwise established, their practical rule accords with Rambam as explained here. Examine this carefully, for the matter is clear. Citation references: 81, 100, 159, 506.
Since, had he wished, he could have said, 'They are purchased,' etc. Rambam could equally have written that he was believed because he could have said, 'It never belonged to your father.' Since, in this context, the claim that it never belonged to their father and the claim that it is now purchased are effectively one, Rambam used the language of purchase. The Gemara may be explained similarly according to Rambam. This also answers Raavad's objection that Rabbah bar Rav Huna should have relied on the migo that he could have said, 'It is mine.' And he thereby collects, etc. The Sema writes that this cannot mean he may begin collecting after the present claim, because the Tur in siman 150, seif 4, rules that he is believed only regarding produce already consumed and may not retain the land until he consumes produce equal to his claim, since that would be a migo used to extract. The Tur and Mechaber there also speak of one who wishes to retain and consume the produce, but later explain that the case concerns produce already consumed. In my view, Rambam's words, 'he thereby collects,' do not support the Sema. Raavad and Maggid Mishneh clearly understand Rambam to mean that he has not yet consumed the produce. Otherwise, Raavad's objection would have no force, and Maggid Mishneh would not need to distinguish between movable collateral and land. The Ir Shushan and the Sema himself below likewise treat Rambam's case as one where the produce has not yet been consumed. Responsa Maharit, siman 112, explicitly states that Rambam disagrees with Rabbeinu Yonah and the Tur in siman 150, who reject a migo from one asset to another, and instead follows the alternative answer of Tosafot and the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia that a migo is not used to extract. Sefer HaTerumot, gate 14, part 1, cited below in siman 150, seif 4, likewise proves this. See my extended discussion there. Citation references: 100, 110.
He collects from its enhancement. The Sema says that this is the correct reading and that manuscripts reading, 'with an oath,' contain a scribal error, because Rambam permits collection in such a case without an oath, as the Mechaber explicitly states in seif 22. Maggid Mishneh explains there that no Torah oath is imposed regarding land and that an oath of hesset was not instituted on an uncertain claim by heirs who cannot plead with certainty. Therefore, the Sema says, the text must read, 'from its enhancement,' meaning from produce that grew upon the land. In my view, however, all new and old editions of the Mechaber read, 'with an oath,' as does the Ir Shushan. The 1550 edition of Rambam and other editions read the same, as does the Semag at the end of positive commandment 95. Hagahot Maimoniyot notes that the Semag quotes Rambam as saying he collects with an oath and asks what benefit the migo then provides. Maharash, in his glosses to the Semag, likewise supports the reading, 'with an oath.' Seif 22 is not a contradiction. There the possessor completed a legally valid chazakah, and had he asserted purchase he would not have been required to take an oath of hesset because the heirs plead only uncertainty. Here, however, even a claim of purchase is believed only by virtue of the migo that he could have said, 'It never belonged to your father,' because he did not complete three years during the father's lifetime. Had he made that alternative claim, he would have been required to take an oath of hesset, since the heirs contradict him with certainty and know that it belonged to their father. Therefore, when he claims purchase or a debt, he too is believed only with an oath. The oath mentioned here is an oath of hesset. Citation references: 40, 100, 120.
But if a report emerged concerning it, etc. I have already written that even if the report arose after three years of possession in the minor's presence, he is not believed. This appears to include even a claim, 'It is purchased in my hands.' The reason is that one who admits that the field belonged to another and claims purchase, without three valid years of chazakah, is believed only by virtue of the migo that he could have said, 'It was never yours.' This is explicit in the second chapter of Ketubot and in the Rif, Rosh, Maggid Mishneh, and Beit Yosef. Here, because a report established that the property belonged to the orphans' father, he is reluctant to assert, 'It never belonged to your father.' Accordingly, he cannot rely on that migo for a claim of purchase or of another debt. This parallels the Tur in siman 150, seif 5, where a claim of purchase is weakened by a report that the property came as collateral, and one therefore cannot assert an additional debt through that migo. Nevertheless, if he actually claimed, 'It never belonged to your father,' he would be believed. A report is not equivalent to witnesses and does not defeat a direct claim. The authorities agree that a mere report does not nullify a chazakah where the possessor directly contradicts it. The Sema's strained suggestion that Rambam merely adopted, as a matter of logic, the view that this kind of migo is not recognized is therefore unnecessary. We generally recognize even a migo involving a brazen alternative claim. The point is instead that the direct alternative remains legally valid but is psychologically inferior because of the report, so it does not support the indirect claim. This is also the clear implication of Maggid Mishneh: he says the possessor is not believed by virtue of a migo that the property never belonged to the father because, given the report, investigation would likely establish the father's ownership. Had the direct claim itself been legally barred, there would have been no reason to discuss migo at all. The Ir Shushan likewise says that it is not a good migo because the possessor fears saying that the property never belonged to the father. The Semag and its glosses state the same principle: there is no migo against a report, meaning that one is reluctant to plead against it, but if he actually does so he is believed. Rambam, Semag, and Tosafot therefore follow one approach. Citation references: 80, 90, 100, 130, 159.
