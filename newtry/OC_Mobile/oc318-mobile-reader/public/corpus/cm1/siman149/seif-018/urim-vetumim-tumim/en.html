One who possesses property belonging to the community, some say that this does not constitute chazakah. See Bach, who writes that although Agudah’s comparison to property of the house of the Exilarch, where no chazakah is established, is not precise, nevertheless there is independently no chazakah here because one cannot establish chazakah over public property, such as a synagogue and the like. In truth, however, in my view this rule is disputed among the poskim. Rashba in a responsum, as cited by Beit Yosef in siman 417, holds that neither a claim of waiver nor a claim of purchase is effective against the public at all. The Gemara’s rule that one who purchased a courtyard containing projections into the public domain is left in possession because we make a claim for a purchaser means that we presume the original owner had withdrawn the wall into his own property. But if it is known that he did not do so, we do not claim for the purchaser that the public waived their rights or sold them to him. Rashba’s proof is from the end of Chazakat HaBatim: “Who among the public can waive?” He therefore explains the Gemara in Lo Yachpor, which says, “Had it taught only the case of an individual, I might have said that he appeased that individual, but with the public, whom did he appease and who could waive? Therefore it was necessary,” not as implying that the conclusion recognizes waiver by the public. Rather, the conclusion means only that he has chazakah because we presume that he had withdrawn into his own property. Rashba likewise intended this in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef concerning treasurers, where he writes: “I maintain, who can waive and who can abandon, as appears at the end of Chazakat HaBatim. Although the passage in Lo Yachpor concerning a quarter kav area appears otherwise, you have already seen what I explained concerning that passage at the end of Chazakat HaBatim,” end quote. Rabbenu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef below in siman 417, likewise holds that if it is known that he did not withdraw into his own property he has no chazakah against the public, because no one has authority to waive. The Tur, however, below in siman 155 concerning the required distance of a dovecote from a city, writes that if one purchased such a dovecote it remains in his possession, because we claim for a purchaser that it was constructed with permission, whether against an individual or against the public. There the explanation cannot be that he withdrew into his own property, but only that the public granted permission, as the Tur explicitly writes, “that he constructed it with permission,” and he states the same rule against both the public and an individual. Thus the Tur understands the Gemara according to its plain conclusion that even against the public we can claim that the act was done with permission. This does not conflict with the Gemara at the end of Chazakat HaBatim, or with the Tur’s explicit statement below in siman 417 that with the public no waiver can be given. The distinction is that the public cannot waive, because who could gratuitously surrender public rights, and even the seven town representatives would not ordinarily give away public property without reason. A sale, however, is possible. Perhaps the seven representatives needed money and sold this right, for if they may sell an entire public street when necessary, certainly they may sell the right to project a beam or the like. Tosafot likewise write so. Therefore, when one purchases a courtyard already containing projections, or a dovecote located near a city, we claim that the seven representatives sold the right to the previous owner. Accordingly, Rif and Rosh read the Gemara there: “Had it taught only the case of the public, I might have said that perhaps he had withdrawn into his own property, but regarding a dovecote not,” and omit the reading found in our texts, “or perhaps the public waived it to him.” Their reason is not that they deny the possibility of a sale by the public, as Tosafot recognize, but because waiver or sale is plainly more common with an individual than with the public. If the Gemara had already taught that we assume waiver by the public, certainly the same would apply to an individual. The initial thought must therefore have been specifically that he withdrew into his own property, which does not apply to a dovecote. There is thus no contradiction between Rif and Rosh and the Tur, as Mahari ben Lev thought in his responsa. In any event, it is clear that the Tur holds that even against the public we make a claim for a purchaser that the possession began with permission. Rashi likewise implies this when he writes that the first owner either withdrew into his own property or appeased the public. Mordechai likewise writes that the seven representatives may sell or waive public rights, unlike Rashba. Since the Mechaber below in siman 155, seif 24, quotes the Tur’s ruling without any dissent, and Rama adds no qualification, the halachah there is explicitly not like Rashba, and chazakah can be established over communal property. One might ask how we can claim that he purchased from the public with the consent of the seven representatives, when the Tur writes here that with our synagogues there is no chazakah because had they been sold the sale would have become publicly known. This itself proves the opposite when the Tur’s language is examined. He first writes that there is no chazakah because there is no one whose silence can serve as a protest, since people may assume that the possessor entered the property for the benefit of the poor and the like. He then adds that if the possessor claims that he purchased it, such a sale would have become publicly known. Why are both reasons necessary? If absence of protest alone defeats chazakah, as with the property of minors, the second reason is unnecessary. Conversely, if he cannot claim purchase because such a sale would be publicly known, the first reason is unnecessary. The first reason also does not apply where the possession consists merely of opening windows or causing other encroachments against consecrated or public property, since no one can say that he did so for the benefit of the poor. Mahari ben Lev struggled with this issue, as did Maharam Alshich in a responsum, siman 183. Rather, if a person claims that he purchased the property recently, at a time still within living memory, the Tur’s second reason applies: such a sale would have become publicly known. But if he has possessed it from ancient times, extending back many years, the fact that such a sale once had publicity proves nothing, because there is no memory of earlier events and the matter may have been entirely forgotten. Knesset HaGedolah likewise cites his teacher Maharit. Conversely, regarding a claim that his ancestors or predecessor possessed it from ancient times, we can say that originally he actually entered with permission to collect produce and manage the property for the poor, and that he then remained there until the present, just as one does not establish chazakah over a minor’s property even after the minor grows up and in similar cases. No one paid attention to protest. But if he entered only recently, the first reason does not apply, because the treasurers know that he gives nothing to the poor and that the consecrated property or public receives no benefit from his consumption of the produce. Why then did the treasurer not protest? Thus the Tur’s reasoning forms a dilemma. If he claims ancient possession, we answer that at the beginning he truly provided produce to the poor, and therefore the treasurer did not protest; with the passage of time the matter was forgotten and he continued to possess without providing produce. If he claims that he began possessing only recently, when it is known that he provided nothing to the poor, then although the absence of protest would ordinarily support chazakah, his claim has no plausible basis because had the seven representatives sold to him recently the sale would have become publicly known. He therefore loses either way. This resolves all of the commentators’ difficulties with the Tur. Accordingly, when the Gemara says concerning a dovecote that we claim for a purchaser that it was purchased lawfully, this applies where the possession has continued for so long that no one remembers the transaction and the publicity of the sale is no longer relevant. Otherwise, one cannot claim purchase, because a sale by the seven representatives would become known. Another possible explanation of the Tur’s two reasons is this. If a particular field had never been under the direct control of the communal treasurers or officers, one may say that the treasurers did not protest because they assumed that this person was managing it for the poor. But if the field had always been supervised by communal officers, and then left their possession and this person entered it, that explanation cannot apply. The property was already being managed by the community’s officers, and there is no reason one officer would be displaced so that another person might enter. Maharam Alshich similarly writes in his responsum. In that case the possessor must claim that he purchased it from the seven representatives, and the Tur answers that such a sale would have become public. But if the field was never in the hands of the treasurers, the possessor may claim that it never belonged to the consecrated property in the first place, and no question of public notice arises. Thus the Tur again creates a dilemma. Nevertheless, the first explanation appears more correct because it accords with the Tur’s ruling below in siman 155. It is therefore clear that the Tur holds that chazakah can be established even over public property. Agudah consequently derived his rule from the property of the Exilarch. Accordingly, the ruling here requires study, because the Shulchan Aruch below implies that chazakah may be established against the community. In Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 183, he inclines to say that nowadays, when the town representatives act in communal affairs as they see fit and the townspeople do not restrain them, chazakah is effective. The matter is entrusted to beit din.
