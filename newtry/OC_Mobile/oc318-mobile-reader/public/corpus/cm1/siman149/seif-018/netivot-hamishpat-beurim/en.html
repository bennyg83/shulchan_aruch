Their consumption is no proof. The reason given is that they have no valid claim. At first glance this is difficult. If the possessor became deaf or mentally incompetent after establishing chazakah, so that when he comes before beit din he cannot make a claim, should his prior chazakah then fail? The correct explanation is as follows. Chazakah derives its force from a deed. Its central function is to allow the possessor to say, "My deed was lost." A minor, deaf mute, or mentally incompetent person is not legally capable of holding such a deed and cannot make a claim that a deed was lost. Therefore, chazakah does not operate for them. The principal purpose of protest is to warn the possessor to preserve his deed, as Ramban writes in Milchamot at the beginning of Chazakat HaBatim. Since these people are not capable of having a deed at all, Rambam in Hilchot Gerushin, chapter 6, likewise describes them as not competent witnesses, and the owner has no reason to protest in order to warn them concerning a deed. This differs from one who later became mentally incompetent, or from a case where the father possessed for one year and his minor son for two. In those cases, see Sema in siman 144, seif katan 3. They are legally persons for whom deeds can exist, but they may be incapable of guarding the document. The owner must still protest, because perhaps they will preserve it, or their relatives will warn them to preserve it. They are at least sometimes capable of safeguarding property, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 9. But where the possessor is not legally a person of deeds at all, so that no protest can serve its purpose, there is no chazakah. As for freely roaming livestock, although such animals are not matters of deeds and some authorities nevertheless recognize a three year chazakah in them, that appears to be a special rabbinic enactment concerning movable property. With land, several sugyot indicate that where protest cannot function to warn about preservation of a deed, chazakah does not arise.
