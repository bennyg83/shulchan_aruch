Rambam rules that possession by a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor is not evidence because their legal claims do not have the same status as ordinary adult claims. Maggid Mishneh clarifies that this does not mean they lack every mode of acquisition. Even a mentally incompetent person can receive a gift through another competent person, and minors can certainly acquire in recognized ways. The problem is evidentiary: their own claim cannot support a land presumption in the usual way. One might ask that a minor who says, "You gave it to me," should be able to rely on a gift, since a minor can acquire a gift by Torah law where another person's intent transfers it. The answer is that the land presumption itself requires a legally cognizable claim. A gift plea is ordinarily believed as an ownership explanation only because it could have been framed as purchase or another substantive claim. Where the claimant's own statements do not have ordinary litigational force, the court does not advance an unproven gift theory merely from his possession. Rashi's approach in Ketubot suggests that where a litigant would have had a valid claim if competent, the court sometimes speaks for a person unable to speak for himself. Tosafot limits that principle to cases where the law itself already favors the person even on uncertainty. Here a land presumption requires a definite claim, so we do not invent one for a minor or incompetent person. Only where the minor comes through his father do we advance the father's claim for him, just as for other heirs.
