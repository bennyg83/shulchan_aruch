Sema explains that no one establishes possession against a married woman's property because she relies on her husband to protest. He eats the fruits during her life and may inherit her, so he has reason to protect the estate. Therefore, according to Rif, Rambam, and Rosh, who allow a husband to waive inheritance rights by condition, if he has also removed himself from both fruits and inheritance, an outsider should be able to establish possession because the wife can no longer rely on him. Beit Shmuel questions why the Gemara did not simply establish the relevant baraita in a case where the husband had waived inheritance. Tosafot answers the issue. Rav himself held like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that a husband cannot effectively waive the eventual inheritance. Therefore the Gemara, when discussing Rav's position, could not use such a case. According to Rif and Rosh, who rule that inheritance can be waived, the outsider's possession should indeed be effective when the husband has no remaining property interest. Rashba's description of the husband as a kind of administrator does not necessarily mean the wife relies on him even when he has no benefit or legal connection to the property. It can be read as applying where he still has rights in fruits or inheritance. Thus Sema and Chelkat Mechokek are well supported.
Even HaEzer rules that possession against a married woman's property becomes effective only after three years following the husband's death. The Gemara itself discusses one who possessed partly during the husband's life and then three years after his death. Ramban explains that the woman had no witnesses proving the possessor entered during the husband's lifetime. He is therefore believed through a migo that he could have said, "I bought from you after your husband's death." Sema reads the Shulchan Aruch as though there had been no possession at all during the husband's lifetime. That is difficult, for then the law would be obvious and the property would not meaningfully be "a married woman's property" during the prescriptive period. Ramban's reading is better: there was earlier occupation, but no witnesses to its beginning. Tosafot asks why the Gemara needs the elaborate plea, "You sold to my seller and he sold to me," rather than simply believing the possessor to say, "I bought directly from you." Under Ramban this becomes sharper, because the direct purchase plea itself would also depend on a migo due to the earlier unauthorized entry. The answer follows the dispute of Rabbah and Rav Yosef concerning a defective document. Rav Yosef does not allow a migo to extract land where the possessor admits he lacks an independently valid deed or possession. Therefore Rav Yosef frames the case with a claim that the wife sold to the husband in the possessor's presence and the husband then sold to him. On that plea, the possessor's years during the husband's lifetime are themselves valid because his possession derived from the husband, not from unauthorized entry against the wife. He therefore has a complete possession accompanied by a claim, and the migo is used only to strengthen that claim. According to our ruling like Rabbah concerning land, even a direct claim that he bought from the woman can ultimately be believed through the migo. This explains the Gemara's formulation.
