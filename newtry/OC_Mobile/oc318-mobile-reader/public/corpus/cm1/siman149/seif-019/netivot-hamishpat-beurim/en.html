And some say that it is not a chazakah because he entered the field while the owner was a minor. See Shach, seif katan 15, who writes that this is proved by the Gemara at the beginning of HaMafkid. The Gemara derives from Rav Huna's rule permitting appointment of a stranger over a minor's property that no chazakah can be established against a minor's property. According to Rambam, however, one might still worry that the appointee will hold it for three years after the minor reaches adulthood and then claim purchase. Rambam's position can be reconciled. Nimukei Yosef asks why a relative may not be appointed over a minor's property, since a relative plainly cannot establish chazakah against him, just as partners do not establish chazakah against one another. Why then should a relative differ from a stranger? A simple answer can begin from the rules of improvement. When beit din appoints a relative, Rashi holds that both as to fruits and improvement he receives only the share of a sharecropper. Tosafot hold that he may consume the fruits without charge but receives only a sharecropper's portion of improvement. Simanim 285 and 287 discuss that where adult siblings improve inherited property through their own efforts, the improvement may belong to them. Rama limits this to siblings maintained together, but Tosafot in Bava Metzia and many poskim cited by Beit Yosef do not distinguish. Accordingly, there is reason not to appoint a relative. With a stranger, there is no serious concern that he will deny the entire arrangement. Even if a stranger could in theory establish chazakah, the minor will eventually protest, as Tosafot explain, and the stranger will be forced to admit that beit din appointed him in order to receive only the sharecropper's rights. If he denied the appointment, he would at most be treated as one who descended without permission. A relative, however, might deny the appointment and claim the entire improvement for himself, as an adult sibling who improved the inheritance. Even where the property has not been divided, there is concern. If it had already been divided the concern is obvious, because the relative could say that the better field fell to his share, leaving the poor land to the minor. Even if he would ultimately fail to prove that claim, the division could be undone and the minor would lose. The Gemara's reason that relatives may come to establish themselves in the property can therefore be understood primarily after division. Before division, as is evident from the incident of the grandmother, the concern also exists for the reason just given. Even according to Rama that the entire improvement belongs to an adult sibling only where the siblings are supported together, there is concern that the older relative will arrange to be supported together with the minor precisely in order to claim all the improvement. According to Rambam in siman 287, the Gemara's proof can be sharpened. Rambam holds that even improvement produced by the older sibling's own efforts ordinarily belongs to all heirs unless he first says, "See what father left me," meaning that he declares that he will improve only for himself. When minors are involved he must make that declaration before beit din. Therefore, while the heir is still a minor there is little concern. If the relative seeks to withdraw from beit din's appointment and declare that from now on he will improve only for himself, beit din can simply appoint someone else for the minors' benefit. The real concern arises immediately when the minor becomes an adult. The relative can then say before witnesses that he intends to improve only for himself. Although the now adult minor will eventually hear of it because matters said before witnesses become known, there is concern that when the adult minor comes to demand division, the relative will deny and say that the property had already been divided, that the better field fell to his share, and that the barren field is the other's. Even if the younger heir expects to challenge this in beit din, he may not think to issue a formal protest before reaching beit din. Tosafot make a similar point regarding a stranger. A person may expect to say in beit din, "Bring proof of your sale," yet still fail to make a prior formal protest. There is no reason to distinguish the relative. Because the newly adult heir does not yet know to make the necessary protest, the relative may wrongfully retain the improvement created during the interval until litigation. Legally, any improvement after the younger heir demanded division should belong jointly if the younger heir wished to work for himself and the relative wrongfully denied him access. Even where both could have performed the work, siman 287 states that the improvement is shared. Here the younger heir actually wanted division, but because the relative denied him and because the younger person is not yet capable of effective formal protest, he may lack witnesses to the demand and lose the improvement. This explains the Gemara's proof from Rav Huna. If a minor were fully capable of legal protest immediately on reaching adulthood, there would be no concern in appointing the relative, because he could promptly prevent any adverse chazakah or loss. The fact that the Gemara recognizes such concern shows that the minor does not become capable of effective protest at once. Consequently, chazakah cannot be established against property that began to be held while he was a minor merely by completing the three years after he matures. Rambam can therefore hold that three full years beginning after adulthood do create chazakah. Once the former minor is mature, he will certainly protest so that the possessor cannot later say, "I bought it from you," particularly because there may be no witnesses proving that the possessor originally entered while he was a minor. If he remains silent for a full three years after adulthood, that supports the possessor's title. The Gemara proves only that partial possession during minority cannot be combined with a later period of adulthood. We do not say that because the principal protest occurs at the end of the chazakah period, as explained at the end of siman 143, it should suffice that even one day of the three years occurred after maturity. If the new adult were immediately capable of protest, appointing a relative would create no concern, because he could protest as soon as he matured and there would be no loss of improvement. Since the Gemara does worry, it follows that he does not immediately know how to protest effectively. Therefore a chazakah that began during minority and merely concluded after maturity is ineffective. This interpretation can also illuminate Tosafot in Bava Metzia, whose language is otherwise difficult. They say that one cannot answer that the minor simply does not know how to protest against a relative, because then he would likewise not know how to protest against a stranger. They then distinguish that with a relative the minor may initially trust him. Their intent need not be that the minor will trust the relative forever. Even with a relative it is unreasonable that he would trust him after the relative takes a field worth a thousand and leaves him one worth a hundred. Rather, when he hears the relative's denial he may plan to sue in beit din, but not think to make a formal protest beforehand. That is enough to expose him to loss of improvement. With a stranger, by contrast, because the minor expects ongoing loss of fruits and does not trust him at all, he will immediately demand proof and go to beit din. This is the force of Tosafot's distinction. As to Tosafot's principal question on Rashi, that perhaps the Gemara's proof from appointing a stranger fails because the minor does know how to protest and therefore there is no concern of chazakah, one can answer that beit din does not appoint a guardian where there is a meaningful concern of outright denial. Beit din chooses someone reliable. Otherwise there would always be concern that he might sell all the orphans' assets, take the money, and claim that it had been spent for their benefit, or that he might die and his heirs deny the trust. Since one who deposits does so with the knowledge that the custodian's household may hold the property, beit din necessarily selects someone whose family is also presumed trustworthy. The Gemara's real proof is different. If chazakah could arise against a minor, there would be concern that the guardian might die and his heirs would inherit the property while there are no witnesses that it originally belonged to the minor's father. Even if the guardian's heirs admit that their father had been guardian, if possession against a minor were treated like possession against an adult, another person might later hold the guardian's property for one day and beit din could advance a purchase claim for his heirs, because the guardian himself would have been believed by a migo that the property had never belonged to the minor. Even if the guardian's heirs admit his prior status, there could be a case where, after the minor reaches adulthood, the guardian's appointment ends and he remains in possession of some fields. At that point he may become believed concerning the field left in his hands, as stated in seif 30. His post-appointment residence could then count as one day of possession from which beit din would advance a purchase claim for his heirs. The Gemara therefore proves that no chazakah arises against a minor's property in this manner. Even after the minor matures, if the heirs admit that the property originally came to their father from the minor while he was under guardianship, the guardian's earlier possession is not treated like the one day residence of a valid seller, just as a non Jew's residence does not serve as such one day when he himself had no Jewish law chazakah.
