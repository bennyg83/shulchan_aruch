Sha'ar Mishpat writes that this language implies that voice recognition is effective for testimony, and that a blind person is disqualified only because the verse "or he saw" teaches that a witness must be capable of seeing. A sighted witness may therefore identify a litigant by voice even if he did not see him. However, according to Perisha, the correct text in Rambam and Tur is that they "recognize the voice and know the people," with the conjunction joining two requirements rather than presenting alternatives. If so, there is no proof from here, for perhaps a sighted witness may testify only when both elements are present, recognition of the voice and independent knowledge of the person's body through touch or another means. See what I write below in siman 81, seif 16, s.v. he hid. Birkei Yosef, note 9, cites a manuscript responsum of Ri Migash on this subject. He also discusses witnesses who saw an event through what was reflected in a glass mirror. If they saw the object itself through glass, that is clearly full visual perception. If, however, they did not look at the object itself at all and saw only its reflection in a mirror, Halachot Ketanot, part 2, siman 83, was uncertain. Although Divrei Shmuel, siman 242, and Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 126, appear to hold that this is not called seeing, perhaps their cases differ because there was a realistic possibility of visual error. Where no alternative reality can reasonably be suspected, perhaps it is considered seeing. See there at length, and my brief discussion in Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer, siman 169, seif 8, seif katan 1.
