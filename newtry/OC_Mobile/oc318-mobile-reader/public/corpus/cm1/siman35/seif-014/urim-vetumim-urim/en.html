A woman is disqualified. For the verse states "men," excluding women. See Tumim, where I explained Rambam's words.
Even in a place where qualified witnesses are not commonly found, etc. Rambam explains the Mishnah this way, and Terumat HaDeshen likewise explains the Mishnah in Bava Kamma that requires witnesses to be free persons and members of the covenant, excluding slaves and gentiles. The Mishnah stated this specifically there lest one think that in cases of damage occurring around horse stables or flocks, where only such persons are commonly present, they should be accepted. It teaches that they remain disqualified. See there.
All this is the strict law, etc. Sema asks that below, in siman 408, the Mechaber cites Rambam's ruling that in damages neither slaves nor similar persons are believed even where qualified witnesses are not commonly present, and neither the Mechaber nor Rama adds that by enactment they may be believed as here. He answers that damages are common, whereas the present cases are occasional. In a common matter the Sages did not institute such a relaxation, because otherwise every wrongdoer could hire gentiles or otherwise disqualified persons to testify that his fellow caused damage, and no property would remain secure for qualified people. So writes Terumat HaDeshen, siman 353.
Such as in the women's section of the synagogue, etc. Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 1, concludes that if a person has maintained possession of a place there for three years, testimony of women does not suffice to remove him from that possession. Likewise, one qualified witness is more credible than two women. Shach.
Or a relative. Maharshal writes that a person should not testify for the benefit of his relative, because when he sees his relative being struck his emotions are aroused and he may fail to distinguish who began the altercation, since his inclination favors his relative. See there. Perhaps Maharshal follows his own view, mentioned above at the beginning of siman 33, that an intense enemy, such as one whose fellow seeks to kill him, is disqualified even according to the Sages. On that basis he extends the enactment here and holds that even a relative of the person being struck cannot testify. But according to what I wrote there, that relatives of the person being struck may testify, there is no distinction under this enactment, as Maharik writes.
Provided that the claimant asserts a definite claim. If the claimant himself does not know what occurred and brings the claim only on the basis of these witnesses, their testimony is ineffective. See Maharik, siman 93, where this appears to be stated concerning one such disqualified witness. Two such witnesses are not mentioned there, and perhaps the Rama equated the cases. In any event, one should not extend the enactment beyond its stated scope.
