A woman is disqualified. Rambam writes in Hilchot Edut 9:2 that women are disqualified from testimony, as Scripture says, "by the mouth of two witnesses," using the masculine form and not the feminine. Kesef Mishneh asks why Rambam omitted the Gemara's proof from "the two men shall stand," which refers to witnesses: men and not women. He also asks how Rambam can prove anything from masculine language, since the Torah generally speaks in the masculine. The difficulty is substantial, but Rambam's wording can be explained precisely. The Yerushalmi asks in another context: if the Torah already speaks of two goats, why repeat "two"? It answers that the repetition teaches they should be alike. So too here, if the Torah could simply say "witnesses," why specifically say "two witnesses"? The word "two" is therefore extra and can be expounded. Since it is written in masculine form, it teaches two male witnesses, not two female witnesses. This removes the objection that Torah generally speaks in masculine language. Here the number word itself is unnecessary. Still, one may ask that the extra word "two" is needed to exclude minors. The Gemara derives from "men" and not minors. But why is a verse needed for minors if they lack full legal understanding? The answer is that minors are believed in certain matters, such as identifying a father's handwriting. Chazal relied on them because in matters not requiring great intellectual judgment they possess a lesser degree of understanding. Without an explicit exclusion, one might therefore have thought that in Torah matters of similarly limited factual complexity a minor could also testify together with an adult. Certainly we would never have thought that two minors alone could establish Torah testimony, because even in rabbinic matters we do not rely upon them alone. The possible novelty would only be that a minor could join an adult. Scripture therefore says "two," requiring both witnesses to be of equal testimonial standing. Once "two" excludes a minor from joining an adult, the word "men" becomes available to exclude women. Alternatively, if "men" excludes minors, then the extra masculine "two" excludes women. Either way, women are excluded. This also clarifies the Gemara's question whether "the two men shall stand" refers perhaps to the litigants. At first glance that seems impossible, because there would be no reason to exclude women from being litigants. But according to what I have explained, the natural first use of "men" is to exclude minors, as in many Torah passages. If the verse concerned litigants, it could teach that a minor is not a full litigant, consistent with the rule that one does not take an oath on a minor's claim. The Gemara therefore proves from "those who have the dispute" that the litigants are already mentioned separately, so "the two men" must refer to the witnesses. Then the combined wording necessarily excludes both minors and women. Thus Rambam's formulation is exact: "men" can exclude minors, while "two" excludes women. His words are carefully grounded.
Where men are not commonly present. Shev Yaakov, Even HaEzer part 1, siman 3, writes that were he not hesitant to dispute Rambam, he would say women are legally valid witnesses. He argues from the Mishnah in Bava Kamma that specifies free persons and members of the covenant. The Gemara explains the need for each category: slaves lack lineage and non-Jews are not bound by mitzvot. A woman has lineage and is bound by mitzvot, so perhaps she should be valid. I do not know what novelty this adds. Terumat HaDeshen siman 353 already raises exactly this possibility and rejects it. Certainly one cannot conclude that women are valid. If so, the Mishnah that the oath of testimony does not apply to women would be wrong in damage cases where women could testify. Likewise the Gemara derives a slave's disqualification by a kal vachomer from woman and robber. If a woman were valid, that derivation would collapse. Rather, women are plainly disqualified. Why then does the Mishnah in Bava Kamma not explicitly add women to its exclusions? Because women are not ordinarily present in stables and similar locations involving unruly slaves and non-Jews. It would have been improper and uncommon for them to be there. This is also essentially Shev Yaakov's own explanation, and it is correct.
