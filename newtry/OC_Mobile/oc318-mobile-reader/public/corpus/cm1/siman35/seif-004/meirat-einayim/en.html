He is believed to say, 'This is my father's handwriting,' etc. The Gemara says, and the Tur cites, that this applies only when there is another witness with him who saw the father's handwriting while already an adult. Then the Sages believed the one who was a minor at the time and later became an adult to combine with him, even if he never saw the handwriting of his father, brother, or teacher after reaching adulthood, as where they died while he was still a minor. The adult witness too may be related to the witnesses signed on the document, provided the witnesses authenticating the signatures are not related to each other. This is so only where his father or the others signed as witnesses on the document, because by Torah law their testimony in the document is regarded as though it had already been examined in beit din, and the Sages required authentication only rabbinically. In that setting they believed even one who had known the handwriting as a minor, even to extract money. But if his father or brother are themselves the obligors who undertook in their own handwriting to pay someone a sum of money or another obligation, the son certainly cannot authenticate his father's or brother's signature. This is not only where he knew the signature as a minor and later matured. Even if he says that after adulthood he saw this very signature of his father, he is not believed, because that falls under 'fathers shall not be put to death through the testimony of sons,' as explained in siman 33. Since the signature is otherwise unrecognized, it is as though he is giving oral testimony against him and the handwriting itself has no standing.
And some say, etc. The Mechaber continues, 'but not regarding others.' It is surprising that he phrases this as 'some say,' for I found no authority who disagrees. The Mishnah and Gemara in chapter 2 of Ketubot plainly imply that he is believed specifically regarding these persons, because he commonly has access to them, and the Gemara explains why each case is necessary. One might perhaps suggest that an opposing authority understands the Gemara's necessity differently: had it taught only his father, one would have thought he is believed only because he is constantly with him, but not regarding a brother or teacher, with whom he is less familiar. The Mishnah therefore teaches that he is believed also regarding his brother and teacher. Once it teaches that he is believed regarding them even though he is less constantly with them, perhaps the same should apply to other people's signatures that he recognized in childhood and testifies to after adulthood. The Mishnah simply mentioned the ordinary cases because presumably those are the signatures he was likely to know. More likely, however, is what I wrote above in siman 16, seif 2, that even where the Mechaber writes 'some say' there need not be any actual dispute.
