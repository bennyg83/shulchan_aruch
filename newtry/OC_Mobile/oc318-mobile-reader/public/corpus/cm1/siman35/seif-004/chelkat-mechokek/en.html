And this is not called an admission, etc. The plain wording implies that he must admit by way of a complete admission, meaning that he says before witnesses, "It is true," or, "You are witnesses against me that I appointed so-and-so as my agent to betroth a woman for me." But if he merely said it conversationally, he may later deny the agency and the woman is not betrothed. I do not fully understand this. In monetary matters one can claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, or, if sued, that he was jesting. Those claims do not ordinarily apply to a matter of forbidden relations. It is possible, however, that an explanatory pretext applies here as well. For example, if one person says, "I intend to betroth a certain woman," and the other answers, "I already sent an agent to betroth her for me," he may later retract and explain that he said this only to prevent the other from preceding him. Therefore, the statement must be made in the manner of a true admission. One who studies the Rivash's responsum will see that he speaks there about an agent appointed to receive kiddushin for the woman and writes that even the principal's admission does not suffice without witnesses to the agency. He adds that in that case there was not even an admission, since the principal merely related events and did not say, "You are my witnesses," which is required for a monetary admission. In my view, however, concerning an agent to deliver kiddushin for the man, there is no need for the words, "You are witnesses." It is enough that he not speak jokingly or with some other pretext, as explained.
