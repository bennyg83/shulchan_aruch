Regarding the handwriting of these people, etc. The reason is that a child is ordinarily familiar with the handwriting of these close family members. I have a doubt where the person does not testify that he recognizes his brother's signature by visual familiarity, but only says, "He signed this in my presence." He cannot otherwise identify the handwriting. If we say he is not believed, the distinction is understandable: where he says he knows his brother's handwriting because he is very familiar with it, we do not fear error. But where he merely says he saw the act of signing and has no independent signature familiarity, perhaps his childhood recollection is unreliable. Yet this creates a question regarding Rosh's limitation to relatives. Rosh proves from the Yerushalmi, which says one may testify, "This is my father's handwriting," concerning matters with which he is familiar. Perhaps the Yerushalmi means only that he may identify a signature through longstanding visual familiarity, not that relatives are uniquely valid. If, however, the Mishnah's general wording includes even a child who says only, "He signed in my presence," then the opposite seems true. That kind of testimony does not depend on familiarity with handwriting at all. He simply testifies to something he saw. Why should there be any difference between a brother and any other person? Logic suggests there should be no difference in such a case, though I have not found the point explicit in the authorities and remain hesitant. Sema asks why Mechaber presents the rule as "some say." Shach answers that Bedek HaBayit records it but that the silence of other poskim does not support it. There is no need for that inference. Raah and Ritva in Ketubot state explicitly that the same rule applies to others as well, and that the Mishnah merely mentions close relatives because that is the ordinary case of familiarity. This resolves the wording.
