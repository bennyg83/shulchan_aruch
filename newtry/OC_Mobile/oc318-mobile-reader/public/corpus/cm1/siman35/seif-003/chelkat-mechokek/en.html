She is doubtfully betrothed. At first glance this ruling is surprising. Why are the witnesses believed sufficiently to require a get even out of doubt? They are as personally interested as possible, since she claims the money never reached her hand and they must then return the money or take a shevuat heset. What difference does it make that by Torah law they would be exempt from an oath if nowadays they are in fact compelled to swear? The Torah itself disqualifies testimony of one who must swear concerning his testimony. One can answer that if the woman were to admit that the witnesses spoke truthfully and that they gave her the money as kiddushin, she would certainly be betrothed. The deficiency is only from our perspective because we do not know who is telling the truth. Since we therefore impose an oath, the matter never leaves the category of doubtful kiddushin. Another possibility is that a witness with a financial interest is disqualified in such a case only rabbinically. Or perhaps there is not complete personal interest here at all: if the witnesses say they never received the kiddushin money from the man or that they returned it to him, they would have to swear against him; now they must swear against her. Either way they must swear. The only issue would then be the principle that any witness who must swear is not a witness, and that defect may itself be only rabbinic. If there was only one agent, together with one independent witness who accompanied him and testifies that he carried out his agency and delivered the money to the woman, it might seem that the kiddushin are definite because the agent has a supporting witness and is therefore not personally interested. This resembles a case where the principal initially exempted the witnesses from an oath. Nevertheless, this conclusion appears correct only according to Rambam and those following his approach, who hold that when the woman admits receiving kiddushin and there is no oath issue, the kiddushin can be definite like kiddushin by document. According to Ran and those following him, even her admission would not make the kiddushin definite because we require independent testimony. So too here, if the supporting witness contradicted the agent and agreed with the woman, the agent's testimony would be worthless, since he would have to return the money or take a Torah oath. Thus only the supporting witness provides independent testimony, leaving only one witness to kiddushin. Moreover, Scripture requires two witnesses, not one whose testimony gives additional legal force to the other. Here the supporting witness also rehabilitates the second witness, resembling the principle that one quarter of the money should not be extracted on the mouth of a single witness.
