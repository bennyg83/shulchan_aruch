After he converted. The Shach discusses testimony remembered from before conversion and whether the defect of beginning in disqualification applies when the witness had it in his own power to convert. Mishneh LaMelech cites Maharit as uncertain whether a wicked person who repents resembles a minor who matures or a gentile who converts. Thus for a sinner who repents there are two possible doubts: perhaps he can even testify regarding Torah matters because repentance was in his power, or perhaps he cannot be trusted even in rabbinic matters because while wicked he did not pay careful attention. Regarding one disqualified only rabbinically for misconduct, there is reason to say that beginning in disqualification is not required at all. Rabbinic disqualification for sin generally requires financial motive. Such a person is not bodily unfit as a witness. His defect is suspicion arising from monetary desire, like an interested witness. An interested witness can testify after removing his interest even though he was interested when he saw the event. Thus a rabbinically disqualified witness based merely on financial temptation should similarly be able to testify after the defect is removed. Yet Rosh in Gittin implies that a wicked person who repents does share the ordinary rule of beginning in disqualification. He discusses witnesses who were disqualified when appointed and repented before delivering a get, and compares them to one whose eyes were open and later became blind. This suggests that for Torah matters a sinner who repents is treated like a bodily disqualification, not like an interested witness, and we do not rely merely on the fact that repentance was within his power. The Tosefta in Sanhedrin is even more explicit: one who became a Sabbatical produce dealer and later repented is included in the rule that testimony requires both beginning and end in qualification. That appears to extend the rule even to rabbinic misconduct. Even HaEzer 42 rules that forced converts do not become disqualified merely because they failed the mitzvah of sanctifying Hashem's Name. This does not contradict Ketubot, where Rabbi Meir says witnesses who claim they signed falsely under threat make themselves wicked because they should have allowed themselves to be killed rather than sign falsely. Even where an act does not formally create testimonial disqualification, a person ordinarily cannot make himself into one who admits wicked conduct. The same is true of witnesses who say their document was merely a fictitious accommodation: the act is improper, though it need not always create full testimonial disqualification. Thus the forced convert can remain formally qualified while a person's own confession of religious failure may still face the rule that one does not establish himself as wicked through his own mouth.
