After thirteen complete years. Shach cites Lechem Mishneh's doubt concerning one born in the middle of Rosh Hashanah: does he become thirteen at the beginning of the night of his thirteenth birthday, or only when the same hour of the day arrives? This is the same question discussed by Tosafot. Rabbenu Elchanan holds that hours are counted for legal majority, just as hours are counted for sacrificial age. Shach decides like Bach that hours are not counted. Magen Avraham and Taz likewise rule so in Orach Chaim. I have written at length elsewhere in Even HaEzer and found strong support for this conclusion. Rashi in Yevamot 34a explains a case in which several prohibitions take effect simultaneously when boys and girls reach majority. He says the males were born on the first of Tishrei and the females on the first of Tishrei the following year, so both sets reach majority together. But exact birth moments cannot ordinarily be synchronized. Rashi must therefore hold that legal majority follows the day, not the precise hour. Whether one was born at the beginning or end of Rosh Hashanah, all become adults when the relevant birthday begins. This is a powerful proof. Tosafot's version there is different. They explain the case through betrothal by agent and do not require all parties to reach majority at the identical moment. That was Rabbenu Elchanan's text, so his hourly view remains possible within Tosafot. Still, another proof comes from Niddah 49a. Tosafot, citing Rabbenu Tam, explain the dispute whether signs appearing "within the period" count as before or after the period as referring to the day after twelve complete years for a girl and thirteen for a boy. According to R. Yehudah, signs from the beginning of that day can count; according to R. Shimon, only from the end. Rosh's wording is different. He says the dispute concerns the final day of the twelfth year for a girl and the thirteenth for a boy. Maadanei Melech wished to emend Rosh so that he would match Tosafot, but that cannot be correct. Tur and Rama in Even HaEzer siman 155 use the same wording as Rosh, and Kesef Mishneh expressly notes a disagreement between Tosafot and Rosh. The difference becomes understandable if Tosafot follow Rabbenu Elchanan and count hours. Suppose a boy was born late in the day. At the beginning of the calendar day following thirteen full years, his precise birth-hour has not yet arrived. He is not yet fully adult, but he will certainly become adult later that same day. That day can therefore be called "within the period." R. Yehudah can hold that signs appearing from the beginning of that day count because the day itself is the day of majority and signs can precede the exact hour slightly. R. Shimon treats them as premature until the birth-hour arrives. But Rosh, Tur, and Rama do not count hours. For them, once thirteen complete years have ended and the next day begins, the boy is already fully adult. There is no reason to call that new day "within the period." They must therefore place the dispute on the final day of minority itself, the day at whose end the thirteen years become complete. R. Yehudah holds that signs can appear on that final day and count because majority is imminent. R. Shimon holds that until the period fully ends, the signs are treated as immature. This difference in wording is therefore strong evidence that Rosh, Tur, and Rama reject Rabbenu Elchanan and do not count hours. One may ask from Niddah 48b, where the Gemara asks why the Tanna who listed age rules for valuations did not also list the age rules in Perek Yotzei Dofen. Perhaps the two categories differ because valuations count precise hours while the other laws do not. The answer is that the same list includes six years for a Hebrew slave. There, hours clearly are not counted. If a slave was sold at midday, we have never heard that he works six full years plus another half-day. Scripture says, "in the seventh he shall go free." Thus the Tanna's age periods are not generally hour-based. Accordingly, the practical ruling of Bach, Shach, Magen Avraham, Taz, Rosh, Tur, and Rama is correct: legal majority begins with the day, without waiting for the precise hour of birth.
Presumed to be an adult, etc. It appears that if he has fathered a child by his wife, he should be presumed an adult, because we rule that a minor does not father children. Rambam says so in Hilchot Gezelah and in Hilchot Mamrim, where the period of ben sorer u'moreh begins when he is capable of fathering, since a minor cannot father. Kesef Mishneh likewise discusses this in the laws of chalitzah. It is somewhat surprising that Rambam, in Hilchot Ishut, states that a girl who gives birth has signs of majority but does not similarly state that a boy who fathers a child is adult. Perhaps for prohibitory matters such as kiddushin this is not an absolute sign. Who says the child is certainly his? Perhaps his wife conceived from another man. Rambam does say that Torah holds a man liable for striking his father without worrying that perhaps he is not really the father, because we follow a Torah presumption and the principle that most marital relations are with the husband. But here the very issue is whether the husband is a minor. If he is a minor, his intercourse is not capable of conception, so the ordinary majority that conception follows the husband cannot be invoked in the same way. Also, the wife's presumption of sexual propriety is weaker in such a case, because if he is truly a minor the kiddushin may not create a full prohibition of adultery. Only unusual cases, such as a minor yavam who acquires his yevamah, would create a full married status, and those are uncommon. The better explanation is therefore that for severe prohibitory matters Rambam did not treat fathering a child as an absolute sign because paternity itself is not certain enough. But for civil testimony, where the standard is monetary rather than a severe prohibition, the birth of a child attributed to his wife is certainly sufficient to presume he has reached majority. Accordingly, if he has a child, he is treated as an adult for testimony.
