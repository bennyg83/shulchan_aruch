Seif 1. With the presumption that he is an adult. The Urim VeTumim notes that Rambam does not mention children as equivalent to physical signs of majority in a male, and explains that the usual rule that most marital relations are with the husband cannot be invoked when the very issue is whether the alleged husband was halachically capable of marriage. He also questions use of the woman's presumption of fitness, because if the male was a minor the kiddushin might not have taken effect. It appears, however, that a presumption of fitness can still operate. Even if one assumed with certainty that kiddushin of a minor carries no rabbinic concern, there remains a Torah doubt whether he actually developed signs, supported by the presumption of Rava that ordinary youths develop signs at the normal age. Even if examined later and found without hair, there may be a Torah doubt that the hairs fell out. Since Torah doubts are treated stringently, the woman's presumption of chastity supports the conclusion that she did not live illicitly. A presumption of compliance even with rabbinic prohibitions likewise remains a genuine presumption. The Gemara's need for the rule that most relations are with the husband does not prove otherwise. Before the law that we follow a majority was established, one could not simply rely on a presumption against a competing majority. Once majority and presumption are accepted legal tools, the woman's presumption of fitness can support attribution of the children to the husband. After the family status is established, we rule that legal presumptions are strong enough even for capital consequences. The Torah's discussion of one who strikes his father also supports this. If parentage required proof by physical signs in every case, liability would be extraordinarily rare. We do not require proof that the father had been physically examined before conception or had already passed most of his lifespan. The ordinary halachic practice is likewise to rely on a man's recognized children as evidence of his adulthood for testimony, agency in gittin, and eligibility in chalitzah, even when nobody has inspected his physical signs. The phrase "most relations are with the husband" itself may ultimately rest upon the general presumption that most Jewish women act with modesty and fidelity. Even where immorality exists in the world, the majority of Jewish women are chaste. Thus the child's attribution to the husband is a combination of majority and moral presumption. A wife's public treatment of a man as her husband likewise draws strength from her presumption that she does not violate prohibitions such as seclusion or illicit relations. It is possible that a social presumption of family identity has independent force as well, but this requires further analysis. For monetary law, where ordinary statistical majority alone generally does not extract money, recognized children are nevertheless treated as children for inheritance. The presumption of fitness may be stronger than ordinary majority, and once the familial status has already been established in practice it can then operate even in monetary matters. Perhaps the rule that legal presumptions can support even capital punishment itself rests on the broader majority that people generally do not brazenly establish false family identities. The matter requires further study.
