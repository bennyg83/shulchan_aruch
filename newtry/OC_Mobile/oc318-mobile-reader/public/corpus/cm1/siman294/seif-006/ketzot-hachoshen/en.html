The deposit was stolen through an unavoidable accident and afterward the thief became known. At the end of Perek HaGozel Kamma, Rabbah Zuti asks: if a deposit was stolen unavoidably, then the thief returned it to the custodian's premises, and it later died through the custodian's negligence, is the original custodial relationship considered ended by the first unavoidable theft, or does it revive upon return? Rambam cites the unresolved question and therefore exempts the custodian from payment unless the owner seized. It is surprising that Rif, Rosh, Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch omit it. The omission can be explained through the dispute when a custodian's liability begins. One Talmudic formulation treats the liability as arising from meshichah. On that view, an exempting unavoidable accident can resemble a completed return and may terminate the custodial relationship. Rabbah Zuti's question is then meaningful. But according to the accepted view of Rif, Rosh, and most decisors, actual payment liability arises only when the negligent or otherwise obligating event occurs. An unavoidable accident is not a legal return. It simply does not create liability. Therefore, if the item later returns to the custodian's premises before actual restoration to the owner, the original custody has not ended, and later negligence obligates him. There is an additional proof from Rava's ruling that once the thief becomes known, even an unpaid custodian may be required to litigate with the thief for recovery of the deposit. If the first unavoidable theft had fully ended the custodial relationship, why must the custodian continue to act for the owner? The obligation to pursue the known thief shows that the custody did not terminate merely through the accident. Thus Rabbah Zuti's doubt fits the view that liability is fixed from the initial meshichah and an exempting accident can function like return. Rif, Rosh, and Tur, who follow the view that liability arises only with negligence or another obligating event, need not preserve the doubt. Under their view, once the object returns and is later lost through negligence, the custodian is liable.
He paid and did not wish to swear. Bach argues that an unpaid custodian who merely admits negligence does not acquire the right to the thief's double payment unless he also says, "I will pay." He cites the baraita of a renter whose cow was stolen and who said, "I will pay and not swear." That proof is not compelling because the baraita's claim of theft is interpreted in the Gemara as a plea that could have exempted the renter, such as armed robbery or according to a view that the renter has the law of an unpaid custodian. When the custodian advances an exempting plea, he must expressly choose payment in place of the oath before the owner transfers him the future double payment. Rav Pappa's statement that an unpaid custodian who says, "I was negligent," acquires the double payment teaches precisely that an admission of an obligating plea can itself be enough even without the additional words, "I will pay." The very admission creates an immediate monetary obligation. The Mishnah's wording, "he paid and did not wish to swear," does not disprove this. Tosafot explains that the wording is needed for special cases, such as where the custodian had previously sworn and only later paid, in order to distinguish Abaye and Rava concerning whether the double payment is then transferred. Rivan's reading of the Mishnah, where "they were stolen" itself means negligently and "he did not wish to swear" means he refused to swear falsely, can also be reconciled. The decisive proof from the separate baraita remains that when an exempting plea was raised, the express statement "I will pay" is necessary. Where the custodian admits an obligating fact such as negligence, Rav Pappa's rule can transfer the double payment immediately through that admission.
