Where witnesses are readily available, he must bring proof through witnesses. Isi ben Yehudah derives that when the event occurred in a place where there were observers, the custodian must bring witnesses and thereby be exempt. A difficulty arises from migo. If the custodian could have denied ever receiving the deposit or could have claimed that he returned it, why is he compelled to pay unless he brings witnesses to the accident? Migo ordinarily works to retain money, even where the alternative plea would be bolder. The usual explanations for the custodial oath do not fully answer this. Rambam says migo does not eliminate an imposed oath; Rosh says the alternative plea of total denial is bold. But here, where witnesses were available, the law seems to impose payment unless witnesses are brought, not merely an oath. Why should the migo not retain the money? Maharit indeed rules that even where an accident allegedly occurred in a place where people were present, the custodian can sometimes be believed through a migo that he could have claimed the accident occurred in a less observable place. Mishneh LaMelech likewise discusses circumstances in which migo remains available. Baal HaMaor writes that a migo is not accepted where the claimed fact was easy to prove by witnesses. On that approach Isi's rule is understandable: because witnesses were readily available, failure to produce them defeats the migo. But Ramban in Milchamot strongly disputes Baal HaMaor and maintains that migo can operate even when proof would have been easy to bring. If Ramban is correct, the original difficulty returns. Isi's unconditional rule seems to leave no room for the custodian's migo of denial or return. It is difficult to establish the entire passage as a deposit documented by instrument, since both the Gemara and the decisors state the rule generally. The matter therefore remains unresolved.
If he does not bring witnesses for his account, he must pay. Even according to Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch, who treat the oath that the item is not in the custodian's possession as the principal oath, where the claimed accident occurred in a place with observers the custodian must bring witnesses to the accident itself. It is not enough to bring witnesses merely that the object was found broken or dead. The cases at the end of Perek HaOmanin show that he must establish that the loss occurred without negligence. Rava told custodians to bring proof that they had not been negligent, and Rambam writes that a custodian claiming a major unavoidable accident in a place where witnesses are available must bring proof of the claim and is thereby exempt from the custodial oath. Thus the phrase "the custodial oath" in this context is not limited to one verbal formula. It refers to the custodian's substantive defense. Where witnesses were available, the custodian must prove the circumstances that establish the exempting accident, even if the principal issue that the item is no longer in his possession is already known. This is true even according to Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia, who limits Isi's special rule to the custodial oath and not to an ordinary plea that a deposit was returned. The requirement concerns the substantive claim that the loss occurred through an exempting custodial event. See also what I wrote in siman 187.
And by gilgul they require him to swear that he was not negligent. This formulation follows the view that the principal custodial oath is that the object is no longer in his possession, while the oaths that he was not negligent and did not misappropriate it arise through gilgul. Accordingly, where the fact that the object is gone is independently established, there is ordinarily no separate Torah oath merely that he was not negligent. The Mishnah concerning one carrying a barrel, where an unpaid custodian swears that he was not negligent, can be understood where the breaking itself was not independently witnessed and the custodian's entire account remains to be established. Similarly, the Shulchan Aruch's statement that one whose object broke in a place without witnesses swears that he did not break it through negligence concerns a case where the broken object itself was not before beit din in a way that removed the principal uncertainty. Otherwise the negligence oath would not independently remain under this approach. Rambam writes that where the custodian claims an accident in a place without witnesses, he swears to the accident and is exempt, but if he brings witnesses that he was not negligent he is exempt even from the oath. His wording mirrors the earlier rule for a place with witnesses and should not be taken to prove that lack of negligence is always an independent principal oath. Maharshal and others read Rambam differently, but the formulation of Maggid Mishneh and the Shulchan Aruch supports the gilgul structure.
