(There is not even an oath of inducement.) These words of Ramah cited by the Tur require great study. Since he compares a doubtful claim to a definite claim regarding whether the garment before us is the deposited garment, why does Ramah formulate the case of an unavoidable accident as one in which the custodian is believed? This implies that he is exempt specifically when the custodian makes the definite claim that the item destroyed by accident belonged to the depositor. Yet even if neither party makes a definite claim, but the custodian says, "Perhaps this is the deposited garment," while the depositor says, "Perhaps it is not," the depositor certainly cannot extract another garment, since he too is uncertain. Accordingly, when both parties are uncertain which item was destroyed, the custodian should likewise be exempt. I further find it difficult that the two cases are not truly comparable. Where the garment is before us and the depositor is uncertain, there is no basis at all to extract another garment from the custodian, because the depositor does not know what he seeks to extract. In the case of an unavoidable accident, however, the depositor can argue: "Perhaps the item destroyed was yours and mine remains with you. Go and pay me, for you must return what I gave you, and if you seek exemption bring proof." One may further distinguish that when the garment is before us and the claim is uncertain, no oath is imposed upon an uncertain claim. In a case of unavoidable accident, by contrast, an oath is imposed even upon a doubtful claim, as all custodians' oaths respond to uncertainty. Here too, the custodian should swear in response to the depositor's uncertainty that the depositor's item was the one destroyed. Ramah's words require extensive study, for they are exceedingly difficult.
