See Shach, seif katan 6. From the Shach in siman 292 concerning specifically identified property it appears that the rule in siman 294, seif 4, is not limited to proof by witnesses. The same result follows where the custodian swears that an equivalent item of the same value was lost. A similar principle may apply from time to time between partners. If one partner failed to guard properly while the other did not know of the danger of spoilage, or the negligent partner claimed that the endangered goods were his own share and purported to divide them without the other's knowledge, and only later it became clear in beit din that everything was jointly owned, further spoilage after the other partner learned of the facts may present a doubt. For example, if grain at the bottom of a storehouse spoiled and it is unknown whether the damage occurred before or after the second partner learned of the problem, the second partner may say, 'I do not know whether I ever became liable to guard against this damage,' because perhaps the spoilage already occurred before his duty arose. Even though a presumption may operate against a claim of 'I do not know whether I repaid,' it does not necessarily overcome possession here. Although both partners are uncertain, and with custodial oaths the claimant's uncertainty can sometimes be treated as sufficient to require an oath, the analogy to siman 294, seif 4, may mean that no Torah oath applies, and consequently perhaps no hesset oath applies either.
