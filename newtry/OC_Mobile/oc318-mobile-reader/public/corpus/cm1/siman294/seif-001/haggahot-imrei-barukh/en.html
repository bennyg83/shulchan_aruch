(Shach, seif katan 6) And this resolves the Bach's question against Rambam. Note: This requires examination. Perhaps where the borrower claims that his pledge was worth three dinarim, while according to the lender it was worth only a shekel, the borrower can say: "According to your own words I have a certain claim against you that the garment burned before witnesses was not mine. My garment was worth three dinarim, while you say the garment that burned was worth only a shekel." The Ramah's case, however, is where the depositor himself is uncertain, as explained in the Tur. A further difficulty with the Ramah is that otherwise every guardian who sets his eyes on a deposit could fraudulently arrange for a similar object to be lost before witnesses. He would not care about the loss of that substitute object, because he would keep the deposited object he desires. It is therefore advantageous to him to sacrifice the lesser object. The Ramah's words indicate that he stated his rule only where it is certainly known that the object was lost through an unavoidable accident and not through negligence caused by the guardian. In such a case there is no basis for suspicion. This can answer the Bach's question: Rava's answer that there are witnesses that the garment burned must, according to authorities who hold a lender on a pledge is not liable for unavoidable accidents, concern negligence. Since there was negligence, one can suspect that because the lender desired the pledge he caused the burning of his own low-value garment so that he could retain the valuable pledge. Rambam writes in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, in the mishnah concerning one who lends on a pledge, that even if the lender says to the owner, "I value it more than you claim it was worth," we still consider the possibility that he set his eyes upon it.
