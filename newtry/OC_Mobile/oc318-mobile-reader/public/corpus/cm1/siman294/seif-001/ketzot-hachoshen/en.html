He demanded the deposit and the custodian denied it. A responsum attributed to Rosh says that one who delays dividing or returning another's money after repeated demand can become liable even for unavoidable accidents, because withholding another person's property against his will is oppression. I was initially surprised. I found no general rule that mere delay in returning a deposit turns a custodian into a robber liable for unavoidable accident. Rashbatz does state something similar concerning gold held by an artisan. If the owner merely presses the artisan to complete the ring and the artisan delays because of ordinary workload, he remains a paid artisan and is exempt from unavoidable accident. But if the owner says, "You have delayed my work enough; return my gold," and the artisan refuses, Rashbatz treats him like a custodian who wrongfully withholds a deposit and makes him liable when the loss resulted from that wrongful detention. The general decisors, however, do not appear to adopt such a broad rule. Tur and Shulchan Aruch discuss a creditor who, after the debtor paid, was told to return the pledge and said, "Go now and come tomorrow." If the pledge was stolen before the promised time, the creditor is liable only for theft and loss as a paid custodian, not for unavoidable accidents, even though he was withholding property after demand. This implies that only actual denial of the deposit, or a genuine act of misappropriation, turns the custodian into a robber liable for all accidents. Mere delay or refusal to return promptly, though wrongful, does not automatically impose the law of a robber. This appears to be the better rule.
