He brought witnesses that a cloak was lost through accident. Tur cites Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia that if witnesses saw a cloak of the same kind lost through accident but did not know whether it was the depositor's cloak, the custodian can be exempt from the Torah custodial oath. Bach challenged this from Perek HaMafkid. The Gemara asks, under Rav Huna's rule requiring an oath that the item is not in the custodian's possession, how the allegedly lost object could later appear. Rava answers that witnesses had seen it burned. Bach asks why the Gemara did not simply say witnesses saw a similar garment burn but could not identify it. The answer is that Rav Huna's oath is a separate rabbinic oath instituted because perhaps the custodian set his eyes upon the deposited object. As long as witnesses did not establish that this very object was destroyed, that concern remains. A similar cloak burning does not remove the suspicion that the custodian kept the deposit. Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia is discussing the Torah custodial oath, where the question is whether an accident occurred. Once the accident itself is objectively established and the remaining uncertainty is only whether the lost cloak was this particular cloak, that is no longer the special Torah oath of custodians. The custodian may be exempt from that oath even though another rabbinic oath or evidentiary issue could remain. Thus the Gemara concerning Rav Huna and Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia's rule address different oaths.
Because if the cloak were before him and the depositor said, "This is not mine." Bach misunderstood this phrase as a migo. He therefore asked how Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia could use migo to eliminate the custodial oath, especially when the alternative plea is bolder. That is not the point. The reasoning is substantive. If an actual cloak were before beit din and the custodian said, "This is the deposited cloak," while the owner said, "It is not mine," the dispute would be one of identification, not the Torah custodial oath concerning loss through negligence or unavoidable accident. So too, where witnesses saw a cloak of this description destroyed but cannot identify whether it was the deposited cloak, the remaining uncertainty is only identity. The accident itself is known. The Torah's special custodial oath was not imposed for that kind of identification dispute. Therefore the phrase, "if the cloak were before him," is an analogy defining the legal category of the dispute, not a migo. Bach's objection consequently falls away.
