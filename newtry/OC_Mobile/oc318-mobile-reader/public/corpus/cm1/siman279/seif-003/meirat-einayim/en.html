Then he retracted and said, "He is my slave," etc. The Tur writes that he is not believed in the later statement that the person is his slave. If the person were truly his slave, he would not first have called him his son, even if the person serves him like a son. The Tur concludes that this applies specifically after the period of immediate speech. If he says "my son" and within immediate speech says "my slave," he is believed, because throughout the Torah immediate speech is treated as continuous speech, except in divorce, idolatry, betrothal, and blasphemy. In the Perishah I wrote that this case, and the later case at the tax station where he can say "he is my slave," must concern one whom he describes as his son born from a maidservant, so that he is both his biological son and legally a slave. Otherwise, if he simply says that a person who is not his son is a slave, he is not believed to create slave status, because the Torah gives him special credibility only concerning his own child.
Even though he serves him like a slave, etc. This is also the Rambam's wording. It means: Even if he serves him like a slave, so that one might have assumed he is certainly a slave and not a son, the father's later statement that he is his son is accepted. The Tur's version is different. He says that if the father first called him "my slave" and later "my son," he is believed, because calling him a slave may have meant only that he serves him like a slave. The father is believed even if the person does not actually perform slave like service. It appears that the Rambam and Mechaber agree. Their concluding rationale, that the person is serviceable and attentive to all his needs like a slave, explains why the initial expression "my slave" can be nonliteral.
