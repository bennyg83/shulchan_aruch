And he fathered a son from her. Rashba, Responsa 610, writes concerning a man who had illicit relations with an unmarried woman, sent her from his house, later brought her back, and declared her child to be his son. Rashba ruled that he is believed and we do not say he spoke from mere supposition. The Rosh, Responsa klal 87, siman 12, writes that this applies specifically in a case where she served him and was regularly with him, so we attribute the child to him. Darkei Moshe cites both responsa.
If he is a Torah scholar. Meaning, if the man who fathered a son from the maidservant is a Torah scholar, we preserve his presumption of proper conduct and say that he surely did not violate the prohibition against prostitution. We assume he freed her and married her, rather than having illicit relations. Therefore the son inherits him. Nevertheless, we do not rely on this presumption strongly enough to permit the son to marry an ordinary Jewish woman without clarification.
She performs chalitzah but not yibbum. In questions of prohibition we act stringently because of the doubt. Likewise, the son may not marry a Jewish woman without a bill of emancipation, and if he betrothed a woman without such a document she requires a get from him. So writes the Tur.
For in monetary law the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Meaning, he does not inherit, because the money is in the possession of the other heirs. Nevertheless, his brothers, and all the more so other heirs, cannot sell him as a slave, because he himself possesses his own person and one who seeks to remove that possession bears the burden of proof. Rama states two "some say" opinions here. The first apparently holds that only in prohibitory law do we rule stringently, so she performs chalitzah and not yibbum, whereas in monetary law the son is treated leniently as a son and inherits. The second holds that even for money he is not certainly a son. The Tur, however, writes that the opinion requiring chalitzah and forbidding yibbum likewise treats money stringently: he does not inherit, though they also cannot sell him. It is difficult to say that Rama's second view follows those authorities cited by Rambam who hold that he does not inherit but his father's wife may undergo yibbum, unlike the first view, because Rama then should not have concluded "and so it appears to me." Rambam, the Rosh, and Rav Natronai do not follow that position. This requires study.
