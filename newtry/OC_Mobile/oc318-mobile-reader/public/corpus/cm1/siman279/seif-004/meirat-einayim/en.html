He was passing a tax station and said, "This is my son," then later said, "He is my slave," he is believed. Even according to Rabbi Yehudah, Scripture gives the father special credibility only concerning his son, through "he shall recognize," enabling him to say about one established as his son that he is not his son, or about a younger son that he is firstborn, thereby automatically disqualifying older sons. We do not find that he has credibility regarding an unrelated person to declare him a slave or illegitimate. Here, after passing the tax station he retracts from "he is my son" and says, "he is not my son but my slave," which would ordinarily resemble declaring an unrelated person a slave. We must therefore say that the case is specifically where the person has no established presumption of free Jewish status. Even though he grew up in this man's house, so long as it was never known whether he was his son or slave, the man is believed to say that he is not his son but his slave. But if his mother had been established as a Jewish woman, etc. The Tur cites the Ramah: Scripture believed a father regarding his own son, for example when he says, "This son of mine is illegitimate," where it is possible that the child was born from a forbidden union, or where it is known that the wife bore the child and he says, "He is not my son," which automatically makes the child illegitimate. But he is not believed regarding unrelated people. Therefore, once the mother is established as a Jewish woman, he cannot declare her a maidservant and thereby make the child a slave. The meaning is as follows. Where it is not known who gave birth to the child, the father may say concerning his son that he is illegitimate, even if until now people assumed he was born from the father's wife. Since the Torah believed the father, we do not follow that prior assumption and can say the child was born from a prohibited union. But if it is known that the wife gave birth to him, and the father admits the child is his, he cannot say simply, "He is my son and he is illegitimate," unless he also says that the mother, his wife, is herself illegitimate so that the child follows the defective status. The Torah did not give him credibility to make the mother, who has a presumption of fitness, into an illegitimate woman, just as he cannot make her a maidservant. Nor can we interpret his statement as meaning that he himself is illegitimate and therefore the child is illegitimate, because the father has a presumption of fitness and a person does not make himself wicked or remove himself from his own established status through such a statement. The only way he can disqualify a child known to have been born from his wife is by saying, "This child whom my wife bore is not mine; she did not conceive from me," so that the child becomes illegitimate automatically. Although the wife herself has a presumption of fitness, the Torah's special credibility regarding his son indirectly casts suspicion on her. That does not apply when he says the child is a slave and the mother is known to be Jewish. He cannot make her a maidservant. Nor can one say that she was Jewish but conceived from a slave, because the law is that the child of a slave or gentile man and a Jewish woman is kosher and is not a slave. Therefore his claim necessarily requires saying that the mother was a maidservant, and he lacks credibility to do so against her established Jewish status.
