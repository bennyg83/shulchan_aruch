Who was established as his son. Responsa Chavot Yair 231 relates a remarkable case. An unmarried maidservant in a wealthy man's home became pregnant and accused the master, who denied it. The matter reached the local duke. She demanded monetary satisfaction, and through bribes the master obtained a delay until she would state the truth at childbirth, but she repeated her accusation. He was fined heavily, though he secured an order banishing her and her child permanently from the region. The communal leaders also wished to disgrace and fine him. He pleaded tearfully and swore a severe oath that he had never had relations with her, and they treated him leniently. The woman named her son after the wealthy man's father, and the boy grew up publicly bearing the wealthy man's name in signatures, Torah calls, and communal records. He later traveled elsewhere and was taken into the home of a childless nobleman, who raised him and arranged his marriage. After the nobleman's wife died, the nobleman married the boy's mother, saying she was a capable household mistress, and she bore him two sons. Near the end of his life the nobleman practiced severe self-affliction. Before his death he confessed that the stepson, as the public thought of him, was in fact his own biological son. At the time of conception he had been a neighbor of the wealthy man and had impregnated the maidservant. He had given her a formal pledge to marry her on condition that she accuse her employer, hoping the employer would be forced to pay a large sum. When the wealthy man paid nothing, he violated his pledge. His self-affliction was for disgracing the wealthy man, relations with a niddah, and violation of that undertaking. This was why he had raised the boy and later married the mother. The mother also stated that no man other than the nobleman had ever been with her. The son became very wealthy and died without surviving descendants. Two sets of supposed brothers then claimed the estate: the sons of the wealthy man who had denied paternity, and the sons of the nobleman who had confessed. Chavot Yair rules that the son was correct not to seek inheritance from the wealthy man. A person is believed to say even of one established as his son, "he is not my son." The name used for the child, his Torah calls, and his signatures prove nothing, because all derived from the mother's assertion, and she is not believed to establish paternity. Even if the wealthy man certainly had relations with her and she conceived, the child would not inherit from him if he remained silent or said he did not know whether the child was his, because a woman who was available to him may likewise have been available to others. By contrast, a man who expressly says, "this is my son," is believed for inheritance, even though biological certainty may be impossible. Therefore the nobleman's acknowledgment was effective, and had the son come to inherit together with the nobleman's two other sons he would plainly have shared with them. After the son's death, Chavot Yair says the wealthy man's sons should be silenced with rebuke. Besides lacking a legal claim, they would disgrace their father in the grave by portraying him as an adulterer and oath violator. The nobleman's sons, by contrast, initially appear to inherit on the strength of their father's acknowledgment. Although after their father's death they denied it and argued that even he could not know whether the child was biologically his, once the law recognizes the father's statement they may later say that the deceased was their brother and claim his estate. Nevertheless, Chavot Yair concludes that if another person seized the estate first, that person would retain it, comparable to one who takes possession of the estate of a shetuki or foundling. He also cites another extraordinary case, Responsa Chavot Yair 32, where a kohen on his deathbed declared that one of the sons he had raised was not his son but a Muslim child who had been exchanged. Chavot Yair ruled that the father was not believed for any purpose, whether prohibition or inheritance, after having always maintained him as his son, supported him, married him off, and treated him publicly as a kohen. Although common sense might find such a deathbed confession difficult to dismiss, we do not decide against the Sages' established rules by conjecture, even in monetary law and certainly not in matters of prohibition.
