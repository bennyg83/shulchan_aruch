And this is my brother, etc. See Beer HaGolah. This follows Rashbam, Tosafos, and Maharam there, who explain the Gemara's question "is this not obvious?" because the matter had already been raised as a question, etc. As stated there 127b, "for what practical law, etc." Regarding property that came afterward, or where he is dying, he is not believed. The Rosh challenges them and explains that he is always believed. The Gemara's question "obvious" refers to the Mishnah in the chapter HaOmer, "I have sons, etc.," which the Gemara cites below, from which it is clear that at least for inheritance he is believed. The Gemara then asks that even regarding yibum this was also taught there, since it says that if he says "I have brothers" he is not believed. If it were only for inheritance, what difference would there be? This is the meaning of "this too was taught," rather than simply "it was taught." It appears that Rambam also understood the sugya this way. This is why the Tur and Rama write "and he is believed, etc.," apparently even regarding brothers and other relatives. This requires study, however, for regarding his son he is believed by the rule of "he shall recognize," but why should he be believed about his other relatives? See seif 2, "that is, etc." The Gemara infers from "this is my brother" because the Mishnah says generally that he is not believed, which implies every case. See Lechem Mishneh. Ran brings proof for the Rosh from 127b, "one who says, 'this is my son,' etc." Since the baraisa states it without qualification, it implies every case. He explains that the Gemara asks "obvious" because even according to the Sages he is believed when recognition is needed, and according to Rabbi Yehudah even against a presumption; the same applies here.
Even property, etc. This novelty is only according to Rabbi Meir, as stated 127b. Rashbam there says likewise. The Rosh mentioned only property that came afterward.
