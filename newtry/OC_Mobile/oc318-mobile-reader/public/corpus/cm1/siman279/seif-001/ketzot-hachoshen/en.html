One who says, This is my son. Sema writes that if the person was not previously known to be his son, the father is believed only through a migo, since he could give him his property, not through the Torah law of paternal recognition. If the claim contradicts an established presumption, the migo is insufficient. Yet the Gemara's discussion of the verse of recognition can imply that Rabbi Yehudah's rule allows recognition even against a contrary presumption, while the Sages without that verse would rely only on the migo. Ran elsewhere distinguishes a migo based on an alternative claim from a migo based on present power to perform an act. A person can sometimes be believed against a presumption where he currently has the power to produce the same result. This raises the question why the Gemara needs the recognition verse even according to Rabbi Yehudah when the father can simply give the claimant all his property. The answer may be that the Torah recognition rule is necessary against actual witnesses or for future property that he cannot presently transfer by Torah law. In any event, the scope of paternal recognition where paternity itself was never established remains disputed.
And he is believed concerning all the property. Lechem Mishneh asks that if one who says "this is my brother" is believed only through a migo that he could give him the property, his credibility should extend only to property presently owned. It should not extend to property acquired later, because he could not have given that earlier. Nowadays, however, one can create a very large personal monetary obligation and encumber both present and future acquired property under the rabbinic rule of later acquired assets. Thus in practice a broad migo can exist: he could obligate himself in an amount large enough to cover whatever later property might arise. By Torah law this would not solve the Gemara's question, because future acquired property is not subject to an ordinary Torah lien. The rabbinic enactment allowing later acquired property to become encumbered was created for commercial policy. Therefore the recognition verse was still needed at the Torah level for property acquired afterward. Under present law, however, the expanded lien rules strengthen the practical migo.
