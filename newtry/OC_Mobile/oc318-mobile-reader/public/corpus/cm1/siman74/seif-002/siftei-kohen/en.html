If the lender is not present, it is effective for the borrower to deposit the money with beit din or with a person trusted by beit din, even without an ancient communal enactment authorizing this. So writes Responsa Maharash Kohen, book 2, siman 99, and such was the custom in our country in the years 5410 and 5418. Maharash Kohen further writes that the borrower may likewise transfer ownership of the money to the lender through another person. In my humble opinion this is not effective, as I explain below at the beginning of siman 125.

But if, etc. The Bach writes that the borrower’s right to compel early acceptance applies only where the loan was originally made under circumstances like a loan made in an inhabited place, with no danger that the currency would be changed. In such a case the fixed term was established for the borrower’s benefit, and if there is still no danger of currency change, he may compel the lender to accept early payment. If, however, at the time of the loan there was concern that the currency would be changed, the term was set primarily for the lender’s benefit, on the expectation that the danger would pass with time. Even if that concern has now passed and the borrower wishes to pay only to remove the money from his responsibility, the lender cannot be compelled to accept before the term, because the term was originally for his benefit and he still fears a possible loss. The Bach derives this from Tosafot in Gittin 75 concerning Hillel’s enactment for a purchaser of a house in a walled city to deposit the redemption money in a chamber. Tosafot ask why an enactment was needed, since involuntary payment is normally valid, and answer that where the lender or recipient suffers a loss through the payment, involuntary payment is not effective. Thus, even after the due date in the case of a walled-city house, payment that causes a loss is ineffective; all the more so before the due date where the term was fixed for the lender’s benefit. The Shiltei Giborim similarly writes that one who lends to shopkeepers on terms that yield him profit cannot retract during the term, because receiving the money early would leave it idle and deprive him of profit. The Bach reasons that concern for loss of principal is certainly no weaker. The Baal HaTerumot likewise writes that where a lender eats the produce of mortgaged land, the borrower cannot compel him to accept payment before the term. In my humble opinion these proofs are not conclusive. Once the danger of currency change has passed, the borrower may discharge the loan. Although the term originally benefited the lender because of that danger, when the danger no longer exists and the borrower seeks to end his responsibility for the money, the lender must accept it. The case in Gittin is different because the payment itself causes the recipient an immediate loss. This must be so, since there the due date had already arrived and yet involuntary payment was ineffective absent Hillel’s enactment. The Shiltei Giborim is likewise distinguishable, because there early payment deprives the lender of the very profit for which the loan was made, just as a mortgagee was to eat produce until the stated time. That is not the present case.
