The law is with him. If the creditor is absent, the debtor deposits the money with beit din. Maharshal of Lublin ruled this, although Knesset HaGedolah reports that a handwritten responsum challenged him. The Shach testifies that leading Polish sages followed this practice in the years 5410 and 5411. If the rule rested only on a special enactment, as Maharshal of Lublin suggests, one could question whether every generation has sufficient authority to confiscate money for such a purpose. In my view, however, it is substantive law. Rava, in the second version of the passage, infers from Hillel's enactment concerning a house in a walled city that payment made in the recipient's absence is ordinarily not payment. But that inference must be limited to the walled city case. Rosh expressly distinguishes it from an ordinary creditor: redeeming a sold house requires a new transfer back from the purchaser, whereas repaying a debt requires no new act of acquisition by the creditor. Rashba likewise writes that although forced redemption of a sold house is ineffective without Hillel's enactment, forced payment of a debt is valid. The same distinction applies to payment in the creditor's absence. Hillel's case is a sale; an ordinary debt is different. Tosafos similarly distinguishes a creditor from a walled city because in an ordinary debt the creditor's loss is not presently apparent. The authorities compare repayment in a settlement, even if the creditor must travel through dangerous country, to repayment in a place where no actual loss is before us. Here too the feared loss has not yet materialized. Moreover, many authorities do not decide conclusively whether the halachah follows Rava's first or second version. Under the first version the entire issue is whether forced payment is payment. Once the poskim conclude that forced payment of an ordinary debt is valid, there is no reason to distinguish whether the creditor is physically present. A possessor can therefore say that the halachah follows that version. Ramban's rule below also suggests this. If the debtor tells the creditor, "Your money is in my house, take it," and the creditor refuses, the debtor can be exempt because forced repayment is valid. Yet the creditor has not actually gone into the house or seen the money. This indicates that the same underlying rule supports repayment even without the creditor's immediate presence. The Polish sages therefore ruled correctly as law, not merely as an enactment.
Where the danger is not presently evident. Meaning, the unavoidable loss has not yet taken effect and is only approaching. If the danger has already materialized, the creditor need not accept payment. That resembles one who lent in an inhabited place and is offered repayment in a desert. The Tosefta likewise says that if a violent person is visibly approaching and the debtor says, "Take your money," the creditor need not accept it. One should not ask why, according to Rava's second version, forced payment is generally valid except for payment in absence. Here the distinction is that the loss has already become concrete. Hillel's case involves a normal and legally structured consequence, whereas this is an unusual, presently evident danger. The creditor need not accept such a loss.
But if the time has not yet arrived, etc. The Bach asks why the Gemara, which contrasts a Mishnah saying that one who borrowed in a settled place may not repay in the desert with a baraita saying that a loan may be repaid anywhere, does not distinguish simply that the Mishnah concerns repayment before maturity while the baraita concerns repayment after maturity. From this he wants to distinguish a loan with an expressly fixed term from an ordinary thirty day loan, saying that the latter does not give the lender the same protection against early repayment. The question is not difficult. Maggid Mishneh writes that where no loss is involved, a lender may be compelled to accept early repayment, but a depositary cannot compel the depositor to accept return before the agreed term, because he undertook custody through that time. The Mishnah groups both loans and deposits: one who borrowed or received a deposit in a settled area may not return it in the desert. Therefore the Mishnah cannot be established as merely before maturity, for with a deposit even return in the same settled area would not necessarily be enforceable before the term. The Mishnah must concern the period when repayment or return is otherwise due. Once the Bach's question falls away, there is no source to distinguish between an expressly fixed term and the Torah's ordinary thirty day term. A thirty day loan is itself a legal term derived from Scripture, and comparable sources concerning customary due dates support treating it as a true term.
But if there is no danger. The Bach writes that if at the time of the loan the currency was unstable, and the lender fixed a term in the hope that the crisis would pass, then even after the danger passes the borrower cannot compel early payment because the term was for the lender's benefit. He compares this to a walled city sale where repayment would cause a loss. The Shach correctly rejects the analogy. There the loss is present. Here no present loss remains. In addition, Rosh and Rashba distinguish a debt from a sold house because the latter requires a new conveyance. The Bach cites Shiltei Gibborim concerning merchants who receive funds from investors in order to lend them to non Jews at interest and remit an agreed share of the profit. He says that where the investment term benefits the owner of the capital by preventing idle funds and loss of profit, the merchants cannot withdraw before the term. The Shach appears to accept this. I do not understand it. A mortgage in a place where the borrower may remove the mortgagee early can be terminated even though the original term obviously benefited the creditor, who expected to consume produce cheaply. An iska manager, who is paid for his labor on the deposit half, can also withdraw during the term like a worker who may leave in the middle of the day. Why should these shopkeepers be different? They are paid for managing the owner's capital and earning profit for him. Even the rule that an unpaid depositary cannot return a deposit before the fixed term is not decisive. Rashba questioned it from the worker's right to withdraw, and one must distinguish an unpaid custodian, whose duty requires only avoidance of negligence, from a paid custodian whose enhanced duty resembles service and therefore falls under "the children of Israel are servants to Me." The shopkeepers here certainly receive compensation or a share for their effort. They are therefore like workers and can withdraw. The rule of Shiltei Gibborim has no solid basis and should not be followed.
