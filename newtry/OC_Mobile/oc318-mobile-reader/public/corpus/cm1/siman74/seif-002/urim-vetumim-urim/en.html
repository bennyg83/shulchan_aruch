He may be sued wherever he is. Rabbeinu Yerucham and Yam Shel Shlomo write that the same applies to robbery. Shach says that if the stolen object has not yet been acquired by despair together with change of possession, then the object itself still belongs to the victim and its law resembles a deposit. According to what I wrote, however, the distinction concerning deposits is that the custodian need not pursue the owner. A robber is different: if a practical route exists, he certainly must go after the victim. Who compelled him to steal his fellow's property? In that sense he is even more obligated than a borrower. The authorities therefore correctly equated robbery to a loan. The same applies to wages, rental of houses or utensils, and purchase money. See Tumim, seif katan 1, regarding Rav Huna's view that permitted money may be treated as a loan even after an accident, and the practical consequences regarding proceeds of a lost object according to Rif and his supporters.
