Seif 3, gloss. Take your money. A borrower received coins that were later invalidated and greatly diminished in value. The note required payment in good, freely circulating currency. When the due date arrived, he told the lender, "your money is tied and set aside," at a time when those same coins still circulated freely and qualified as good currency, although even worse coins were also in use. The coins remained in his possession, and only afterward were invalidated, while the newly circulating currency became worth many times more. Even according to Urim VeTumim in siman 74, who cites the view that liability for accidental loss can continue after tender, and even though money before expenditure may still carry continuing loan liability, it appears that in this case the Taz and Urim VeTumim would agree that the special wording of the original note requiring "good currency" does not continue to govern after valid tender. It is as though the lender had then freshly received those particular circulating coins, and we rule that one who received valid coins may repay in those same coins even after they are later invalidated. Once payment was validly tendered against the lender's will, the original document's lien was discharged. If the tender was publicly announced and the fact that the money physically remained with the borrower was less public, it is clear that the old document could no longer be used to collect from encumbered property, for it became a document whose lien had been discharged and could not thereafter support a new loan. Thus there is no basis for the old phrase "good currency" to generate a new obligation after the later monetary change. This is especially so where the borrower could not have anticipated such a dramatic appreciation of the replacement currency. His statement "your money is ready" was made precisely in response to reports that the currency might change. The continuing accidental-liability rule rests on the borrower's ability to use the funds commercially, but even if he does so he ordinarily remains entitled to repay the kind of currency he actually received. The Taz's wording, "so long as he has not taken them out of his possession," may perhaps mean that if he actually removed and abandoned the set-aside funds from his control, even the continuing accidental liability would cease. This requires further study. In an actual currency change, a properly constituted beit din of three sat that day and ruled that the debtor need not pay the newly issued, more valuable currency. They further held that paying the new currency could itself involve a prohibition because it contained additional silver, consistent with the law in Yoreh Deah siman 165. Their ruling was correct.
