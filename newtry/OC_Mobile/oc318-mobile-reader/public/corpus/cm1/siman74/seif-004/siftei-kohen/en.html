Some say that the lender is not, etc. In my humble opinion the Rif disagrees, for he omits Rava’s statement in the chapter HaOmanin that payment coin by coin is valid. The Rif evidently understands Rava as speaking only after the fact. One might have thought that the lender could tell the borrower, “Take back each partial payment and bear responsibility until you pay the entire debt; if you refuse, the money remains with me at your risk.” Rava teaches that once paid, each installment is a valid payment. The Rif did not need to cite this separately, because the Rosh explains that the same principle follows from the earlier ruling that if a purchaser paid five hundred zuz, he acquires the property and may pay the balance even years later. The Rif cites that ruling. The Rambam at the end of chapter 23 of Hilchot Malveh and the Semag likewise imply this. They rule that if a creditor produces two notes of fifty each and asks that they be combined into one note of one hundred, this is refused, because it benefits the borrower to have two notes so that the creditor cannot compel him in one proceeding to pay everything. The Baal HaTerumot was forced to explain that this does not mean the creditor may legally compel full payment, but only that the borrower fears making partial payments lest the creditor later deny them and sue again. Yet the Rambam and Semag plainly mean actual judicial compulsion. They must therefore be discussing attempted payment before maturity, when the lender need not accept installments, for after maturity even two separate notes can be collected together. The Bach’s understanding in siman 53, that they speak after both notes matured, is therefore very difficult. Accordingly, Rava’s statement addresses only the validity of installments already accepted. This conclusion is already implicit in the rule of the purchaser who paid five hundred zuz. The Rambam and Semag omitted Rava’s statement for the same reason, relying on their ruling in Hilchot Mechira chapter 8 that a sale is acquired upon partial payment and the balance may be paid years later. The Rosh too can agree with the Rif that initially the lender need not accept payment coin by coin. If the Rosh understood Rava to compel acceptance in the first instance, the earlier rule would not establish that, because it proves only the effect of partial payment after the fact. The Rosh’s statement that Rava’s principle is learned from the earlier case therefore shows that both concern only the effect of payment after the fact. This makes the Tur’s ruling in accordance with the Baal HaItur, apparently against the Rosh, difficult, though it may perhaps be reconciled. The matter requires study.
Nevertheless, if he mortgaged to him, etc. The Tur’s language indicates a field mortgaged for a fixed term which the borrower wishes to redeem early. If so, even where the lender does not eat the produce, the borrower cannot compel him to return half the collateral. The lender may say: “Since you seek redemption before the term, I am not obligated to release any part of the pledge. I desire adequate security, and I want the entire pledge to remain with me so that you will be pressed to pay at maturity and recover it.” The case cannot readily concern a lender who eats produce, because then even full payment could not be compelled before the term, as stated in seif 3. Alternatively, it may concern a place where early redemption is customary. In the Baal HaTerumot, shaar 69, the source is readily understood in its plain sense. The property was pledged so that the lender would eat its produce until payment, without a separately fixed term. Full payment would therefore compel release, since the arrangement was only that he eat produce until paid. But if the borrower offers half, the lender may refuse, saying, “The entire field, not half, was given to me; until you pay all of the debt I will continue eating the produce, in accordance with the condition.” These rules do not differ between land and movable collateral.
