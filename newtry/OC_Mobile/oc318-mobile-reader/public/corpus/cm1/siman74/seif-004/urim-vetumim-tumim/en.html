Some say that the lender cannot refuse. Bava Metzia 77 states that if one lends one hundred zuz and the borrower repays one zuz at a time, it is repayment, although the lender has grounds for complaint. Rosh explains that the lender cannot say, "I refuse to accept my money except all at once." He supports this from the earlier baraita where a purchaser paid five hundred zuz and the seller may recover the balance years later, showing that partial payment is legally payment. Shach and Gidulei Terumah object that the baraita proves only that if the creditor voluntarily accepts partial payment, it counts as payment. It does not prove he can be compelled to accept it. Shach therefore interprets Rava's statement narrowly: if the lender accepted the installments, he cannot later say they remained in his hands merely as a deposit until the entire debt was paid. But Rosh's language is explicit that the lender cannot say, "I do not wish to receive my money." He plainly means compelled acceptance. The reason Rosh cites the earlier baraita is not merely to support Rava. He is proving which of two possible readings of Rava is correct. Since the baraita already established that accepted partial payment is legally payment, Rava would add nothing if he meant only that. Rava's novelty must therefore be that the borrower may compel acceptance of installments. This also explains Tur, who follows Rosh. Why then did Rif and Rambam omit the rule? The answer may be that Rava's conclusion depends on the separate dispute whether repayment against the creditor's will counts as repayment. Rava elsewhere follows the view that forced repayment is effective even where it causes some loss. R. Pappa is stricter. Here the lender has a real disadvantage, as the Gemara itself says he has a grievance because repeated small payments diminish the usefulness of his capital. Under the stricter view, the lender may therefore refuse. Rif and Rambam may have omitted the rule because the matter remains doubtful, and the lender in possession can say kim li like the view that forced payment is not effective in a loss-producing case. There is also room to distinguish an oral loan from a written loan. With a written note, the lender may say he does not wish to impair his document by repeated partial payments. Bava Batra recognizes the lender's legitimate interest in not having a note partially paid. Thus even according to Rosh, compelled installment repayment is strongest in an oral loan. In a written loan the lender may have a valid ground to refuse unless proper receipt arrangements protect him.
However, if he mortgaged his field. Taz writes that where the lender receives no produce or other benefit, as with movable collateral, the borrower may say: "Your security is worth the full debt. Return half of it to me." Since the lender loses nothing, refusal would be middat Sedom. I am hesitant to accept this. The lender can answer that the more valuable the collateral relative to the debt, the more secure his repayment is and the more pressure the borrower has to redeem it. A larger note or stronger collateral has recognized practical value precisely because it induces payment. The Yerushalmi likewise recognizes that a larger instrument creates greater fear than a smaller one. Accordingly, a creditor who wishes to retain the full collateral may have a legitimate financial interest, even if the remaining half would technically cover the debt. That is not obviously middat Sedom.
