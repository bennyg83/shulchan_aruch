If he has enough for his needs. See Perishah, who explains that this ruling is based on the law of arranging minimum sustenance for a debtor. At first glance this would imply that if the borrower traveled with money to a distant place for business, and the lender found him there, the borrower must give the lender the money and return home empty handed, thereby losing the travel expenses he incurred from home. He could not say, "I left money at home for repayment because I did not know I would encounter you here." This is unreasonable, however, because the borrower could suffer a major loss. For example, he may have traveled with wagons in order to load merchandise, and if he must hand over his money there the wagons will return empty. It therefore appears that where repayment at once would cause a loss to the borrower, the case is no worse than where the debtor's property would be sold at a depressed price, discussed in siman 24, where beit din grants more than thirty days, and similarly in siman 100 seif 1 in the gloss. Here too, an appropriate period should be granted so that the debtor not incur a loss. Although it is explained there that with movable property beit din does not grant time, that is because, as Sema writes there in seif katan 2, there is a concern that movable property may be concealed or removed. Here too, if there is such a concern, the borrower must place the merchandise or similar property in the lender's hands as collateral until he reaches a place where it can be sold without loss. Tumim seeks to derive a new rule that if the borrower has a route leading here, the lender may compel him to bring the money here. See there. This does not appear correct, because the borrower can say, "I do not wish to transport the money at my own risk." What is his proof from the rule that a deposit cannot be demanded away from its place? If the deposit is in the bailee's hands, he certainly must return it; if it is not in his hands, even a loan cannot be demanded immediately. Certainly the bailee need not give some other property in place of the deposit that is at his home. The case must therefore be of this sort, and Tumim derives his ruling from it. But one can answer that the novelty concerns unrestricted deposited money that the bailee is permitted to use. One might have thought that because he may use it, it is treated like a loan presently in his hands and he must give other money that he has with him. The ruling teaches otherwise, because Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses to Taz, siman 73, and Tumim decide that such money retains the status of a deposit rather than a loan, and therefore the identical money must be returned. It is also possible that even according to the view that such money is treated like a loan for liability for unavoidable loss, it is not treated like a loan for the purpose of requiring the bailee to pay with other money while the deposited money itself still exists, since he did not originally receive it as a loan. It further appears that if the lender finds the borrower elsewhere, although he cannot compel him to bring the money to that place, he can compel him to return home and pay there, where the money cannot be obtained without the borrower himself. For example, if the money is in the hands of a non-Jew, the borrower may be coerced until he agrees to return home and satisfy the debt, because his person is subject to the debt. If he is occupied with business, beit din grants him time where immediate departure would cause him a loss. A deposit is different. The depositor cannot compel the bailee to return home, and the deposit can be demanded only when the bailee is at the place of the deposit. The depositor must wait until he returns home, because the bailee never undertook a personal debt obligation toward him.

See Shach, seif katan 5, who writes that if the borrower finds the lender outside the lender's city, in an inhabited place, and wishes to pay him with coins that circulate there but do not circulate in the lender's city, the lender must accept them. See there. At first glance this is difficult, because siman 42 seif 14 explains that one must pay with the currency of the place of the loan even if the promissory note is produced in another city. See Sema, seif katan 37, and Perishah there, that if the place of the loan can be established, the debtor must pay with the currency of that place. It therefore appears that there the currency of the place of the loan has greater purchasing value even in the present location. If one buys produce there with the currency of the place of the loan, he receives more produce because that currency is heavier or more valuable. Therefore he must pay with the currency of the place of the loan. Here, however, the case is where, in the place where the borrower encountered the lender, there is no difference in purchasing power between the two currencies. The borrower can therefore say: even if I had purchased produce here, I could have paid you by appraisal with that produce. Although seif 6 explains that a debtor cannot compel a lender to accept payment by appraisal when the lender prefers to wait, because a creditor's proper payment is money and the debtor is offering movable goods, here the borrower originally borrowed ordinary currency and the local coin has the legal name of currency, with no difference in its purchasing power. He can therefore discharge the debt with the currency of that place.
