A loan may be demanded, and so forth. Shiltei Giborim is uncertain whether a lender who encounters the borrower away from the place of the loan can require the borrower, who has no cash there, to travel back home specifically to obtain the money and return with it. Shach considers it obvious that the borrower cannot be forced to do so. I would distinguish. If the borrower has no reason to return home and would have to make a special round trip solely to satisfy the creditor's demand, it is unreasonable to impose that burden. The loan was not made on the understanding that the borrower becomes the lender's servant for unlimited travel. But if the borrower is in any event returning home, or trusted travelers regularly go between the places and can safely carry the money, the borrower can be required to arrange payment. He cannot dismiss the demand merely by saying the logistics are inconvenient. Bava Kamma 118 supports this. The baraita says a loan can be demanded in any place, while a deposit or lost object need be returned only in its proper place. What is the difference? If the borrower has the money with him, of course the loan can be demanded. If the deposited object is also physically with the custodian, it can likewise be returned. And if neither is present, why should the borrower have any greater duty than the custodian? The novelty must be that a loan obligation is portable. Where the borrower can reasonably obtain and deliver equivalent money, he must do so even away from the original place. A deposit is different because the owner is entitled to the specific object. The custodian need not transport that object from the place where it was properly deposited merely because the owner encounters him elsewhere. Gidulei Terumah suggests the difference concerns an oath about whether the borrower has money available, but the baraita predates hesset and cannot primarily be about that. Nor is it plausible that a custodian must substitute his own property for a deposited object left safely at home. If the specific deposit later suffers unavoidable accident, he would ordinarily be exempt. There is no reason to force him now to pay from his own assets and later bear the risk of seeking reimbursement. Thus the correct practical distinction is reasonable portability. If the borrower can obtain and bring the loan money through an ordinary route he is already using, the lender may demand it. If payment would require a special burdensome journey solely for this purpose, the borrower can require the lender to collect at the ordinary place of repayment.
And he said to him, "I also wish to go out," and so forth. Rif and Rambam omit this detail. Beit Yosef explains that they read the Gemara as follows: where the borrower said at the time of receiving the money, "I intend to go into the wilderness," he can later return the money there. The Gemara asks that this is obvious and answers that the novelty is where the lender replies, "I too am going into the wilderness. If you wish to return it there, return it to me there." According to Beit Yosef, the statement must be express, but because its legal effect is obvious once stated, Rif and Rambam had no need to codify it. Gidulei Terumah objects: if the Gemara asks "obvious" and then gives an answer, it plainly thinks the Mishnah teaches a real novelty. How could Rif and Rambam call it too simple to mention? The novelty is indirect. The lender's words "if you wish, return it to me there" could theoretically mean only: "If you want me to demand payment there, I will have that right." It might not mean the borrower can force repayment on the lender there against the lender's later will. But that interpretation is impossible because a lender can already demand a loan in the wilderness under the separate baraita. No condition is needed to give him that right. Therefore the lender's statement must mean something new: the borrower too receives the right to repay there and compel acceptance. That is what the Mishnah teaches implicitly. The baraita states independently that the lender can demand payment there. Once Rif and Rambam codify that baraita, the meaning of "if you wish, return it there" becomes self-evident, and they need not repeat the specific exchange. This also shows that the express statement remains necessary. Beit Yosef and Yam Shel Shlomo are correct: the borrower's right to compel wilderness repayment arises from the lender's assent, not automatically.
