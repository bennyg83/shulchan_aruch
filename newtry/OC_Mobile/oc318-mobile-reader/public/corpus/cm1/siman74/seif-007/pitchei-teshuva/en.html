And he shall pay in accordance with what was enacted. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 62, concerning one who in his youth lent money under a heter iska according to the ruling of one of the decisors, but later, after studying the Gemara and poskim, concluded that the arrangement was not valid and wished to return the interest. In that country the bank notes had greatly depreciated since the time of the loan, while the government had enacted that debts must be repaid in sound bank notes according to their former value, which would make the repayment enormous and difficult for him. The Chatam Sofer ruled that he need return only the same number of bank notes that he had received, notwithstanding their present depreciation. Under Torah law it is clear that one repays the currency that was lent, even if it later became entirely invalid, so long as it can still be taken elsewhere, as in Bava Kamma 97b; all the more so where it still circulates locally and merely depreciated. The greater payment is required only because the law of the kingdom is law and the sovereign ordered repayment in sound currency according to its former value. Thus, whichever system is followed, the borrower need not make the larger restitution: under Torah law he repays only the currency lent, while under civil law he would not be required to return the interest at all. Although one could argue that Torah law itself requires a full restitution measured according to the government's rule, and there is some support from the Gemara's question there from maaser sheni against Rav Nachman, nevertheless the practical ruling stated above is correct. Had the government made no rule concerning payment of debts, Torah law would govern. The governmental enactment is treated as part of the law of the kingdom because it ordinarily concerns the sovereign's fiscal order. That reasoning applies to actual repayment of debts, but not to return of prohibited interest, where the law is lenient toward penitents and which is an uncommon matter that the government did not contemplate. We therefore return to the original rule: he shall repay exactly the quantity of that currency that he received, whether its present value is little or much. See there.
