He gives him, etc. The Shach and Sema write that if the original coins themselves remain intact, there is a dispute whether the debtor can say, "Here is your property before you." That discussion follows Rashi, who understands the loan as made without an express condition. According to Tosafos and the Rama, however, the case concerns an explicit condition regarding the currency to be used for repayment. Every monetary condition is binding. If the parties clearly stipulated payment in currently circulating currency, the fact that the original coins still physically exist does not allow the debtor to return invalidated currency. Mordechai, following Rashi, could say otherwise because under his reading the condition concerned only payment in money rather than merchandise, not a specified circulating coin.
Who lent to him in every circumstance. Maharam, cited in Mordechai, ruled concerning one who divorced his wife and promised to pay her by Chanukah in currency then current. The currency was invalidated on the first day of Chanukah. Maharam held that "by Chanukah" meant by the eve of Chanukah, so he could pay with the currency that had circulated then. Maharshal sought to derive that if a borrower was repeatedly demanded at maturity and put the creditor off until the currency was invalidated, he must pay the later valid currency. The Shach correctly notes that Maharam's case proves the opposite: he allowed the former husband to pay with the earlier currency even after it had been invalidated. Although Maharshal's proof fails, one might still argue that a borrower who deliberately delayed caused the creditor's loss, since timely payment could have been spent elsewhere. I nevertheless do not think we can extract money on that ground. At most he caused loss indirectly. More importantly, Tosafos asks why, without an express condition, invalidated currency can be returned at all, since a robber may say concerning the stolen object, "Here is yours before you." Tosafos's question assumes that if the actual coins are not intact, the borrower does not receive the special protection of returning the very object. Yet a robber is worse than an ordinary borrower who delayed: had he not stolen, the owner could have used the money before invalidation. Still, the mere fact that his wrongful act caused the loss does not automatically create monetary liability for the later currency change. All the more so should we not extract from an ordinary borrower merely because he put the creditor off. Maharshal's ruling therefore cannot be used to remove money from the possessor.
And some disagree. In Yoreh Deah, siman 165, Darchei Moshe says that Rivash and Rosh disagree concerning whether dina d'malchuta governs repayment after a royal currency change. The Shach argues that Rosh's responsum does not prove this. Rosh was asked whether to follow royal law or Torah law when a king debased or enhanced currency. He answered that he did not understand why dina d'malchuta was relevant, because the king's concern is only that the old currency cease circulating, not how private borrowers repay their creditors. This sounds as though Rosh would agree that if the king expressly legislated repayment rules, dina d'malchuta would apply. The Rama apparently understood the case differently. If the king had merely invalidated currency and said nothing about repayment, the questioner would have had no question, because that is precisely the ordinary Talmudic case of currency invalidated by the king. The question must therefore have arisen because the sovereign also established a repayment rule. Rosh's answer was that where that repayment rule is not necessary to the sovereign's currency policy and merely imposes a separate private law, it does not displace Torah monetary law. If the repayment rule were necessary to make the currency decree effective, then it could be part of dina d'malchuta. But when the old currency is already invalid for trade regardless of how old debts are settled, the private repayment provision is an independent civil rule and, according to Rosh, cannot uproot Torah law. On this reading the Rama's citation is accurate. As practical law, the Shach in Yoreh Deah cites many authorities who hold that in currency matters royal law does govern. I have some difficulty with that view from Bava Kamma 97. The Gemara asks how one can transport currency where kingdoms object to exporting it, and answers that they do not actively search travelers. But if every royal currency command is halachically binding under dina d'malchuta, why should lack of inspection matter? It would remain forbidden to violate the sovereign's order even if enforcement is lax. Perhaps dina d'malchuta applies specifically to monetary regulations and not every nonmonetary command. Once the coin is being taken outside the kingdom, the prohibition on export may not itself regulate a monetary right between private parties. I discussed this further in Yoreh Deah and in my novellae to Rambam.
