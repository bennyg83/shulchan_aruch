They do not remove him until the time arrives, etc. The reason is that this period was not fixed at the outset solely for the borrower's benefit, but also for the lender's benefit, so that he could consume the produce until that time. For the same reason, the Shiltei HaGibborim writes in the last chapter of HaGozel, page 47a, that contemporary commercial loans made by shopkeepers, where they fix a period during which the borrower will provide them a return until a certain date, establish that term also for the lender's benefit. The lender may therefore refuse to accept early repayment, because his loss is plainly evident. End quote.
And it is within the term. It appears that this means that the matter occurs while the loan is still within its fixed term. Rama added these words in order to avoid a contradiction with what the Tur writes, and the Mechaber likewise rules, below at the end of siman 120: If a borrower tells the lender, "Your money is wrapped and lying in my house, come and take your money," and the lender refuses to accept it, and it is then stolen or lost, the borrower is exempt. Here, by contrast, the Mechaber writes that the borrower remains liable. My master and teacher, Rabbi Moshe Isserles, therefore explains that here the case is still within the term of the loan. See what I wrote further in resolving this issue there at the end of siman 120 in the Sema. This resolution itself presents a difficulty. If the case here is within the term, why does Rama conclude that in a comparable case involving a deposit the custodian is exempt? The Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 7 of Hilchot Sheelah that a custodian cannot compel the depositor to take back a deposit before the term when it was deposited for a known period, because the depositor may say: You accepted its safekeeping until the stated time. The Mechaber likewise rules this in siman 293:1. Unless one distinguishes that there the custodian refuses to guard it at all, which is comparable to damaging it directly, as I write below in siman 95:4, whereas here the case is one where it was stolen or lost without his negligence.
The borrower is liable to pay. So writes the Beit Yosef, who found this ruling and notes that it is also written in the name of the Raavan. The reason given is that if a business opportunity arose, would the borrower not use these very funds to purchase with them? A deposit, by contrast, once the custodian tells the depositor, "Take what is yours," is no longer subject to his duty of safekeeping. The cited source concludes: I say that if the money is a deposit and is not tied with an unusual knot, why should the custodian not remain liable for it, since he is permitted to use it? End quote. The Beit Yosef cites this without comment. Darkei Moshe brings it and writes: I do not know what difficulty he saw in asking why the custodian should not remain liable. Once his duty of safekeeping has ended, he cannot be made liable against his will, as I will write below, God willing, in the laws of custodians. I wrote there that Rama's objection itself is surprising. It may be that the Raavan and the authority who wrote this were discussing a custodian who ordinarily does business with money, as most people do nowadays. In such a case, it is explained in siman 292, at the end of seif 7, that since he is permitted to use deposited money if he wishes, provided it is not tied with an unusual knot, he becomes liable for theft and loss even if he never actually used it. According to the Rambam, the same rule applies to money found as a lost object even when the finder did not use it, as the Tur and Mechaber state at the end of siman 267. See there. As for the statement that his duty of safekeeping ended when he said, "Take it," one can argue the opposite: Since he told the depositor to take it and the depositor did not do so, the depositor thereby revealed that he does not presently need the money, and the custodian consequently has permission to use it. Furthermore, according to what was written above that the case is still within the fixed term, the duty of safekeeping has not ended merely because he said, "Take it," as stated in the name of the Maggid Mishneh. The matter requires study. Rama abbreviated here and wrote that a deposit in such a case is exempt, meaning a case where the custodian likewise told the depositor, "Take your deposit," because the period of safekeeping had already ended and the depositor refused. Rama follows his own view as stated in Darkei Moshe. In my view, however, his words are not compelling so long as the money is not tied with an unusual knot, as I have explained. Examine this carefully.
