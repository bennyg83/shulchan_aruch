And it is within the term. Sema suggests that Rama intentionally means the loan is still before maturity. That would distinguish this rule from siman 120, where Mechaber says a debtor who tenders money can sometimes become exempt from further responsibility. According to Sema, before maturity the money remains at the borrower's risk because if a business opportunity arises he may still use it. After maturity, by contrast, he expects the creditor to demand payment and will not normally reuse the money. But Sema and Taz themselves note a difficulty. Rama continues, "But with a deposit in the same circumstances he is exempt." A depositor ordinarily cannot force the owner to take back a deposit before the agreed custodial term. The distinction can be answered if the deposit consists of unrestricted money. Such money makes the custodian a paid custodian under Rav Nachman's rule, and a paid custodian who performs ongoing service can sometimes terminate the relationship, much like a worker. The owner cannot necessarily force him to continue until the original date. Thus the deposit clause does not conclusively refute Sema. Shach gives another distinction. Here the borrower merely set the money aside but did not tell the lender that it was sealed and no longer available for use. If a business opportunity appears, he can reopen and use it, so the original loan responsibility continues. In siman 120, Shach says, the lender was informed the money was tied and set aside, so the borrower no longer intended to use it. I do not find this convincing. Where a depositor initially gives sealed money, the seal shows the owner does not permit use. But here the borrower himself tied the money after receiving it as a loan. He can undo his own knot whenever he wants. The creditor has not acquired the coins merely because the borrower wrapped them. Thus the seal does not create the legal distinction Shach wants. The better answer is Bach and Taz's: Rama below in siman 120 expressly records dissenting authorities, and the present ruling follows those dissenters. Rama often states the view he considers primary in one place and later notes the opposing opinion. There is still a conceptual question. The source says the borrower remains liable because if a profitable transaction becomes available he may use the money. But ordinary permission to use funds does not by itself create borrower liability for unavoidable accident. Rav Nachman says unrestricted deposited money is only a paid-custodian case until actual use. The answer is that this borrower already became fully liable as a borrower when the original loan was made. We are asking whether his later tender terminated that preexisting status. As long as he still treats the money as available for his own use, the original loan relationship has not ended. We do not need the use privilege to create borrower status from scratch; we need only say it prevents the old status from terminating. That distinction explains the rule.
In such a case he is exempt. Shach and Maharshal write that he is exempt even for negligence. In truth, this is disputed. Mordechai in Perek HaNizakin implies that when the owner says, "Take your money," and the other party does not take it, he may cease to be even an unpaid custodian. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ramah, Ritva, and Tosafot similarly. The case of an artisan who says, "Take your object and bring my money," yet remains an unpaid custodian, is different because the artisan still holds the object against his fee. But Tosafot in Perek HaZahav distinguish differently. They ask from that artisan case against the episode of the sesame purchase, where Rava says that after the seller says, "Take your money," he is not even an unpaid custodian. Tosafot answer that the artisan began as a paid custodian, so his statement is understood only as reducing his status to unpaid custodian rather than eliminating custody entirely. According to that approach, unrestricted money here, where the holder was initially a paid custodian, should likewise leave him at least an unpaid custodian and liable for negligence. Beit Yosef at the end of siman 198 indeed cites the Geonim that where a seller received purchase money and later says, "Take your money," he remains an unpaid custodian. The apparent contradiction in Tosafot can be resolved from the Talmudic development. The early assumption in the sesame case followed the view that the transaction did not create full Torah ownership, and therefore the seller may originally have been only an unpaid custodian. Once the law follows R. Yochanan that money effects Torah acquisition, the seller's original responsibility is stronger and his later tender merely reduces him to an unpaid custodian. Alternatively, the sesame case involved only an agreement to supply an available commodity not yet owned, so the initial acquisition itself was only rabbinic, and the money therefore never entered the seller's full Torah ownership. Then he was only an unpaid custodian from the outset. Either way, our accepted law in siman 198 follows Rambam that a seller who says, "Take your money," remains at least an unpaid custodian. The same should apply here. "Exempt" means exempt from theft and loss, not from negligence. Sema's question why the lender remains responsible when he can still use the money is answered similarly. Once he says, "Take your money," he withdraws from the paid-custodian aspect even though physical use might still be technically possible. The law treats him as an unpaid custodian. Thus he remains liable for negligence.
