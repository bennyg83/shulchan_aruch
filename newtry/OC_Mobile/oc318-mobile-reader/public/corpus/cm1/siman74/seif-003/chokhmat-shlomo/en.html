Seif 7. One who lends another against a currency and that currency is invalidated, etc. See Shach, seif katan 25, citing Maharshal that the old currency must at least remain accepted by merchants of that country. This is difficult because elsewhere Maharshal is cited as holding that if merchants of that country commonly travel to a place where the currency is accepted, that is equivalent to the debtor himself having a route there. If so, even where the government objects to exporting the coins, they can still constitute valid repayment. Why then is it necessary here that the government not object to their being taken out? This requires study. I was also asked by Rabbi Mordechai Zeidman, dayan of Oziran, concerning banknotes whose market value had declined. He reported that Rabbi Meshulam of Pressburg had ruled that banknotes are not treated as coinage but as debt instruments, and that one who borrowed such notes and they became invalid must repay their monetary value. The questioner suggested that this applies only where they became completely invalid, not where they merely depreciated. I answered that this distinction is correct. Even if banknotes are not coinage, they are at least comparable to one who borrowed a seah of produce. The Gemara and Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah teach that if the produce falls in value, the borrower may return the same quantity of produce, whereas if it rises in value different rules apply because of interest concerns. Thus, when the thing borrowed merely depreciates, the borrower may return the same kind of thing. The same is true of debt instruments. If he returns instruments of the very same issuer and kind that he borrowed, their depreciation does not prevent repayment in kind. If they have become wholly invalid, however, they are like produce that has rotted. An item no longer usable at all has suffered an intrinsic defect and cannot be returned as equivalent performance. Mere depreciation is not such a defect. In any event, where the law of the kingdom recognizes the banknotes as legal tender, dina de-malkhuta dina governs. If governmental law permits or requires debts to be paid with those notes, they can be used for repayment. Certainly where the original loan itself consisted of banknotes and the law treats repayment in the same notes as valid, the borrower may repay in that form. I wrote further on these matters in my fourth edition to Choshen Mishpat, in a responsum to Rabbi Meshulam Lohr of Sinowitz. The continuation of this discussion also bears on procedural oaths. Where the claimant must swear and collect, or where an oath has been shifted to the claimant, the claimant too must accept the communal ban that his claim is truthful. This is contrary to the Taz’s view. In the era of the Gemara, before the geonic enactment requiring such a ban, there was practical need for the separate admonition “distance yourself from falsehood.” After the enactment, much of that practical difference disappears. A claimant who lies would first have to accept a severe ban falsely, while a defendant now faces at least the oath of hesset. One who is prepared to violate the ban or ignore formal excommunication will not be restrained merely by an additional abstract warning against falsehood. There, in the same seif: they tell him, clarify your words. See Shach, seif katan 2, who concludes that the position of Radakh is primary and that this was the accepted practice. In my opinion that was especially appropriate in earlier generations when Jewish courts possessed effective enforcement power. In our unfortunate times, when people often resort to secular courts and Jewish enforcement is weak, if beit din sees that justice requires ordering the disputed money deposited with the court, this can fall within judicial discretion according to the circumstances. Even as a matter of law, one should distinguish cases. If according to the defendant’s own account the disputed object is fully his, Radakh’s reasoning applies that beit din should not impose needless burdens merely to conduct the litigation. But where even the defendant admits that he holds the object only as collateral and must return it when the underlying money is paid, he concedes that the object itself is not ultimately his. In that case he can properly be required to deposit the collateral, while the borrower deposits the corresponding money. This resembles cases of mutual uncertain possession in which neither side has an absolute present title. Radakh also appears to address primarily a claimant who seeks to force the defendant both to litigate and to deposit his money, thereby imposing two burdens upon him. If Reuven sues Shimon and Shimon answers, “On the contrary, you owe me more; deposit what you owe me and I will deposit what you claim,” the defendant’s position is stronger. A plaintiff who insists on proceeding can be required to deposit as well. That does not impose two burdens on the defendant, especially when both sides are prepared to place the disputed property with beit din. Minimizing disputes is preferable. If a poor claimant sues a wealthy defendant and the wealthy party raises such a counterclaim merely because he knows the poor claimant cannot afford to deposit the money, beit din must be alert to possible manipulation. The wealthy party should be warned regarding the prohibition of false claims and the communal enactments against them. In our circumstances, where enforcement difficulties are real, a dayan who understands that the situation requires a deposit may order it. Siman 72, seif 14, itself indicates that a borrower can be considered the party in possession even while the collateral physically remains with the lender, which further supports requiring deposit in an appropriate case.
