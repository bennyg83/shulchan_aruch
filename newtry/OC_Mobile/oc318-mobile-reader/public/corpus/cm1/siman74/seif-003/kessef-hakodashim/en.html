See Shach, seif katan 11. The Bach, cited by the Shach in siman 74, holds that where the fixed due date was chosen partly for the lender's benefit, the borrower cannot insist on early payment even if that particular benefit no longer exists. At first glance this is difficult from the law of a mortgage in a locality where the borrower cannot ordinarily remove the lender before the end of the term. That arrangement is clearly beneficial to the lender, yet the borrower may tender the money before the due date provided the lender remains entitled to consume the fruits until the term expires. The answer may be that in a mortgage the period of enjoying the fruits can remain fixed exactly as agreed, while the date for returning the principal was intended only for the borrower's benefit. By contrast, where the agreement concerns a change in currency or a similar circumstance, the very due date itself may have been fixed partly for the lender, so he retains the option to insist on it. There is, however, a further difficulty concerning the rationale permitting a mortgage because the lender is considered as though the property were acquired to him for the term. Once the borrower tenders payment early, the property may no longer be considered acquired to the lender. Why then are the later fruits not prohibited as late interest? Perhaps because the property was legitimately acquired to him at the outset, the Sages did not prohibit the later fruits as rabbinic late interest. But if the principal had been due before the agreed end of the lender's residence, there could be a Torah-level ribbit concern, because the lender would thereafter receive compensation for the earlier extension while no longer possessing the property as purchaser. One might try to say that his later enjoyment is treated as though the principal had been accepted only then, so where there is less delay it should be no worse than where there is more. This reasoning is not clear to me, because he is still receiving compensation for waiting after the debt has effectively become payable and the property is no longer acquired to him. This consideration may support the Shach and Urim VeTumim against the Bach. The matter requires further study.
