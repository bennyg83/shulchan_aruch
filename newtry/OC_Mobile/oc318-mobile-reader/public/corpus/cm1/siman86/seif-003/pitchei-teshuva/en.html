Beit din does not attend to the claim. See Baer Heitev. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 53, which cites what he wrote in the first edition, siman 34, namely that the rule that a creditor may collect even from heirs out of a debt owed to their father by third parties, as explained in siman 107, is not based solely on Rabbi Natan's lien. Rather, in the present era, when our principal livelihood and support depend upon receivables and creditors primarily rely on them, such receivables are comparable to ordinary commercial assets. He explains the matter as follows. If Reuven is owed money by Shimon and Shimon is owed money by Levi, Reuven's ability to obtain payment from Levi rests on two distinct bases. First, Reuven may collect from Shimon, and he may collect from any property belonging to Shimon. Although Shimon may ordinarily satisfy the creditor with whichever property he chooses, no particular free asset is more encumbered than another; the debt rests equally upon all of Shimon's unencumbered property. The debt owed by Levi to Shimon is itself one of Shimon's assets. Accordingly, beit din may give Reuven that receivable, by transferring Shimon's document against Levi to Reuven as payment at the assessed value of the document. Such a transfer is comparable to a sale, and none of this requires Rabbi Natan's lien. For this purpose there is no distinction whether Shimon has other assets besides receivables, because at that stage beit din is dealing only with Shimon's assets, all of which are unencumbered in his hands, including the very debt Levi owes him. Once beit din has awarded Reuven Shimon's claim against Levi, or Shimon himself has assigned that claim to Reuven as payment, Reuven becomes Levi's creditor and can collect from him. Levi's property is not regarded as purchased or otherwise encumbered property in relation to this collection. Yet without Rabbi Natan's lien, until the receivable has actually been transferred to Reuven as payment, Shimon could still waive Levi's debt, just as a seller of a debt may later waive it, and Reuven has no direct claim against Levi before the transfer. Rabbi Natan's lien creates an additional right: Reuven may proceed directly against Levi without first collecting from Shimon, and Shimon cannot waive Levi's debt to Reuven's detriment. For that direct right, however, Shimon must lack other property. If he has other assets, Reuven has no direct right against Levi by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien. This resolves the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in siman 86, which state that where Shimon has other property beit din does not proceed against Levi directly, as well as the Shach's discussion. Even then, however, Reuven may collect Shimon's receivable as an item of Shimon's property, just like any other asset. The responsum further writes that even if Shimon has no other property and Reuven therefore has rights in the debt under Rabbi Natan's lien, if Reuven waived Levi's debt before Shimon or beit din actually transferred the receivable to him as payment, Reuven's waiver affects only his lien right. Reuven did not own the underlying debt itself, which remained Shimon's. Thus Reuven can no longer sue Levi directly by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien, but the underlying debt has not been extinguished. It remains Shimon's property, and Reuven may still collect that receivable from Shimon as payment of Shimon's debt. Once Shimon or beit din gives it to him in payment, Reuven can collect from Levi, no longer by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien, which he waived, but because he now stands in Shimon's place like a purchaser of the debt. On this basis the responsum explains the Rosh, rule 36, siman 4, cited in Noda BiYehudah, siman 34. See there.
