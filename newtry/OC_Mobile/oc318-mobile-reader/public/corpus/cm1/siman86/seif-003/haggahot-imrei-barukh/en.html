(Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 3) And it appears possible to resolve all the difficulties, etc. Note: See the later edition of the Rashba's novellae to Kiddushin, where he discusses at length that Yehudah's property is not encumbered to Reuven by ordinary lien. He ultimately leaves the matter difficult from Pesachim 31, which explains Rabbi Nathan's rule by saying that just as the second debtor is obligated to the first, he is obligated to the father's creditor. See also the Rashba's responsum in Toldot Adam, siman 389. In his novellae to Kiddushin 15 he writes that Rabbi Nathan's law is because the case resembles needless circling of payments. According to this, it is not analogous to a guarantor. The second debtor holds money belonging to the first debtor. If the first debtor collects from him, the first debtor's creditor will then collect that very money. We therefore take it directly from the second and give it to the creditor, avoiding pointless transfer. Had Scripture not taught, "and he shall give it to the one to whom he is guilty," the second debtor could have said, "you are not my litigant." The Rashba's statement that if the first debtor owns other property we do not collect directly from the second because he can say, "I left you property from which to collect," can be explained because in that case the pointless-transfer rationale does not apply. Even if the first debtor collects from the second, it is not certain that those proceeds will go to his creditor, since he can satisfy that creditor from his other property. Anyone who studies the Rashba carefully in the places cited will see that his approach does not correspond to the author's explanation here. The author's idea that the second debtor is merely like a guarantor is cited in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Kamma in the name of early commentators. See there.
