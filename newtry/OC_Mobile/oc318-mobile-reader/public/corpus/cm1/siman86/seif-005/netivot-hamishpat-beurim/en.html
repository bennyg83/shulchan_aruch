Shimon cannot. See Shach, seif katan 11, who concludes that Shimon cannot waive the debt. The authorities ask from Bava Kamma, where the Gemara asks why the woman should not sell her ketubah to the person whom she injured, and answers that with respect to her husband she would certainly waive it. This is so even though her ketubah is subject to the injured party under Rabbi Natan's lien, yet her waiver is effective. Shach answers that once a ketubah of two hundred has already been collected at a valuation of one hundred, she is no longer indebted to the injured party, and it becomes like an ordinary sale of a promissory note, which the seller can waive. Tumim challenges this. Even where one sells a promissory note and can waive it, the reason is that the personal lien cannot be sold, only the lien on property. When the seller waives the personal lien, the lien on the property also falls away. Here, however, she sells the ketubah to the injured party himself, who already possesses a personal lien against her. When she also transfers the property lien to him, he holds both the personal lien and the property lien. Why, then, should she be able to waive it? One cannot answer that if she owes one hundred and sells a ketubah of two hundred for one hundred, he has no personal lien over the second hundred and therefore she may waive that amount, because she could instead sell half the ketubah, worth one hundred, for fifty. A debt of fifty would still remain to him, so he would have a personal lien for the full hundred and she should not be able to waive it. These are Tumim's words. I do not understand what he means and could not explain his position, because in the end, if she waives the ketubah, the husband certainly will not need to pay more than the amount that is actually subject to Rabbi Natan's lien, and not the excess. Shach also answers Maharshal's question that perhaps we should be concerned that she borrowed money, saying, "Who would be foolish enough to lend her money?" At first glance this is difficult. Tosafot in Bava Metzia write that we need not fear that she sold the ketubah after divorce through ma'amad sheloshtan, because after divorce she would sell it for a high price, and if the husband then produced a receipt she would have to reimburse the purchaser, so she would gain nothing from collusion. According to this, Shach has not answered the concern that perhaps she borrowed after divorce. On reflection, however, Maharshal's question itself must concern a case where she borrowed from others and then assigned them her ketubah, for otherwise there would be no collusion. If she merely borrowed, she still must repay what she borrowed. Moreover, why would collusion be relevant at all, since she can even now give the husband a receipt for payment and thereby discharge him? If he paid when he did not know whether she was entitled to collect, he need not pay a second time, as Shach explains in seif katan 14. It is also difficult what Maharshal gains from Abaye's answer that the ketubah document is still in her possession, because we could still fear that she borrowed. We must therefore say that Maharshal's concern is that she borrowed from others and then assigned the ketubah to them, in which case she would not be able to waive it, as Ran asks in HaChovel concerning the suggestion that she sell her ketubah to her husband, cited by Shach. Shach's answer is then sound. There is no concern that she borrowed and assigned the ketubah to her creditor after divorce, for Tosafot's reasoning applies: after divorce she would ordinarily sell it for a high price and would gain nothing from collusion. The only remaining concern is that she borrowed and assigned the ketubah before divorce, and to that Shach correctly answers, "Who would be foolish enough to lend her money before divorce?"
He can waive Levi's debt. Tumim asks from siman 85 seif 6. There it is explained that if Shimon sold to Yehudah the promissory note he holds against Levi, and Yehudah collected movable property on that debt, Reuven, who is Shimon's creditor, cannot seize those movables from Yehudah. If so, when Shimon waives Levi's debt and Levi owns only movables, why should the waiver not be effective? What difference is there between giving the note as a gift to Yehudah and giving the benefit of it to Levi? On this basis Tumim proposes a new rule that if Levi owns only movable property Shimon can waive the debt. This is certainly incorrect, because the authorities would not have stated the law without qualification if such a major distinction existed. The question itself is no difficulty. We see plainly that there is a difference between Shimon's waiving Levi's debt and Levi's paying Shimon. If Shimon waives before it becomes established that he lacks assets with which to pay Reuven, and afterward it is established that he lacks assets, his waiver is ineffective and Levi must pay Reuven. If Levi paid Shimon before it became established that Shimon lacks assets, however, Levi is exempt from paying Reuven, as Shach explains in seif katan 14. Accordingly, siman 85 presents no difficulty. There Yehudah purchased the note and already collected, and the case must concern collection before it became established that Reuven's debtor lacked assets, as Sema explains there. It appears that if at the time Yehudah collected it was already established that Shimon lacked assets, Levi would be forbidden to pay the purchaser and would have to pay Reuven, the first creditor. Just as he may not pay Shimon, the seller, he may not pay Shimon's purchaser, because the first person could sell only whatever right he himself possessed. Moreover, the reason a seller of a promissory note can waive it is that the authorities explain that he cannot sell the personal lien, only the property lien. When he waives the personal lien, the property lien also falls away. Here, once it is established that Shimon has no assets, Levi's personal obligation is transferred from Shimon to Reuven under Rabbi Natan's lien, as the Mechaber states in seif 5: "for Shimon's lien has fallen away." Consequently the property lien likewise falls away from Shimon. If Levi nevertheless paid the purchaser, he must pay Reuven again, just as where he improperly paid Shimon, as Shach explains in seif katan 44. Thus siman 85 necessarily concerns a case where Yehudah collected before Shimon's lack of assets had been established. It is then clear why Reuven cannot collect from Yehudah. From whom could Reuven claim? He cannot claim from Levi, who has already paid. Paying Yehudah at Shimon's direction is equivalent to paying Shimon, because the sale of the note is effectively an instruction to Levi to pay Yehudah, and payment discharges Levi as explained above. Nor can Reuven claim from Yehudah, because Yehudah's collection is treated as though Shimon first collected the movables from Levi and then sold them to Yehudah. Yehudah collected only by virtue of Shimon's right, as Chacham Tzvi explains in his gloss to Taz at the end of siman 85, and a creditor does not seize movable property from purchasers. In contrast, where Shimon waives Levi's debt, Levi himself remains the borrower. The movables in his hands are therefore not called transferred movable property, and especially since Levi's personal obligation is itself subject to Reuven, because Shimon could not waive that personal lien. Reuven can therefore collect from them. Ketzot HaChoshen's question in seif katan 7 from a deposit is the same as Tumim's question in siman 88 seif katan 10, which I answered there. In Shach, seif katan 12, there is a textual error. The correct reading is: "as Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote above in seif 1, namely with respect to a claim that it is a trust document or paid, even though there is no distinction..." The words "when he owes him" are an error.
And if after he became obligated. Knesset HaGedolah writes in this siman that if one wrote in the promissory note, "to him and not to his creditor," Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply. In my view this is incorrect. In an ordinary condition, if the condition is violated the transaction is void, as where one gives a gift to a slave on condition that the master have no rights in it. If the master would acquire rights, the gift itself is void and the money returns to the donor, so the master indeed acquires nothing. Here, however, if we say that by virtue of the condition the loan is void whenever it would become subject to the creditor, then the money remains the property of the second lender, and precisely for that reason it becomes subject to the first creditor. Thus the condition cannot prevent Rabbi Natan's lien.
