Shimon cannot waive Levi's debt. The authorities explain that although a sold debt instrument can ordinarily be waived, whether because sale of debt instruments is rabbinic or because the seller retains the debtor's personal obligation according to Rabbeinu Tam, Rabbi Natan's lien is biblical. The Torah itself subjects even the personal obligation of the final debtor to the first creditor. Shimon therefore cannot waive Levi's obligation. A difficulty arises because the Gemara says Rabbi Natan's lien does not ordinarily operate with movables. If Levi were to pay Shimon with movables and Shimon then give them back to Levi, Reuven could not ordinarily follow those movables. Why, then, should Shimon's mere verbal waiver be less effective than actual repayment and return? The answer is that even where the movable property lien is weak, the personal obligation itself remains strong. Shimon's waiver cannot destroy the personal debt belonging through Rabbi Natan to Reuven. Even if the lien on particular movables does not pass with the same force, Levi still personally owes Reuven. A debtor remains obligated to pay even where there is no enforceable property lien, just as under the view that ordinary liens are not biblical a debtor nevertheless must repay his debt. Thus if Levi owns land, Reuven can collect because the land lien remains. If he owns only movables, Reuven still has Levi's personal obligation. Shimon's waiver cannot release that obligation because it is no longer solely his to forgive. This differs from an actual purchaser of the debt instrument, who acquired only the transferable documentary right and not the original personal obligation. Accordingly, the weakness of Rabbi Natan's lien over movables concerns the ability to follow particular transferred assets. It does not mean the final debtor's personal obligation disappears. This distinction resolves the apparent contradiction.
To waive Levi. Ramban, Rashba, and Ran ask from the case of a married woman or slave whose damages cannot ordinarily be collected immediately. The Gemara suggests selling the woman's ketubah to the injured party, but answers that she would certainly waive it to her husband. How could she waive if the ketubah obligation was already subject to the injured party under Rabbi Natan? The Shach answers correctly. If the ketubah is sold to the injured party in satisfaction of the damages, the injured party has now been paid by receiving the value of the ketubah. He stands from that point as a purchaser, not as the original first creditor enforcing Rabbi Natan. The special Rabbi Natan lien has ended because his damages claim has been satisfied through the transfer. A purchaser of a debt instrument remains subject to the seller's power of waiver. Tumim objects that if beit din appraised the ketubah at its current benefit value and transferred it to the injured party, and the woman later waives it, perhaps the original damages claim should revive because the appraisal was mistaken. That is not so. The appraisal was correct when made. The later waiver is a subsequent act that destroys the acquired asset, like a seller who sells land and the land is later flooded. That later loss does not turn the original sale into a mistaken transaction. Tumim further argues that even if the woman's own obligation toward the injured party has been satisfied, the husband's personal obligation on the ketubah was already subjected to the injured party and should not disappear. But once the intermediate obligation that connected the two was extinguished through payment, Rabbi Natan's relationship likewise ends. The final debtor's personal obligation belonged to the first creditor only through the intermediate debt. When that intermediate debt is fully paid by transfer of the ketubah, the special lien terminates. The injured party thereafter holds the ketubah only as purchaser. The Shach's explanation is therefore fully sound.
To extend his time. The Shach writes that even with a deposited or borrowed object, where the owner entrusted it onward, he cannot extend the final custodian's time because of Rabbi Natan's lien. At first this is difficult because Rabbi Natan ordinarily does not apply to movables. The Shach's reasoning must be that a borrower cannot sell another person's object. The owner's right is therefore strong, like a land right, and the final custodian cannot evade it through transfer. Nevertheless, the Mishnah at the end of Ketubot creates a serious difficulty. If a person dies leaving a wife, creditor, and heirs, and has a deposit or debt in another person's hands, Rabbi Tarfon says it is given to the weakest claimant while Rabbi Akiva says it is given to the heirs. If an intact deposit were truly governed by Rabbi Natan like land, the ordinary creditor should have a direct Torah claim to it already during the debtor's lifetime. Why should it pass to heirs? One could force a distinction and say the Mishnah's cases are divided: the deposit goes to the wife and the debt to the creditor, depending on which liens apply. But the more correct approach is what I wrote above. An intact deposit is not subject to Rabbi Natan at all. The custodian's property does not become indebted until negligence or another event creating liability. While the object remains intact, it is simply the depositor's property. The creditor may reach it through ordinary collection, but the depositor may also transfer it according to the normal rules governing movables. Therefore the Shach's use of Rabbi Natan for an intact deposit is not persuasive.
And Levi became subjected to Reuven. Knesset HaGedolah cites Rashach that the intermediate debtor may stipulate at the outset that his debtor's obligation shall run only to him and not become subject to another person's claim. In that event, he says, he may later waive. In my view, if Reuven's debt already existed before Shimon created Levi's obligation, such a stipulation is ineffective. It attempts to exclude a Torah lien already belonging to Reuven and is comparable to stipulating against Torah law. The Rosh's responsum permitting a donor to give property on condition that the recipient's creditor have no right in it is different. There the creditor has only an ordinary lien that takes effect upon collection, while ownership remains with the recipient. The donor can therefore shape the gift before the creditor acquires anything in its body. Rabbi Natan's lien is stronger. As explained above, the underlying debt itself is treated as belonging directly to the first creditor. Shimon's hand regarding Levi's debt is therefore like Reuven's own hand. A condition saying that the debt should belong only to Shimon attempts to remove Reuven from something the Torah already places in his ownership. There is also a distinction between a gratuitous undertaking and a debt created for consideration. A donor can shape his gift with conditions. A borrower who received money cannot ordinarily use a condition to eliminate liens that the law itself attaches to the obligation. Thus even apart from the special strength of Rabbi Natan, the proposed condition has limited scope.
An accommodation document. The Tur rules that where the intermediate debt instrument contains no credibility clause, the first creditor cannot collect until the intermediate creditor takes the oath that would ordinarily be required. If that creditor says the instrument is paid, the collection fails. The Taz asks how a credibility clause helps, since the intermediate creditor is now admitting payment and the borrower never gave him credibility to say the opposite of his own admission. The Shach rejects the Tur and Baal HaTerumot and says that because Rabbi Natan removes the intermediate creditor's power of waiver, his refusal to swear should not defeat the first creditor. The Tur can be defended through the structure of Rabbi Natan's lien. The first creditor receives only the right that Shimon truly possesses against Levi. If Shimon's right is weak and cannot presently be collected without Shimon's oath, it has not yet acquired the strength necessary to pass as a collectible right to Reuven. Rabbi Natan does not create a stronger debt than the intermediate creditor himself had. Where the instrument contains an effective credibility clause, Shimon's right against Levi is independently strong. He could collect without an oath. The debt is therefore treated as fully established and as though already within Shimon's effective control. It then becomes subject to Reuven through Rabbi Natan. Shimon's later statement that it was paid cannot injure Reuven, because at that point the debt has already passed into Reuven's right and Shimon is no longer believed to prejudice another. The credibility clause thus does not help because we believe Shimon's present words. It helps because it made Shimon's underlying lien strong before his present admission. This preserves the rulings of Baal HaTerumot and the Tur.
The money he returned to the recalcitrant party. Rosh explains that one who returns property or payment to a person in defiance of an existing lien can be liable for damaging another person's lien. This principle also explains the opinion in siman 66 that if a debtor pays the original creditor after the debt instrument was sold, he is not discharged from the purchaser. At first one might ask why payment is worse than waiver. A seller of a debt instrument can ordinarily waive the debt, so why should the debtor who actually pays the seller remain liable to the purchaser? The difference is who causes the damage. In waiver, the debtor does nothing. The seller himself destroys the purchaser's right by waiving, and the seller is liable for that damage. In payment, however, the debtor affirmatively directs the money to the wrong person. Since repayment against a creditor's will can count as repayment, the debtor's act itself destroys or impairs the purchaser's lien. He therefore damages the purchaser's right. Baal HaTerumot proves that payment to the seller does not discharge the purchaser from the sugya concerning a found receipt, where there is concern that a debtor may have paid the original creditor after the debt was sold. The point of the proof is especially that the debtor cannot excuse himself by saying he did not know of the sale. A sale of a debt instrument has publicity sufficient for this purpose. The basic liability follows naturally from the rule of damaging another person's lien.
