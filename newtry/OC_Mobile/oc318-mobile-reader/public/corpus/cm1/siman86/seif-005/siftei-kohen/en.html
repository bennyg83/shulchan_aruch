Shimon cannot forgive the debt, etc. At first glance Mechaber’s categorical ruling, following Tur, is surprising because many authorities appear to disagree. Rosh in the second chapter of Ketubot, Tosafot’s first answer there, Baal HaMaor, Ramban in Milchamot and in the responsum cited by Baal HaTerumot, Maggid Mishneh, Nimukei Yosef, and Rabbeinu Yerucham can be read as allowing the intermediate creditor to forgive the debt even after Rabbi Natan’s lien has arisen. Ran’s final explanation also appears to accept that possibility. After careful analysis, however, the primary ruling is Tosafot’s second answer and the view of Raavad and those who follow him: once the second debtor is directly obligated to the first creditor through Rabbi Natan, the intermediate creditor cannot forgive the debt. Rosh adopted this in later responsa and retracted the implication of his earlier ruling. Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, Or Zarua, Maharam Recanati, Ritva in the name of Raavad and Rashba, Maharshal, and Sema likewise rule so. There are several proofs. Ketubot says that the second debtor cannot tell the first creditor, “You are not my litigant,” because Rabbi Natan makes the first creditor his direct claimant. A debt directly owed to the first creditor cannot be extinguished by the intermediate creditor. Pesachim likewise treats the second debtor as though he had borrowed from the first creditor himself. Otherwise, land collected by heirs would be newly acquired property unavailable to their father’s creditor. Further, an admission that a note is paid is itself the strongest possible forgiveness, because payment extinguishes the note and its lien. Yet the poskim rule that once the debt is subject through Rabbi Natan, the intermediate creditor is not believed to call the note paid or a trust arrangement. If he retained power to forgive, his statement would at least operate as forgiveness even if false as a factual admission. The same reasoning applies to a condition or any other assertion that invalidates the note. Once the debt belongs by Torah lien to the first creditor, the intermediate creditor cannot nullify it in any form. The general rule that a seller of a promissory note may later forgive it does not prove otherwise. In an ordinary sale, according to Rabbeinu Tam, the seller transfers the property lien but cannot transfer the debtor’s personal obligation. The debtor’s body remains obligated to the seller, and when the seller forgives that personal obligation, the property lien falls because property is only a guarantor. Under Rabbi Natan, however, the second debtor’s personal obligation itself passes by Torah law to the first creditor. He cannot say, “You are not my litigant.” Nothing remains in the intermediate creditor’s hands to forgive. This distinction remains valid even for authorities who hold that sale of notes is Torah law. The inability to transfer the personal obligation in an ordinary sale explains the power of forgiveness, whereas Rabbi Natan expressly creates a direct personal obligation. Ran and Ritva likewise distinguish the cases, even while treating sale of notes as Torah law. This also disproves rulings that make the difference depend solely on whether sale of notes is rabbinic. It follows that the intermediate creditor is not believed to assert any matter that would invalidate the note, including trust, payment, condition, or waiver. Conversely, in an ordinary sale of a note, the seller who says it was paid, a trust arrangement, or otherwise invalid is effective at least as one who forgives it, since Shmuel teaches that he may forgive even gratuitously. It also follows that if, only later, it becomes known that at the time of the alleged forgiveness or admission the intermediate creditor already owed the first creditor, the act was void from that earlier time. Rabbi Natan’s lien attached immediately even though no one then knew of it. Bach’s distinction between a known and unknown prior debt is therefore incorrect. The French authorities cited by Baal HaTerumot distinguish judicial collection only because they hold Rabbi Natan does not attach until beit din acts. We do not follow that view. Pesachim and Ketubot show that the lien attaches at the time the relevant obligations exist. Indeed, once beit din transfers the note to the first creditor in formal payment, the result may be weaker rather than stronger for this purpose. The transfer then resembles a sale of the note in satisfaction of the debt, and the former owner may afterward possess the ordinary seller’s power of forgiveness, as explained below in siman 101, seif 5. Ramban relies principally on Bava Kamma’s case of a married woman who injured another. Since she has no present assets, the Gemara asks why she cannot sell her ketubah for its present speculative value to the injured party. It answers that she would certainly forgive it to her husband, so beit din will not engage in a futile procedure. Ramban argues that her husband should already be directly obligated to the injured party through Rabbi Natan, which would make forgiveness impossible. He also cites the sugya of one who finds a receipt. Ran answers that Rabbi Natan requires payment only when the debt becomes due and does not necessarily create a present lien, especially with a ketubah that may never become collectible. This wording might suggest that an unmatured loan can be forgiven. Ritva narrows the exception to a ketubah, whose liability is contingent and not collectible during the husband’s life. Rashba’s responsum concerning unmatured notes is based on the separate position that forgiveness remains possible under Rabbi Natan until collection. In my opinion, even a ketubah cannot be forgiven when it is presently subject through Rabbi Natan. Once the husband is directly obligated to the wife’s creditor as he is to the wife, she cannot extinguish that creditor’s right. Ramban himself correctly rejects the distinction based merely on the ketubah’s deferred nature, though I disagree with his conclusion that forgiveness is therefore always possible. The Bava Kamma case is resolved differently. Before the woman sells the ketubah, her husband is indeed directly obligated through Rabbi Natan, but the injured party cannot collect until divorce or widowhood, and if she dies first he may receive nothing. If she sells the entire ketubah to the injured party at its present value and he accepts it as payment for the injury, her debt to him is discharged immediately. Rabbi Natan then ceases to apply, because she no longer owes him anything. The husband’s future obligation to the purchaser arises solely from the purchased ketubah, like an ordinary note sale, and she may forgive it under Shmuel’s rule. The same is true if Reuven owes Shimon one hundred and sells him, in satisfaction of that debt, a note for two hundred against Levi. Once Shimon accepts the note as payment, Reuven’s debt is extinguished. Shimon cannot later revive his original claim merely because Reuven forgives the purchased note. The sale was payment, and the purchased note remains subject to the ordinary rule allowing the seller to forgive. Therefore, while the woman has not sold her ketubah, she cannot forgive it because Rabbi Natan protects the injured party. If she sells it to him and he accepts it as payment, she may thereafter forgive it to her husband. This resolves the Gemara without distinguishing ketubah from other debts and without weakening Rabbi Natan before maturity. Ramban, Ritva, and Ran struggled unnecessarily because this distinction between direct collection and a completed sale in payment was not drawn. Maharshal raises a further question from Bava Metzia. Perhaps the woman had borrowed against the ketubah, in which case Rabbi Natan should prevent forgiveness. The Gemara, however, is concerned with the ordinary possibility that she sold her ketubah for its speculative value. People commonly purchase such a claim because they may profit if she is divorced or widowed. We do not ordinarily suspect that someone lent her money against it, because that lender risks losing everything if she dies first and gains no upside beyond repayment. There is therefore no need to construct a Rabbi Natan debt in that sugya. The conclusion is as follows. There is no difference between a ketubah and any other obligation. Whenever Rabbi Natan’s direct lien applies, the intermediate creditor cannot forgive the debt, even before its due date. Therefore, a married woman who injured or borrowed from another cannot forgive her ketubah while her obligation to that person remains outstanding. If, however, she sells the ketubah to another when she owes no one, or sells it to the injured party or lender and they accept it as payment, she may thereafter forgive it under the law of an ordinary note sale. Likewise, if Reuven owes Shimon and transfers to him Levi’s note as full payment, Reuven may thereafter forgive that note. Until Shimon accepts the note in satisfaction of his debt, however, Reuven cannot forgive it, even though the note has not yet matured.
And not to extend his time. The Rosh wrote in a responsum, cited by the Tur at the end of this siman: You were uncertain whether, so long as Shimon owes Reuven anything, Shimon is regarded as his servant and has no power to confer a benefit with his master's money, meaning to lend money to Levi for a term. This is not so, for Shimon could have given movable property to whomever he wished, and Levi became bound to Reuven only after Levi received the loan. Once the loan was made, however, Levi became bound to Reuven with the entire lien that Shimon possessed against him, and Shimon thereafter has no power either to forgive the debt or to extend its time. This also appears to be the view of the Mechaber here and at the end of this siman. Thus, even if it was not known when Shimon forgave the debt or extended the term that he then owed Reuven, but it was later established that he did owe Reuven at that time and Reuven now has no source of collection other than this debt, the forgiveness or extension is void, as explained above at length. If, however, Shimon did not yet owe Reuven at that time, his forgiveness or extension is effective, even though he later borrowed from Reuven and then became bound to him. The same applies if Shimon then declared that the document was a document of trust or had already been paid. Although collection under Rabbi Natan's rule does not generally depend on whether Shimon already owed Reuven when Levi's debt arose, that rule concerns a statement made after Shimon became indebted. If Shimon forgave Levi, extended the term, or declared the document fictitious or paid before Shimon became indebted to Reuven, the document was already nullified. Likewise, the Rama's ruling in Even HaEzer that if a wife forgave her husband the creditor has no claim must be understood as referring to forgiveness made before she borrowed and consumed the creditor's funds.
He is not believed. The Sema writes that this applies specifically where the document contains a clause of credibility, or where Levi did not say to Shimon, "Swear to me," for otherwise Shimon cannot collect without an oath and nothing may be extracted from Levi. The Tur and Mechaber ruled similarly above, following the Baal HaTerumot. I challenged that ruling above and demonstrated that even where there is no credibility clause, and Levi demands an oath but Shimon refuses to swear, this does not invalidate the document. The document retains its presumptive force, and collection is made from Levi under Rabbi Natan's rule.
If, after Levi became liable to Reuven in beit din, and so forth. It appears that liability established by beit din is not essential. Once Levi is bound to Reuven under Rabbi Natan's rule, the first creditor, Reuven, is himself Levi's litigant, as stated in the Gemara. There is therefore no legal distinction between a judgment already issued and one not yet issued. The formulation merely reflects the ordinary case, because before proceedings Levi can say that he did not know Shimon owed Reuven, or that he believed Shimon possessed other assets from which Reuven would be paid. The responsum of the Rosh and the Mechaber's ruling in the following seif should be understood similarly. Authorities cite Rabbi Meshullam son of Rabbi Natan as ruling that if Levi returned the money to Shimon after Reuven asserted his claim, even before final judgment, Levi must also pay Reuven. Although one responsum expresses uncertainty, Rabbi Meshullam's ruling is primary. The Rama likewise rules in Even HaEzer that a depositary who returns property to the husband after the wife has sued him must pay her, which indicates that final judgment is unnecessary. Indeed, even the phrase "after he accepted the claim" is not exact. If Reuven warned Levi not to pay Shimon because Shimon owed Reuven and had no other assets, and Reuven later proves this, or Levi admits that he knew both facts yet nevertheless paid Shimon, Levi must pay Reuven. At the time of payment Levi knew that the debt was bound to Reuven and that Reuven was his direct claimant. Payment to Shimon therefore did not discharge him. Levi cannot excuse himself by saying that he did not know the law of Rabbi Natan, for one who is legally bound must ascertain the law and his lien is not removed until he pays the person entitled to payment. The Beit Yosef cites a responsum that was uncertain whether, if Levi says he had already paid Shimon before Reuven sued him, Reuven may compel an oath. The Beit Yosef answers that no oath can be imposed on a claim of uncertainty, though a general ban may be declared. The source of that uncertainty followed the view opposed to Rabbi Meshullam and reasoned that, because Reuven has a direct interest in the money and knows that Shimon remained indebted, there are grounds for suspicion sufficient to impose an oath even on an uncertain claim. According to the primary view, however, whenever Levi knew that Shimon owed Reuven and had no other assets, Levi must pay Reuven despite having paid Shimon. The question of an oath arises only where Levi says that he did not know of the debt or of Shimon's lack of assets. In that case there are no supporting circumstances, and all agree that no oath is imposed upon an uncertain claim.
