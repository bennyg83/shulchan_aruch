To waive, etc. A purchaser of a debt instrument acquires the document only by rabbinic law, whereas Rabbi Natan's direct lien is Torah law. Even according to the opinion that sale of a debt instrument is effective by Torah law, the seller cannot transfer the debtor's personal obligation itself to the purchaser, because the debtor never personally obligated himself to that purchaser. In an ordinary chain of loans, by contrast, the intermediate debtor personally obligated himself to his own creditor, and that very personal obligation becomes subordinated to the first creditor through Rabbi Natan. Therefore the intermediate creditor cannot waive it. See Tumim. I wrote that a married woman who is entitled to a ketubah, even for a thousand, but owes money to others, may waive her ketubah while she remains married because Rabbi Natan's lien has not yet attached to a presently collectible obligation. After her husband's death or divorce she can no longer waive it. As to dowry property, I wrote that according to the Rosh she may be unable to waive it even during marriage. I further wrote in Tumim that inability to waive applies where the final debtor owns land. If he owns only movable property, he may be able to claim that the waiver is effective, and likewise where the first creditor's claim is only oral. See there at length. If the second debtor wrote in his debt instrument to the intermediate creditor an express condition that the debt shall not become subject to the latter's creditors, that is a valid monetary condition and Rabbi Natan's lien does not attach.
And not to extend his time. There is no practical distinction between outright waiver and extension of the due date, because an extension is itself a partial waiver of immediate rights. Just as the intermediate creditor cannot waive, he cannot extend. He is, however, permitted from the outset to lend on a long term, and the first creditor cannot prevent him from doing so. At most this resembles making a gift of movable property, which the creditor ordinarily cannot prevent. So writes the Rosh. Although the first creditor may have a document containing a movable property clause, the market protection enactment now limits collection from transferred movables, as discussed above in siman 60. If the intermediate creditor extended the term or waived the debt while genuinely unaware that he was indebted to Reuven, and only afterward the prior debt became known, the waiver and extension are void. Shach, and this is clear. See Tumim. I wrote that one who undertakes to support another person for a number of years, fails to provide the support, and the recipient borrows money from a third party for his needs, creates a debt recoverable directly from the supporter through Rabbi Natan. After the support period has passed, the recipient cannot waive that reimbursement claim against the supporter. But if he waives before the support period passes, no matured reimbursement obligation yet exists and the waiver can be effective. See there.
An accommodation document. This applies where the document contains a credibility clause. Without such a clause the holder could collect only by oath, and once he admits that the document was only an accommodation he cannot take that oath. See what I wrote above in siman 47 at length.
Or it was paid, etc. Meaning payment allegedly made before it became known that the debt was subject to Rabbi Natan. If payment occurred afterward, even if the claim is factually true, the second debtor must nevertheless pay again because he knowingly paid the wrong person. See what I wrote above in simanim 54 and 65 regarding why he is not believed through a migo that he could have written an undated receipt. See there.
He is not believed. If the second debtor says, "I paid before I knew that my creditor owed you," while the first creditor claims the payment occurred afterward, then if the first creditor asserts certainty he may impose the appropriate oath; if he is uncertain, only a general ban applies. Shach. This concerns an oral debt or a document without a credibility clause. If the document contains a credibility clause, even where both immediate lender and borrower agree that it was paid they are not believed against the first creditor. This is clear.
And if after he became obligated, etc. The Shach concludes that formal adjudication is not required. Once the first creditor warns the final debtor, "Do not pay your immediate creditor because he owes me," and the final debtor knows that this is true, if he nevertheless pays him he must pay a second time. See Tumim. Although regarding a sold debt instrument several authorities hold above in siman 66 that if the debtor mistakenly pays the seller he need not pay the purchaser again, there the seller retained the power to waive the debt, and payment is therefore no worse than waiver. Here, by contrast, the intermediate creditor has no power to waive. Accordingly, based on what I concluded above that where the final debtor owns only movable property waiver may still be effective, payment should likewise exempt him unless beit din specifically ordered him not to pay and he violated that order. See Tumim.
He must pay. After Reuven swears that his own document is valid. Even if Reuven's document contains a credibility clause and Shimon admits the debt, because direct collection causes a loss to Levi it is treated like collection from encumbered property, which requires an oath. The credibility clause does not eliminate that oath.
