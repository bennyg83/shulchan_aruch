Shimon cannot waive it, etc. The Shach discusses this at length, because many authorities disagree and hold that he can waive it. Although the Shach writes extensively and ultimately agrees with the Mechaber that he cannot waive it, he nevertheless analyzes with great acuity whether, with respect to a ketubah during the husband's lifetime, Rabbi Natan's rule applies so that she cannot waive it, or whether it does not. This depends on the Ramban's question regarding the Gemara's proposal, "Let her sell it to the injured party," to which the Gemara answers that with respect to her husband she will certainly waive it. The Ramban asks: how can she waive it, since the husband is obligated directly to the injured party under Rabbi Natan's rule? From this the Ramban concludes that even under Rabbi Natan's rule the intermediate creditor can waive the debt. The Ran, however, answers that Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to a ketubah. The Shach objects that there is no difficulty at all. If a woman who caused injury sells her ketubah to the injured party for its present speculative value, and he accepts the ketubah as payment for the injury, then he has received payment for the value of his injury and her liability for that injury is extinguished. He now stands only as an ordinary purchaser of the ketubah, and she can waive it, since Rabbi Natan's rule no longer applies after the injury debt itself has been discharged. This is the essence of the Shach's argument. At first glance his words appear sound. Upon reflection, however, they are insufficient to reject the words of the Ramban and Ran. First, if she voluntarily sold her ketubah in payment of the damages, it would indeed be obvious that the Shach is correct, because payment would extinguish the injury debt completely. Here, however, beit din assesses and transfers her ketubah to the injured party as payment at its present speculative value. Does that assessment itself extinguish the original lien absolutely? Consider a creditor to whom beit din transferred land in payment, after which it was discovered that the land did not belong to the debtor. Did the creditor's original lien thereby disappear? Certainly not. He may still collect from the date of his original lien, because the judicial collection was mistaken. So too here, if the ketubah is waived and the transferred asset consequently disappears, the judicial collection was void from the outset. There was no valid collection at all, and the original injury debt was never extinguished. Second, the matter is even clearer. The entire reason a seller of a promissory note can waive the underlying debt is that the bodily obligation, the personal debtor obligation, was never transferred to the purchaser. It remains with the seller, because that personal obligation cannot be sold. Were it possible to sell the personal obligation, the seller plainly would no longer be able to waive it. By contrast, under Rabbi Natan's rule the personal obligation of the ultimate debtor is directly owed to the first creditor. Suppose, then, that the middle creditor, who has a claim against the ultimate debtor, comes afterward and strengthens the first creditor's position further by transferring his own rights to him through a sale in payment. Does the existing personal obligation somehow disappear merely because the middle creditor added a sale to the lien that already existed? On the contrary, he strengthened the first creditor's position. If the personal obligation could be sold, the wording by which the middle creditor transfers all his rights would transfer it as well. The only reason ordinary sale does not transfer it is that it is intrinsically nontransferable. Here, however, the first creditor already possesses that personal obligation under Rabbi Natan's rule. Why should the subsequent sale extinguish it? The Shach is correct that through the sale the middle creditor's own lien is extinguished, for he has used his claim as consideration. But that does not explain why the ultimate debtor's personal obligation to the first creditor should disappear. The ultimate debtor was already directly obligated to the first creditor before the sale, and he remains so afterward. Nothing occurred that could extinguish that obligation. The Baal HaTerumot speaks explicitly in accordance with this analysis. With due respect, the Shach erred by equating the extinguishment of the middle creditor's claim against the ultimate debtor with extinguishment of the ultimate debtor's direct obligation to the first creditor. They are not the same. The middle creditor's claim can indeed disappear through the sale, but the ultimate debtor's obligation under Rabbi Natan's rule does not thereby disappear. Before the sale he was obligated directly to the first creditor. Even an ordinary sale, if personal obligations were transferable, would only reinforce that result. Where that direct obligation already exists by force of law, how can an additional sale remove it? The early authorities are therefore correct without difficulty. See Baal HaTerumot, Gate 51, Part 2, who writes explicitly that the seller can waive an ordinary note because the purchaser had no prior right in the debtor. This demonstrates what I have written: where the first creditor already has a direct lien against the ultimate debtor, the middle creditor's later act cannot by itself remove that prior right. This is clear. However, there remains an apparent difficulty. Suppose the damages amount to ten dinars. Under Rabbi Natan's rule, only ten dinars of the ketubah are directly encumbered to the injured party. If the woman now sells a ketubah of one hundred dinars to him for ten dinars, reflecting its speculative value, what has he gained? As to the remaining ninety dinars, she can waive them, because Rabbi Natan's rule created a direct lien only to the extent of the ten dinar injury debt. The injured party's additional interest in the other ninety arises only through the sale, and that sale interest is subject to waiver. It therefore seems that the entire exercise would still be pointless. According to what I wrote above, however, the matter is resolved. It is true that if beit din transferred to him a portion of the ketubah whose face amount exceeded the damages, the question would remain. Rather, the Gemara's proposal is that only ten dinars of the ketubah's face amount, corresponding to the injury debt, should be sold to him. In the case of a woman who injured her husband, the Gemara says that her ketubah is much greater than the damages, implying that when she injured another person the proposal works even when the ketubah is not much greater than the damages. The proper arrangement is therefore as follows. She sells him a ten dinar face amount of the ketubah for, say, five dinars because of the speculative nature of future collection. The remaining five dinars of the injury debt remains an ordinary personal debt against her and will be collectible if she later acquires property from elsewhere. She cannot waive the ten dinar portion sold to the injured party, because the only reason an ordinary seller can waive is that the husband was never personally obligated to the purchaser. Here, however, the husband was already directly obligated to the injured party under Rabbi Natan's rule. What difference does it make to the husband that the woman also remains personally indebted to the injured party for the unpaid portion and may later have to pay from other property? Ultimately, the full damages remain a present debt. So long as the injured party has not actually received payment from elsewhere, the husband's direct obligation under Rabbi Natan's rule remains in force for the full amount of the existing injury debt. It is true that if the woman acquired an inheritance during the husband's lifetime and the injured party then collected five dinars from that inheritance, she could thereafter waive five dinars of the ketubah to her husband. Rabbi Natan's rule would then support only the remaining five dinars, because only that amount of the injury debt would remain unpaid. But as long as she has not paid from other property, the direct obligation remains in full force. The mere fact that she is also personally liable to pay from assets she may later acquire does not presently extinguish the husband's direct liability. When she later acquires assets, the creditor can of course collect from them, but until then Rabbi Natan's obligation continues. Thus the injured party has a genuine possibility of profit from the transaction, and the Gemara's concern cannot be dismissed as mere futile exertion. This is precise, and many have erred on this point. Indeed, according to the Shach's approach, one could explain Tosafot there, s.v. "and even if she waived it." Tosafot asks about the Gemara's suggestion that she sell the ketubah to the injured party. The Gemara assumes that even if she later waives it, he has lost nothing because at present he has nothing in hand. Tosafot objects that he certainly does lose: without the sale he could eventually collect the damages from her ketubah, whereas if he buys the ketubah and she then waives it, he loses that avenue of collection. At first glance, if Tosafot's question concerns a case in which even without a sale she could waive the ketubah to her husband and thereby prevent collection, the question would arise only where the ketubah exceeds the damages. For example, if the damages are ten and the ketubah is twenty, she would have to waive the entire ketubah to defeat the injured party, because whatever the husband owes her is directly subject to Rabbi Natan's rule. In that case she would herself sacrifice the remaining ten dinars that otherwise would have belonged to her. By contrast, if she first sold the entire twenty dinar ketubah to the injured party in payment of the ten dinar damages, she would retain nothing and would have every incentive to waive it. I wrote earlier in siman 66 that selling half a ketubah raises its own difficulties. Why, then, should Tosafot force itself into a strained answer? It could simply say that at this stage the Gemara has not yet limited the case to one in which the ketubah greatly exceeds the damages. The Mishnah states generally that one who injures a married woman faces difficulty, even where the ketubah does not exceed the damages. In such a case the Gemara's question, "Let her sell it," would indeed be strong. Later, when the Gemara discusses a woman who injured her husband, it likewise initially assumes that the Mishnah speaks generally, including a case in which the ketubah does not exceed the damages. If Tosafot's answer were merely that the ketubah exceeds the damages, the Gemara could itself have answered that directly. Besides, as the Maharsha notes, Tosafot's entire question belongs to the Gemara's initial stage, before the final answer involving Rabbi Meir's prohibition against leaving a wife without a ketubah. Once that final answer is reached, Tosafot's question disappears. Therefore Tosafot's question must be addressing the initial analysis. According to the Shach's interpretation, however, Tosafot's question is readily understood. Without a sale, she cannot waive the amount corresponding to the damages because it is directly subject to Rabbi Natan's rule. If she sells the ketubah to the injured party, however, the Shach contends that the injury debt itself is thereby extinguished, Rabbi Natan's lien disappears, and she can then waive the ketubah. Thus the injured party truly loses by accepting the proposed sale. Yet even if one grants this reading, Tosafot's answer is extremely strained. Tosafot says that because the injured party currently has nothing, the possibility that he may fail to collect is not called a present loss. But the contingent right to collect is plainly property with value. People pay money for such a right, as the Gemara itself says when it speaks of selling the ketubah for its present speculative value. False witnesses who attempt to cause a woman to lose her ketubah are liable according to the amount a person would pay for that chance of collection, as taught at the beginning of Makkot. How, then, can Tosafot say there is no loss at all? One could answer that there is a double uncertainty: perhaps she will never reach actual collection, and even if she does perhaps she will not waive it, because at the initial stage the Gemara has not yet concluded that she certainly will. But this is difficult. Once the Gemara answers that she certainly will waive, there remains an actual prospective loss, and it cannot be dismissed as futile exertion. Tosafot's question therefore remains powerful, and the Ran was justified in distinguishing a ketubah from an ordinary promissory note. Even according to the Shach there is a further proof for the Ramban and Ran from this very sugya. Why should beit din require the woman to sell the ketubah itself? They could instead assess how much the damages are worth as a contingent future claim against the ketubah, given that collection will occur only if she is widowed or divorced. That contingent claim may be worth more than a fixed present payment. She could then execute a separate promissory note to the injured party for that assessed amount, secured by her ketubah through Rabbi Natan's rule. The injured party could sell that promissory note for its present speculative value. What difference is there between selling the ketubah and selling a separate note whose payment is secured by the ketubah? If the ketubah ever becomes collectible, the holder of the note collects. Such a note would actually create a stronger relationship to the ketubah under Rabbi Natan's rule than an outright sale of the ketubah, which according to many authorities operates only by rabbinic enactment. There would then be no concern that she would waive in favor of her husband, because the husband's debt to her would stand directly encumbered to the noteholder under Rabbi Natan's rule. This argument avoids the Shach's objection and all the difficulties discussed above. One might respond, with difficulty, that selling a one hundred dinar ketubah for ten does not involve ribbit, because the purchaser is buying an uncertain asset. But if the injured party is presently owed only ten and the woman executes a note for a larger amount because collection is uncertain, perhaps that increase would be treated as ribbit. Even this is strained. The Hagahot Ashri in Ketubot, chapter Af Al Pi, explains that when a husband adds one fifth to a ketubah there is no ribbit because the increase is not tied to time. If the obligation becomes payable immediately, he must pay the entire amount immediately. Here too the additional face amount is not compensation for time. If she were widowed immediately, the full amount would become collectible at once. The discount reflects only the uncertainty of whether the condition for collection will ever occur. Furthermore, in the case where she injured her husband, the Gemara says that when the ketubah exceeds the damages she cannot sell it to him because Rabbi Meir forbids leaving a wife without a ketubah. Suppose the damages are fifty and the ketubah is one hundred. Beit din could award the husband fifty dinars of the ketubah at a present speculative value of, say, twenty five. The remaining twenty five of his damages could be represented by a separate promissory note secured against the remaining fifty of the ketubah. The husband could then sell that note for its speculative value, perhaps twelve dinars. This would not make divorce easier in his eyes, because if he divorces her he must still pay the full remaining fifty, twenty five to her and twenty five to the noteholder. Yet the husband would gain the additional twelve dinars from selling the note, over and above the fifty of the ketubah that was judicially awarded to him at its present speculative value. The note purchaser would have no fear of waiver because Rabbi Natan's rule would directly secure his debt against the remaining ketubah. This is a clear proof for the Ramban and Ran. Examine it carefully. See also what I wrote above in siman 66 concerning the Mishnah's statement that if she is divorced she becomes liable. What novelty does that teach? And how can the Mishnah state categorically that she is liable? She could presumably waive her ketubah to the husband to defeat the injured party. The Mishnah's certainty therefore indicates that she cannot waive the portion encumbered to the injured party under Rabbi Natan's rule. That is precisely its teaching: after divorce she is definitely liable, without any possibility of eliminating the claim through waiver. We now return to Tosafot and the Rosh and ask which view they follow. It appears that the logic of Torah law supports the Ramban and Ran, quite apart from the proof I wrote at the beginning of this siman from the Rosh and Tur. I explained there at length that they too hold that the intermediate creditor cannot waive the debt, although I suggested that their basis is not specifically Rabbi Natan's rule but another principle. Tosafot asks how the woman can waive her ketubah, since it is forbidden to keep a wife without a ketubah. Tosafot answers that the husband can write her a new ketubah after the waiver. Tosafot then asks that the woman would still owe the purchaser damages under the law of dina d'garmi for causing the purchased ketubah to be lost, and answers that since she presently has no property from which to pay, this does not help the purchaser and the entire arrangement remains futile. This is very difficult. The initial acquisition was a purchase of her right to eventual ketubah payment. Even if she waives the first ketubah, the husband must write her a new one. She could then sell the new ketubah for its present speculative value to satisfy the damages she caused by waiving the first. If she waived again, he would again have to write a ketubah. The cycle would continue until actual collection became possible. Thus the purchaser does not necessarily lose anything merely because she waives the first ketubah. The Maharsha appears to have intended this difficulty, although his words are somewhat compressed. It is difficult to answer that before waiving she could collusively execute a promissory note to a third party, so that when the husband writes the new ketubah that third party's earlier note would take priority over the purchaser's damages claim. If we were concerned about such a device, the Gemara could never prove Shmuel's rule that a seller can waive a sold note. One cannot force the woman to sell her ketubah in a single instant. A day before the sale she could execute a note to someone else, and if it contained a broad acquisition clause, all her later property might already be encumbered. Yet the commentators repeatedly explain that we do not ordinarily suspect a person of arranging such a fraudulent collusion merely to injure another. With the husband himself there is also no concern that she will simply execute a debt equal to the ketubah in his favor, because that would make divorce easy in his eyes and would violate the prohibition against living with a wife who lacks a meaningful ketubah. Tosafot itself, s.v. "such as," indicates that only the special circumstance caused by her own act of injury permits the exceptional arrangement. Therefore this kind of artificial note cannot explain Tosafot. Nor is it persuasive to say that after the waiver the husband could evade the new ketubah by transferring away all his assets before writing it. If that were the concern, Tosafot need not say that she waives the ketubah entirely. She could simply release the purchaser's rights while retaining her ketubah against the husband, and he could continue living with her. Moreover, we do not generally assume such a deliberate scheme to strip the husband of assets merely to defeat the purchaser. To resolve Tosafot, there are two possible approaches. First, it may be said that when the Gemara states that with respect to her husband she will certainly waive, it does not mean only a waiver directly to the husband. The same concern exists after his death with respect to his heirs, because she is close to them and prefers that the money remain with them rather than pass to a stranger. This must in fact be said according to the Rambam, who holds that while a woman remains married she has no legal power to waive the ketubah to her husband. How then can the Gemara say that with respect to her husband she will certainly waive? See the Beit Shmuel. It is strained to say that the Rambam adopts Tosafot's device that the husband first divorces her, she then waives, and he later remarries her, for the Rambam rules without qualification that a married woman who injures another is difficult to collect from, while the wife of a kohen is not difficult to collect from because she can sell her ketubah, even though a kohen cannot divorce and then remarry her. I discussed the Tosafot issue in my novellae and above in siman 67. Rather, the Gemara must mean that after the husband's death she will waive the ketubah to his heirs because she is close to them and prefers them to an outsider. Tosafot can likewise accept this understanding. The Ramban's question then immediately arises: why should she be able to waive to the heirs when the ketubah is directly encumbered to the injured party under Rabbi Natan's rule? One must answer in accordance with the Ran that while the husband is alive the time for collection has not arrived and the ketubah is only a contingent future claim. Yet if the concern is specifically waiver to the heirs after the husband's death, the debt is then fixed and complete, and at that point the heirs should be directly obligated to the injured party under Rabbi Natan's rule. Tosafot therefore answers differently: she will waive to her husband during his lifetime, and he will write her a new ketubah. We may now say that Tosafot holds, as the Maharshal explains, that according to the view that liability for dina d'garmi is a rabbinic penalty, Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to that liability. I wrote above that the same type of distinction appears with a moser according to those who understand dina d'garmi as a penalty. Tosafot holds in several places that dina d'garmi is a penalty. Thus Tosafot's answer works as follows. When she waives the first ketubah she becomes liable to the purchaser for the loss under dina d'garmi. If the purchaser then seeks to reach the new ketubah that the husband writes for her, she can wait until the husband dies and waive that new ketubah to the heirs. At that point the heirs are not directly obligated to the purchaser under Rabbi Natan's rule, because the purchaser's claim against her is only the penal liability of dina d'garmi, to which Rabbi Natan does not apply. The purchaser therefore loses. Those who adopt this distinction can derive support from our sugya, since they accept Tosafot's question and answer and need a way to avoid the problem just described. Yet the reason cannot simply be that a rabbinic debt never falls under Rabbi Natan's rule. The Shach correctly argues that when the Sages created monetary obligations they generally fashioned them after Torah law. Indeed, instead of asking where in the Talmud we find Rabbi Natan in a rabbinic obligation, one can point to the opposite. In the case of one who was owed money by his brother, the Gemara says that we do not combine two stringencies in a ketubah. Rashi explains that one of the relevant leniencies is due to the ketubah being rabbinic, which shows that Rabbi Natan's rule by itself can operate even with a rabbinic ketubah. Even according to the Rif and those who explain that passage as referring to two enactments rather than two stringencies, the enactment of the Geonim concerning collection of movables can coexist with Rabbi Natan's rule. And we rule that a ketubah is rabbinic while Rabbi Natan still applies to it in appropriate circumstances. Rather, the distinction concerning dina d'garmi is that Rabbi Natan operates where the debtor's property is firmly and absolutely encumbered. According to the view that dina d'garmi is a penalty, if the tortfeasor dies before standing in judgment, the penalty is not imposed on his heirs even if he left encumbered property. There is therefore no fixed property lien at the moment of the damaging act from which Rabbi Natan can derive a direct obligation. The point is not simply that the obligation is rabbinic, but that a penalty of this type does not yet create an absolute lien. Even if this approach can explain some authorities, it is difficult to attribute it to Tosafot, for we do not find Tosafot explicitly stating anywhere that Rabbi Natan does not apply to dina d'garmi, and such a significant rule should have been explained. More importantly, this cannot explain the Rosh. The Rosh states explicitly in Bava Kamma that dina d'garmi is ordinary monetary law and not a penalty, following the Ramban. That is why the Rosh must distinguish between grama and garmi, a distinction that would be unnecessary according to the view that the entire category is merely a penalty. See below in siman 386. There is therefore another possible route in Tosafot. Tosafot may agree with the view of the Tosafot Yeshanim in Ketubot 19b, s.v. "such as," cited in Hagahot HaRosh and by many authorities, and see Bach at the end of siman 85 and Shach at the beginning of this siman. According to that view, Rabbi Natan's rule applies only if Levi was already indebted to Shimon before Shimon borrowed from Reuven. If Shimon first borrowed from Reuven and only afterward lent to Levi, Levi does not become directly obligated to Reuven under Rabbi Natan's rule. Accordingly Tosafot is readily explained. As noted, Tosafot could not answer that the woman will simply waive the original ketubah to the husband's heirs, because once the husband dies the ketubah becomes a fixed debt and the heirs would then be directly obligated to the purchaser under Rabbi Natan. Tosafot therefore says that she waives the first ketubah while the husband is alive and the husband then writes her a new ketubah. Her liability to the purchaser under dina d'garmi arises at the moment she waives the first ketubah. The replacement ketubah is created only afterward. Thus the purchaser's claim against her precedes the husband's new ketubah obligation to her. Under the Tosafot Yeshanim's view, a debt of the ultimate debtor created after the first creditor's claim does not become subject to Rabbi Natan. Therefore when the husband later dies she can waive the replacement ketubah to his heirs without the purchaser being able to invoke Rabbi Natan. This resolves the entire chain. This also explains another difficulty. When a woman sells her ketubah she ordinarily does not lose the right of ketubat benin dichrin, whereas a complete waiver may destroy that right. Why then are we certain she will waive? According to what has just been explained, the concern can be that she will waive to the heirs after the relevant sequence has been arranged, and the effect on ketubat benin dichrin can be understood accordingly. The same approach explains Tosafot's question based on Rabbi Meir, who holds that at the end of cohabitation a woman cannot waive the minimum ketubah. At first glance, why is this difficult? Rabbi Meir prevents waiver of the basic two hundred, but if her ketubah were four hundred she could certainly waive two hundred while retaining the required minimum. Since Tosafot already says the husband can write her a new ketubah, let him write the replacement first, leaving her with four hundred, and she can then waive the two hundred that she sold to the purchaser. That would preserve the minimum ketubah. According to our explanation, however, that arrangement would not work. If the husband writes the replacement ketubah before the waiver, then when she later waives the sold ketubah and becomes liable to the purchaser under dina d'garmi, the husband's replacement ketubah obligation already exists. The purchaser's new damages claim would then be later than the replacement ketubah and Rabbi Natan's rule would attach to it. She could not defeat the purchaser by later waiving to the heirs. Tosafot therefore needs the replacement ketubah to be written only after the first waiver. The purchaser's damages claim then precedes the new ketubah obligation, so Rabbi Natan does not attach under the Tosafot Yeshanim's temporal rule. Tosafot adds that if Rabbi Meir prevents waiver at the end of cohabitation, the husband can divorce her and she can waive, and afterward he can remarry her. The intention need not be that she waits until after the divorce to waive, for then the ketubah debt is already fixed and Rabbi Natan's rule would apply. Rather, she waives in anticipation of immediate divorce. Rabbi Meir's objection is to a husband continuing to live with a wife after she has waived the minimum ketubah. If the waiver is immediately followed by divorce, and if he later remarries her with a valid new ketubah, there is no ongoing marriage without a ketubah. This is the proper understanding of Tosafot. See Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 65, seif katan 9. This also resolves a difficulty in the Maharsha. According to his understanding in Bava Batra 147, Rabbenu Tam holds that if a married woman sells her ketubah and later waives it in connection with divorce, she must pay the purchaser the full value. How then can Tosafot's answer work according to Rabbenu Tam? According to what I have written, she waives before the divorce, and the divorce follows immediately so that the husband does not continue living with her after the waiver. Thus the issue is not identical to a post divorce waiver of an already collectible ketubah. This framework also clarifies Tosafot in Ketubot 19, s.v. "where he injures others." I wrote above in siman 66, Tumim seif katan 351, that Tosafot in Bava Batra 147 can also be understood this way according to the Maharsha. Tosafot asks how, in the case of a found receipt, the woman can be believed because she has a migo that she could waive the ketubah. Perhaps she does not want to waive it to her husband and thereby suffer a real loss. The Maharsha wonders why she cannot simply trust her husband, just as in the receipt case she participates with him in collusion, as the Rosh explains. According to our analysis, the distinction is clear. Tosafot says in Bava Batra that a wife is embarrassed to sue her husband in beit din. Before she asks beit din to return the receipt to him, the husband can protect her by writing a replacement ketubah of the relevant amount. If the purchaser of the first ketubah later says, "You sold me a valid claim and now the receipt proves otherwise, return my money," and attempts to collect that refund from the replacement ketubah, she can wait until the husband's death and waive the replacement to the heirs. Rabbi Natan does not assist the purchaser, because if the sale is treated as void retroactively, his refund claim arose from the original sale, whereas the replacement ketubah was written only later at the time of the receipt's return. Under the temporal view of Tosafot Yeshanim, the later ketubah does not become directly encumbered to the earlier refund claim. But if she relies on the alternative of personally waiving the sold ketubah, the sequence is reversed. If she waives first, her husband may simply refuse afterward to write the replacement, and she is embarrassed to litigate against him, as Tosafot says. If he writes the replacement first and she then waives the sold ketubah, the purchaser's dina d'garmi claim arises only after the replacement ketubah. Rabbi Natan would then attach, and she could not defeat the purchaser by waiving to the heirs. Thus in either order she risks actual loss, and the migo of waiver is not equivalent to the receipt arrangement. This is precise. The same point resolves what I asked elsewhere according to Ri, who holds that even without dina d'garmi the seller must at least return the purchase money when she waives. Tosafot in Bava Batra says that if the baraita in Bava Metzia follows the view that does not adjudicate dina d'garmi, there is no difficulty for Ri. One might ask that even then the woman who waives must return the purchase money. The answer is that the entire problem involving waiver and the replacement ketubah arises where the damages obligation from the waiver comes only after the replacement ketubah, so Rabbi Natan can attach. If, however, the seller's obligation is merely to return the purchase money because the sale is treated as void retroactively on the implied condition that she not destroy the purchased right, then that refund debt is treated as arising from the original sale. It therefore precedes the replacement ketubah, and under the temporal rule Rabbi Natan does not attach. Furthermore, according to Tosafot's explanation that the power to waive resembles writing "I have received payment," partial receipt language has its own limitations. The difficulty therefore falls away. And this appears from several points to be the direction of Tosafot: their view inclines toward Hagahot HaRosh and the authorities who hold that Rabbi Natan's rule does not attach where the ultimate debtor's obligation arose only after the middle creditor had already become indebted to the first creditor. These authorities have clear support from the analysis just given. Nevertheless, although this is a coherent and defensible reading of Tosafot, it is difficult to attribute the same temporal limitation to the Rosh. The Tur, his son, plainly does not accept it in many places, including this siman, siman 85, and siman 111. More decisively, in responsa, klal 35, concerning a man who gave sivlonot and the woman afterward married another, the Rosh rules that if dowry property was given to the second husband, the giver of the sivlonot can collect from that husband under Rabbi Natan's rule. Yet the sivlonot debt clearly preceded the second husband's later obligation arising from the dowry of the second marriage. This shows that the Rosh does not generally require the ultimate debtor's debt to predate the first creditor's claim. One might try to harmonize the sources by an approach similar to what must in any event be said concerning the Mordechai in Ketubot. The Mordechai says that if a woman waives her ketubah she does not lose ketubat benin dichrin. He asks how she can waive at all, since it is forbidden for a husband to live with his wife without a ketubah, and answers that she can waive conditionally: if the husband dies first, the ketubah is waived, but if he divorces her, he must still pay it. Since divorce does not become easier in his eyes, the concern underlying the ketubah enactment is preserved. Yet in Bava Kamma, in our sugya concerning a woman who injured her husband, the Mordechai asks why she cannot sell the ketubah to the husband on precisely such a condition, namely that if he dies she loses the ketubah but if he divorces her he must pay it. The fact that this proposal is rejected appears to show that according to Rabbi Meir even such a conditional reduction is prohibited because the Sages did not distinguish. The two passages in the Mordechai therefore seem contradictory. The proper explanation is that when the Mordechai invokes the principle that the Sages did not distinguish, he means that beit din should not advise or compel such a device. Since the Sages instituted the ketubah, beit din must reinforce that enactment rather than furnish techniques that weaken it. If judges themselves compel women to structure ketubot in a reduced or conditional form, others may learn from the practice and the rabbinic safeguard could be undermined. Beit din must therefore maintain the enactment with special firmness. But if husband and wife themselves, without any directive or coercion from beit din, arrange that the ketubah is waived only upon his death and remains fully payable upon divorce, beit din need not invalidate what they have done. The fundamental concern that divorce not become easy in his eyes is absent, since he must still pay the full ketubah if he divorces her. The Sages' rule against distinctions does not necessarily require beit din to interfere with every privately created arrangement that preserves the core purpose of the enactment. This reconciles the Mordechai. In Bava Kamma the entire discussion asks whether beit din should compel the woman to sell or restructure her ketubah in order to pay the injured party. In that context the Mordechai properly says that beit din may not weaken the ketubah enactment through such advice or coercion. In Ketubot, however, the woman herself waives conditionally without any judicial direction. There the Mordechai can recognize the arrangement because the husband remains fully liable upon divorce. If so, one could attempt a similar explanation of Tosafot and the Rosh. Beit din cannot initially instruct the woman to use this conditional device, because judges may not weaken the ketubah enactment. Yet when evaluating whether a purchaser will actually profit from buying her ketubah, we must fear that husband and wife themselves will privately arrange a new ketubah under which she is fully protected upon divorce but nothing is payable if he dies first. Such a private arrangement would not make divorce easier, and beit din need not stop it. The purchaser would then receive nothing, because no rational purchaser would pay even a perutah for a claim collectible only upon divorce from a husband with whom the wife continues living securely. Death is common, but divorce in such circumstances is not sufficiently probable to create meaningful market value. Although this explanation is intrinsically reasonable, it is difficult to fit into the language of Tosafot and the Rosh. If this were their intention, they did not need to say that the husband writes her a "new ketubah." They could have answered directly that she will waive on the condition that the ketubah remains payable upon divorce but is waived upon death. If a complete waiver is within her power, such a conditional waiver should likewise be possible and would directly explain the purchaser's loss. Moreover, writing a ketubah in an unusual conditional formulation may itself be prohibited because one should not alter the standard form fixed by the Sages. We are therefore left, with respect to the Rosh, with the explanation cited by Knesset HaGedolah in the name of the Rashach: if a debtor writes in a promissory note that he obligates himself to the creditor personally but not to that creditor's creditors, the condition is valid because a stipulation concerning monetary rights is effective. The husband can therefore write the replacement ketubah after the waiver with an express condition that the obligation is to the wife but not to her creditors. The rabbinic purpose of the ketubah remains fully intact, because divorce is no easier in his eyes and he still owes her the entire ketubah upon divorce. Yet the replacement debt does not become directly encumbered to her creditor under Rabbi Natan's rule because the husband expressly excluded that derivative right. After the husband's death she can waive the ketubah to his heirs, and Rabbi Natan does not interfere because the very terms of the replacement obligation excluded her creditors. It follows that the Ramban and Ran's central rule is confirmed even from Tosafot and the Rosh: where Rabbi Natan's rule actually applies, the intermediate creditor cannot waive the ultimate debtor's obligation. A ketubah while the woman remains married is exceptional. According to the Ran, Rabbi Natan does not yet apply because the time for collection has not arrived and the debt is contingent. According to the Rosh as I explained him above in seif katan 1, the distinction rests on the special structure of the ketubah rather than necessarily on the Ran's exact rationale. There is, however, a practical difference concerning dowry property. According to the Ran's reasoning, even dowry may remain outside Rabbi Natan while the marriage continues, since collection is also contingent and if she dies first there may be no later collection. According to the Rosh as explained above, Rabbi Natan does apply to the dowry. Later, in siman 424, I explain that the Tur and Rama rule according to the Rosh. This is the preferable ruling, because the Ran's position is otherwise singular and was introduced only because of the particular difficulty in the ketubah sugya. We should not extend that exceptional reasoning to the dowry property that she actually brought into the marriage. It is true that in my novellae to Ketubot I found some support for the Ran from the Rosh himself. The Rosh rules that where a wife borrowed for necessary support, her lender can collect directly from the husband under Rabbi Natan's rule without a prior assessment by beit din. Yet concerning a minor wife who borrowed and did not exercise her right of refusal, the Rosh requires a judicial assessment before the husband must pay, and the Gidulei Terumah questions why. According to the Ran the distinction is understandable. In the case of the minor, Rabbi Natan does not yet apply because she can still refuse the marriage, so the husband's obligation is not fixed. It resembles a ketubah during the husband's lifetime, and therefore prior assessment is required. I discussed this there at length. The foregoing also yields a rule where Shimon's loan to Levi was made after Shimon had already borrowed from Reuven. Tosafot appears to incline toward the authorities who hold that in this temporal order Levi does not become directly obligated to Reuven under Rabbi Natan. If Shimon then waives Levi's debt, Levi can certainly say kim li that the waiver is valid. Many authorities hold even more broadly that an intermediate creditor can waive despite Rabbi Natan, and here there are additional authorities who say that Rabbi Natan never attached in the first place. Thus the possessor can combine the two independent bodies of authority, as I explained in the pamphlet concerning rules of possession. A difficulty for those authorities is how later dated promissory notes can be valid. Suppose Reuven lent to Shimon in Nisan, Shimon lent to Levi in Iyar, and the witnesses dated Reuven's note in Sivan. If Levi is not directly obligated to Reuven when his debt arose only after Reuven's original loan, then under the true chronology Shimon could waive Levi's debt. By moving Reuven's note to Sivan, however, the witnesses would make it appear that Levi's Iyar debt preceded Reuven's note and would thereby subject Levi to Rabbi Natan, preventing waiver. The witnesses would have prejudiced Levi. The answer is that those authorities can agree where Reuven's note contains an after acquired property clause. If Shimon expressly encumbered to Reuven all property and rights that he would later acquire, then a debt that arises later can become subject to Reuven notwithstanding the sequence. The temporal limitation applies only where no such clause exists. We rule that a deliberately later dated note is valid only when the appropriate after acquired property language is present. There is therefore no difficulty. From all this it is established that the Rosh holds that where Rabbi Natan's rule does apply, waiver is ineffective. The Tur therefore correctly rules here that Shimon cannot waive. Knesset HaGedolah and the Taz ask why the Tur relied on a responsum of the Rosh rather than the Rosh's ruling in Ketubot, where he appears to allow waiver. According to what I have written, there is no contradiction. The Rosh's own discussion in the chapter HaChovel supports the same conclusion, as I explained at the beginning of this siman at length. The Shach also addresses the Ramban's question, which the Maharshal raises from the law of a found receipt. How can the Gemara say that a receipt should be returned because the creditor has a migo that she could waive the underlying debt? Perhaps this woman owes money to others and therefore cannot waive under Rabbi Natan. The Shach answers that it is uncommon for someone to lend money to a married woman who is living with her husband. Apart from the intrinsic difficulty of this answer, it does not resolve the Mishnah's general case of found receipts. The Mishnah says not to return them because perhaps they were written in advance and the parties later reconsidered, but if the obligor says, "Give it," one gives it. The Gemara asks why we should not suspect collusion. One cannot simply invoke Shmuel's rule that the creditor could waive, because perhaps the creditor owes others and waiver would be ineffective under Rabbi Natan. The Shach's rarity answer does not fit the Mishnah generally. I do not wish to lengthen this further, for I have already discussed the matter at length above in siman 66 and siman 54. After all this labor and clarification, however, my mind is still not settled by the categorical conclusion that the intermediate creditor cannot waive. If I could only fully understand the principle. It is agreed above in siman 85 and later in siman 111 that if Reuven lent to Shimon and Shimon lent to Levi in a way that subjects Levi directly to Reuven under Rabbi Natan, and Shimon then sells or gives Levi's note to Yehudah, then if Yehudah collects movable property from Levi, Reuven has no claim against Yehudah. Since Levi paid in movables, we treat the matter as though the movables first reached Shimon and Shimon then sold or gave them away, and ordinary movables are not encumbered to Reuven. I explained this in siman 85 at length. Why then should Levi's position be worse if Shimon gives the note to Levi himself by forgiving his debt? If Shimon gives the note as a gift to Yehudah, Yehudah acquires it and Reuven's effective claim to the movables is lost. If Shimon instead gives the same debt to Levi himself and relinquishes collection, why should Levi have a weaker position and permit Reuven to object? Levi is the possessor of his own movables. Why should saying "it is forgiven to you" or "it is given to you" be worse than giving the right to a third party? Indeed, the entire debate whether Shimon can waive seems avoidable, because he could simply say, "I give you the debt as a gift." This is extremely difficult. See the Shach in this siman, seif katan 5, in the passage beginning "however, it appears to me," where he writes explicitly that saying a note is a trust document, paid, or forgiven cannot be weaker than giving the note away as a gift. His reasoning there is correct. Tosafot in Ketubot 81b likewise implies that a debtor's power through waiver can be stronger than through gift. Tosafot asks why the yavam cannot say, "Since I am the heir, I have taken possession," by waiving the debt to himself. Yet the Gemara states that he may not give the property away as an ordinary gift. Tosafot's question therefore presupposes that self waiver may work even where an ordinary gift is restricted. The difficulty is thus sharpened. What difference should there be between Shimon's giving the debt to a third party and giving it to Levi himself? The reason a third party can retain collected movables is that we deem them as though they first came into Shimon's possession and were then transferred by him. The same should apply to waiver. It should be as though Shimon had already collected the movables from Levi and then immediately returned them to Levi as a gift. If Levi actually paid Shimon in movables and Shimon returned those very movables to him, Reuven could not object. Why should direct waiver be different? The Rosh writes similarly in a responsum cited by the Tur at the end of this siman. Shimon may lend his money to Levi for a long term and Reuven cannot prevent him, because if Shimon wanted to give the money away as a gift, Reuven could not stop him. If so, even after Shimon has lent the money, an extension of Levi's due date or a waiver of the debt should be treated like collection followed by a gift or sale, with no greater power in Reuven to object. The Rosh himself treats an extension of time as a gift. Yet with respect to movable property it is clear that an earlier creditor cannot prevent such a gift. Why then should he be able to prevent an extension or waiver? In short, I remain astonished and do not know how to resolve it. My eyes await Hashem to illuminate the matter. I am therefore compelled to suggest a new distinction. Quite apart from the foregoing problem, another difficulty points in the same direction. The Rosh in Bava Batra, chapter HaSefinah, writes that sale of promissory notes is rabbinic, and he connects the seller's power to waive with the rabbinic nature of the sale. In Perek Mi SheMet the Gemara likewise explains a power of waiver by reference to a rabbinic transfer. Rabbenu Tam, who holds that note sale is biblical, requires the separate theory of two obligations, the property lien and the personal obligation, with the personal obligation remaining in the seller's hands. But for the view that note sale is rabbinic, that theory is unnecessary. Waiver follows precisely because the purchaser's acquisition is only rabbinic. The Rosh's own words in HaSefinah plainly indicate that he accepts the rabbinic view, as the Tur records in his abridgment. How then could a direct Rabbi Natan obligation, which is full Torah law, be eliminated through the same waiver doctrine whose rationale is that an ordinary note purchaser has only a rabbinic acquisition? This strongly suggests that the cases must be divided according to the type of property available from the ultimate debtor. Where Levi owns land, the principal lien of his debt rests on that land, and under Rabbi Natan the land is directly encumbered to Reuven. If Shimon sells Levi's note to Yehudah and Yehudah collects land from Levi, we rule above in siman 85 that the transfer does not defeat Reuven. Reuven can recover the land from Yehudah. Therefore, in a case of waiver, we presume that collection would ordinarily have been from the land, for that is the primary property lien. Shimon cannot validly transfer that land away to defeat Reuven, whether to another person or back to Levi, and he likewise cannot waive the direct land based obligation. Levi cannot answer, "Had Shimon demanded payment I would have paid him with movables." Until payment actually occurs, the ordinary collection lien rests on the land. Tosafot in Bava Batra 76 asks why sale of notes should not be biblical because the land lien is transferable by deed and therefore should not be subject to waiver. Tosafot does not answer that the debtor might have chosen to pay movables, in which event the land lien would never have been used. Evidently such a hypothetical assertion by the debtor is insufficient. The existing lien is measured by the land that is presently encumbered. But where Levi has no land at all and possesses only movables, why should waiver not be effective? The entire practical reach of the obligation is then into movables. If Levi paid Shimon with movables, Shimon could give them to another, and certainly could return them to Levi. Waiver is no weaker than that result, as the Shach himself says. There is no existing land lien that survives transfer and that Reuven can follow. In such a case the logic for denying waiver collapses. This distinction appears to me clear and true. Therefore, all the authorities who speak categorically of inability to waive should be understood as speaking where the ultimate debtor owns land and the first creditor possesses a lien capable of reaching it. This is especially evident in the case of the woman who caused injury, because a ketubah historically rested on land and not on movables. The same is true in the case of a found receipt in Bava Metzia, where the concern likewise presumes a collectible land lien. By contrast, in the Ketubot case of one who says that his note is a trust document, the statement is general and includes a debtor with only movables. The Rosh therefore correctly argues that the creditor has a migo of waiver or gift. His words are exact, and there is no contradiction in his rulings. The Shach questions the Rama on another point. Here the Rama follows the Mechaber and does not dissent from the rule that the intermediate creditor cannot waive. Yet in Even HaEzer siman 70 the Rama rules that if a wife borrowed for necessary support, her lender can collect from the husband under Rabbi Natan's rule, but if she waived her support claim against the husband, the lender loses. The Shach answers that the Rama means she waived before borrowing. This is strained, because then the ruling teaches very little. Moreover, the source is the Mordechai, and the Mordechai speaks of a waiver after the loan. The Mordechai follows his own view that even under Rabbi Natan's rule the middle creditor can waive, as the Beit Shmuel notes. The Beit Shmuel's alternative, that the wife has independent property from which the lender can collect, is likewise difficult. One possibility is that the wife borrowed in advance for support covering several future months and then immediately waived her claim to future support before those months arrived. The husband's support obligation does not arise as one fixed debt for all future months. It accrues day by day. Rabbi Natan therefore does not presently create a direct lien for support that has not yet accrued. The waiver can consequently be effective as to those future amounts. A better answer is that the Rama distinguishes between a full Torah level Rabbi Natan obligation and a support obligation whose source is rabbinic. Where Rabbi Natan operates upon an underlying Torah debt, the resulting direct lien has Torah force and cannot be waived in the manner of an ordinary sold note. But the husband's support obligation is rabbinic. The lender's derivative right is therefore no stronger than that rabbinic obligation, and waiver can operate much as it does with an ordinary sale of a note whose acquisition is rabbinic. I discussed this at length in my novellae to Even HaEzer, and this is not the place to expand further.
And if after he became obligated, etc. The Shach concludes that the wording "after he became obligated" is not literal. Rather, even if the first creditor merely warned the ultimate debtor, "Do not pay so and so, because he owes me and you are directly obligated to me under Rabbi Natan's rule," and the ultimate debtor nevertheless paid the middle creditor on his own initiative, he must pay the first creditor as well. The Shach's reasoning is persuasive. At first glance, however, one might infer otherwise from Bava Kamma, in the chapter concerning an ox that gored four or five times, where the Gemara speaks specifically of a case in which beit din first seized the ox. It does not say that the injured party himself seized it. This seems to imply that Rabbi Natan's direct lien requires formal judicial action. If the injured party himself seized the ox, the owner could then say, "You should have returned it to me," and the injured party could not answer that he was directly entitled to it under Rabbi Natan. The Gemara therefore had to say that beit din seized it, because only then does Rabbi Natan's rule become effective. Nevertheless, logic supports the Shach. The direct obligation arises already at the time of the underlying loan, as indicated by the verse "to the one to whom he is guilty." Beit din does not create the lien. Its function is only to adjudicate and enforce what already exists. I already explained above in seif katan 5 why the Gemara specifically described judicial seizure. It is true that the Mordechai in the second chapter of Dayanei Gezeirot writes that if, after the wife sued the depositary in beit din, the depositary returned the property to the husband, he must pay her. That language implies that a prior suit in beit din is necessary. The Ran also appears to require a judicial demand. Even more strongly, the Rosh's responsum concerning a recalcitrant debtor, which the Mechaber cites in this seif, states explicitly that the third party must pay because beit din ordered him not to return the property to the recalcitrant debtor. The Rosh explains that the judicial order is as though beit din had already collected the property for the creditor, thereby divesting the recalcitrant debtor of his control. This appears directly contrary to the Shach. The answer is that the Mordechai follows his own view that the intermediate creditor can waive a Rabbi Natan claim. Likewise, this responsum of the Rosh reflects the view he held before retracting that position, or, according to what I wrote above, concerns movable property, where everyone may agree that waiver can work. If waiver is effective, then ordinary voluntary payment by the ultimate debtor to the middle creditor is no better than a waiver. The first creditor therefore needs the additional fact that beit din expressly prohibited payment and effectively collected the property for him. Once judicial seizure has occurred, the middle creditor no longer has power to defeat it. But according to our accepted ruling that the middle creditor cannot waive a valid Rabbi Natan lien, there is no distinction between waiver and payment. The ultimate debtor is already directly obligated to the first creditor, and no additional act of beit din is needed to create that obligation. This is clear and also explains the Rosh's responsum according to the view there that waiver was effective. One should not try to use the explanation I gave above concerning the Rosh in Bava Kamma, namely that he may have held that Rabbi Natan prevents waiver. That source is not decisive here, because where payment rather than waiver is involved beit din could issue an order and thereby effect a judicial collection, while with a pure waiver beit din cannot simply confiscate the creditor's own property right in the same manner. Consider this carefully.
