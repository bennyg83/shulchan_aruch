(Shach, seif katan 11) In my humble opinion there is no difficulty from there, etc. Note: The Rashba answers in this manner in his novellae to Ketubot 19, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. See also below, siman 101, Shach seif katan 3. One who studies the Ritva's words, and especially the Ran, will see that they too intended the same answer as the Shach. At the beginning of his question the Ran assumed that since the Torah established Rabbi Nathan's lien, the husband is immediately obligated to the injured party. Of the two hundred of the ketubah owed to the wife, only the one hundred corresponding to the injury claim is treated as a definite debt subject to Rabbi Nathan's lien, and she should no longer be able to waive it. The Ran initially understood the Gemara's suggestion, "let her sell her ketubah," to mean that beit din should award the injured party the entire ketubah of two hundred in satisfaction of his present claim of one hundred, because its current market value as a contingent right is only one hundred, through Rabbi Nathan's lien. He therefore asked why the Gemara says that anything transferred to the husband will certainly be waived, and why it asks, "if she waives it," since a right subject to Rabbi Nathan's lien cannot be waived. The Ran answers that the Gemara does not act there through Rabbi Nathan's lien at all. The husband has no present duty to pay the ketubah, so the rule of taking from this debtor and giving to that creditor does not yet apply. Instead, the proposal is that she sell the ketubah to the injured party, and the Gemara rejects this because she will certainly waive anything sold to her husband. The Ran's wording is precise: "there we do not proceed by Rabbi Nathan's rule; rather, we say that she should sell the ketubah to the one she injured, and to what we asked, we answer that anything concerning her husband she will waive." This shows that his original question assumed an award through Rabbi Nathan's lien. The Rashba's novellae to Bava Kamma, passage beginning "from here it is implied," likewise show that if collection through beit din under Rabbi Nathan's lien were available, and beit din awarded a ketubah worth two hundred to satisfy a present claim of one hundred because its current contingent value was only one hundred, the wife could no longer waive it, since judicial collection is effective by Torah law. But in truth, before the ketubah comes due Rabbi Nathan's lien does not apply. Beit din therefore cannot award it as a collection; only a sale is possible, and that sale can be waived. This is exactly the explanation given above of the Ran.
