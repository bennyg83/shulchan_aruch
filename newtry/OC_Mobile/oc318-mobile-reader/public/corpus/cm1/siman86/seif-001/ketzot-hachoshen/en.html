Reuven is owed one hundred. Rabbi Natan's rule is derived from the verse requiring payment "to the one to whom he is liable," rather than saying merely to the one whom he robbed or borrowed from. The verse teaches payment to the true owner of the principal claim. One must ask why this verse is needed. All a debtor's property, whether in his house or elsewhere, is subject to his creditor. Why should beit din not simply collect the debt owed to the intermediate debtor as one of his assets? Rashba and Ran answer that debt instruments and intangible claims are not ordinary items that beit din can seize as property. They have no tangible body. But this answer follows their view that debt instruments are not themselves ordinary collectible property. According to the Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, who allow debt instruments to be subjected together with land, the question returns. The Shach answers that without Rabbi Natan's rule beit din could give the first creditor no greater right than the intermediate debtor himself could convey. A seller of a debt instrument may still admit that it is an accommodation document or paid, because he has a migo of waiver. Rabbi Natan's verse, however, makes the first creditor himself a direct litigant against the final debtor, so the intermediate debtor can no longer undermine the claim by such admissions. This explanation works well under our present rabbinic treatment of movables, which ordinarily cannot be followed into a purchaser's hands. But under Torah law, according to the view that liens are biblical and even movables sold by the debtor remain subject to collection, the difficulty returns. If the intermediate debt was already subjected to the first creditor, an attempted waiver should no more defeat him than sale of other encumbered property. One might answer that an ordinary lien reaches only the debtor's property, not the personal obligation owed to him, and therefore the special verse is needed to transfer the bodily obligation itself. Yet even that is not fully satisfactory. A person's inability to sell an intangible personal obligation is because it is outside his ordinary transferable possession. A lien, however, can sometimes reach rights more readily than sale. The Rosh expressly distinguishes between sale and lien and notes that one can encumber after acquired property even where one cannot presently sell it. Thus there is room for the first creditor's lien to reach even the intermediate debtor's personal claim. The stronger explanation is that Rabbi Natan's verse is needed where the final debt arose only after the first loan. By Torah law, property acquired later was not automatically subjected unless a valid mechanism of after acquired lien exists. The first creditor's ordinary lien therefore would not reach a claim that did not yet exist when his loan was made. Rabbi Natan's rule applies even there. More fundamentally, however, the chief distinction is not merely that the final debt becomes collateral. Rabbi Natan's rule makes the first creditor the true creditor of the final debtor. If the right were only an ordinary property lien, the final debtor could discharge it with money and the first creditor would be collecting only from then onward. Under Rabbi Natan's rule, the Torah says the final debtor must give payment directly to the person to whom the principal truly belongs. The first creditor can even transfer that right because it is treated as his actual debt, and the final debtor cannot simply insist on paying a substitute sum to the intermediate debtor. This also explains why the verse appears in the section dealing with denial of a deposit or loan and the added fifth. If the final debtor falsely denies the claim, the added fifth is paid to the first creditor, even though the first creditor's own loan may equal only the principal and not the added fifth. That proves the underlying debt itself is treated as the first creditor's. The denial was a denial of his money. Rashi's wording in Ketubot is exact: the verse does not say to the one who lent, but to the one to whom the liability belongs, meaning the one who owns the principal. Thus the verse establishes not merely an ordinary lien but substantive ownership of the debt for Rabbi Natan's rule.
