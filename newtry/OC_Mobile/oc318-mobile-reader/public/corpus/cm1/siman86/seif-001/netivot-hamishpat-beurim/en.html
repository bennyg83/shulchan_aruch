Whether by way of a loan. See Shach, seif katan 2, through his inclusion even of a deposit. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply to a deposit, because even the obligation to return the object does not begin until negligence occurs. This is certainly incorrect, as is clear in siman 58, Shach seif katan 9, where he writes that because the item came into the custodian's hands as a deposit, he must make a complete return. Ketzot's proof from Ketubot, concerning one who had a borrowed cow and slaughtered it on Shabbat, is no proof. There the discussion is of the monetary damages obligation created by slaughtering the cow, which certainly begins only at the moment of slaughter. The obligation of return exists only while the deposit itself is intact. Once it no longer exists, the return obligation ends and a new payment obligation begins, and the custodian's property becomes encumbered for that payment. While the deposit remains intact, however, there is a personal obligation to return it, though not yet an ordinary property lien for damages. Ketzot also attempts to reject the proof from the case of one who borrowed an animal presumed harmless, which shows that Rabbi Natan's lien applies to a deposit, by saying that the Gemara there refers only to the custodian's negligence liability toward the depositor. That is mistaken. The alleged negligence consisted of returning the animal to the injured party, but at the moment he returned it the tortfeasor no longer owed the injured party, so Rabbi Natan's lien would not apply if the rule concerned only later negligence damages. It follows that the underlying obligation to return the deposit itself is also subject to Rabbi Natan's lien. There is further proof that an intact deposit carries a return obligation. The Gemara says the custodian could have driven the animal into the marsh, and Tosafot explain that he could have reached a settlement with the owner. The Gemara answers that Rabbi Natan's lien applies. If so, one might ask why, under the rule that one who seizes for a creditor where no one else is harmed acquires for him, the person making the seizure is not liable to the original owner, since Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply to that seizer. We must distinguish. A custodian already has a personal return obligation. Returning the item to his own creditor does not necessarily discharge that obligation where there remains a possibility that the owner would have settled for less. This resembles a case of "I do not know whether I repaid you." Just as one who pays another person's debt without authority cannot charge the debtor because the debtor can say, "Perhaps I would have persuaded the creditor to accept less," so too the custodian cannot automatically discharge his existing return obligation where this unresolved possibility remains. In contrast, one who seizes an asset for a creditor had no prior obligation before the seizure. The possibility that the debtor might have negotiated a settlement creates only a doubt whether any liability toward the debtor ever arose, which is like "I do not know whether I ever became obligated to you," and therefore does not create liability. Ketzot further writes that according to Shach, a depositor cannot give the deposit as a gift to the custodian. This is certainly incorrect, as I will explain below in seif 6. He also answers Rashba's question why Rabbi Natan's lien is needed at all, since all of a debtor's property is in any event encumbered to his creditor, by saying that the practical difference is that under Rabbi Natan the first borrower cannot discharge the creditor with cash. That is certainly incorrect, as is clear to anyone who examines the law. Ketzot also says that Rashba's answer that debts are mere intangible rights is inconsistent with our ruling that documents can be encumbered through agav. This too is incorrect. Documents can be encumbered through agav only because we rule like Rabbi Natan and the first creditor thereby acquires a personal lien against the second borrower, enabling a property lien to attach as well. Without Rabbi Natan, the debt itself is not encumbered. This is proven by Ketubot 82 concerning one who was owed money by his brother, where the Gemara says no Tanna combines the two stringencies of Rabbi Meir, that movables are encumbered to a ketubah, and Rabbi Natan. This shows that absent Rabbi Natan, debts are not encumbered even if movables generally are. Ketzot's further suggestion that even without Rabbi Natan the personal lien can itself be encumbered because a lien may attach to something outside one's present possession is likewise incorrect. Without Rabbi Natan no personal lien at all is transferred, as the same Ketubot passage proves. Ketzot does, however, correctly state one practical difference created by Rabbi Natan: if the first creditor sues the second borrower, who denies owing anything and falsely swears, the second borrower must pay principal plus the additional fifth, because the first creditor is treated as the owner of the claim. Without Rabbi Natan, the first creditor would come only by way of a property lien, and the denial would not be a denial to the owner requiring the added fifth. This point is correct.
Whether by way of wages. See Shach, seif katan 3, and his answer to the question from the law of the emancipation grant. The primary answer is that of Tumim. The essence of his answer is this. At first glance, even after the servant has collected the emancipation grant, his creditor cannot seize it from him. What, then, does Rabbi Natan's lien have to do with that law? We must say that although after collection the creditor has no property lien over the grant, the servant still has the mitzvah to pay his debts from assets beyond the amount protected for his basic support. Accordingly, the passage in Kiddushin follows Abaye, who holds that property liens are not Torah law. On his view, ordinary debts carry no Torah property lien at all, so the verse excluding the emancipation grant must be excluding only Rabbi Natan's lien, and their dispute therefore depends on Rabbi Natan. According to us, however, since we rule that liens are Torah law, the verse excludes even an ordinary property lien from the emancipation grant, so the creditor cannot seize it even after the servant has received it.
