We take it from Levi and give it to Reuven. The Tur and the poskim repeatedly state this in the name of Rabbi Natan, who derives it from the verse, 'and he shall give it to the one to whom he is indebted.' The verse does not say merely to the person whom he robbed or borrowed from, but to the person to whom the principal truly belongs. Thus if Reuven lends Shimon and Shimon later lends Levi, the money in Levi's hands may be viewed as Reuven's principal that passed through Shimon. The rule also applies if Shimon's loan to Levi preceded Reuven's loan to Shimon. One rationale is that once Shimon recovers from Levi, Reuven would immediately collect the same funds from Shimon, so the money in Levi's hands is ultimately Reuven's principal. Another, stronger rationale is that when Reuven later lends Shimon, Reuven's lien attaches to all Shimon's assets and rights, including Shimon's claim against Levi. Conversely, even where Reuven lent Shimon first and Shimon only later acquired a claim against Levi, the rule still applies. This latter formulation is primary, as the Bach writes, and as the Mechaber rules in siman 111:21.
In every manner in which Levi becomes obligated. The Gemara excludes the severance gift due to a Hebrew servant, which is not extracted from the master for payment of the servant's debt. Since Hebrew servitude and that gift are not practiced today, the Tur and Mechaber state generally, 'in every manner in which he becomes obligated.' Alternatively, the severance gift is not a debt in the strict sense but an act of generosity and favor. The phrase therefore means that Rabbi Natan's rule applies to obligations that are legally due as debts.
And he collects his debt from the excess, etc. The source concludes that the pledge is subject to him by Rabbi Natan's rule, and also limits the ruling to a case where this will not expose Reuven to loss. Rama's gloss at the end of this siman expresses the same limitation regarding a deposit. Its natural place is here. Rama may have stated it specifically regarding a deposit because a gentile depositor has a stronger complaint against one who interferes with his deposited property than against one who transfers a pledge, where interest continues accruing in any event.
