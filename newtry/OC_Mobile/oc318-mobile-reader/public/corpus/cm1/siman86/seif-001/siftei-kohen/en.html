There is no distinction whether he became obligated to him afterward. This rejects the view of Rabbeinu Yitzchak Or Zarua, cited in Mordechai in HaManiach and in Hagahot Ashri in the chapter Shor Shenagach Arba’ah VaChamishah, who distinguishes based on the order of the obligations. Maharam in Mordechai disputes him and agrees with Tur and Mechaber. This also appears to be the view of the poskim in many places, and Maharshal rules so in Bava Kamma, chapter 4, siman 19. This is the primary ruling.
Whether by way of a loan, etc. Hagahot Ashri in Shor Shenagach Arba’ah VaChamishah cites Or Zarua that Rabbi Natan’s rule applies to a loan, deposit, damages, wages, theft, and every comparable obligation. Maharshal likewise writes in siman 19 that there is no distinction whether both obligations are loans or one is a deposit or another form of debt. Even though these obligations differ in other respects, Rabbi Natan’s rule applies to all of them. Hagahot Ashri further cites Or Zarua that the rule applies where the intermediate obligor is a custodian and an informer: property held by the informer’s custodian may be taken and given to the person harmed by the informer. Rabbeinu Simchah disagrees regarding an informer. Maharshal explains that Rabbeinu Simchah treats liability for indirect damage as rabbinic. Ramban, who regards dina de-garmi as Torah law, would certainly apply Rabbi Natan’s rule to an informer. Maharshal therefore rules that collection is made even from an informer. I wrote above in siman 66, seif 32, seif katan 100, that whether dina de-garmi is rabbinic is a doubt in law. One might therefore think that collection cannot be made from the informer through Rabbi Natan. Nevertheless, it appears to me that it can. Once the Sages imposed monetary liability, they structured their enactment like Torah law. Even if the liability is penal, wherever collection is otherwise possible it may be made. Neither the Gemara nor the poskim distinguish Rabbi Natan’s rule based on whether the underlying debt is Torah or rabbinic. This is therefore the correct conclusion, unlike the implication of Maharshal’s discussion.
Whether by way of wages. Bach at the end of this siman emends the wording of Baal HaTerumot cited by Beit Yosef. Examination of Kiddushin 15a and Tosafot there shows that no emendation is needed, and Bach’s reading actually makes Baal HaTerumot impossible to explain. Mahara Shach, responsum, part 1, siman 12, and Gedulei Terumah at the beginning of gate 51 question Rambam. Rambam rules in chapter 3 of Hilchot Avadim like the first tanna that one who sells himself does not receive the severance gift, but in chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh he rules like Rabbi Natan. Kiddushin says the first tanna does not accept Rabbi Natan. They therefore explain Rambam according to Rabbeinu Netanel in Tosafot, that the first tanna rejects Rabbi Natan only regarding wages. Lechem Mishneh gives the same explanation. This does not appear correct. If Rambam excluded wages from Rabbi Natan’s rule, he should have stated that distinction somewhere. The question itself is not difficult. The Gemara repeatedly follows Rabbi Natan in Ketubot, Bava Kamma, Gittin, and Pesachim, and all the poskim rule accordingly. At the same time, the halachah follows the first tanna against Rabbi Eliezer, as Tosafot and other poskim state. Both rulings can therefore coexist. Rashba in his novellae to Gittin offers one answer. Even if the halachah follows the first tanna regarding the severance gift, it may also follow Rabbi Natan generally. The verse’s word “to him” is located in the section concerning the slave and naturally excludes another category of slave, namely one who sold himself, rather than an outside creditor. The Gemara prefers to attribute the first tanna’s view to a substantive disagreement with Rabbi Natan rather than to a dispute in exegetical emphasis, but the actual halachah may accept Rabbi Natan while reading the first tanna as Rashba explains. Ritva in Kiddushin gives a stronger answer. The Gemara’s statement that the first tanna rejects Rabbi Natan is only according to Rav Tavyomi, who assumes the first tanna derives the laws of one who sold himself through the verbal analogy of “hired servant.” On that assumption, the word “to him” is available to exclude the self-sold slave rather than the creditor. But if the first tanna does not use that verbal analogy, as the earlier sugya may indicate, he excludes the self-sold slave because those laws are not written concerning him, leaving “to him” available to exclude the creditor in the special severance-gift context. Thus the first tanna can agree with Rabbi Natan generally. When the Gemara elsewhere calls the view “Tutai’s,” it merely cites the sage who stated the exposition explicitly, not to imply that it is a rejected individual opinion. Ritva says he found Rambam ruling both like Rabbi Natan and like the first tanna, which confirms that they need not conflict. Mahara Shach, Gedulei Terumah, and Lechem Mishneh evidently overlooked these passages in Rashba and Ritva. Maggid Mishneh’s statement that the issue is disputed may refer to Rav Tavyomi’s understanding or to the simple reading of the sugya, since other passages also imply that some tannaim dispute Rabbi Natan. A third explanation is also possible in Rambam. Rabbeinu Netanel says the first tanna rejects Rabbi Natan “generally” only with respect to wages. Rambam may understand “generally” differently: the first tanna rejects the application of Rabbi Natan to charitable grants, such as the severance gift, poor tithe, and other charity. The severance gift is plainly a form of charity, as Scripture and the sugyot indicate. The verse therefore need not exclude an ordinary creditor individually. The Tosefta in Peah states that poor tithe may not be used to pay a debt, and Rambam codifies this in chapter 6 of Hilchot Matanot Aniyim. Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Batra likewise cites Avi HaEzri that where a debtor receives charity, he need not use that money to pay his creditor. This is what the Gemara may mean by saying that the first tanna does not accept Rabbi Natan “generally,” meaning in the general category of charity. This explanation is clear.
