We take from Levi and give to Reuven. Levi cannot say, "You are not my litigant." We derive the rule from the verse requiring payment to the person to whom the ultimate liability is owed. Even according to the opinion that a general property lien is not Torah law, the second debtor's personal obligation is nonetheless subordinated to the first creditor just as it is to his own immediate creditor. See Rashba at the end of Kiddushin.
There is no distinction whether he became obligated to him afterward. See what I wrote above in Tumim. This ruling requires study because it is disputed. I wrote in seif katan 10 that if Levi became obligated to Shimon only after Shimon had borrowed from Reuven, and Shimon then waived Levi's debt, Levi may be able to claim that the waiver is effective, provided Shimon had not written Reuven an after acquired property clause. See there.
In every manner in which he becomes obligated, etc. See Tumim. If Levi's obligation arises only by way of a gift, it requires study whether Rabbi Natan's rule of direct collection applies. See there.
In which he becomes obligated, etc. Where a party who caused actionable financial damage becomes obligated to pay, Maharshal holds that collection through Rabbi Natan depends upon whether liability for indirect damage is true law or merely a rabbinic fine. If it is only a rabbinic fine, Rabbi Natan's Torah rule does not apply. The Shach disagrees and holds that Rabbi Natan's collection applies even to rabbinic monetary obligations. See what I wrote in Tumim.
Wages. But money given to the debtor as charity or gratuitous support is not collected through Rabbi Natan's rule. The wording implies that even after such money actually reaches the debtor, the creditor has no proprietary lien in it. See Tumim, seif katan 3. It appears that the meaning is only that the funds themselves are not directly liened and beit din does not descend to them through Rabbi Natan, but the debtor personally remains obligated by the mitzvah of repaying debts if he has more than the protected minimum left for his needs.
And interest that accrued upon it. The Shach holds that if a Jew lent money to a non Jew for a year at a stated amount of interest, and stipulated that if the non Jew redeemed the loan early the interest would be reduced proportionately, then when another Jew comes to redeem the collateral through Rabbi Natan he must nevertheless pay the first Jew the full year's interest. I wrote in Tumim that this does not appear correct. He should pay only according to the elapsed time, unless under the agreement the non Jew himself had no right to redeem early. In that latter case the full year's interest must be paid even if the Jewish claimant removes the collateral in the middle of the year.
And he collects his debt from the excess. The reason is that the collateral itself is subordinated to Shimon through Rabbi Natan's rule. This applies only where doing so will not expose Reuven to liability or other loss. Sema.
