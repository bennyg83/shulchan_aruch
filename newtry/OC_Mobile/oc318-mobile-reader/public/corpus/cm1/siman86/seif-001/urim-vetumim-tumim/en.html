Whether by way of a loan, etc. The Tur and Mechaber list all the forms of obligation, but their wording leaves unresolved whether Rabbi Natan's rule applies when the original transfer was by way of a gift. Baal HaTerumot cites authorities who hold that Rabbi Natan does not apply to a gift. At first glance, the Rosh's words in Bava Kamma, in the chapter HaChovel, concerning a married woman who causes damage, appear to indicate the same. The Gemara asks that she should sell her ketubah for its present value, and answers that with respect to her husband she would certainly waive it, etc. The Rosh writes that this applies specifically where she has no nichsei melog or nichsei tzon barzel, but if she has such property she should sell it for its present value. The Shiltei Giborim and Maadanei Melech ask: with respect to nichsei tzon barzel, she has only an ordinary debt against her husband, so why should she not be able to waive it like any other debt? If so, the same concern remains that with respect to her husband she would certainly waive it. The Maadanei Melech thought to answer that she would have no incentive to waive it because she would then have to pay for the loss under the law of garmi. With a ketubah, by contrast, she can waive it because it is forbidden for the husband to live with her without a new ketubah, and he will write her another one, so there is no ultimate loss. This answer has no substance. The Rosh himself later asks how she could waive the ketubah if she would then have to pay for the loss. According to the Maadanei Melech, that question was already answered by the Rosh's own circumstances, since the husband would simply write her another ketubah and she would suffer no loss. Moreover, the Rosh's view, as the Tur cites it, is that under garmi she pays only the amount she received for selling the claim, not its full value. If so, even with nichsei tzon barzel she could arrange to waive the claim. She would pay only the amount for which she sold it, while she and her husband would gain the balance, exactly as Tosafot write there. According to Rabbenu Tam's explanation, that she pays only what she received, the Gemara is readily understood for precisely this reason, because she profits from the difference. The Rosh likewise writes this rationale in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, that she could waive and divide the excess with her husband. The Maadanei Melech himself also noticed that later, where a woman injured her husband, the Gemara establishes the case where her ketubah is not larger than the minimum Torah amount. This implies that where she injured others the rule may apply even when the ketubah is larger, and nevertheless she cannot simply sell the excess because she could waive it to her husband. According to the Maadanei Melech, however, even when she injured others she should sell the excess because she would not waive it, since she would have to pay, and the husband would not need to write her a new ketubah for the amount of the additional ketubah. There is another difficulty. The Rosh writes in responsum, klal 35, siman 7, cited by the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 91, concerning one who gave a woman engagement gifts, which she then lost, after which she rebelled against him and married another man. The Rosh writes that although she holds a dowry instrument against her present husband, we do not say that she should sell it for its present value and use the proceeds to pay for the lost gifts, because with respect to her husband she would certainly waive it. See there. Yet a dowry claim is comparable to nichsei tzon barzel, and nevertheless the Rosh writes that she can waive it and that we are concerned she will do so. This contradicts the Maadanei Melech. Below in siman 424 the Tur cites this view concerning a woman who caused damage and owns nichsei tzon barzel or nichsei melog, that she should sell them for their present value. The Perishah addresses the problem and answers that the case is where those assets still exist in their original form. I do not understand this. What difference does that make? They are nevertheless fully under the husband's control, because we rule that a loan is not given for return in specie, even if the money still exists. If his intent is the Tur's statement in Even HaEzer siman 118, as the Perishah writes, there the case is one where she has the right to demand payment from her husband whenever she wishes. If so, why should she need to sell the claim for its present value? Beit din can compel the husband to pay directly under Rabbi Natan's rule, as the Rosh explicitly writes in the responsum concerning engagement gifts cited above. The words of the Rosh and Tur therefore require further study.
What appears to me is that independently of this difficulty there was a reversal in the Rosh's position. In the second chapter of Ketubot the Rosh holds that a creditor can waive a debt even where Rabbi Natan applies, whereas in his responsa he rules that he cannot waive it. This shows that the Rosh retracted his earlier view. The Tur agrees with the ruling of the responsum, which indicates that he knew it to be the later position. It therefore appears that the discussion in Bava Kamma follows the final conclusion that where Rabbi Natan applies the intermediate creditor cannot waive the claim, while the responsum in klal 35 was written before the retraction. This explains why the Tur never cites that responsum. The matter should be understood as follows. The Ramban and Ran ask about the Gemara's suggestion there that the woman should sell the ketubah to the injured party himself. The Gemara answers that with respect to her husband she would certainly waive it and the process would therefore amount merely to troubling her for nothing. But the injured party is her creditor, and under Rabbi Natan's rule the husband should be directly obligated to him, in which case she cannot waive the debt. See the Ran's strained answer. Regarding the principal ketubah itself, it appears that the Rosh holds Rabbi Natan does not apply while she remains married to her husband. This is not for the Ran's reason. Rather, such was the nature of the rabbinic enactment of the ketubah. Rabbi Natan's basic case is Reuven who lent to Shimon and Shimon who lent to Levi. Levi's true creditor is Reuven, and Reuven is Levi's direct litigant. Shimon cannot waive the debt because the personal obligation of the second debtor is transferred to the first creditor. The Shulchan Aruch rules in seif 3 that Shimon is not obligated to collect from Levi at all; Levi himself becomes the debtor of Reuven. See what I write there. If we were to say that while a woman remains married and owes money to others her husband becomes directly obligated to her creditor through Rabbi Natan, then the woman herself would be removed from the chain and the husband would owe the creditor directly. She would then effectively be without a ketubah. Although divorce may not be easy, Tosafot in Bava Kamma write that this is why one cannot simply write the ketubah instrument in another person's name, because it is forbidden to maintain the marriage when the ketubah is not in the woman's own name. The same should apply under Rabbi Natan. See what I wrote above in Tumim to siman 66 in resolution of the Rosh's words in a different context. It follows that Rabbi Natan cannot apply to the principal ketubah while she remains married. The same Sages who enacted that she must have a ketubah also determined that Rabbi Natan's rule does not displace it. The Gemara's statement concerning one who was a creditor of his brother and the brother died, where Rabbi Natan does apply even to a ketubah, is different because there the woman herself is the first creditor seeking payment of her ketubah. That is straightforward. But this resolves only the principal ketubah. What of the additional ketubah? The Gemara concerning a woman who injured others implies that even when her ketubah exceeds the Torah minimum her creditor still cannot recover by purchasing the excess, because she could waive it to her husband. Yet under the conclusion that Rabbi Natan prevents waiver, why should this be so? We must say that the Rosh holds Rabbi Natan does not apply to a gift, and the additional ketubah is a complete gift. There is therefore no difficulty. This answers the additional ketubah. The dowry, however, which is nichsei tzon barzel, is a genuine debt. Rabbi Natan therefore applies and she cannot waive it. The Ramban's question then becomes a powerful proof: she should sell the dowry claim to the injured party and would be unable to waive it. Therefore the Rosh and Tur write below that she should sell the nichsei tzon barzel. Although the Tur's wording implies that she may sell to another person, while according to what I have written the protection against waiver exists specifically if she sells to the injured party himself, this is not difficult. Even if she sells to another, the injured party can transfer his claim to that buyer through maamad sheloshtan, or beit din can compel the woman to execute an instrument in favor of the injured party, who can then sell the ketubah claim to the purchaser for its present value. The purchaser is then secure because the woman cannot waive the claim, since she is indebted to the injured party through Rabbi Natan. When the Gemara says she should sell it to the injured party, it includes one who derives his power from the injured party. There is no difference between the injured party himself and his successor in right. This is what the Tur and Rosh intended. The explanation is clear and correct for halachah. By contrast, the responsum in klal 35 was written before the Rosh's retraction, when he still held that even a claim subject to Rabbi Natan could be waived. He therefore wrote that there was no effective solution through sale of the dowry instrument, because with respect to her husband she would certainly waive it. This is satisfactory. It follows from the Rosh and Tur that Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to a gift. This resolves all the preceding difficulties in a correct and clear manner. The Sema in seif katan 2 likewise appears to hold that a gift is not governed by Rabbi Natan. See there. The practical halachah therefore still requires study.
Whether by way of wages. Many have wondered about the Rambam. In the first chapter of Kiddushin the Gemara says that according to the one who accepts Rabbi Natan's rule, the verse concerning the grant to a Hebrew servant, "you shall grant to him," excludes a creditor, so that the grant is given to the servant and not to his creditor. But according to the one who elsewhere also rejects Rabbi Natan's rule, the word "to him" is needed to exclude one who sold himself into servitude, who does not receive a grant. The Rambam rules like Rabbi Natan, yet nevertheless also rules that one who sold himself does not receive a grant. He appears to combine two incompatible approaches. This is especially difficult because he also rules that the grant is given to the servant and not to his creditor, writing explicitly that the creditor has no right at all in the grant. The Shach and other authorities answer that the Rambam sees no necessary connection between these rules. Although we rule like Rabbi Natan, the verse is not needed to exclude the creditor from the grant because the grant is in the nature of a gratuitous benefit and charity. The Sema calls it a concession and gift. Rabbi Natan's rule therefore does not apply, and the word "to him" can instead exclude one who sold himself. This is the essence of their answer. The reasoning is sound in itself, but a difficulty remains. The Gemara explicitly equates the matters and says that the interpretation depends on whether Rabbi Natan's rule applies. How can we introduce a distinction that the Gemara itself did not accept? What do we know that the Gemara did not? It appears that the Rambam can be reconciled as follows. In ordinary debts, the assets of the final borrower are certainly subject to the second creditor. Since that creditor himself owes the first creditor, the final borrower's assets become subject to the first creditor just as though the second creditor had sold his debt to the first creditor. Indeed, a statutory lien is stronger than an ordinary sale. Therefore one may collect from one debtor and give to the creditor of that debtor. A servant's termination grant is different. Although beit din compels the master to make the grant, the master's assets are not thereby encumbered to the servant. It is merely a mitzvah incumbent upon the master personally. Rabbi Natan therefore does not ordinarily apply, for what lien exists here that could pass to the servant's creditor? Nor have we found that the master's person itself becomes directly obligated to the servant's creditor merely because of this mitzvah, and there is no reason to assume such a rule without proof. Thus no verse is required to exclude the creditor. This works according to the view that property liens are of Torah origin. According to the view that liens are not of Torah origin, however, even an ordinary debt creates no Torah lien on property. Payment of a creditor is simply a mitzvah incumbent on the debtor personally, as is clear in Ketubot according to Rav Pappa. Yet Rabbi Natan is still derived from the verse "and he shall give it to the one to whom he is liable." We must therefore say that the Torah imposed the personal obligation of the second debtor directly toward the first creditor. The Rashba explicitly writes this at the end of Kiddushin according to the view that liens are not of Torah origin. On that premise, the servant's grant is comparable, because it too is a mitzvah incumbent personally on the master, and beit din compels him to perform it just as with payment of a debt. Without the exclusion "to him," we could therefore have compared it to an ordinary debt and said that the master's personal duty is redirected to the servant's creditor. This is satisfactory. The entire sugya in Kiddushin follows Abaye. Abaye is the one who introduced the proposition that everyone derives the gezeirah shavah from "hired servant" to "hired servant," and that the dispute instead concerns whether "to him" excludes the creditor and whether Rabbi Natan applies. Without Abaye, one might simply have said that they disagree whether that gezeirah shavah is made at all. Abaye follows his own position that property liens are not of Torah origin, as Tosafot explicitly write in Gittin 50. Thus according to Abaye the grant is indeed comparable to an ordinary debt. But since we do not rule like Abaye on this point, and we hold that property liens are of Torah origin, a servant's grant is not comparable to an ordinary debt. The phrase "to him" is therefore not needed to exclude the creditor under Rabbi Natan and may instead exclude one who sold himself. Nevertheless, the independent rule that the creditor has no right in the servant's grant remains valid. The Rambam's rulings are thus well understood. There is a second way to explain this. From the Rambam's language it appears that when we say the grant belongs to the servant and not to his creditor, we mean that even after the servant actually received the grant, the creditor has no right to seize it. The Shach likewise says that the Rambam compares the grant to poor-tithe funds, from which the Rambam rules that a creditor cannot collect. This must concern funds that have already reached the poor person, since before that they are property with no specific claimant. The Maharit in his novellae to Kiddushin makes the same inference from the Rambam's wording that "the servant's grant belongs to himself and not to his creditor," and the Mishneh LaMelech similarly infers from the Rambam's commentary to the Mishnah in the first chapter of Kiddushin, where he writes that the servant's grant belongs to him and his creditor has no right in it and may not collect his debt from it. Everyone wonders: if so, how can this rule depend on Rabbi Natan? Rabbi Natan concerns collection directly from the second debtor to the first creditor. Once the intermediate creditor has already collected his own debt, it is obvious that his creditor can ordinarily collect from him. Yet with a servant's grant, even after the servant received it, the creditor cannot seize it. It would therefore seem obvious that the exclusion "to him and not to his creditor" is necessary according to everyone. This requires explanation. The previous answers are all strained. It therefore appears that when the Rambam says that even after the servant received the grant his creditor has no right in it, he means that beit din does not descend upon those funds and seize the grant in payment of the debt, as the Mishneh LaMelech explains. The grant itself was never encumbered to the creditor. But certainly the servant remains personally obligated to satisfy his debt if he has means beyond what is protected for basic support. What difference does it make whether those means came from this source or another? Payment of a debt is a mitzvah, and if he has the ability to pay, why should he refrain? This is straightforward. According to the view that property liens are of Torah origin, therefore, where ordinary property is encumbered to the creditor, the verse "to him and not to his creditor" is meaningful with respect to the grant: it teaches that the grant itself is not encumbered, beit din does not seize it, and it cannot be distrained. According to the view that liens are not of Torah origin, however, no ordinary debtor's property is itself encumbered. There is only a personal mitzvah upon the debtor to pay, as Rashi explains in Ketubot and the Rashbam at the end of Bava Batra. What then would "to him and not to his creditor" teach regarding the grant's property status? No verse is needed, because no property is directly encumbered anywhere. As to the personal obligation of the servant himself, that certainly remains, because he is an ordinary debtor and payment of a debt is a mitzvah. The verse must therefore teach that the master himself is not directly obligated to the servant's creditor. That in turn depends on whether the second debtor generally becomes directly obligated to the first creditor, namely Rabbi Natan's rule. This explains Abaye, who holds that liens are not of Torah origin, as stated. For him, the dispute over "to him and not to his creditor" properly tracks the dispute about Rabbi Natan. But according to our ruling that liens are of Torah origin, "to him and not to his creditor" can bear its ordinary meaning, that even when the grant reaches the servant it is not itself encumbered to the creditor. That interpretation does not depend on Rabbi Natan at all. Even if the halachah were not like Rabbi Natan, we would still derive "to him and not to his creditor," as the Rambam rules. The dispute about one who sold himself then turns on whether the gezeirah shavah from "hired servant" to "hired servant" is derived. We rule that it is not, and therefore one who sold himself does not receive a grant, as the Ritva explains. This is satisfactory, and the Rambam's rulings are clear and well reconciled.
Furthermore, the first difficulty raised by the Shach and the other authorities can be answered another way. In the dispute between Rabbi Natan and the Sages whether one takes from one debtor and gives to the creditor of that debtor, the commentators ask for the Sages' rationale. Is this not merely avoiding a circular transfer? If the intermediate creditor collects, his creditor will immediately take the money from him. Why require this pointless sequence? They answer that the Sages hold we do not apply the principle that a needless circular transfer should be avoided. The second debtor can say to the first creditor, "You are not my litigant." Rabbi Natan alone teaches through the verse that the circular transfer may be bypassed. The Rashba writes this in the first chapter of Kiddushin. At the end of Kiddushin, however, it appears that in the debtor's lifetime there may in truth be no practical difference, because the circularity argument applies regardless. The distinction appears after death. If orphans collected land in payment of a debt owed to their father, their father's creditor may collect that land from them only because it was subject through Rabbi Natan, as Pesachim states. Without Rabbi Natan, the heirs could argue that they acquired the land as purchasers and that the creditor has no claim against it. Thus there is a substantial practical difference between the Sages and Rabbi Natan after death. According to this latter explanation, during the debtor's lifetime even the Sages would agree that one can collect directly. Consequently, with a living Hebrew servant there is no disagreement between the Sages and Rabbi Natan, and the phrase "to him" is needed according to everyone to exclude the creditor from the grant. If so, why would the master not be obligated? The answer must be, as above, that even after the grant reaches the servant the creditor has no specific right in that grant. There is no circular-transfer argument. One who holds that a self-sold servant receives no grant cannot therefore derive that rule from "to him"; rather, he must hold that the gezeirah shavah of "hired servant" is not derived. But according to the first explanation, that even during life the Sages reject the circularity principle, one may say that the authority who excludes a self-sold servant follows the Sages, for whom one does not ordinarily collect directly anyway, making it reasonable to use "to him" for the self-sold servant. Abaye follows his own position. In Pesachim he holds that the creditor acquires retroactively. According to him, orphans who collected land are liable to their father's creditor even without Rabbi Natan, as the Gemara there explains. Therefore he cannot use the second explanation that the dispute concerns only the effect after death. He must say that Rabbi Natan and the Sages disagree already during life over whether the circular transfer is bypassed. It follows that according to Abaye the interpretation "to him and not to a self-sold servant" can indeed depend on the dispute between the Sages and Rabbi Natan, as stated. But according to our ruling that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection, and given the general principle throughout the Gemara that a pointless circular transfer is bypassed, as the Rashba says is implied by the sugya at the end of Ketubot, the disagreement can be understood as concerning the post-death case. During life all agree that one collects directly. The phrase "to him and not to his creditor" is therefore needed according to everyone. The authority who denies a grant to one who sold himself must then do so because he does not derive the gezeirah shavah. These are precisely the Rambam's rulings. This is satisfactory. Consider this carefully.
And the interest that accrued on it. The Shach, in seif katan 4 and at greater length above in siman 72 seif katan 162, holds that if interest was fixed with a non-Jew for a year and collateral was taken for that year, then even though the non-Jew may redeem the collateral and discharge the debt before the year ends, thereby stopping future interest, the Jewish creditor nevertheless acquires a right to the interest through the end of the pledged term. This does not appear correct. The Rashba's proof in his responsum is from the case of one who undertakes to support his wife's daughter for five years, where she may collect support even from encumbered property. What proof is there if, in the collateral case, the non-Jew may redeem early? The Shach says that even in the support case, where the undertaking is only "so long as you remain with me," the daughter collects from encumbered property even though the husband has the power to divorce the mother. But there is no comparison. There, purchasers cannot say, "We divorce your wife." She remains securely married to her husband, and so long as he does not divorce her the daughter's lien remains in force. Here, by contrast, the later Jewish creditor who stands in the non-Jew's shoes can say, "I will discharge you now." What difference is there between the non-Jew himself and one who comes through him? The later Jewish creditor, as creditor of the non-Jew's creditor, proceeds through Rabbi Natan, and his legal power is the same as that of the non-Jew. Therefore the Bach is clearly correct. The Rashba's responsum concerns only interest that had already accrued before the second Jewish creditor sought to discharge the earlier creditor. In the actual case before the Rashba there was no collateral. Rather, there were an earlier and a later creditor. The later creditor came to collect and argued: how can you take interest that accrued day by day when I lent in the meantime, so that my principal debt predates the interest that accrued afterward? From this the Rashba proves from the case of daughters' support that they collect from encumbered property even when purchasers acquired the property before the five years ended, provided the support obligation had already been fixed. The purchasers remain liable for the support throughout the full five years.
