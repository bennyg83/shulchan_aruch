Reuven is owed one hundred by Shimon. Dagul MeRevavah writes that Rashba, responsum 571, is uncertain whether Rabbi Natan's lien applies when the borrower is a non-Jew. Beit Yosef cites this response, and Dagul MeRevavah wonders why the issue is omitted from the Shulchan Aruch and later authorities. It is difficult, however, why he did not mention the Sema's ruling in seif katan 3 that the pledge is subject to the creditor by virtue of Rabbi Natan. Although the Sema discusses a case where the first borrower is a non-Jew, logic indicates the same, and all the more so, where the second borrower is a non-Jew. Netivot HaMishpat at the end of this siman likewise rules simply that Rabbi Natan's lien applies with a non-Jew, whether the second borrower or the first borrower is the non-Jew. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 2, writes that Responsa Ramban, siman 133, treats the law as obvious even when the borrower is a non-Jew. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 52, responds that no proof can be brought from that responsum. It requires study why these authorities did not mention the Rosh, Bava Batra chapter 8, siman 11, and Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer siman 100 seif 2, which explicitly show that Rabbi Natan's lien applies even where the borrower is a non-Jew. This omission is especially surprising in Noda BiYehudah, who cites the beginning of that Rosh, that a woman may collect her ketubah from a debt owed to her husband because Rabbi Natan's lien treats the debt as presently held for purposes of the ketubah, but does not cite the end of the Rosh, which states the same regarding a loan owed by a non-Jew. Tumim at the end of this siman also cites the Rosh and concludes as follows. If Reuven lent to Shimon and Shimon lent to a non-Jew, the non-Jew is certainly subject to Reuven under Rabbi Natan's lien. If Reuven lent to a non-Jew and the non-Jew lent to Shimon, then if the non-Jew's loan to Shimon preceded Reuven's loan to the non-Jew, Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply. If Reuven's loan to the non-Jew came first, the matter is doubtful. If, however, the non-Jew gave Reuven a lien upon movable property together with land, all agree that Shimon becomes subject to Reuven. Likewise, if Shimon holds money stolen from the non-Jew, it is certainly subject to Reuven. If a non-Jew lent to Reuven and Reuven lent to Shimon, and the non-Jew sues Shimon in a Jewish court, Rabbi Natan's lien certainly does not apply, because the verse 'and he shall give it to the one to whom he is guilty' was stated within Israel. The Mechaber's expression 'and Shimon is owed by Levi' plainly applies equally if Levi is owed by Yehudah and onward. The final debtor is required to pay Reuven, for in each link the verse applies to the person to whom the principal obligation ultimately belongs. This also follows from Divrei Mishpat below, seif 5, concerning the rule that Shimon cannot forgive the debt.
