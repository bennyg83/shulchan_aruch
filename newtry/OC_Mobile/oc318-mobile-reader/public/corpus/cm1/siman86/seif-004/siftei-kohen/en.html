Some say, etc. Mechaber found this rule only in a responsum of Rashba and therefore presents it as “some say.” As I proved at length above in seif 2, seif katan 5, however, the rule is compelled by the Gemara and many poskim. Rabbi Natan’s lien exists even when Shimon has property, but the Sages refrain from coercive collection where Levi would suffer a loss. With a deposit, where the custodian has no beneficial interest in the money, there is no such loss. Money held on deposit may therefore be taken from the custodian even if the depositor owns land or movables. See below in siman 101 and my comments there in seif 4.
They are money held on deposit, etc. This applies specifically to a deposit, not to a borrowed article. A borrower has the beneficial use of the article for the agreed term. Therefore, even if the owner has no other property, the article is not taken from the borrower before the term ends. The same applies to one who rents an article for a fixed term. Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer, siman 70, rules that where a husband travels overseas after depositing articles with another person or lending them for use, the deposited articles may be taken for his wife’s support. An article lent for use may not be taken before the loan term expires, because during that term it has the law of encumbered property, from which a wife’s support is not collected. Mechaber codifies this in Even HaEzer 70:7. Once the term ends, the article may be taken from the borrower where the husband is absent and has no property, through Rabbi Natan. The borrower cannot unilaterally retain it longer. Even the husband, were he present, could not extend the loan after the borrower became directly obligated through Rabbi Natan, just as with a monetary loan after the lien attached. Where the husband owns other property, however, rabbinic protection may prevent collection if the borrower would suffer a loss. There is an additional distinction. Once the term for a borrowed article ends, the article itself belongs to the owner wherever it is found, and the borrower has no authority to keep it merely because the owner is absent. With money lent, by contrast, if the lender is absent the borrower may argue that he will retain the funds until his own creditor demands them. If the lender has other assets and the borrower would lose by early payment, the Sages do not compel him through Rabbi Natan, although the Torah lien exists. Rama in Even HaEzer writes that property held by others is not taken while free assets are available, citing Rashba. This is not correct as a general statement. Since Rabbi Natan applies even where the owner has other assets, money held as a true deposit, and an article whose loan term has ended, may be taken when the custodian or borrower suffers no loss. Rashba expressly writes in responsum 1122 that beit din may seize money of Shimon held by Reuven for a debt Shimon owes Levi even though Shimon owns other property, when the money is a deposit. Rama himself rules similarly below in siman 101, seif 4. If the borrower would suffer a loss because the agreed term has not ended, then the article is not taken even when no free property exists. Rashba’s reason concerning a wife’s support makes this explicit. Rama’s wording, which conditions the matter only on the existence of free property, does not accurately reflect Rashba. Rashba’s statement that a wife does not collect support from encumbered property does not mean that an ordinary creditor could always take a borrowed article before its term. As Rosh, Tur, and Ritva explain, a person may originally lend his movables for any lawful term even though he has creditors, because he could have given them away. Rabbi Natan attaches to the obligation as created and does not ordinarily accelerate that original term. The case in Rashba may instead involve movables expressly subjected together with land for the wife’s support. Without the special rule that support is not collected from encumbered property, such a lien could reach the article even before the borrowing term, just as it could reach a completed gift. Where a creditor has a valid lien on movables together with land and the debtor later gives or lends the identified movables, collection may be made from them. Rosh and Tur’s rule allowing the debtor to lend movables for a term concerns movables not subjected in this manner, or a customary general clause that does not reach such property. Money, moreover, ordinarily cannot be identified for a movables-together-with-land lien. This is the correct distinction.
