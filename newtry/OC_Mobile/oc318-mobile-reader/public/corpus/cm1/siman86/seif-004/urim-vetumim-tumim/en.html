They are money held as a deposit, etc. With an ordinary deposit it is obvious that one may always collect directly from the bailee, even where the intermediate creditor has other assets, because the deposited property is not merely encumbered property at all. The rule that one does not collect from encumbered property when free assets exist is therefore irrelevant. This is clear with sealed or designated money that the bailee may not use. But where the money was deposited for unrestricted use, so that the bailee is permitted to use it, one might think there is a loss to him and that his status resembles a borrower. Yet the Rashba's responsum states without qualification that if Levi holds the money as a deposit, one may collect it directly from him, and he does not distinguish between sealed money and money permitted for use. Where the bailee's legal status is like that of a moneychanger who may use the funds, it appears that the Rashba nevertheless holds that so long as he has not actually used them, the funds remain the depositor's property. As I wrote at length above at the end of siman 73, since we rule like Rav Nachman regarding liability for unavoidable loss, if the depositor comes and demands his deposit the bailee may not substitute other money for it. The original funds remain in the depositor's legal possession and there is no issue of misuse so long as they remain untouched. Therefore the Rashba holds that one may collect them directly, and the rule concerning encumbered property does not apply because the bailee has not yet spent them and they are still the depositor's property. This also answers the Shach's question against the Mechaber for presenting the ruling in the name of "some say," as though there were uncertainty. It is not correct to say that the bailee suffers no possible loss. While the money remains with him, he may encounter a business opportunity and use it to earn profit. Once it is taken from him, that expected opportunity is lost. Nevertheless, because the funds still legally belong to the depositor, they may be taken. The matter still requires study because I have not found this point stated explicitly, although the unqualified language of the Rashba and Mechaber clearly points this way.
As a deposit. If a person lent an object to another for a fixed borrowing period, and during that period the owner himself owes money to a creditor, it is obvious that the object cannot be taken from the borrower even if the owner has no other assets. A borrower for a day is, in effect, like a purchaser for that day. If the owner had actually sold the object's use for the term, could an earlier creditor seize it free of that right? But if the owner wrote to his creditor a clause encumbering movables together with land, the creditor may collect even during the borrowing period. Where the owner has other assets, however, one does not collect the borrowed object during the agreed term in any circumstance. After the term ends, the creditor may collect it because the borrower's status then becomes that of a bailee. This is the same rule as the Rashba cited in this seif. The Shach writes accordingly, and on this basis he questions the Rama. In Even HaEzer siman 70 seif 7, the Mechaber rules that if a man went overseas and left property either as a deposit or on loan with others, one may take it from them for his wife's support, except that property held by a borrower cannot be taken until the borrowing period ends. The Rama adds that one does not take property held by others so long as unencumbered assets are available. The Shach objects that before the borrowing term ends one can never take it, while after the term ends one can always take it, so what room is there for the Rama's distinction? In truth there are two answers. First, it appears from the Shulchan Aruch there that the support is not being collected for a past debt, but for her future maintenance. As I wrote earlier, beit din releases support only for six months at a time. Rabbi Natan therefore does not yet apply, because the husband has not yet become indebted for future support. Even with a deposit, one therefore does not collect from the third party where the husband has other free assets, because there is not yet a present Rabbi Natan obligation. That is one answer. The second, and more important, is that the Rama was troubled by what I wrote above. In the Bava Kamma case of one who borrowed an ox believed to be innocuous, the borrowing period must apparently have ended, and yet Rabbi Natan applies only when the owner lacks other assets. The distinction I suggested above is strained. Therefore the Rama holds that specifically where the object was lent for a fixed period, once that term ends the borrower no longer has any right to use it and the object ceases to be treated as encumbered property. It is then merely a deposit. But with an open-ended borrowing arrangement, under which the borrower may continue using the object until the owner demands its return, if the owner has not demanded it the object remains treated as encumbered property because the borrower still has the right to use it. The enactment that one does not collect from encumbered property when free assets exist therefore applies. This explains the Bava Kamma case, which can concern an unspecified borrowing. The owner can still argue that the borrower should have returned the ox once circumstances changed, as explained above, yet until actual demand the borrower's use right remains. This, it appears, is what the Rama intended in Even HaEzer when he wrote that where the husband's property is in another's hands, one distinguishes between whether he has other assets. The case can concern an unspecified loan of use, and then there is indeed a difference between having other assets and not having them. This also explains why the Rama did not cite in this siman the Beit Yosef's view, although in Darkei Moshe he agreed with the proposition that once the due date of a monetary loan arrives the rule against collecting from encumbered property no longer applies. According to what I have written, so long as the debtor may continue using the money because the creditor has not demanded payment, the debt still resembles encumbered property even after the nominal term has passed. Therefore the Rama omitted the ruling here. Consider this carefully, for this appears correct for practical halachah.
