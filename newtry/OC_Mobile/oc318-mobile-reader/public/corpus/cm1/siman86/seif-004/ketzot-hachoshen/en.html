They are deposit funds. Rashba's responsum discusses money held on deposit. Maharshal and the Shach say that Rabbi Natan's lien applies even where the final relationship is deposit rather than loan, citing the sugya of the ox held by a custodian. I find this very difficult. If Rabbi Natan applies to an ordinary deposit, then just as the intermediate creditor cannot waive the final debt, the depositor should be unable to give the deposited object to the custodian. Yet ordinary deposited movables are no worse than other movables, which the owner can generally give away even though he has creditors. In my view Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply to an ordinary deposit while the object remains intact. Under the accepted view, a custodian's property does not become subjected until negligence or another event creating liability. Before that moment there is no debt at all. The object itself remains the depositor's property wherever it is. When the owner demands it, he is taking his own property, not collecting a debt from the custodian. There is therefore no intermediate debt upon which Rabbi Natan's lien can operate. This is proven by the rule that a borrower or custodian who incurs a capital liability at the same time as negligence can be exempt under the principle of the greater liability. If a full personal debt already existed from the moment the object was entrusted, that result would be difficult. The accepted rule is that the monetary obligation of the custodian arises at the later event of liability. The ox case does not prove otherwise. There the injured party seeks collection from the ox itself. The custodian's failure can create a liability at that point, and Rabbi Natan's rule can explain why the injured party reaches the relevant right. The Gemara asks why the custodian did not hide the ox and negotiate with the injured party. Once his failure to do so creates liability, the resulting obligation can become subject to Rabbi Natan. That is not the same as saying an intact ordinary deposit is already an intermediate debt. Accordingly, a creditor may collect an intact deposit held by a third party simply because it remains the debtor's own property. No Rabbi Natan rule is needed. The depositor can also give it to the custodian like other movables, subject to ordinary creditor collection rules. If the deposit is money that remains intact, it likewise remains the depositor's property until actually spent where spending is permitted. This also explains Rashba's language. His reference to Rabbi Natan is not necessarily the foundation of the rule but an additional strengthening. The primary reason an intact deposit can be reached is that it remains the debtor's property. Thus the law of deposits differs fundamentally from an ordinary loan.
