Wild. “And I swear to him, I will not swear to him.” Name:
Against them. The Sema cites authorities who explain that "he returns against them" means he may recover from the heirs' own property, because they caused him damage by refusing to swear, under the law of indirect causation. The Sema rejects their view and explains that it means only that he returns against property they inherited from their father. But if they inherited nothing except this promissory note and say it has been paid, they must swear that they know it was paid and must include in their oath that they inherited no other property from which their father's debt could be paid. Although the Geonim did not institute the oath of "I have no property" for heirs, here it was imposed through the mechanism of an oath by extension. The Shach disputes the Sema and agrees with the first explanation at length. He concludes, however, that according to what he wrote above, there is ordinarily no practical difference in this law, since Reuven always collects under Rabbi Nathan's rule even if the heirs refuse to swear. Reuven instead swears that Shimon did not pay him and collects from Levi, including in his oath that he does not know Shimon's note to have been paid. If Shimon's note predates Reuven's note, Reuven must also include that Shimon never told him that Levi had failed to pay. The practical difference arises where one sold a promissory note and the debtor says it was paid and demands that the seller's heirs swear. If they refuse to swear and the purchaser thereby loses, the heirs must compensate the purchaser from their own property under the law of indirect causation for the full value of the note, as I wrote in siman 66, seif 15, unlike the Sema there. See there. Shach.
Hesset. The Shach disagrees and holds that the heirs must take a stringent oath while holding a sacred object, like the oath of "I have no property" at the beginning of siman 99. Although that oath was not enacted for an heir regarding his father's debt, here, since the heirs are personally liable to pay from their own property under the law of indirect causation, as explained above, it is treated as their own debt and the special rule of heirs does not apply. Here too there is no practical difference according to what I wrote above, except in the case of one who purchased a promissory note as described above. If the heirs then claim that they have no property with which to pay, they take a stringent oath while holding a sacred object under the enactment of the Geonim. This appears correct. Shach.
