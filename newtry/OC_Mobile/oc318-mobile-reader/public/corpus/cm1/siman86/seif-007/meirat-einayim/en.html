And he says wild Levi. He has no loyalty to the priests as “a father of their father and as a result of it:
If they do not wish to swear, he may return against them, etc. Some explain that he may collect from the heirs' personal property because their refusal to swear prevents Reuven from collecting from Levi and thereby causes him loss. I rejected this at length in Derishah. The Mechaber follows Baal HaTerumot, and their words show that 'return against them' means against the assets they inherited from their father, not against their independent property. Otherwise the later phrase, 'if they have no other assets,' would also have to mean no personal assets, which would not fit the rule. Moreover, even where heirs cause loss they are not ordinarily liable from their own pockets. This can be inferred all the more from siman 107, where heirs who sell their father's movable property are not required to reimburse the creditor from the sale proceeds even though their act caused his loss. A promissory note is treated like movable property in this regard. Thus, if the heirs merely refuse to swear and do not claim the note was paid, Reuven may proceed against whatever other inherited assets exist, and at minimum may compel them through bans to take the necessary oath so as not to cause him loss. If they then claim they inherited no other assets and that they cannot swear because they know this note was paid, we do not simply accept that assertion. Since the note was among their father's assets at death, it retains a presumption of being unpaid. They must swear that they know with certainty that it was paid, and by extension may also be required to swear that they have no inherited assets from which their father's debt can be paid. Although the Geonim did not originally enact an 'I have nothing' oath against heirs, it may be imposed here by extension of the required oath. The objection that Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply where free assets exist can be answered by cases where the inherited assets are elsewhere, not yet due, or otherwise not presently collectible.
And they claim that they cannot swear. This means that they say they know the document was paid. That is the Tur's formulation and is clear from Baal HaTerumot. The Ir Shushan's explanation that they merely do not know is incorrect. Every heir who swears, 'My father did not instruct us,' does not know the father's affairs with certainty. If he actually knew for certain that the debt remained unpaid he would not need that formulation. Yet heirs can collect on such an oath because the document is left in its established presumption of validity.
They take an oath of hesset, etc. The Tur explains that the creditor may impose hesset because he asserts with certainty that they inherited this document from their father and that he has a legal right in it. Ordinarily hesset is not taken on a doubtful claim, and here his knowledge concerning their ultimate liability is not direct. Nevertheless, because the document itself is visibly in their possession, their contention is weakened. If Levi had actually paid, why is Levi's note still in Shimon's estate?
They take an oath of hesset. The Tur writes this as well, because it is as though they are made to swear that what they say is true, namely that they know the document was paid. They are not required to swear while holding a sacred object as one who claims to have no assets ordinarily must, because the Geonim enacted that oath only against the borrower himself, not against heirs. I noted in Perishah and Derishah that in my view the Tur does not require them to swear that they lack assets, but only that this document was paid.
