If they were unwilling to swear, and so forth. The Baal HaTerumot and Tur follow their view that where Shimon or his heirs must swear and refuse, collection cannot be made under Rabbi Natan's rule. I have already demonstrated that this is not the law. With respect to Rabbi Natan's lien, the document retains its force. Even if Shimon or his heirs are obligated to swear, refuse to do so, and say that they know the document was paid, they cannot thereby destroy Reuven's right. The Gemara indicates that once Levi is bound to Reuven under Rabbi Natan's rule, it is as though Reuven himself lent to Levi, and Reuven is Levi's direct litigant by Torah law. The heirs' oath therefore cannot eliminate that Torah lien. The law differs from a sale of a promissory note. A purchaser derives his title from the seller through writing and delivery, and if the seller refuses to swear he is effectively acknowledging payment and is believed through the power he retained to forgive the debt. Under Rabbi Natan's rule, however, Shimon cannot forgive the debt and is not believed to declare it paid, because Reuven is Levi's direct claimant. There is also concern for collusion, which does not apply in the same way to a seller who could simply forgive the note. Accordingly, the correct ruling is the alternative opinion cited by the Baal HaTerumot and Tur: Reuven swears that Shimon did not instruct him that the debt had been paid, and then collects from Levi. The reason for this oath is that Shimon may have made such a declaration before Levi's debt became bound to Reuven, for example where Levi first borrowed from Shimon and only later Shimon borrowed from Reuven. If Reuven's document predates Shimon's document against Levi, Reuven need not swear that Shimon gave him no such instruction. The Baal HaItur does not disagree. His rejection of a purchaser's oath concerns a purchaser who sues on a sold note while the debtor asserts payment, because the seller or his heirs must answer that certain claim and because the written assignment itself attests that the seller represented the note as unpaid. Those considerations do not apply here, where even an admission by Shimon or his heirs would not discharge Levi. Moreover, because Levi claims to have paid Shimon, Reuven must swear on his own document as one who collects from encumbered property, and through an added oath must also swear that Shimon did not tell him before the lien arose that Levi's document was paid and that Reuven himself does not know it to have been paid.
He may recover from them if they possess assets. The language of the Baal HaTerumot and Mechaber indicates that Reuven may recover from the heirs' own property. By refusing to swear they directly cause the loss of his debt and are liable under the law of causative damages, just as heirs who forgive a note may be liable. This remains true even if they assert that they know with certainty that the note was paid, because their statement itself destroys Reuven's document. The Sema's attempted proof from heirs who sell their father's movable property is not comparable. The Geonic enactment allowing collection from heirs' movables applies only while those movables remain in their possession; after a sale the Torah rule reasserts itself. Here, by contrast, Reuven's lien under Rabbi Natan is itself a Torah right. Nor is the note merely ordinary movable property of the heirs. The Gemara teaches that when heirs collect land on their father's debt, their father's creditor may collect that land from them, because the underlying obligation remains. Likewise, the heirs who destroy this debt are liable for the damage they cause. The following clause, dealing with heirs who possess no property, refers to the absence of their own assets from which this liability could be collected.
They take a rabbinic oath, and so forth. The Tur states this and the Beit Yosef attributes it to the Baal HaTerumot, but that exact ruling is not found there. The source states only that where the heirs have no assets and claim that they do not know whether their father was paid, they are exempt. In my view, if Reuven has a direct claim against them because their refusal to swear destroyed his right, the applicable oath depends on the nature of his allegation. Where he alleges with certainty that they know the debt was unpaid, they take a rabbinic oath of denial. Where he merely suspects that they know, no oath is imposed on an uncertain claim, though a general ban may be declared. If they say that they know the note was paid, their own statement may render them liable for the causative loss, as explained above.
