Seif 5. (He went and paid.) If a person seized property from another on the claim, "You owe my creditor," and afterward that creditor himself seized property belonging to the claimant, the rule at the beginning of siman 58, that some say an agent who suffers loss through his seizure may not reimburse himself from the principal's property, does not apply. At the moment the first seizure was made from the defendant, Rabbi Nathan's lien was in force. The claimant does not lose that right merely because afterward the creditor proved stronger and seized from him. This appears reasonable. If the claimant did not actually seize but merely obtained an attachment, once the creditor later seized the claimant's own property, Rabbi Nathan's lien no longer applies and the attachment falls away. One cannot say that the attachment already effected acquisition for the claimant, because that would be seizure on behalf of a creditor where it harms other creditors. If the creditor seized only movable property that still requires appraisal, perhaps Rabbi Nathan's lien has not yet entirely disappeared. The same may be true where the underlying debts are not clear and the parties dispute them, so that the matter remains to be determined by beit din through oath or acceptance of a ban. In such a case one should not necessarily treat the earlier payment or deposit as creating the category of "I do not know whether I paid," because the nature and extent of the debt itself remain unresolved.
