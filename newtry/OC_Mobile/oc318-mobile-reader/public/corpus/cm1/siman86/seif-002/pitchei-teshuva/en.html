Beit din does not attend to it. See Baer Heitev and Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 53. He explains more fully what he wrote in the first edition, siman 34, namely that a creditor's ability to collect even from heirs a debt owed to their father by others, as stated in siman 107, need not rest solely upon Rabbi Natan's lien. In our times, debts are a primary form of livelihood and commercial reliance, so a receivable is itself treated like an ordinary asset. If Reuven is owed by Shimon and Shimon is owed by Levi, Reuven can reach the debt in two distinct ways. First, in collecting from Shimon he may collect from any of Shimon's assets. Among free assets there is no rule that one asset is more subject than another, and the debt owed by Levi is itself one of Shimon's assets. Beit din may therefore transfer that receivable to Reuven as payment, according to its value. Such a transfer is comparable to a sale and does not depend upon Rabbi Natan's lien. For this purpose it makes no difference whether Shimon has other assets, because beit din is not yet proceeding directly against Levi. It is simply collecting Shimon's own property, and the receivable is part of Shimon's free assets. Once beit din has assigned that debt to Reuven, or Shimon himself has transferred it to Reuven in payment, Reuven then stands as Levi's creditor and can collect from Levi. None of this requires Rabbi Natan's lien. The difference is that without Rabbi Natan's lien, even after Shimon transfers the debt to Reuven, Shimon may still forgive Levi, just as the seller of a debt may ordinarily forgive it. In addition, Reuven has no direct claim against Levi until Shimon or beit din transfers the receivable to him. Rabbi Natan's lien, by contrast, gives Reuven an immediate right to sue Levi directly, and Shimon cannot forgive the debt. That direct right applies only when Shimon lacks other assets. If Shimon has other property, Reuven has no direct Rabbi Natan right against Levi, which is the meaning of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch here that beit din does not attend to Levi where Shimon has other assets. The Shach's rulings are likewise understood this way. Nevertheless, Reuven may still collect Shimon's receivable itself as property, like any other asset of Shimon. Noda BiYehudah adds that even where Shimon has no other assets and Reuven therefore has a Rabbi Natan lien on Levi's debt, if Reuven forgives Levi before Shimon has transferred the debt to him as actual payment, the forgiveness removes only Reuven's Rabbi Natan lien. It does not extinguish the principal debt, which still belongs to Shimon. Reuven may therefore afterward collect that receivable from Shimon as payment; once it is transferred to him, he may collect it from Levi, not through Rabbi Natan's lien, which he waived, but as one who has succeeded to Shimon's own right. This resolves Responsa Rosh, rule 36 siman 4.
