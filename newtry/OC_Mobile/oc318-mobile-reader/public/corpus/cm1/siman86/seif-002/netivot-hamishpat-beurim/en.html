When does this apply. See Shach, seif katan 5, who argues at length that Rabbi Natan's lien applies even where the first borrower owns other assets. First, since the lien is Torah law, why should it matter whether he owns assets? He also brings proof from the chapter Kol Sha'ah and elsewhere. From this he concludes that Rabbi Natan's lien exists even where the first borrower has assets, but where collection from the second borrower would cause the second borrower a loss, we do not collect from him, just as one does not collect from encumbered property when the debtor has unencumbered assets, because that would impose unnecessary loss on purchasers. Tumim asks: if the entire reason Rabbi Natan's lien is not enforced where the first borrower has assets is that we do not collect from encumbered property when unencumbered property exists, then the main proof from Bava Kamma, concerning an animal borrowed as presumed harmless, remains difficult. The Gemara does not answer that Rabbi Natan's lien applies in a case where the first borrower has assets. Yet there the custodian suffers no loss, so even if the first borrower has assets Rabbi Natan's lien should still be enforceable under Shach's distinction. The original question therefore returns. It appears that all of Shach's difficulties can be resolved by understanding Rabbi Natan's lien exactly like an ordinary property lien, which is also Torah law. With an ordinary property lien we rule that the encumbered property functions only as a guarantor, and the creditor must first sue the borrower, as explained in the Gemara and authorities. Beit Yosef and Tur cite a responsum of the Geonim that money is not taken from another source until the borrower is first demanded to pay, and only if he refuses is collection made elsewhere. Rabbi Natan's lien likewise requires the creditor first to sue the original borrower. It may even be weaker than an ordinary property lien, because ordinary encumbered property can in some circumstances be reached without first suing the borrower, as explained in siman 104 seif 2 and in Derishah and Perishah there, while under Rabbi Natan the second borrower resembles a personal guarantor and is secondary to the original borrower. We therefore cannot say that Rabbi Natan's law makes the second borrower into a direct borrower of the first creditor. If it did, the first creditor would not need to sue the original borrower first. Moreover, if the first borrower died while movable property remained in the second borrower's hands, it would not become movable property of heirs, because the second borrower is still alive and would supposedly be directly indebted to the first creditor. Rather, Rabbi Natan means that the first borrower's lien against the second borrower becomes encumbered to the first creditor just like the first borrower's other property. Those assets are only guarantors for the debtor, and so too the first borrower's claim against the second borrower is treated only as a guarantor. The land held by the second borrower is in turn security for the first borrower's claim. Consequently, one does not collect from the second borrower when the first borrower has assets, just as one ordinarily does not proceed against a guarantor while the borrower can pay. The authorities nevertheless formulated the reason as "we do not collect from encumbered property when unencumbered property is available" rather than simply saying that a guarantor is exempt while the borrower has assets, because there is a difference. A guarantor can sometimes be compelled even where the borrower owns assets if the borrower is violent or refuses to pay. By contrast, encumbered property purchased by others is generally not seized while the borrower still owns collectible property, even if he is violent. Even according to Shach's stricter position regarding a violent wrongdoer, an ordinary recalcitrant borrower does not justify immediate collection from purchasers. The authorities therefore needed the encumbered property rationale in a case where the original borrower owns unencumbered assets but refuses to pay. The guarantor rationale alone might have required payment, while the rule protecting encumbered property exempts the second borrower. This protection, however, applies only when payment by the second borrower would cause him a loss. If he suffers no loss, he must pay when the original borrower refuses, even though the original borrower owns assets. It appears that this is also Shach's intent. This resolves the questions of Hafla'ah and Ketzot HaChoshen on Shach's ruling that if the first borrower has only inferior land while the second borrower has medium land, the creditor may collect the medium land through Rabbi Natan's lien. They ask from Ketubot, where two parties have promissory notes against each other and one owns medium land while the other owns inferior land, and each collects from the other. If Rabbi Natan's lien attached to the medium land, why would the result be so? According to what I wrote, where the first borrower is willing to pay, everyone agrees that the creditor cannot bypass him and collect from the second borrower. Rabbi Natan's claim against the second borrower is only analogous to a guarantor. Even an ordinary guarantor can direct the creditor to the borrower's inferior land when the borrower is ready to pay, as Sema writes in siman 129 seif katan 37, and Rabbi Natan's lien is certainly no stronger than a guarantor. Proof is found in seif 5. Where the first borrower has cash held as a deposit, he cannot tell the creditor to take land instead, specifically because a deposit remains in the owner's domain. The implication is that if the cash is merely owed to him as a loan by the second borrower, the first borrower may discharge the creditor with land even though the second borrower has cash and is prepared to pay immediately. The first borrower can say, "I do not wish to collect from my borrower, and you have no independent right to collect from him while I am prepared to satisfy you." Rashba states this explicitly in a responsum. All the more so the same applies to the difference between medium and inferior land. Shach's ruling therefore concerns a case where the first borrower refuses to pay. The creditor then turns to the second borrower under Rabbi Natan, as one turns to a guarantor. In that setting, Shach correctly says that if the second borrower owns medium and inferior land, and ordinary encumbered property rules would have limited collection to the inferior land, nevertheless where paying medium land causes him no special loss he is not treated like a purchaser of encumbered property and must pay the same quality that he would owe his own lender. The proof from Pesachim is also no difficulty. From the moment of the original loan, the second borrower's land becomes conditionally encumbered like that of a guarantor, to answer if the first borrower later lacks assets. This can occur even while the original borrower is alive. Movable property of the second borrower is different, because it is not ordinarily subject to such a Torah lien, as Tosafot explain there. The proof from prozbul is likewise no difficulty, because a prozbul can be written where the guarantor owns land, and Rabbi Natan's lien functions like a guaranty. The case in Ketubot of one who was owed money by his brother is also no difficulty, because there the debtor is effectively without free assets, since all relevant assets are already encumbered. Tumim's question is likewise answered. The Gemara's proof is intended to reject the idea that Rabbi Natan makes the second borrower a direct borrower of the first creditor. If that were so, the Gemara could indeed have answered with Rabbi Natan even where the original borrower owned assets. Since the second borrower is only analogous to a guarantor, however, where the original borrower has assets there is generally no present obligation upon the second borrower to pay, so the proposed answer would not work.
