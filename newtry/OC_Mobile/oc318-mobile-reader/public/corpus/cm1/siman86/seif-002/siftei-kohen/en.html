When does this apply? When Shimon has no property, etc. This law requires extensive analysis, and because it affects many other places I must explain it at length. Mechaber follows Baal HaTerumot and Tur. Rashba in responsum 1122, Ran in the second chapter of Ketubot, Rivash in siman 323, Baal HaIttur at the end of the laws of debt, Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh, Ramban in the responsum cited by Baal HaTerumot at the beginning of gate 51, and Rabbeinu Yerucham all write that Rabbi Natan’s rule is stated where the intermediate debtor has no other property. If their words are read literally, however, that cannot be the primary law. The correct view appears to be that of Hagahot Ashri in Shor Shenagach Arba’ah VaChamishah, citing Or Zarua in the name of his father, as quoted by Shiltei HaGibborim in Get Pashut, and of Maharam Recanati, Riaz, Maharshal, Gedulei Terumah, and Lechem Mishneh: Rabbi Natan’s lien exists even when the intermediate debtor owns other assets. The Gemara in Bava Kamma does not prove otherwise. It first says that when the tortfeasor owns assets, he himself must pay from superior land without recourse to Rabbi Natan, and it invokes Rabbi Natan only to solve the case where he lacks assets. The sugya does not say that the lien itself fails whenever other assets exist. The authorities compare the second debt to encumbered property, from which one does not collect while the debtor has unencumbered assets. Read literally, that analogy is difficult. The rule protecting purchasers was enacted to avoid their loss, whereas the second debtor ordinarily loses nothing by paying the money he already owes. The proper explanation is that those authorities speak where payment to the first creditor would cause the second debtor an actual disadvantage, such as where he says the note is a trust arrangement or was paid, where the intermediate creditor forgave or gave away the debt, extended its term, or agreed to leave the funds with him longer. In that situation the second debt resembles encumbered property: although the Torah lien exists, the Sages protect the second debtor from loss while the intermediate debtor has other free assets. Maharshal correctly argues that Rabbi Natan’s rule is derived from Scripture and creates a Torah lien, so its existence cannot depend on whether the intermediate debtor owns property. He nevertheless says beit din is not obligated to litigate directly against the second debtor while the intermediate debtor has assets, though a seizure is not reversed and the intermediate creditor cannot forgive a documented debt. The first point is difficult. If the first creditor has a Torah lien, why would beit din not enforce it? Rabbi Natan’s language, “we take from this one and give to that one,” speaks precisely of judicial enforcement. Baal HaTerumot himself says that beit din, rather than the intermediate debtor, effects the collection. This is language of obligation, not mere permission. Maharshal also seems to read Baal HaTerumot as accepting Rabbi Natan even where there are other assets but limiting judicial enforcement. Baal HaTerumot actually writes explicitly that Rabbi Natan’s case is one where the intermediate debtor lacks property and cites Bava Kamma as proof. Tur and Baal HaTerumot below in siman 125 likewise appear to say that where the intermediate debtor owns property Rabbi Natan does not operate even after seizure. Their words require explanation, as I discuss there. Maharshal’s proposed consequence concerning forgiveness is also not the proper practical distinction, as will be explained. There are clear proofs that the Torah lien exists even when the intermediate debtor owns assets. First, if ordinary property liens are Torah law and attach regardless of whether the debtor later retains free property, Rabbi Natan’s direct lien must do the same. The rabbinic protection of purchasers has no automatic application where the second debtor suffers no loss. Second, Pesachim 31a states that although Rava holds a creditor acquires only from the time of collection, a creditor may seize land that heirs collected on debts owed to their father. The Gemara explains that the second debtor was directly obligated to the father’s creditor through Rabbi Natan. This ruling is unqualified and therefore applies even if the father left movables. Were Rabbi Natan inactive whenever other property existed, the land collected by the heirs would be newly acquired property beyond the father’s creditor’s lien. The same follows from the yavam who is himself indebted to his deceased brother, from the rule that a prozbul may be written where the debtor’s debtor owns land, and from other sugyot. Rashba and Rabbeinu Yerucham also write elsewhere that a creditor or custodian may voluntarily pay the claimant of the person to whom he is obligated, without limiting the rule to a case where that person owns no other assets. The distinction is therefore not whether the Torah lien exists, but whether enforcing it causes the second debtor a loss. Where there is no loss, collection may be made even though the intermediate debtor owns other assets. Thus, if Shimon owns only inferior land while Levi owes him money or owns intermediate land and is ready to pay, Reuven may collect Levi’s money or intermediate land. Levi cannot say, “You are not my litigant,” because Rabbi Natan makes him directly obligated to Reuven. Likewise, if Shimon owns only land while Levi owes cash or movables and is ready to pay, Reuven may collect those assets directly. Where Levi would suffer a loss, however, the Sages do not force payment while Shimon has free property. Examples include Levi’s claim that the note is paid or a trust arrangement, Shimon’s forgiveness or gift of the debt, an extension of time, or Levi’s present need to retain the funds with Shimon’s consent. Although the lien exists by Torah law, it is no stronger than an ordinary Torah lien on land sold or given away, which the Sages postpone while the debtor has free property in order to protect purchasers and donees. A claim of payment or forgiveness creates at least as real a loss as a gift. Rambam at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh and Semag support this distinction. They first state Rabbi Natan’s rule without qualification: if Shimon owes Reuven one hundred and Levi owes Shimon one hundred, collection is made from Levi for Reuven. They then say, “Therefore, if Shimon has no property and possesses a note against Levi, and Shimon admits that the note was a trust arrangement or paid, his admission is disregarded lest they be conspiring. Reuven swears and collects from Levi like any creditor collecting from encumbered property.” If Rabbi Natan existed only where Shimon had no assets, Rambam should have included that limitation in the initial statement. Elsewhere Rambam rules that a creditor may collect land that heirs recovered on their father’s debts even where the father left movables. The words “if Shimon has no property” therefore introduce the special case in which Levi claims a loss. When Shimon has property, rabbinic protection prevents collection from Levi in such a disputed case. When Shimon has none, Reuven collects, but only after swearing, just as one collecting from purchasers must swear. The oath itself proves that Levi is protected as one who would suffer loss, even though the Torah lien attaches directly. This also explains Rambam’s prozbul ruling. A prozbul may be written on the basis of land held by the debtor’s debtor through Rabbi Natan, without regard to whether the intermediate debtor owns movables. Lechem Mishneh’s forced distinctions are unnecessary. Rabbi Natan always creates the lien; the limitation concerns only coercive collection where the second debtor suffers a protected loss. Accordingly, when the many poskim say Rabbi Natan applies specifically where the intermediate debtor lacks property, they refer to compulsory collection against the second debtor’s interest, such as where he asserts payment, trust, forgiveness, gift, extension, or another disadvantage. They do not deny the underlying Torah lien. This resolves Maharshal’s objection to Ran. Ran’s discussion concerns precisely a note alleged to be a trust arrangement or paid. In such a case Or Zarua may agree that rabbinic protection operates while Shimon has other assets. The same explains the authorities’ proof from Bava Kamma. That sugya invokes Rabbi Natan only in the no-asset case and therefore does not establish coercive collection in a case of loss while other property exists. Yet Rashba himself rules that money held merely as a deposit may be taken even when the depositor owns other assets. There is no contradiction. A custodian has no beneficial interest in retaining deposited money, so no protected loss exists. Rabbeinu Yerucham similarly requires a custodian to pay the depositor’s creditor through Rabbi Natan without such a limitation. Rashba further writes that if Reuven’s debtor or custodian voluntarily pays a creditor of Reuven from property in his possession, he may do so. He does not distinguish based on Reuven’s other assets. When the second debtor chooses to pay, there is no need for the rabbinic protection intended for his benefit. This is not the rule of one who pays another’s debt without authorization, because Rabbi Natan makes the payee his own Torah creditor. Ritva’s language in Ketubot also accords with this. He asks why Rabbi Natan is necessary, since an ordinary creditor can collect encumbered property, and answers that Rabbi Natan makes the second debtor personally obligated. Collection can then be made without the ordinary requirements governing purchased property, including in an appropriate case without an oath, because the second debtor suffers no loss when payment is due. Ritva’s qualification, that collection occurs when the term arrives and there is no loss to the second debtor, confirms the distinction. Maharshal himself effectively concedes it by asking what enactment or loss exists in the ordinary case. This implies that where a genuine loss exists, the analogy to encumbered property is sound. His statement that Shimon cannot forgive a debt despite owning assets should therefore be limited. If Shimon owns sufficient property at the time collection is sought and forgiveness would cause Levi a loss, rabbinic protection prevents direct collection from Levi. It is possible Maharshal meant that Shimon had assets when he forgave the debt but no longer has them when Reuven seeks collection. In that event the Torah lien had already attached, and once no free property remains there is no basis for the rabbinic postponement. Understood this way, Maharshal’s practical conclusion agrees with mine. The primary rule is therefore as follows. Rabbi Natan’s lien attaches by Torah law whether or not Shimon owns other assets. If Levi loses nothing by paying, Reuven may collect directly from him even while Shimon owns property. If immediate payment or disregard of Shimon’s act would cause Levi a genuine loss, the Sages treat the debt like encumbered property and require Reuven first to collect Shimon’s free assets. If Shimon has no such assets, Reuven may enforce the Torah lien against Levi, subject where appropriate to the oath imposed on one collecting from encumbered property.
After beit din arranged his exempt property, etc. Maharshal writes that if Shimon is absent, Reuven may collect directly from Levi. He distinguishes a guarantor, whom we do not trouble while the borrower has property and whom we require the creditor to await, because collection from the guarantor would later require further proceedings against the borrower’s property. Here, he says, there is no reason to force the creditor to wait unless Shimon is actually in town. This does not appear correct. Maharshal follows his view that Rabbi Natan is enforced even where Shimon owns property. According to what I explained above, where payment would cause Levi a loss, the Sages protect him just as they protect a guarantor. There is also the possibility that if Shimon were present he would prove the note paid. Beit din must therefore notify him and wait the appropriate period, as below in siman 126. Binyamin Ze’ev, responsum 399, rules accordingly. The geonic responsum cited by Beit Yosef here likewise says that Rav Akiva had a tradition not to take Levi’s money for Reuven until Reuven first sued Shimon. Rav Hai approved this and cited the Yerushalmi’s question, “May one collect from a person in his absence?” Rabbi Yirmiyah answered that three notices are sent to him. See also siman 106.
Beit din does not attend to the claim, etc. I wrote above that Ran and the other poskim compare the debt to encumbered property, which is not collected while free property is available. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe infer that they speak before the second debt’s due date, because after maturity it would not have the law of encumbered property. Sema objects that Ran’s continuation appears to discuss a matured debt and nevertheless denies collection while Shimon has assets. Bach attempts to reconcile the matter by saying that Rabbi Natan does not presently create a full lien before maturity but only commands payment to the first creditor when the term arrives. His explanation is unclear. If after maturity the debt no longer resembles encumbered property, why should Shimon’s other assets prevent collection then? Ran’s words do not support Bach’s formulation. On careful analysis, Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe are correct, but their reference to an unmatured debt must be understood precisely. Where Shimon already owed Reuven when he lent the money to Levi, Levi is treated as having received money already subject to Reuven. Shimon cannot extend to Levi a term that prejudices Reuven beyond the lawful arrangement. If Shimon has no assets, the first creditor may sometimes collect even before the nominal term, because the funds were already subject to him. If Shimon does have assets, forcing Levi to pay early creates a loss, and the debt is treated like encumbered property. A ketubah is different. When the husband’s obligation was created, the woman did not yet owe the person she later injured. The deferred and contingent term of the ketubah was lawfully established before Rabbi Natan could attach. The husband therefore cannot be forced to pay early merely because she later incurred a debt. This reconciles Ran’s statement concerning a ketubah. Nevertheless, Rosh’s responsum, cited by Tur at the end of this siman, allows a person who already owes money to make a new loan for whatever term he chooses. Mechaber and Ritva likewise imply this. The reason is that the lender could have given away his movables outright, and Rabbi Natan attaches to the later debt only when that debt is created. Therefore Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe should not be read as invalidating an original, lawfully fixed term. Rather, they speak where Shimon later extends Levi’s term, or where Shimon and Levi merely assert that the term has not arrived, or where Levi wishes to retain the money longer with Shimon’s indulgence, or where another present disadvantage would result from immediate payment. If Shimon owns property, the Sages do not force Levi to suffer that loss. If Shimon has no property, Shimon cannot create or assert a later extension that defeats Reuven’s lien, and collection is made. If it is known that the extension was part of the original loan, the ketubah analysis applies. If Shimon and Levi merely claim a later due date, they are not believed against Reuven where Shimon has no property. This resolves the apparent contradiction in Ran. The practical conclusion is that the encumbered-property analogy applies only where Levi has some real disadvantage in paying now. If there is no such disadvantage, the analogy does not apply. Thus, if Shimon owns only inferior land while Levi owes cash or movables, the debt has matured, and Levi loses nothing by paying, Reuven may collect directly from Levi, contrary to Sema. Rashba’s responsum 1122 and the other poskim who do not expressly mention maturity should be understood as cases where Levi wishes to retain the money for some benefit or may persuade Shimon to extend or forgive it. Gedulei Terumah similarly explains that because the money is not physically in the creditor’s hand, Levi may ordinarily say that he hopes to continue benefiting from it or to obtain indulgence. When no such loss exists, direct collection is proper.
