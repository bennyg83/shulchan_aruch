This applies when Shimon has no assets. Many authorities limit active use of Rabbi Natan's lien to a case where the intermediate debtor has no property. Maharshal, however, rules that the lien itself exists even when he has assets. Since the law derives from Scripture and is a Torah lien, there is no conceptual distinction based on solvency. Beit din may not be required to proceed against the final debtor while the intermediate debtor has available property, but the lien itself remains. Thus if the final debtor holds property or payment subject to the intermediate debt, the first creditor's right is not displaced merely because the intermediate debtor also owns other assets. The Shach agrees that Rabbi Natan's lien exists even where the intermediate debtor has assets, but he allows the intermediate debtor greater freedom where enforcing the lien would cause him loss. Thus if Shimon extends Levi's time or waives Levi's debt, the first creditor cannot necessarily proceed against Levi while Shimon has free assets, because creditors do not collect from encumbered property when free property is available. Where no loss results to Shimon, however, the first creditor may reach Levi directly. For example, if Shimon has only inferior land and Levi has medium quality land and is willing to pay, Reuven can collect the medium quality land from Levi. Similarly, if Shimon has only land and Levi has cash or movables, Reuven may collect those from Levi. In my view Maharshal's position is stronger. As explained above, Rabbi Natan's lien is not like an ordinary lien on assets still owned by the intermediate debtor. The body of the final debt belongs to the first creditor. If Shimon purports to sell or waive what is no longer solely his, that transaction cannot defeat Reuven. This is unlike ordinary encumbered property, where title remains with the debtor and the creditor has only a collection right. A practical distinction arises where the quality of land differs. If Shimon's debt to Reuven is secured by inferior land, while Levi's obligation to Shimon would ordinarily be paid from medium land, does Reuven receive only the weaker inferior quality right or the stronger right Shimon had against Levi? Since Rabbi Natan makes Reuven the true creditor of Levi, there is reason that the entire right Shimon possessed passes to him. Still, the extent to which the first creditor can improve his collection position through the intermediate debt requires attention to the exact strength of Shimon's own right. The essential principle remains that Rabbi Natan's lien exists by Torah law regardless of whether Shimon owns other assets. The question whether beit din initially directs Reuven elsewhere is procedural. It does not restore to Shimon a power of waiver over a debt that has already become substantively subject to Reuven.
Beit din does not attend to it. Rambam rules that the grant made to a Hebrew servant belongs to the servant himself and an ordinary creditor does not collect it. The Gemara derives "to him and not to his creditor," and says that the opposing view does not accept Rabbi Natan's rule in that setting. Rambam does accept Rabbi Natan generally, so why is the grant protected? The answer is that the Torah's special grant to a Hebrew servant has the character of charity and support. It is given for the servant's own rehabilitation, not as ordinary property to be diverted immediately to his creditor. The Torah therefore excludes the creditor from direct collection. Even if the servant has already received the grant, the creditor still may not collect it automatically, because the master's gift is presumed to be given for the servant's own support and not to satisfy old creditors. This is analogous to the rule that a donor may sometimes give property on condition that a creditor have no right in it. The Rosh explains that such a condition works because an ordinary creditor has only a lien, not present ownership of the property. According to the understanding of Rabbi Natan developed above, however, where the special lien truly applies the first creditor is treated as owning the intermediate debt itself. A donor cannot ordinarily stipulate away another person's already existing ownership. Therefore the protection of the servant's grant must be understood as a special Torah exclusion from Rabbi Natan's mechanism, comparable to charity. The grant is directed to the servant personally for his support and is not diverted to the creditor.
