When does this apply, etc. The Shach discusses this at length and holds that the restriction applies specifically where direct collection would cause a loss to the second debtor, but if he suffers no loss it does not apply. None of his proofs has substance. He argues that Rabbi Natan's rule is derived from the verse "and he shall give it to the one to whom he is liable," and asks how a distinction of this kind could be read into the verse. This has no force. All the poskim explain that the restriction here is the rule that one does not collect from encumbered property when unencumbered property is available, and that rule is not from the Torah but a rabbinic enactment. What then is difficult from the Torah verse? In truth, the first creditor's lien on the second debtor's property remains in existence even while the intermediate creditor owns other assets. Those other assets may later be lost or ruined, in which case the first creditor can collect from the second debtor just as one eventually collects other encumbered property. So long as the other assets remain available, however, he does not actually collect through Rabbi Natan, though the underlying lien still exists. This also answers the Shach's question from the rule that one may write a prozbul where the creditor himself owns no land but the debtor's debtor owns land, because that is treated as property subject through Rabbi Natan. The authorities do not distinguish whether the intermediate creditor has other property. The reason is that the lien rests on all of the second debtor's property even though actual collection cannot occur while another source of payment is available. A prozbul can therefore be written. This is straightforward. Likewise there is no difficulty from the case of one who was a creditor of his brother, where the money must be used to purchase land, etc., because the property is subject through Rabbi Natan even though the debtor has other assets. What follows from that? Even if a wife's ketubah is only one hundred and her husband owns one thousand dinar, he still may not sell property worth even the amount of her ketubah in a way that diminishes her security, because perhaps the remaining assets will later be lost while this asset remains. So too, he cannot simply retain this particular debt for himself. Perhaps all the other assets will later be lost and this debt will remain available for payment of her ketubah upon divorce. Her lien rests on all his property, including this claim. The debtor cannot say, "You are not my litigant," because the debt is subject to her through Rabbi Natan. Although she cannot presently collect while other assets remain, her lien extends to all, and her security may not be diminished. As stated, even one who owns a thousand dinar may not sell away one dinar in a manner that improperly diminishes her lien. He therefore cannot keep this debt for himself. This is straightforward and there is no difficulty. Certainly there is no difficulty from the case of orphans who collected land in payment of their father's debt, from whom the father's creditor may collect because of Rabbi Natan. At the time of collection the deceased debtor has no other available assets. He died and left only movables that were not subject under the original law. Suppose instead that a debtor died leaving one thousand dinar of movables not subject to creditors and one field in the hands of a purchaser. Would the creditor not seize the field from the purchaser? Could the purchaser say, "Your debtor left you available property in the form of movables"? Certainly not, because those movables cannot be collected. The same is all the more true for Rabbi Natan. The Rashba writes this explicitly in his novellae to Bava Kamma. I therefore do not understand the relevance of the Shach's lengthy discussion on these points. However, regarding the basic proof brought by all the authorities who adopt this view, from the Bava Kamma case of one who borrowed an ox believed to be innocuous and it was found to be forewarned, there is room for analysis. The Gemara asks that the owner should be able to say, "You caused me to lose my ox, for I could have driven it to the marsh." The Gemara answers that the borrower would have had to pay from superior property. It then asks: this works where the owner has other assets, but what can be said where he has none? It answers that the borrower is subject to the injured party through Rabbi Natan. From this the authorities infer that when the owner does have other assets, the Gemara does not use Rabbi Natan, proving that Rabbi Natan does not permit direct collection in such a case. The Yam Shel Shlomo and Shach object that the Gemara had no need to answer that way where other assets exist, because the true answer is simply that the borrower must pay from superior property. Therefore no inference can be drawn from the Gemara's failure to use Rabbi Natan in that case, since the first answer was valid and not withdrawn. This is a strong objection. One can add that the Gemara initially seeks to establish the baraita according to everyone, even the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Natan. So long as the case can be explained according to all opinions, why establish it only according to one? This is especially true in this sugya, where the Gemara repeatedly seeks interpretations that fit as many views as possible, as the Rashba notes. Thus, according to the objectors, there is no proof from the fact that the Gemara uses "he pays from superior property" in one case and Rabbi Natan in the other. It was simply preferable to give an answer that works whether or not the owner had other assets. Even so, according to what I wrote earlier, the authorities' inference still has force. In my novellae I offered a proof for their ruling from another part of the sugya. The Gemara says that the borrower should be able to argue, "Had the ox remained innocuous I would have admitted liability and been exempt from the penalty." According to the view that half-damages are compensatory rather than penal, he could instead say, "I would have driven it to the marsh." The Gemara answers that the case is where judgment had already been rendered and beit din had seized the ox. We must understand why the Gemara answers with the uncommon circumstance that beit din itself seized the ox. Why should beit din seize another person's working animal? Nor does the Gemara say explicitly that testimony had already been accepted. Without accepted testimony, the first objection remains, because he could still have admitted and been exempt. A critical element appears missing. We must also say that testimony was accepted only after the seizure, because if testimony had already been taken then the owner of the ox necessarily appeared before beit din, and he could certainly have taken the ox from the guardian and driven it away. Why then does the Gemara later still ask that the owner could say, "I would have driven it to the marsh"? The seizure must therefore have preceded testimony, or perhaps testimony had not yet been accepted at all. Yet the Gemara does not worry that an admission would exempt him from a penalty. We must say that with half-damages classified as a penalty, we rule according to the Maggid Mishneh, Tur, and other authorities that if one admits to the penalty and witnesses later testify, he is liable where his admission did not create any independent monetary obligation. All this is strained. Why does the Gemara not answer more simply that the injured party himself seized the ox first? That is common and natural, because he seized what he claimed as his own. Then all difficulties disappear. The argument that the owner would have driven the ox to the marsh no longer applies once the injured party already holds it. Likewise the admission issue disappears, because with half-damages, once the injured party has seized the animal, a subsequent admission does not release the defendant from the penalty, as the Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 9 of Hilkhot Nizkei Mamon. Everything would be satisfactory. Why, then, did the Gemara specifically say that beit din seized it? We must say that had the Gemara established the case as seizure by the injured party, the owner could answer: "Had you not allowed the injured party to seize it, I would have fled to the marsh or elsewhere, and testimony could not have been accepted because testimony is not accepted outside the presence of the litigant. As a result, the injured party would have been forced to compromise," as Tosafot write s.v. hava ma'arikna. But now that the injured party seized the ox, flight would no longer help, because where one party holds the disputed property, testimony may be accepted even outside the other litigant's presence in order to preserve the possessor's position, as stated above in siman 28 seif 18 and below in siman 110. The owner could therefore say, "I did not flee because once the injured party possessed the ox, flight would have accomplished nothing, and that caused me loss." The Gemara therefore establishes the case where beit din, not the injured party, seized the ox. Then the owner still could have fled when testimony was to be taken, and if he did not do so he has no complaint against the guardian for that decision. Yet a difficulty remains. Why not establish the ordinary case where the injured party seized the ox and say that the owner has no claim against the guardian because the guardian can answer, "I am subject to the injured party just as I am subject to you through Rabbi Natan," just as the Gemara says in the case where beit din seized it? We must therefore say that this answer applies only where the owner has no other assets. Where he does have other assets, Rabbi Natan does not permit such direct collection. The Gemara would then face the reverse difficulty: this works where he has no other assets, but what can be said where he has assets? In that case the answer "he must pay from superior property" also does not resolve the owner's proposed flight, because the owner says that he could have fled so testimony would never be accepted and he would never have become obligated to pay. Therefore the Gemara establishes the case where beit din seized the ox. The only remaining claim is that the owner could have hidden the ox itself. The matter then works whichever way one turns: if the owner has other property, he must pay from superior property; if he has none, the guardian is subject to the injured party through Rabbi Natan. This fully explains the sugya and makes the authorities' ruling necessary. Consider it carefully, for the analysis is sharp. Still, it is somewhat distant to say that this was their intended proof. In my view, however, it strongly supports their ruling, and certainly one should not move away from their conclusion merely because of a difficulty. The Shach's claim about what they supposedly meant, namely that they wrote only that there is no proof Rabbi Natan applies where the intermediate creditor has other assets because the Gemara never explicitly says so, is not correct. One who examines their words, especially the Rashba's novellae to Bava Kamma, will see this.
However, regarding the substantive rule, the Shach says that all this applies only where direct collection causes Levi a loss, but if Levi suffers no loss and wishes to pay, he may pay. At first glance no lengthy proof should be needed. The entire reason given for withholding direct collection is that the second debtor's property is comparable to encumbered property where free property is available. In that very rule, if the purchaser says, "I do not want the protection enacted by the Sages," and prefers that the creditor seize from him, we listen to him, as is explicit in Bava Kamma 8b. All the more so here. Yet this creates a serious problem. The authorities' entire foundation is that since the Gemara does not say in the case where the owner has other assets that the guardian is subject through Rabbi Natan, we see that Rabbi Natan does not apply in such circumstances. But if the debtor may always waive the rabbinic protection and say, "I do not want this enactment," because he is no worse than an ordinary purchaser of encumbered property, then even where the ox's owner has other assets the guardian should be able to say, "I am subject to the injured party just as I am subject to you through Rabbi Natan. I waive the rabbinic protection." Rabbi Natan's lien would then attach despite the owner's other assets. How could the owner recover money from the guardian for handing over the ox? This difficulty remains even according to the Shach's interpretation of the authorities, that the Gemara's failure to use Rabbi Natan where other assets exist merely leaves us without proof. Even had the Gemara used Rabbi Natan there, it still would not prove the general rule, because that case could be different precisely because the guardian says, "I waive the enactment." It occurred to me to answer from a different direction. Tosafot and the Rashba explain the owner's claim, "I would have driven the ox to the marsh," to mean that through various tactics he could have forced the injured party to compromise. But is it certain that the injured party would have compromised? Perhaps he would have been resolute and refused every compromise regardless of cost. Can beit din be entirely certain of such an outcome? It is only an assessment. Therefore, in the Gemara's first question, where the ox's owner seeks to recover money from the guardian, such an argument is sufficient because money cannot be extracted from the guardian in a case of doubt. But in the second question, where the owner invokes the same claim in order himself to extract money, how can beit din award money from the current possessor based on an assessment and doubtful prediction of a compromise? The rule is that the burden of proof rests on the one seeking to extract, even where the opposing possibility is a minority. The Yam Shel Shlomo on page 52 similarly struggles with this distinction between one who holds the money and one who seeks to extract it, because the latter needs a stronger claim to take money. We must therefore say that the Gemara's question, like its broad question "this works where he has assets," seeks to establish the baraita in every possible circumstance. So too here. Is there not a case where the owner of the ox already seized property belonging to the borrower or guardian? How could the baraita state categorically that the owner pays half-damages? If he is holding the guardian's property, he can say, "I would have driven the ox to the marsh." With such uncertainty he can retain what he holds, because property is not taken away on a doubt. According to this, the guardian cannot answer, "I am subject through Rabbi Natan and I waive the rabbinic protection," because the Rivam writes in Mordekhai, chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot, that one cannot use the argument "I do not want the Sages' enactment" in order to extract money. See what I wrote above in siman 102. Here it would be an attempt to extract, because the ox's owner already possesses the guardian's property. This would also appear to disprove the Shach's rule that wherever the second debtor suffers no loss Rabbi Natan always applies. If that were so, the Gemara could still have answered that the guardian is subject through Rabbi Natan even where the ox's owner has other assets, since the guardian suffers no independent loss whether beit din seized the ox for the injured party or he returned it to its owner. Either way at least half-damages would ultimately have to be paid. One can respond that had the ox been returned to the owner, the owner could have driven it to the marsh and thereby escaped the half-damages entirely, or at least reduced the payment by forcing a compromise. Thus some portion of the potential compromise benefit would have accrued to the owner, just as the Gemara argues that had the animal remained innocuous he could have driven it away. In fact the owners did remove it and it did not come before beit din. The guardian therefore was not fully obligated to hand it to the injured party, and the Gemara needed the answer that compensation comes from superior property. Even so, the more persuasive conclusion emerges from the unqualified language of all the poskim, especially the Rashba in responsum 1122, who distinguishes between a deposit and a loan by saying that a loan is treated as encumbered property. He does not say that where the second debtor suffers no loss a loan is treated like a deposit and may be collected directly. Why distinguish deposits specifically if an ordinary loan without loss would be the same? It therefore appears that the enactment that one does not collect from encumbered property when free property exists, enacted for the stability of commerce, was not enacted solely for the purchaser's personal benefit. It also benefits the seller by encouraging people to purchase from him. Therefore, if the purchaser alone says, "I do not want the Sages' protection," he cannot do so where that choice affects the seller's interests, because the enactment was made for both. The Bava Kamma case where a purchaser can waive the enactment is one where the seller has died and neither the seller nor the heirs have any remaining practical interest. Even where the seller himself has no direct monetary loss and the only effect is between the purchaser and the creditor seeking to seize, the purchaser may waive the protection. But where the waiver weakens the seller's position, he cannot. Rabbi Natan's limitation here is part of the same enactment concerning collection from encumbered property. Therefore, even if the final borrower says, "I waive the protection," he cannot do so when that would diminish the rights of his own creditor. Accordingly, even if the final borrower personally suffers no loss, if his creditor has an interest in preventing the direct collection he may object, because the enactment was made for both parties. This resolves the earlier difficulties. In the ox case, the guardian alone cannot waive the protection, because the owner of the ox can object. The guardian may suffer no loss, but the owner does. This appears correct for halachah, especially since all the authorities, and particularly the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, state the rule without the Shach's qualification.
Beit din does not intervene. The Beit Yosef writes that once the due date arrives, beit din will intervene even if the intermediate creditor has other assets. The Shach explains that this means where the creditor does not extend the payment term. If he grants an extension, beit din does not collect directly from the second debtor while the intermediate creditor has other assets. But where the intermediate creditor has no other assets, an extension granted when the debt becomes due does not help, and beit din may collect from the second debtor. His reasoning is sound. The entire reason one does not collect directly is the rule that one does not collect from encumbered property when unencumbered property is available. Once the debt has matured, the special status of the intermediate debt as merely encumbered property falls away. Consider one who mortgages a field to another in a Sura-style mortgage for ten years. When the ten years expire, is the field still called merely encumbered property in the sense that collection must be deferred? This case is comparable. Nevertheless, there is a difficulty. All of their proof comes from the case in Bava Kamma of one who borrowed an ox believed to be innocuous and it was found to be forewarned. The Gemara does not answer that the borrower is directly obligated through Rabbi Natan except where the owner of the ox has no other assets. Yet there the borrowing period must already have ended. Otherwise, how could beit din have seized the borrower's ox? The ox was forewarned, and it was not more specifically subject than the owner's other assets. How could beit din seize it from the borrower and deprive him of its use? Moreover, how could the owner argue, "Had you returned it to me I would have driven it to the marsh"? During the agreed borrowing period the borrower has the right to use it. Why should he have been obligated to return it early merely because the owner might suffer a loss? The Gemara must therefore be speaking, at least as one of the general cases included in the baraita, where the borrowing period had ended, just as it asks broadly, "This works where he has other assets, but what can be said where he has none?" Even so, Rabbi Natan applies only when the owner has no other assets. This appears to contradict the Beit Yosef and Shach. It is difficult to distinguish by saying that the Beit Yosef spoke only of a loan with a fixed due date, where after the term the obligation resembles collection from unencumbered property, whereas with an ordinary loan after thirty days, although the lender may demand payment, so long as he has not actually demanded it the debt remains in the category of encumbered property whenever other free assets exist. In Bava Kamma the case would then be an ordinary borrowing arrangement under which the borrower may continue to use the item until the lender demands its return. If that distinction were correct, there would likewise be no difficulty from Rabbi Natan in the ketubah case, since a ketubah has no fixed due date either. But the distinction is strained. Ultimately, the debtor did not give the money or item for use beyond its legal term, namely after thirty days in an ordinary loan. It is also strained to say that the Bava Kamma case always occurs during the borrowing term, and that the owner's claim "you should have returned it to me" means that he would have provided the borrower with another comparable ox for his use. In a case of loss to the owner, perhaps the borrower cannot insist on retaining this particular ox. Similarly, when beit din seized the ox at the injured party's request, perhaps that was only where the injured party supplied another comparable ox for the borrower's use. The matter requires study.
