Shaar Mishpat writes that the same rule plainly applies where the defendant holds a deposit belonging to a violent Jew who is established as an extortionist and fears that he will be sued a second time. He need not turn the deposit over to the depositor's creditor if doing so exposes him to a second collection. Mahritatz, responsum 49, however, ruled in a case where Levi lent to Reuven, Reuven lent to Shimon, and Levi sought to collect directly from Shimon under Rabbi Natan's lien. Shimon argued that Reuven still held the note and was a violent person whom he feared would sue him again. Mahritatz ruled that Shimon must nevertheless pay Levi. Beit din can give Shimon a receipt and order Reuven not to commit injustice. The fact that Reuven may act violently cannot be allowed to cause Levi a loss; otherwise one could raise the same concern whenever property is seized from purchasers. At first glance this conflicts with the present ruling from Responsa Maimoniyot, which takes account of the possibility that an apostate Jew may sue a second time and therefore permits the custodian to withhold payment from the creditor. Shaar Mishpat suggests a distinction between a deposit and a loan. With a deposit, the custodian may withhold because the actual object remains the depositor's. With a loan, once the debt becomes subject to the first creditor under Rabbi Natan's lien, the second creditor's lien falls away. Even if the second creditor later forces the debtor to pay again, that is a separate act of coercion and does not diminish the first creditor's legal right. On that basis Mahritatz's ruling would be correct in an ordinary loan, even where the intermediate creditor is a non-Jew or an apostate. Nevertheless, where the intermediate creditor still possesses the promissory note and refuses to return it to the debtor, the matter requires substantial analysis. Shaar Mishpat discusses the issue at length and concludes that Mahritatz's ruling requires further study in practice.
