One who has a deposit, etc. At the beginning of this siman the Beit Yosef cites a responsum of the Rashba expressing doubt whether Rabbi Natan's rule applies when the ultimate debtor is a non Jew. This is indeed found in responsum 961. Yet the Rosh, in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, writes that a creditor and a wife may collect from a loan held by others, whether the ultimate debtor is a Jew or a non Jew, because Rabbi Natan's rule applies. This is also the accepted practice. The formulation is that of his teacher Maharam, and the Mordechai in Yesh Nochalin records the same rule. The responsum attributed to the Ramban, siman 23, likewise indicates that Rabbi Natan applies even where the ultimate debtor is a non Jew. At first I did not understand the Rashba's doubt. Why should a non Jewish debtor be different from a Jewish one? Even if the verse "to the one to whom he is guilty" is stated in relation to a Jew, if a second Jew becomes directly obligated to the first creditor, surely a non Jewish debtor should not have a stronger position. I then understood the possible basis from a hint in the Mordechai. After citing Maharam's rule that one may collect even from a non Jew under Rabbi Natan, the Mordechai refers to the discussion in Perek Mi SheMet concerning a dying person who says, "My loan should be given to so and so." According to the Raam, if a dying person says, "My loan held by a non Jew should be given to so and so," the gift does not take effect, because the mouths of non Jews may speak falsely and the special deathbed acquisition was not established for such an uncertain asset. If so, one might reason by analogy to movable property. The Rashba writes at the end of Kiddushin that in truth a lien can conceptually attach to everything, but the Torah recognized that a lender ordinarily does not rely on movable property because the debtor can conceal or remove it. Similarly, where the debt is owed by a non Jew, one could say that because collection is less reliable, a Jewish lender does not rely on that asset, and therefore no Rabbi Natan lien arises. On this reasoning such a debt is even weaker than ordinary movables. The same lack of reliance that defeats a deathbed gift might defeat Rabbi Natan. This would explain why the Mordechai juxtaposed the two subjects. If that is truly the Mordechai's reasoning, then the Rama's ruling below in siman 253 seif 20 becomes significant. There the Rama rules like the Raam that a loan held by a non Jew is not transferred by a deathbed declaration and writes that this is the view he follows in practice. One might then infer that the Rashba's doubt here remains in force as well, and that Rabbi Natan should not apply to a non Jewish ultimate debtor. Yet the two rules are not necessarily dependent. The deathbed gift may depend on an assessment of the dying person's intent and on the special desire not to disturb his mind, so that a debt against a non Jew, on which he did not rely, was not included. Rabbi Natan, by contrast, is a law of direct indebtedness. Why should the property or obligation of a non Jew not become subject to a Jewish creditor? Even according to the view that liens are not biblical property rights but rest on the debtor's personal duty to act honestly, that concept cannot simply be said to have no counterpart for a non Jew. Non Jews are commanded to maintain civil law, and repayment of an ordinary loan is certainly not a matter of ownerless property. Moreover, the very rule concerning a deathbed gift appears to depend on the dispute whether maamad sheloshtan applies when one party is a non Jew. The Gemara explains that a dying person's gift of a loan works because the same transfer can exist for a healthy person through maamad sheloshtan. Therefore, according to Rabbenu Tam and the Mordechai, who hold that maamad sheloshtan does not operate with a non Jew, the deathbed transfer likewise fails. According to those who hold that maamad sheloshtan does work with a non Jew, the deathbed transfer should work as well. Rabbi Gershon in responsa Avodat HaGershuni already connected these two laws, but he objected that Maharam explains the failure of the deathbed gift by saying that non Jews may speak falsely rather than by saying that maamad sheloshtan does not apply. He apparently did not have before him Maharam of Rothenburg's responsum printed in Teshuvot Maimoniyot, Sefer Mishpatim, siman 68, where Maharam gives exactly the same reason for the rule that maamad sheloshtan does not operate with a non Jew, namely that their speech is unreliable. The matter is therefore clear. The two laws share one reason, and whoever holds that maamad sheloshtan applies with a non Jew should likewise recognize the deathbed transfer. Since the law of maamad sheloshtan with a non Jew is itself disputed below, we should not use that dispute to unsettle Maharam and the Rosh's clear ruling here. The halachah should remain that Rabbi Natan's rule applies when a Jew's ultimate debtor is a non Jew. A different case is where Reuven lent to a non Jew, and that non Jew then lent to a Jew. Can Reuven collect directly from the Jewish debtor? Maharam of Rothenburg, Prague edition responsum 253, discusses one who went as Reuven's agent to a non Jew and falsely claimed to be Reuven's son, whereupon the non Jew paid him. Reuven then sought his money, while the non Jew had no resources to pay again. Maharam ruled that because such theft, which involves desecration of Hashem's name, is prohibited according to all opinions, the recipient is liable to the non Jew and is therefore directly liable to Reuven under Rabbi Natan. Maharam adds that one should not distinguish between a non Jew and a Jew because the person to whom the debt is ultimately owed is a Jew. This language implies a distinction. Where the Jewish recipient owes the non Jew because the underlying taking is prohibited, such as theft, Rabbi Natan can pass that enforceable debt through to the Jewish first creditor. But with an ordinary loan from a non Jew to a Jew, we rule that nonpayment of the non Jew's loan may in certain circumstances be permitted as an expropriation of his claim. If the non Jew sued in a Jewish beit din, the Jewish borrower would not necessarily be compelled to pay. Then the moment the non Jew's money came into the Jew's hands, the non Jew's enforceable lien may already have disappeared by operation of law. What remains for Rabbi Natan to transfer to the first Jewish creditor? This explains why Maharam stressed that the underlying taking was prohibited. One might object from the rules concerning a non Jew's deposit or collateral held by a Jew. In our seif, absent concern for hostile accusations, the property is taken from the Jewish holder, and at the beginning of this siman a non Jew's collateral held by a Jew is likewise collectible. This seems to show that Rabbi Natan can operate through a non Jewish middle party. But a deposit or similar property was never given for expenditure. Wherever it physically exists it remains the owner's property. Even for a non Jew, withholding such property resembles theft rather than mere nonpayment of a loan. I discussed this at the end of siman 72, and therefore there is no contradiction. An additional distinction is possible. If the non Jew first lent money to Shimon and only afterward borrowed from Reuven, perhaps Shimon never becomes directly liable to Reuven, because when Shimon initially received the money the non Jew's claim against him was already of the type that Jewish law would not enforce and that lien was extinguished immediately. But if Reuven first lent to the non Jew and the non Jew only afterward lent the money to Shimon, Reuven can argue that his lien already attached to the non Jew's assets and that the later transfer to Shimon cannot strip away his right. At the moment Shimon received the funds, he may be regarded by the Torah's Rabbi Natan rule as having received them on behalf of Reuven, so that he owes Reuven directly rather than merely the non Jew. This distinction is plausible but still requires study. Maharam's case, after all, involved a non Jew who already owed Reuven, and Maharam nevertheless relied specifically on the fact that the recipient's taking was prohibited as theft. It is, however, clear that if a Jewish first creditor lent to a non Jew and the non Jew expressly encumbered movables together with land to him, the first creditor can collect. The non Jew cannot simply give those encumbered funds away so as to defeat the prior Jewish creditor. See the Ritva to Ketubot 82, who writes that where the debtor expressly encumbered money to the first creditor together with land, the Sages do not dispute Rabbi Natan at all. The creditor collects because the money itself is already encumbered to him. The same reasoning applies here. Finally, if the non Jew lent to Reuven and Reuven lent to Shimon, and the non Jew himself sues Shimon in a Jewish beit din under a theory comparable to Rabbi Natan, Shimon is plainly not directly liable to the non Jew. Maharam's formulation is exact: there is no distinction between Jew and non Jew when the person to whom the ultimate debt is owed is a Jew. If that ultimate creditor is a non Jew, Rabbi Natan's verse does not create such a direct right. This is simple.
