If this condition is not explicit in the document, Reuven collects from him. Even if Shimon granted Levi an extension, Shimon has no power to do so after Levi has already become obligated to Reuven by virtue of Rabbi Natan's rule. Nor is Shimon believed to say that he stipulated this with Levi before the loan. If, however, the condition is expressly written in the document, it is effective, because initially Shimon could have given his movable property to whomever he wished. This was already explained above in seif katan 10 and 12. See there.
Rules that emerge from this siman:
1. If Reuven is owed money by Shimon, and Shimon is owed money by Levi, beit din extracts from Levi and gives it to Reuven, because Levi is bound to Reuven by Torah law through Rabbi Natan's exposition of the verse, "and he shall give it to the one to whom he is guilty." It makes no difference whether Levi's obligation to Shimon arose before Shimon's obligation to Reuven or afterward. Nor is there a distinction between a documented loan and an oral loan, provided both debtors admit their respective obligations to their creditors. The rule applies to any obligation, whether arising from a loan, a deposit, damages, wages, theft, informing against another, or a sale, and even to a rabbinic debt. It also makes no difference whether both obligations are loans or one is a deposit or another type of debt. Although such obligations differ in other respects, Rabbi Natan's rule applies to all of them, except for a master's grant to a departing Hebrew servant and other charitable obligations, from which one does not collect by Rabbi Natan's rule. Rabbi Natan's rule likewise applies to collateral belonging to a non Jew. The holder is first given the principal and the interest already accrued, or that will accrue until the fixed term agreed between them, and the creditor collects his debt from the balance, provided this does not expose him to danger from the non Jew.
2. Rabbi Natan's rule applies even if Shimon owns other property, for Levi is bound to Reuven by Torah law. Therefore, if Levi wishes to pay and suffers no loss by doing so, even if Shimon owns only inferior land while Levi owns intermediate land, beit din extracts from Levi for Reuven. Likewise, if Shimon has no cash but does have land or movable property, cash is nevertheless extracted from Levi and given to Reuven, and beit din is obligated to hear Reuven's claim and apply Rabbi Natan's rule. If, however, payment would cause Levi a loss, such as where Levi says that Shimon's note is paid, is a trust document, or is invalid for some other reason, or that Shimon forgave the debt, or that Levi still needs the money for a period and Shimon is willing to leave it with him, then Levi is not compelled to pay Reuven unless Shimon has no property at all. In that case this debt is treated like encumbered property, from which the Sages permitted collection only when no unencumbered assets are available. Reuven must then take a stringent oath that his own note has not been paid, even though Levi does not know whether it has been paid, just as any creditor collecting from encumbered property may collect only with an oath. This applies only after beit din has arranged Shimon's assets, administered the oath, and found no asset other than this debt, even if Shimon owns property in another country. If Shimon owns property in the present country, one does not extract from Levi by Rabbi Natan's rule. If Shimon is absent, one waits the appropriate period prescribed below in siman 106.
3. When Shimon has no property, he must exert himself to collect from Levi so as to pay Reuven, unless he wishes instead to assign the promissory note itself to Reuven in payment at the valuation fixed by beit din. See below, siman 101 seif 5.
4. If what Levi holds is deposited money belonging to Shimon, it is taken from Levi immediately even if Shimon owns movable property or land. Likewise, if Shimon deposited movable property with Levi, it is taken from Levi even if Shimon owns land. But if Shimon lent Levi an object or rented it to him, one does not take it from Levi before the term of the loan or rental ends, even if Shimon has no other property. Similarly, if Levi owes Shimon money under a promissory note and the original loan was made for a fixed term, Levi need not pay before the due date. If Shimon is absent, then in the case of a borrowed or rented object one takes it as soon as the term expires, even if Shimon has property. In the case of a monetary loan, however, one does not take payment from Levi, because Levi may say: since Shimon is not demanding payment, I will keep the money until he demands it. Since Shimon has property, one therefore does not extract from Levi. If Shimon encumbered movable property to Reuven together with land, then when Shimon has no other property one may take from Levi, even before the term, utensils that Shimon lent or rented to Levi. Money lent to Levi, however, is not taken before the due date, because money cannot be subjected to a lien as movable property together with land in this manner, since it has no identifying mark. If one went overseas after lending utensils to another for a fixed term, those utensils are not taken from the borrower under Rabbi Natan's rule to provide maintenance for the lender's wife, even if the husband had encumbered movable property together with land for her maintenance, because a wife's maintenance is not collected from encumbered assets. All this is according to strict law. Nowadays, however, the custom is that an encumbrance of movable property together with land is ineffective to extract property from third parties, and it is treated as though no such encumbrance had been made, as explained above at the beginning of siman 60.
5. Shimon cannot forgive Levi's debt or grant him an extension after the loan once Levi has already become bound to Reuven through Rabbi Natan's rule. Likewise, Shimon is not believed to say that the document was a trust document, that it was paid, or to make any other assertion that would invalidate the note. This is so whether or not the note contains a credibility clause. Even if Levi demands that Shimon swear to him and Shimon refuses to swear and admits Levi's assertion, Shimon is not believed, as explained above in siman 47. Even if, at the time Shimon forgave the debt or declared the note a trust document or paid, it was not yet known that he owed Reuven, or he then had sufficient property, if it is later discovered that at that time he did owe Reuven and now has no property from which Reuven can collect, Shimon's forgiveness or admission is retroactively ineffective. If Shimon currently has property sufficient to pay Reuven, even if it is only inferior land, Levi is not compelled to pay Reuven, as explained in rule 2. There is no distinction between a ketubah and any other debt. Rabbi Natan's encumbrance applies in every case, and Shimon cannot forgive the underlying right even before its ordinary collection date arrives. Therefore, if a married woman injured another person or borrowed money, she cannot forgive her ketubah, because her husband is bound through Rabbi Natan's rule to the injured party or to her creditor. But if she sold the ketubah to another when she owed no one else, or sold it to the person whom she injured or from whom she borrowed and they accepted the ketubah as payment for the damage or loan, or beit din awarded the ketubah to that person at an appraised value in payment of the damage or loan, she may thereafter forgive the ketubah. Similarly, if Shimon owes Reuven and sells Reuven a promissory note against Levi, and Reuven accepts that note as payment of his debt, or beit din awards it to Reuven at an appraised value in payment, Shimon can later forgive Levi's note. So long as Reuven has not actually acquired or collected that note as payment, however, Shimon cannot forgive it, even if its due date has not arrived, because Shimon still owes Reuven. If, after Levi became bound to Reuven through Rabbi Natan's rule, Levi paid Shimon while knowing that Shimon owed Reuven and had no property, Levi must pay Reuven again. But if Levi says, "I did not know, and I paid Shimon," he is exempt and is not made to swear, because Reuven does not know whether Levi had knowledge at the time of payment. A general cherem is merely pronounced. If beit din restrained a person's money in the hands of a depositary, and the depositary violated their order and returned it to the recalcitrant debtor, then if the creditor cannot otherwise collect, the depositary must pay the creditor.
6. Although Reuven warned Levi not to pay Shimon, Reuven is not thereby regarded as being in possession of Levi's debt until it is established that Shimon in fact owes Reuven.
7. If Shimon dies and leaves heirs, and Levi claims that Shimon's debt was paid and demands that the heirs swear, but they refuse and admit Levi's claim, their admission has no effect against Reuven. Rather, Reuven swears that Shimon did not repay him, as required of anyone collecting from encumbered property, and collects from Levi. He must include in his oath that he does not know Shimon's note against Levi to have been paid. If Shimon's note was executed before Reuven's note, Reuven must also include that Shimon never told him that Levi had repaid him.
