Rambam rules that a lender may not take collateral except through beit din, and even a court officer may not enter the debtor's house but must wait outside while the debtor brings the pledge. If the creditor nevertheless entered or seized the pledge by force, he does not receive lashes because the prohibition is linked to the positive command, "You shall surely return the pledge." If the pledge is lost or burned and the positive command can no longer be fulfilled, he receives lashes and the pledge's value is credited against the debt. This is difficult regarding a wealthy debtor, because the positive command to return necessary clothing applies to the poor, while the prohibition against entering to seize collateral includes both poor and wealthy. Why then did Rambam state generally that there are no lashes? Taz answers that because the prohibition is one general prohibition covering both rich and poor, and with the poor it is linked to a positive command, lashes do not apply even with the rich. A similar principle appears in Tosafot concerning the prohibition of moving a boundary marker, which also includes another category where restitution is possible. Yet here the matter is more difficult, because Rambam himself says that even with a poor debtor lashes apply if the pledge is destroyed and the positive command is not fulfilled. This proves the prohibition itself is fundamentally capable of lashes. Why should a wealthy case be more lenient than a poor case where restitution became impossible? The same question can be raised regarding unlawfully taking land and later losing the possibility of restitution. The issue therefore requires study.
