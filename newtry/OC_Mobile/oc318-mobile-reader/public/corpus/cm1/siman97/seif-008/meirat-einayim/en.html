If he took it as collateral, he must return it against his will. This is also the Rambam’s language concerning food preparation implements and a widow’s garment. I have already shown that although the seizure was forbidden, it is legally effective in the sense that the creditor need return the item only at the time it is needed, and need not leave it with the borrower afterward. It therefore requires explanation why the Rambam specially writes “against his will” in these two cases, but does not use that phrase in the later case where the lender unlawfully entered the borrower’s house. We cannot explain that here beit din compels the return at the time of need, whereas elsewhere it does not, because the cases governed by the verse commanding return share the same enforcement rules. Rather, the Rambam’s purpose is to teach that even in these two cases, where Scripture does not explicitly say that an unlawfully seized item must be returned, the creditor cannot keep it altogether. One might have thought, following Abaye’s general principle that when the Torah says not to do something, an act done in violation nevertheless takes effect, that a court agent who seized such an item would not have to return it at all, because the explicit return command could be read as applying only to the lender who himself entered and seized. The Rambam therefore teaches that this is not so. We follow Rava, that where the Torah prohibits the act, the act does not take effect in the prohibited respect, and the creditor remains subject to the command to return the item when required. If the object becomes unavailable for return, he may incur lashes, as explained below. The Rambam did not address the time of return here because it was obvious to him that it need be returned only at the borrower’s time of need. He relied on his later discussion of the repeated return command. The same explains why he did not repeat here that there are no lashes so long as the object can still be returned, because the prohibition is linked to a positive command. The Maggid Mishneh and the Raavad likewise indicate that the rules are the same. Even the Baal HaTerumot’s statement that a widow’s garment must be returned “immediately” should not be read literally as requiring that it remain with her continuously. His meaning is that the seizure does not give the creditor a right to collect the debt from that garment itself. Indeed, the Baal HaTerumot explains concerning food preparation implements that they need be returned only when needed, and the Rambam uses one formulation for both cases. The discussion at the end of HaMekabel concerning a slaughtering knife likewise speaks of returning the item when it is needed for its function. Thus, the correct understanding is that “return it against his will” means compulsory return at the proper time of need, not permanent surrender of possession. See the Derishah for further discussion.
