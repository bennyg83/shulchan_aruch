And if he took it as collateral, he returns it against his will. The Sema and Derishah explain at length that "return" means at the time the borrower needs the utensil. Since they hold that one does not receive lashes for taking such collateral because the prohibition is connected to the positive commandment of returning the pledge, the commandment of "you shall surely return it" must apply here as well. Thus even where the creditor unlawfully took an article used for food preparation, he need return it only at the time of need, as Scripture says, "when the sun comes." Baal HaTerumot states this explicitly. He asks, according to Rambam who classifies a plow as a food preparation implement, how the Mishnah can speak of taking a plow at all, and answers that it concerns one who violated the law and took it. This shows that even a food preparation implement taken unlawfully is returned only during the period of use, not permanently. Therefore the Sema did not need to labor so extensively once Baal HaTerumot had already stated the matter. Rivash, responsum 488, however, appears to say that it must be returned immediately while still treating the prohibition as one connected to a positive commandment, and this requires study. More importantly, that entire approach follows Rambam's view that if the pledge is later lost or burned, the creditor receives lashes. According to Raavad and many other authorities, however, when the creditor took the pledge unlawfully he is liable to pay for it, as Raavad writes and as the Shach explains above in siman 72. If monetary liability remains, the usual categories of fulfilling or nullifying the positive commandment do not fit. Yet the Gemara speaks explicitly of lashes. One is therefore almost compelled to follow Tosafos, who concludes that the prohibition against taking millstones and other food preparation implements is not a prohibition repaired by the positive commandment of returning a pledge at all. When Abaye told the creditor to return it, that was because the seizure itself had been legally ineffective under his mistaken action, not because the positive commandment applies to that prohibition. Accordingly, practical law should be stringent in this Torah prohibition and require immediate return, because we rule like Rava that an act performed in violation of Torah law is ineffective. I am surprised that the Sema did not mention the position of Tosafos and the other authorities. The Mechaber and Tur below say that such utensils are returned "at the time of work," but this does not prove Rambam's position. They may be speaking where the utensil was lawfully taken at a time when it was not being used, according to the many authorities who permit such seizure and require its return only when needed. The Yerushalmi also indicates that a plow may be taken at night and returned by day. Even this permission requires study as practical law, because Tosafos asks how the plow could have been taken at all if it is a food preparation implement, and Baal HaTerumot answers only that it was taken in violation. Had they accepted the rule that it may be taken outside its period of use, there would have been no question. Maggid Mishneh indeed resolves the case by saying that it was taken at night, but Tosafos and Baal HaTerumot apparently do not follow that. Thus there is no clear proof that the Tur accepts Rambam's broader application of the positive commandment. There is, however, a difficulty within Tosafos itself. If this prohibition is not repaired by the positive commandment, then according to Abaye, who holds that an act performed in violation is legally effective, one who intentionally seized a food preparation implement would never need to return it. Tosafos indeed says so. According to Rava he would have to return it immediately because the seizure is legally ineffective. Why, then, when the Gemara in Temurah asks for practical differences between Abaye and Rava, does it not give this obvious case of one who seized a food preparation implement or a widow's garment? One cannot say that the positive commandment applies under the baraita's view but not under our halachah, because Tosafos derives its position from the Mishnah's statement that one violates two prohibitions, which implies lashes. Perhaps the Temurah discussion deliberately seeks cases involving ordinary people who acted inadvertently or under a mistaken understanding, not deliberate sinners openly violating Torah law. In a mistaken seizure, even Abaye would tell the person to return it, just as he said, "go return it," because had he known the prohibition he would not have acted. The disagreement therefore appears only in an intentional violation, and the Gemara did not frame its examples around deliberate wickedness. If so, this may also answer another difficulty raised there about redeeming a firstborn or tithe improperly: the practical difference would arise only in an intentional violation, and the Gemara did not choose such a case. Even so, this remains somewhat strained. For practical law, however, one should certainly be stringent and require immediate return.
And oxen that plow, etc. This too requires study as practical law. Rashi, Rambam, and Raavad hold that plowing oxen fall within the prohibition of food preparation implements. Rabbeinu Tam disagrees, apparently because, as Tosafos says, otherwise why would the Gemara require return of the plow by day? But according to the view explained above, that even food preparation implements are subject to the positive commandment of return after an unlawful taking, Baal HaTerumot's answer can resolve that proof. All the more so if taking the plow at night is lawful, as the Yerushalmi states. Since major authorities classify the oxen as protected food preparation implements, it is difficult to be lenient in a Torah prohibition.
