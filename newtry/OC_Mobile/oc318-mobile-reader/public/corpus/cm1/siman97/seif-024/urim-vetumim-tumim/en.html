Even if she seized property from her husband. The Sema, seif katan 56, appears to say that even if the wife seized property to cover past maintenance expenses, such as money she borrowed in order to support herself, the property is taken from her and given to the husband's creditor. His words are not clear. Why should property she already received in satisfaction of past maintenance be removed from her? If the husband had given her a small amount each day for food and only afterward an earlier creditor appeared, no one would imagine forcing the wife to repay from her own pocket what she had already received and consumed for maintenance. Once the payment has been completed, the matter is finished. What difference is there between money the husband physically handed her and money of his already in her possession that she retained toward an existing maintenance debt? In either case she has already received payment from property that was under her control. Who can now extract it from her? The Bach writes explicitly that seizure is effective according to everyone with respect to past maintenance, and Gidulei Terumah agrees, supporting the point from the Rambam's language. This is simple and correct. The reason a wife cannot retain property for future maintenance is different. The Torah's law of debtor arrangements excludes ongoing maintenance for the debtor's wife. Scripture does not require the creditor to leave assets for her future support. That is all that is needed, and there is no need for the Sema's strained rationale.
Even if her husband went away, etc. The Sema's discussion in seif katan 57 is also confused by excessive length. The Rif's position is actually straightforward. Assume the husband owed his wife maintenance for Nissan, Iyar, and Sivan. She had no independent resources and supported herself by borrowing from someone else. Whatever property she acquired ordinarily belongs to her husband, but she incurred a debt to the outside lender for her maintenance. Through the rule of Rabbi Natan, the husband's obligation to support her can be reached by that lender. Later, in Tammuz, the husband borrowed money from other creditors. The Rif holds that the wife's maintenance claim against her husband has only the status of an oral debt. Even if she borrowed her maintenance money under a written document, the outside lender acquires no greater right against the husband than the wife herself had. His claim against the husband therefore remains equivalent to an oral debt. The Rif also holds generally that where an earlier debt is oral and a later debt is documented, the later documented creditor collects first. Where both debts are oral, there is no priority between earlier and later creditors and they divide. Accordingly, if the husband borrowed from another creditor in Tammuz under a written document, that later documented creditor has priority over the wife's earlier maintenance claim. If the later loan was also oral, both claims are oral and they divide. This is simple and internally consistent. There remains a difficulty. Why does the Rif need to say that maintenance is rabbinic? Even if it were a Torah obligation, the wife's claim would still be an earlier oral debt. And if both competing claims are oral, why should they divide? One could argue that the wife's support obligation, if only rabbinic, has weaker force than the later creditor's Torah debt. Gidulei Terumah explains that the Rif's reason that maintenance is rabbinic concerns future maintenance. With respect to future support, even a later oral creditor takes precedence, because the wife's claim has only rabbinic force. Past maintenance that has already accrued, by contrast, is treated like a completed oral debt, and therefore it divides with another oral debt. According to his explanation one might ask why the Rif needs the rationale that future maintenance is rabbinic. Future maintenance is not fixed in amount, and the rule of tikkun olam already prevents collection of an unascertained obligation from encumbered property. One could answer that the tikkun olam restriction applies against a documented creditor who relied on the public record, but not against an oral creditor. An oral creditor knowingly lent without the protection of publicity and thereby accepted the risk. The Rif therefore needed the additional reason that future maintenance itself is only rabbinic. I am not satisfied with this explanation. First, the Rif already brings proof from the law of Arachin that when arranging a debtor's assets one does not leave money for the wife's maintenance. The same principle applies to an ordinary creditor. It is not proper to feed the wife at the expense of those to whom the husband already owes money. The creditor can say that the husband should resolve his domestic obligations rather than take the creditor's property for future support. Second, I do not understand how past maintenance suddenly becomes a Torah debt if the underlying support obligation was rabbinic. The fact that the amount accrued does not transform a rabbinic obligation into a Torah lien. It therefore seems that the Rif's point is different. He needed to explain why the wife's maintenance does not have the legal strength of a documented debt. The ordinary distinction between an oral and documented loan is the publicity of the lien. Here the marriage itself is public. Everyone knows that a married woman has a maintenance claim. One might therefore have treated it as equivalent to a publicly documented obligation. The Rif answers that if all liens were of Torah law, publicity might determine priority. But if liens are not of Torah law, a documented loan obtains its property lien through the rabbinic system, whereas the Torah or rabbinic duty of maintenance by itself does not automatically create the same encumbrance upon assets. This can be stated more precisely through the explanation of Ramban and Ran. They write that even according to the authority who normally holds that liens are of Torah law, an oral loan can still generate a Torah oath of partial admission without being treated as a claim to land. Once Chazal enacted that an oral creditor cannot collect from purchasers, the creditor's property lien is effectively extinguished. The debtor can sell all his assets and the creditor cannot recover them. This is comparable to the creditor waiving the lien, so the remaining obligation is merely personal rather than a claim on land. Accordingly, an oral loan today does not carry a practical Torah property lien. The same is true of the wife's maintenance claim. Both are personal obligations upon the husband. Once the property lien is absent, it makes little difference that one obligation originates in Torah law and the other in rabbinic law. Chazal strengthened their obligation so that, at the level of personal liability, it is treated like a Torah obligation. Therefore two oral claims divide. One should not infer from this that a rabbinic ketubah could never have priority over a later documented Torah debt. Where Chazal expressly said that the husband's assets are encumbered for the ketubah, they strengthened their enactment as though it were Torah law. The point here is only that with maintenance we do not find an express enactment that the husband's assets themselves become encumbered. Chazal imposed upon the husband a duty to support his wife, but they did not give that obligation an independent property lien. If maintenance were itself a Torah obligation with an ordinary Torah lien, the property would automatically be encumbered just as a debt written in the Torah is treated like one written in a document. That is why the Rif's classification of the obligation matters. This also resolves the Derishah's question why the Gemara needed the reason of tikkun olam for the rule that a wife does not collect maintenance from encumbered property. Why not say simply that the purchaser has a Torah lien while her maintenance claim is rabbinic? The answer is that absent the tikkun olam enactment, Chazal could have strengthened maintenance to collect from encumbered property just as conditions of the ketubah generally have the force of the ketubah itself. The tikkun olam enactment is what prevents that result. Even an explicit undertaking would not necessarily overcome the rule if Chazal defined maintenance as collectable only from unencumbered property. This also clarifies the Beit Yosef's question. He asks that the husband expressly writes in the ketubah that he will support her. If so, why is there not a documented lien of Torah force? At first glance the same question could be asked about every rabbinic ketubah: once the husband writes the obligation, why do we still call the basic ketubah rabbinic rather than a Torah debt created by his document? The answer is that he does not write the obligation spontaneously. He writes because Chazal required it. The writing therefore does not generate a stronger lien than the rabbinic enactment itself. Additions to the ketubah that he voluntarily undertakes are indeed treated differently. According to what was explained, once Chazal required the maintenance clause to be written and intended certain consequences to attach to it, their enactment can be given the strength they assigned. But the writing does not necessarily transform every condition into a full property lien against all third parties. However. All the commentators note that this entire framework reflects the Rif's general rule that an earlier oral loan loses priority to a later documented loan and that two oral loans divide. The Mechaber below, following the Rambam, rules that an earlier oral loan has priority even over a later documented loan, and certainly over a later oral loan. On that ruling, the Rif's result requires a different explanation. With respect to accrued maintenance, either it is a genuine earlier oral debt, in which case it should have full priority over the later creditor, or it is not treated as an ordinary oral debt, in which case the later oral creditor should also collect first rather than divide. This requires explanation. The answer may be that according to our ruling, which recognizes a Torah lien even for an oral debt, there remains a specific doubt whether accrued maintenance creates the same type of property lien. Perhaps the Torah or Chazal imposed only a personal duty upon the husband to support his wife, without encumbering his assets. The fact that the laws of debtor arrangements do not reserve assets for the wife supports that possibility. If maintenance creates a true property lien, her earlier claim has priority. If it is only a personal obligation, the later ordinary creditor has priority. Since neither party is presently in possession and the legal classification is doubtful, they divide, as is common in unresolved monetary cases. A later documented creditor, however, takes all for an additional reason: the rule that maintenance cannot be collected from encumbered property because of tikkun olam. The Derishah attempts to distinguish between maintenance of a widow and that of a wife during her husband's lifetime, but his distinction is strained. Perhaps according to the authority who explains the tikkun olam rule as based on the lack of a written obligation, a distinction can be made. Maintenance during the husband's lifetime is in fact written into the ketubah, which was the Beit Yosef's question. But according to the authority who bases the rule on the amount not being fixed, there is no distinction between the cases. The Rif's responsum may follow the first rationale, which is why he did not invoke tikkun olam in the way one might expect. The Rambam, however, rules that the reason is the lack of a fixed amount. Maintenance is likewise unfixed, so a documented creditor should not be displaced by it. Against an oral creditor, by contrast, the tikkun olam concern does not apply in the same way, as Ran writes at the beginning of the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui. Therefore two oral claims divide, while a documented creditor collects in full. All of this applies to land, or to movable property where the debtor created a valid lien on movable property together with land. The Sema writes merely that the instrument includes after-acquired property, but I do not understand how an after-acquired-property clause alone creates priority in movable property without the standard movable-property-with-land clause. Absent such a clause there is no priority in movable property and the creditors divide. The discussion so far concerns past maintenance. Future maintenance is different. Scripture itself excludes leaving property for the wife's future support when arranging assets for obligations, and the same rule governs an ordinary creditor. Since the protected allowance is taken from movable property as well, the wife has no claim to future support even against movable property. This is why the Tur says that she has no right either in land or movable property, even if she seized it. There is no difficulty in the Tur. One should not object that in the illustration above the later creditor lent to the husband only after Nissan, Iyar, and Sivan, when the wife's accrued maintenance had already become a fixed amount. See below, siman 115, where according to most authorities an obligation that was not fixed at its inception does not become collectible from purchasers merely because its amount later became ascertainable before the subsequent transaction.
She may seize it for maintenance. The Sema asks that this wording implies that any amount beyond her maintenance can be collected by the husband's creditor, whereas below in seif 26 it is stated that she may seize property for her ketubah. He answers that in practice there is no meaningful surplus beyond maintenance, because future maintenance has no fixed upper limit. Even if she seized a loaf of gold, it could theoretically be retained for her continuing support. The Taz objects from Even HaEzer, siman 93, seif 23, where the law clearly contemplates a surplus. There is no difficulty from that source, because it concerns a widow. Once she collects her ketubah, her right to maintenance ends, so the amount of future maintenance can meaningfully be bounded by the value of her ketubah. Here, however, as long as her husband remains alive and does not divorce her, her maintenance obligation continues indefinitely, and only afterward would the ketubah become relevant. Still, the underlying question remains. Why does the later seif need to say that she may seize property for the ketubah, as Mahari Weil's responsum there clearly means? If she can retain any amount for maintenance, the separate ketubah rule seems unnecessary. The answer is that both rules have independent application. If the husband owns land clearly available for eventual collection of the ketubah, there is no need for her to seize movable property for the ketubah because that claim is already secure. She may nevertheless seize for maintenance. Conversely, if she possesses substantial nichsei melog whose fruits are sufficient to support her, she cannot justify a seizure for maintenance, but she may need to seize for the ketubah if that claim is otherwise unsecured. If she has both sufficient land available for the ketubah and sufficient fruits from nichsei melog for maintenance, she has no right to seize at all. The two rulings are therefore both necessary and consistent.
The law favors the creditor. One may ask why the wife cannot say, "If you remain silent, well and good; if not, I will return the document to its prior owner, the non-Jewish creditor," and the court should judge as though it had already been returned. Tosafot and the Rosh recognize this principle. Maharit likewise cites Radbaz in Choshen Mishpat, part 1, siman 39, concerning Reuven who sold land to a non-Jew. Under the non-Jewish law there was no right of foreclosure, so Reuven's wife could not collect her ketubah from that buyer. The non-Jew later sold the land to Shimon, and Reuven's wife then sought to collect by virtue of her ketubah. Radbaz ruled that Shimon may say, "If you remain silent, well and good; otherwise I will restore the land to the non-Jew," and she cannot foreclose from him. Maharit appears to agree with Radbaz's ruling. All the more so, then, why should the wife not use the same argument here? It seems possible to distinguish. Suppose the non-Jewish creditor's loan preceded the Jewish creditor's loan, while the wife herself preceded both, and she collected under the non-Jewish law. When the later Jewish creditor comes against her, she can properly say, "If you remain silent, well and good." Had the non-Jewish creditor come before a Jewish beit din, his earlier lien would have entitled him to collect before you. I merely saved the property from him. You therefore lose nothing through my collection, and the argument is valid. But suppose the Jewish creditor's loan was earlier than the non-Jewish loan, and the only reason the Jewish creditor could not collect under their law was that his document was not drafted in the form their legal system recognizes. Had both creditors come before a Jewish beit din, the Jewish creditor would have had priority. In that case the Jewish creditor properly recovers from the wife. She cannot say that she will return the property to the non-Jew, because she may not deliberately restore the property to one who would use a foreign legal system to defeat a valid Torah lien and thereby damage the Jewish creditor. Doing so would amount to damaging another person's lien and could be treated as mesirah. Perhaps Rabbenu Yerucham's case involved this latter situation. Even in the Radbaz and Maharit responsa, the wife would have been entitled under Torah law to collect from the non-Jew, yet they still denied her a claim against the Jewish purchaser. That may rest on the separate principle, discussed in those responsa, that one who derives title from a non-Jew receives the non-Jew's legal standing even where it benefits him. That rationale does not apply in our case, because the non-Jew did not transfer his legal rights to the wife. He merely allowed her to collect under the governing non-Jewish procedure. She does not derive title through his rights. This distinction therefore appears plausible. One should not object that a non-Jew never has a lien that a Jewish court must respect because avoiding repayment of a non-Jewish loan may be permitted. The responsum of the Rosh, rule 18, does not indicate such a blanket rule. See also above in the relevant siman, where this type of conduct is not necessarily classified as permitted damage. However. The unqualified language of the Rama and Rabbenu Yerucham does not sound like this distinction, and it is strained to force it into their words. One might also argue that even if the wife removes her own lien, the non-Jewish creditor had not previously acquired the property. He would merely have been able to take it if her lien did not stand in the way. Thus the argument "I will return it" may not apply where there was never an actual prior acquisition. Yet that too is strained, because she can simply say that she will remove her lien and permit the result that would otherwise follow. It appears instead that this question depends upon the dispute discussed below in siman 118, seif 2, between the Sema and the Shach. See what I write there, from which the present law can be clarified fully.
