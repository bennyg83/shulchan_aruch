Or, "I paid," through a migo that he returned it. This concerns an oral loan. Why is a migo needed for the claim of payment? Because once the creditor had taken the pledge and then returned it to the borrower before witnesses, it is as though the pledge remained in the creditor's legal possession. Therefore the borrower is not ordinarily believed to say he paid, except through a migo that he could have claimed, "I returned the pledge." Sema, citing Baal HaTerumot. Perishah holds that even with a written loan the borrower is believed to say, "I paid," through this migo of return. See Tumim. I wrote that this is not correct as practical law.
As long as he does not, and so shall he see his glory if he has returned to the thrones of the day, and he says that he would return to him in the day when Dao is faithful to the Almighty during his time, and that he shall bring evidence of his words:
Within its term. The commentators explain this as the original term of the loan. Shach objects: above in siman 78 we rule that a migo can operate against the presumption that a person does not repay before the due date. If so, the borrower should be believed here through his migo. Sema answers with difficulty that here the creditor is partially in possession, and the issue becomes the unresolved question whether a migo works against a presumption. This is strained and Beit Yosef would not have treated the law as simple. See Tumim. I gave a satisfactory answer: whenever the plea of payment is believed only through a migo, and there is also a contrary presumption, the migo does not suffice. The ruling is therefore correct.
