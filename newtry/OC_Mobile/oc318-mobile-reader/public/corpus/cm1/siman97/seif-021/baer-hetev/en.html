Furthermore, since the lender returned it to him in the presence of witnesses, it is considered as though the pledge were still in the lender's possession, and the lender is believed to make a claim against it up to its value. Sema.
Its due date. Meaning that before the due date he is not believed to say, "I paid," through a migo, because this would be a migo against the presumption that a person does not pay before a debt is due. Although siman 78 explains that if the loan was made without witnesses, or there are no witnesses to the due date, the borrower is believed to say that he paid even before the due date through a migo that he could have denied the loan or said that the due date had passed, the Gemara leaves unresolved whether a migo is effective against a presumption. Because the burden of proof is on the claimant, we ordinarily accept such a migo before the due date. Here, however, there had been a pledge. Even though it was returned, since it was returned in the presence of witnesses, the borrower is at least not considered the one in possession so as to be believed to say that he paid. Although I wrote above that a pledge taken other than at the time of the loan may be taken only after the due date, while a pledge voluntarily given at the time of the loan need not be returned at all, nevertheless at times, when it is seen that the borrower is dissipating his assets, he may be required to provide security even before the due date. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach writes: It is true that Baal HaTerumot says this regarding the period before the due date, but it does not appear correct at all. Although the Sema and Bach labored to explain his words, the primary ruling is that whenever the borrower has a migo, the burden of proof remains on the claimant, as I wrote at the beginning of siman 78. It appears that for this reason the Tur omitted the conclusion of Baal HaTerumot on this point.
