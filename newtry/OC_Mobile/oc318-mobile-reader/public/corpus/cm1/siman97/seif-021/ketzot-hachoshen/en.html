A borrower who says he paid needs a migo because a lender acquires the pledge. Once it is known that the object came to the lender as collateral, the pledge stands under the lender's possessory presumption to the amount of the debt. When the borrower claims that he later paid and thereby reacquired the pledge, his claim resembles a purchase claim against the lender and therefore requires a migo that the lender returned the pledge. This explains why, if the lender warned him not to return the pledge except before witnesses, thereby removing the migo of return, the borrower is not believed to say he paid. It also suggests that where a Jew pledged an object to a gentile and later redeemed it, the object might require immersion if the gentile actually acquired a pledge interest, though a gentile generally does not acquire a pledge from a Jew in the same manner. See Yoreh Deah siman 120.
A difficulty was raised from siman 78, where even payment within the fixed term can sometimes be believed through migo. One answer proposed that here a claim of returning the pledge would not exempt the borrower from the underlying debt. Even if the lender returned the pledge, the borrower would still owe the loan and could only say, "I will not pay until you return my collateral." Therefore the lender remains the stronger possessor of the loan. I do not accept this. Because a creditor acquires the pledge, the debt is concentrated upon the pledge while it remains in his hand. The borrower is not required to pay from other assets until the pledge is returned. This is not merely an independent counterclaim of "you hold something of mine," where each side collects separately. This is why Rashi and Baal HaMaor hold that if the pledge is lost through unavoidable accident the lender may lose the debt, and even according to our rule that he can collect after accident, that is only because he did not accept unavoidable accident liability. While the pledge exists in his hand, the borrower can say, "Return the pledge that is acquired to you for this debt, and then I will pay." The correct distinction concerns a migo against a known presumption. If the fixed term is presently known to the court, an ordinary migo does not defeat the presumption that a person does not pay before maturity. By contrast, after the term has passed, if the borrower himself says, "I paid during the term," the court knows the early payment circumstance only from his own mouth. That is the rule of "the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted," which can work even against a presumption. This distinction is supported by Ramban regarding a deposit document. Where the very status of the document is unvalidated, the defendant can say forgery and therefore can also claim return, because his mouth creates the document's force. But once the document is independently established, an ordinary migo does not overcome the strong question, "If you returned it, why is the document still in his hand?" The Shach rejects a general distinction between this principle and ordinary migo, but Ramban and the practical rulings support it. Accordingly, where a loan is presently still within its fixed term and the lender holds collateral, the borrower cannot use an ordinary migo to say he paid. Where the case is after maturity and the presumption of early nonpayment arises only through his own admission of the prior due date, the stronger "mouth that prohibited" mechanism can operate. The Sema explains the present collateral case as one where the borrower voluntarily gave the pledge during the term because he was dissipating assets, which is compatible with this analysis.
