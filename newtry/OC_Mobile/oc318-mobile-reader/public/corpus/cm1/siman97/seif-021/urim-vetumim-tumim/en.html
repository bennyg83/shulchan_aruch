Or, "I paid," through a migo that he returned the pledge. The Sema writes that without such a migo the borrower is not believed to say that he paid, even where the underlying loan was oral. Once collateral had been in the creditor's possession and was then returned before witnesses, the pledge itself functions like security placed in the borrower's hands on behalf of the creditor, and the borrower cannot simply assert payment. He is believed only because he has the alternative claim that he returned the pledge. This is correct and is stated explicitly by Baal HaTerumot in the name of Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat. In the Derishah, however, the Sema appears to hold that even where the debt is evidenced by a document, if the borrower says, "I paid," and has a migo that he could have said either "you never returned the pledge to me" or "I returned it to you," he is believed. In my view this is doubtful. The creditor who says, "I returned your pledge and you did not pay me," has his own migo. He could have said, "I did not return the pledge because it was lost through an unavoidable accident." We rule that even collateral taken after the loan does not make the creditor liable for unavoidable accidents. If he swore that the pledge was lost in such a manner, he would still collect the debt, as explained above in siman 72. If so, his present claim that he returned the pledge but was not paid should likewise be believed through that migo. Although this would be a migo used to extract money, the creditor possesses a written debt instrument, and we do accept a migo supporting collection where a valid document is already in his favor. The Sema's ruling therefore requires study.
Provided that it is not before the due time. The commentators ask that above, siman 78, we rule that a migo can be accepted even against the chazakah that a person does not pay before a debt is due. See the Sema's strained answer. According to the Rama's wording, however, the phrase can be understood differently. It need not refer to the maturity date of the loan. It may refer to the period during which the borrower is entitled to retain the returned pledge. For example, the creditor returned a night garment for the night, and immediately after daybreak demanded it back. If the borrower says, "I already returned it to you during the night," he is not believed. Just as a debtor is generally not believed to say that he paid before maturity because people do not ordinarily pay early, a borrower is not ordinarily expected to return a pledge while he still needs it. He was distressed enough that his property had been taken as collateral in the first place. Why would he voluntarily return it while it is still serving the very need for which the Torah required the creditor to return it to him? Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat in fact holds that if the creditor returned the pledge before witnesses, the borrower is never believed to say that he gave it back to the creditor. Baal HaTerumot and the Tur rule more leniently. But while the borrower still needs the pledge, the case is genuinely analogous to payment before maturity, and the borrower should not be believed to have returned it early. This also illuminates Tosafot in Bava Metzia 115a, in the passage beginning "Why does one take collateral." Rabbenu Elchanan asks: once the creditor took the pledge once, the debt is no longer cancelled by Shemittah and the pledge no longer becomes ordinary movable property of the heirs. Why, then, does the creditor repeatedly take it back after returning it to the borrower? Tosafot answer that he does so in order to prevent the borrower from later denying his right. At first glance the answer is difficult. What is gained by retaking the pledge? At the very moment the creditor returned it, the borrower could later claim, "I gave it back to you," or even deny the return arrangement entirely. If there is no concern for such a denial, then the creditor could simply leave the pledge with the borrower permanently. According to what was explained, the answer is clear. When the creditor returns a day garment for daytime use, he comes to retake it as soon as day ends. If the borrower claims while daylight still remains, "I already returned it to you," he is not believed, because while he still needs the garment it is as though the time for return has not arrived. The usual rationale of temporary avoidance does not apply here. But if the creditor were to leave the day garment with the borrower permanently, then at night, when the borrower no longer needs it, the borrower could plausibly say, "I returned it to you for sale, at your request, or for some other purpose." Even under an assumption that the creditor would not automatically retake it, the object still belongs to the creditor as a pledge, and if the creditor asks for it at a time when the borrower does not need it the borrower cannot insist on keeping it. At that stage a claim of return is therefore plausible. This is the meaning of Tosafot: repeated retaking closes the window in which the borrower could plausibly claim that he voluntarily returned the item after his need ended. The explanation is clear. Even though this rule itself appears correct and fits Tosafot well, it is difficult to say that this was the Rama's intent. The Rama follows the Beit Yosef, and the Beit Yosef's discussion based on Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat concerns the actual maturity date of the underlying loan. The basic difficulty can instead be resolved by a broader principle. The Shach asks below in siman 108 about the Tur and Mechaber's ruling that one who deposited property under a written instrument is believed to say "I returned it" through a migo that the property was lost by accident, but that before the time for return he is not believed. The Shach asks why the migo does not overcome the chazakah against early repayment and leaves the matter unresolved. It appears that the Tur holds as follows. A migo can balance the chazakah that a person does not pay before maturity because that chazakah is not overwhelmingly strong. In the ordinary debt case, after maturity a debtor is inherently believed to say "I paid." Before maturity, the chazakah removes that ordinary credibility. If he has a migo of equal strength, the migo and chazakah neutralize one another. Once both are set aside, his basic post-maturity credibility can operate, and he is believed. By contrast, where a person is not inherently believed without the migo at all, the result is different. A bailee who issued a written receipt cannot simply say "I returned the deposit" against the document. His credibility depends entirely upon the migo of accidental loss. If the chazakah against early return stands opposite that migo, the two neutralize one another. Once the migo is removed, nothing remains to support the claim of return against the document. The same is true here. Even with an oral loan, once the creditor held collateral the borrower is not inherently believed to say "I paid," as explained in the preceding seif katan and by the Sema. His credibility exists only because of the migo that he returned the pledge. If the chazakah against early payment stands against that migo, the two cancel one another. Without the migo, the borrower has no independent credibility to assert payment. Thus the Beit Yosef's ruling is straightforward, and there is no reason to think that the rule of siman 78 escaped his attention.
