(Seif 34) “He is therefore believed to say, ‘I paid you,’ with a hesset oath, by virtue of a migo that he returned the pledge.” This is difficult. Even now, when he returns the pledge to the lender, why should he not be believed to say, “I paid you”? The pledge was not given to prevent the borrower from claiming payment, but so that the lender would have a means of collecting the debt. By returning it, why should the borrower lose his original right to claim that he paid? The case must concern an oral debt, because with a written note he obviously cannot claim payment while the note remains in the creditor’s possession. It appears that Rabbi Yehudah of Barcelona holds that when the borrower was told, “Do not return the pledge except before witnesses,” this necessarily included, “Do not pay the money except before witnesses,” because the pledge now stands in place of the money.
