For if the creditor himself takes the collateral, he violates a prohibition. This implies that even seizure outside the debtor's house, when done by the creditor himself rather than by an agent of beit din, violates a Torah prohibition. The Sema explains that although Scripture says, "you shall not enter his house," the word "house" means the debtor's domain or possession generally. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 113a, in the passage beginning "Say," imply that where the creditor merely seizes the pledge from the debtor in the marketplace, the prohibition is only rabbinic. Nimukei Yosef writes likewise. In his Derishah, the Sema says that he found support for the Tur from the Rambam, who writes that if the creditor unlawfully entered the debtor's house or forcibly snatched the pledge, he does not receive lashes because the prohibition is redirected to a positive command. The wording suggests that this applies to every case in the Rambam's list, and that absent the positive command there would have been lashes. That inference can be rejected, as the Derishah itself notes, and Gidulei Terumah also understands the Rambam like Tosafot. It seems, however, that there is a stronger proof. In Bava Metzia 114b, under the Gemara's interpretation according to Rabbi Shimon, the baraita is read as follows: one who lends to another may not take collateral from him, and if he does take collateral he must return it; if he does not return it he violates all the prohibitions stated in that section. Rashi explains "all the prohibitions" as the commands "you shall surely return it," "you shall not sleep with his pledge," and "before the sun sets you shall return it to him." According to Rashi, this passage does not disprove Tosafot. The Rambam, however, in Hilchot Malveh, chapter 3, law 5, enumerates three prohibitions in this context: "you shall not enter his house," "you shall surely return it," and "you shall not sleep with his pledge." He thus interprets "all the prohibitions" as including the prohibition of entering the debtor's house. Yet the baraita merely says that the creditor himself "may not take collateral." This must refer to seizure outside the house, because if it meant entering the house, even an agent of beit din is forbidden to enter according to Shmuel, and there would be no reason to single out the creditor. Nevertheless the Rambam understands the baraita to say that if the creditor does not return the pledge he violated "you shall not enter his house." This proves that, for the creditor himself, the Torah prohibition extends even to seizure outside the physical house. The Rambam likely interpreted the passage this way because Tosafot, in the passage beginning "he returns," ask from the Gemara in Temurah. Rava holds that whenever the Torah says not to perform an act, if one performs it the act is ineffective. The Gemara challenges him from the Mishnah that a creditor who takes a debtor's pillow returns it at night, implying that the pledge itself remains effective. Tosafot ask that perhaps the Mishnah refers to a pledge taken lawfully by an agent of beit din. They answer that the Gemara relies upon the later baraita which states that the creditor may not take collateral and, if he does, must return it. According to the view that seizure outside the house by the creditor is only rabbinically prohibited, there would still be a difficulty. Perhaps the baraita refers precisely to such an outside seizure, so that "he may not take collateral" means only a rabbinic prohibition. If he does take it, the pledge is valid, because everyone agrees that an act done in violation of a rabbinic prohibition can remain effective. The baraita would then provide no challenge to Rava's Torah rule. The Rambam therefore understood "all the prohibitions" to include "you shall not enter his house," showing that even an outside seizure by the creditor is within the Torah prohibition. The Tosafot Yom Tov challenges the Sema's explanation that "his house" is not literal. The Gemara says that even outside seizure by the creditor is prohibited lest he eventually enter the debtor's house. This sounds as though the outside seizure is merely prohibited as a safeguard. This is especially clear according to Tosafot's text, "a creditor may not even seize outside, as a decree lest he enter his house." The matter can be reconciled. If we reject Shmuel's view and hold that an agent of beit din may enter the debtor's house, then the verse "and the man from whom you exact shall bring the pledge out to you" must refer to the creditor himself, because the agent of beit din may enter. On that approach, "his house" must mean the actual physical house rather than the debtor's domain generally. The verse itself then permits the creditor to receive an item brought outside to him, which means that direct outside seizure is not inherently included in the Torah prohibition. Chazal nevertheless forbade it lest the creditor enter the house. According to Shmuel, however, even an agent of beit din may not enter the debtor's house, and the phrase "the man shall bring the pledge out to you" refers to the agent of beit din, who may seize it outside. The creditor himself, by contrast, is forbidden by "you shall not enter his house," and in relation to the creditor the prohibition applies equally inside and outside. Thus, once we rule like Shmuel, there is no need to invent a separate rabbinic prohibition for the creditor's outside seizure. The Gemara's statement that the outside restriction is a safeguard appears only at the stage when it seeks to reject Shmuel and therefore does not support his rule. According to the final ruling like Shmuel, outside seizure by the creditor is prohibited by Torah law. This resolves the Gemara. Indeed, according to the Tosafot Yom Tov's challenge to the Sema, the matter becomes more difficult. He understands the Gemara's statement that outside seizure is prohibited lest the creditor enter the house as explaining the Torah itself: Scripture looked to the likely consequence and forbade the preliminary act that would lead to entry. But at that stage of the Gemara the discussion assumes that Shmuel is wrong and that an agent of beit din may enter the house. If the creditor himself is forbidden by Torah law even outside, where can we place the verse "the man shall bring the pledge out to you"? It cannot refer to an agent of beit din, because on that assumption the agent may enter. Nor can it refer to the creditor, because the Tosafot Yom Tov's reading would make even outside seizure a Torah prohibition. It is strained to say that the verse refers only to a debtor who voluntarily chooses to hand the item outside to the creditor, although that explanation must be used in the baraita according to some views. As an interpretation of the verse itself it is difficult. The Sema's explanation is therefore preferable.
Even outside, etc. The Rambam holds that an implement used in preparing food may not be taken as collateral even at the time of the loan. The Raavad disagrees. See the Maggid Mishneh. The Rambam's position is difficult. If the verse "he shall not take the millstones as collateral, for he takes a life as collateral" applies even at the time of the loan, consider the Gemara's question against Shmuel. Shmuel holds that the creditor may not seize collateral even outside and that an agent of beit din may not enter the debtor's house. The Gemara asks from the verse prohibiting millstones and deduces that other items may be taken. It answers that the special verse concerning millstones creates two prohibitions. But why is there any question? The special verse could be needed to prohibit food preparation implements even at the time of the loan, when both the creditor and the agent of beit din may ordinarily enter and take collateral. This requires explanation. It appears that there are two possible resolutions. First, the Rambam may derive from the reason stated in the verse, "for he takes a life as collateral," that logically there should be no distinction between collateral taken at the time of the loan and collateral taken later. There is also a general interpretive preference: whenever a verse can be given an independent application, we prefer that reading over saying that Scripture merely added a second prohibition to the same act. Thus, absent contrary necessity, it is better to interpret the special verse as prohibiting food preparation implements even at the time of the loan than to say that after the loan the same act violates two prohibitions. The Raavad argues that collateral taken at the time of the loan is comparable to an outright sale to the creditor. That is persuasive where the pledge is taken for actual collection, but not where it is taken merely as a memorial or security. In Bava Metzia, in the discussion of a loan secured by a pledge, and in Shevuot at the end of Shevuat HaDayanim, Shmuel holds that if even the handle of a sickle given as collateral is lost, a debt of one thousand zuz is lost with it. Tosafot on Shevuot 44a explain that Shmuel treats collateral taken at the time of the loan as taken for collection. Those who disagree with Shmuel view it as held only as evidence or memorial of the debt. Accordingly, Shmuel himself accepts the Raavad's premise. For him, collateral taken at the time of the loan is effectively taken for collection and is therefore analogous to a sale. The Gemara's question against Shmuel is then understandable. According to Shmuel there is no reason to apply the food implement prohibition at the time of the loan, because such a pledge is comparable to property sold to the creditor. The verse must therefore concern collateral taken after the loan, and the Gemara asks why a separate prohibition is necessary. It answers that two prohibitions apply. But according to our ruling against Shmuel on this point, as the Rambam rules, and according to the view that a guardian's oath on collateral derives from Rabbi Yosef's perutah principle rather than from treating the pledge as collection, a pledge at the time of the loan is held merely as a memorial. There is then no strong reason to distinguish it from later collateral, and it is preferable to apply the verse to the time of the loan rather than use it only to multiply prohibitions. Alternatively, the distinction can be framed through the law of liens. As stated, the reason "for he takes a life as collateral" logically applies both at the time of the loan and later. The Raavad's argument that taking collateral at the time of the loan is like a sale depends on saying that the debtor creates a present proprietary burden in favor of the creditor. There is no literal sale, because the object remains the debtor's property; it is only encumbered. The comparison therefore works only if a lien is legally comparable to a transfer of ownership, as Tosafot indicate in several places. If liens are of Torah law, however, all of the debtor's property, both land and movable property, becomes encumbered to the creditor when the loan is made. Ramban, in a responsum cited by the Beit Yosef below in siman 104, holds that on the Torah level movable property and land are equivalent in this respect, and the very principle of a Torah lien is derived from the verse "he shall bring the pledge out to you," which speaks of movable property. If so, collateral taken after the loan is also merely a realization of a lien that attached at the moment of the loan. I see no conceptual difference between collateral physically delivered at the time of the loan and collateral taken later, because both embody the same preexisting lien. By contrast, according to the authority who holds that liens are not of Torah law, the debtor's property does not become legally encumbered merely through the loan. A specific pledge handed over at the time of the loan does acquire a special lien, and the Raavad's distinction becomes compelling. Thus the Gemara's question is again understandable because it is directed to Shmuel, who holds that liens are not of Torah law. For Shmuel, the verse concerning millstones need not govern collateral taken at the time of the loan, and the Gemara properly asks why it is required. But according to our ruling that liens are of Torah law, there is no essential distinction between the two times. The Rambam can therefore interpret the special verse as prohibiting food preparation implements even when taken at the time of the loan. This also explains a point noted by Gidulei Terumah. The Rambam writes that if one takes a widow's garment or a food preparation implement as collateral and the item is then burned so that he cannot return it, he receives lashes. He does not write that he receives eighty lashes, although the Gemara's answer says that two prohibitions apply. According to what was stated, the Rambam does not need the verse to multiply prohibitions after the loan. He uses it to extend the prohibition to collateral taken at the time of the loan, so there is no additional set of lashes for the later case. There is even a small textual support for the Rambam. Shmuel says, "If the handle of a sickle is lost, a thousand zuz are lost." Rashi explains that "handle of a sickle" means the wooden handle of the implement. Why did Shmuel not simply say, "If a sickle is lost, a thousand zuz are lost"? There is significance in the wording. A sickle itself may be an implement used in preparing food, like scissors used to cut vegetables, and according to the Rambam it may not be taken as collateral. Shmuel therefore deliberately speaks of the handle, which merely serves the food implement and may be taken. Incidentally, his wording teaches this legal distinction. The words of Chazal are precise and require study. Nevertheless, most authorities, including the Tur, did not accept the Rambam's view, and the Mechaber follows them. They hold that at the time of the loan, and even afterward where the debtor voluntarily gives the item and there is no forcible seizure, food preparation implements may be taken. It is true, however, that forcible seizure by an agent of beit din, even outside the house, is prohibited when the item is used to prepare food, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state explicitly. At first glance this too appears contrary to the Gemara. The Gemara asks Shmuel from "he shall not take the millstones," inferring that other items may be taken. According to these authorities, what is the question? The verse is needed to prohibit an agent of beit din from seizing food preparation implements outside, even though such outside seizure is permitted with other items. Rashi writes explicitly that the general prohibition of taking collateral sounds as though it concerns entry into the house, so there is no textual implication permitting outside seizure of food implements. According to the Tur, however, and the Sema's explanation adopted above, "his house" is not literal but means his domain. The creditor is forbidden by Torah law even from outside seizure. The Gemara can therefore ask why a separate verse is needed. One cannot simply say that the millstone verse concerns an agent of beit din, because that agent is not mentioned in this section. The verse speaks of "the man from whom you exact," and it is not natural to transfer the prohibition to a court agent without indication. It is possible, however, that the Tur follows Tosafot on 115a, in the passage beginning "a widow." Tosafot write that the primary purpose of the special verse is certainly not merely to add a second prohibition to the same act, but to prohibit seizure of food preparation implements in a setting where other collateral may be taken. The Gemara's statement that there are two prohibitions addresses only the difficulty why Scripture phrased the special prohibition using the language of taking collateral from the house. Since even an agent of beit din may not enter the house, if the purpose were solely to regulate the agent's otherwise permitted outside seizure the verse could have used a different expression. Its choice of the ordinary collateral language therefore also creates a second prohibition where both apply. Nimukei Yosef writes in the name of Tosafot and the Rosh that if the verse has no application to the prohibited form of taking, transfer it to the form of taking that is otherwise permitted. Gidulei Terumah already asks how this accords with the Gemara's answer that two prohibitions apply. In Asifat Zekenim it appears that these are really two formulations of one idea. The discussion begins with the "if the verse has no application" principle and concludes with Tosafot's language that the Torah deliberately expressed the law in terms of taking collateral. More precisely. Nimukei Yosef may hold that where it is possible simply to say that one act violates two prohibitions, we do not resort to the rule of transferring a verse to a different case, because that interpretive move is more strained. This is the general pattern throughout the Talmud. But Tosafot write at the beginning of the chapter Eizehu Neshech, regarding the separate prohibitions of robbery and interest, that where a prohibition is redirected to a positive command and therefore does not ordinarily carry lashes, there is no practical point in saying that the same act violates two prohibitions. What difference would two prohibitions make if there cannot be an additional set of lashes? Accordingly, the Gemara's answer that the special food implement prohibition creates two prohibitions fits the authority who holds that failure to fulfill the corrective command can generate lashes, because if the pledge is accidentally burned and cannot be returned the creditor may then be liable to lashes. Two prohibitions could generate eighty lashes. But according to the many authorities who rule that only deliberate nullification of the corrective command matters, there is effectively no case in which lashes can result from this collateral prohibition. If the creditor deliberately burns the pledge, he owes monetary payment, which precludes lashes. There is then no meaningful reason to say that Scripture merely added a second prohibition. We instead apply the verse to a form of taking otherwise permitted, such as seizure by an agent of beit din. This reconciles the authorities. See what I wrote further below on this issue.
