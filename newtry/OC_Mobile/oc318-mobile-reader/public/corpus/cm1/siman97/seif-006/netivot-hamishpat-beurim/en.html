He violates a prohibition. It appears that with an iska that is half loan and half deposit, seizure is certainly permitted with respect to the deposit half. The debt concerning that portion does not arise from a loan. If the recipient used or spent the deposit portion for his own needs, it is in his hands as stolen property and resembles a debt arising from theft. Only with respect to the loan half is seizure prohibited. However, if the creditor seizes a different object whose value covers the entire amount, there is no prohibition. The principal act of seizure is justified by the deposit half, even though he could have seized a smaller object worth only the deposit half and intentionally takes a larger object so that he will also hold security corresponding to the loan half. A proof may be brought from Bava Metzia 30, where the Gemara asks whether one may spread out a lost garment for his own need and for the garment's benefit. It attempts to resolve this from the law that if one brings a red heifer into a yoke so that it will nurse and thresh, it is disqualified, and answers that there the Torah specifically says that no work was performed with it, which includes any work. This shows that in the absence of a specific Scriptural decree, an act may be permitted where one's intent includes both a permitted purpose and a prohibited purpose. It likewise appears that a store credit account, and every other obligation listed in siman 67 regarding shemittah as not being classified as a loan and therefore not being cancelled, is likewise outside the prohibition of entering the debtor's house to take collateral. Although the Sema there gives another reason for store credit, which does not apply here, Tumim concludes there that the primary reason is that stated by Tosafot in Ketubot, chapter Af Al Pi, namely that the obligation did not arise from a monetary loan. That rationale applies here as well. Nevertheless, because of the severity of the prohibition, I do not write this as a practical ruling.
