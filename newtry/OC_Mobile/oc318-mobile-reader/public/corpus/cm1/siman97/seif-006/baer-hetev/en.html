Himself. If the lender takes a pledge against the borrower's will, even outside the borrower's home, he violates the prohibition, "You shall not enter his house," and the reference to "his house" is not literal. The same rule, or all the more so, applies that he may not forcibly seize an object from upon the debtor or from his hand when he encounters him outside. A beit din agent is prohibited only from entering the debtor's house. Thus writes the Sema. See Responsa Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 241.
To the lender. Some explain that if, after the lender has seized the object, the borrower comes to take possession of it and remove it from him, the lender may defend his possession even by striking him. See Darkei Moshe. Sema.

And returns it. The Sema writes that this proves that, in the view of the Mechaber, the same rule applies to every implement used for preparing food that the lender unlawfully took as a pledge. Although he violated a prohibition, his act is nevertheless effective, and he need return it only when the borrower needs it for his work. After the borrower has used it, he must return it to the lender, and this cycle continues. A practical consequence is that after the lender's death the object is not treated as ordinary movable property in the hands of his children, and the Sabbatical year does not cancel the debt, as the Tur and Mechaber write in seif 16. Likewise, if he unlawfully took a widow's garment as a pledge, although he committed a prohibition, once he did so he need not return it except when she needs it. The Taz explains that as soon as the item is detached from the ground it has the status of movable property, and even a beit din agent may not take it as a pledge if it is an implement used for preparing food. Moreover, as movable property it must be returned when needed for work, and this applies even to an implement not used for preparing food.
