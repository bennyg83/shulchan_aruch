The lender himself may not seize collateral. From the wording of Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber it is precise that if the lender himself forcibly seizes collateral without the borrower's consent, even outside the borrower's house, he violates the prohibition, "When you lend your fellow any matter, you shall not enter his house to take his pledge." "His house" is not literal; all the more so he may not forcibly snatch what is on the borrower or in his hand when meeting him outside. Therefore they write generally that if the lender himself seizes collateral he violates a prohibition. A beit din agent, however, is forbidden only to enter the borrower's house. This is learned from the extra phrase "and the man," which refers back to "you shall stand outside."
To enter the borrower's house and write, etc. Since the Torah insisted that he stand outside, entry for purposes of taking collateral is forbidden even if he does not actually take it. Rashba says similarly. Siman 99 explains that the borrower may be placed under a ban until he produces all that he owns, or submits a written inventory to beit din, after which they arrange what is left for him.
From the borrower by force. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that a beit din agent may strike a borrower who resists when the agent seizes collateral from him in the marketplace by force. An ordinary private person may not, and if he injures him or his property he is liable. Some explain that if the lender has already taken hold of the collateral and the borrower comes to wrest it away, the lender may defend his possession even by striking him. See siman 4.
Not at the time of the loan. Meaning after the due date has arrived and the borrower is delaying payment, when the lender takes collateral to secure the debt. This prohibition applies specifically then, because the borrower does not consent. But if at the outset the borrower brings his own food-preparation utensil and asks for a loan against it, it is as though he knowingly sold it, and it is permitted. So writes the Tur in the name of the Rosh, unlike Rambam.
Such as a hand mill and its upper stone. The Tur and Mechaber write "such as" because all food-preparation utensils are forbidden, like the millstones explicitly mentioned by the Torah: "He shall not take a lower or upper millstone as collateral, for he takes a life as collateral." From the reason "for he takes a life," Chazal learned that all utensils used directly in preparing food are included, while implements only remotely related to food preparation, such as a winnowing shovel or fan, are excluded, as stated in seif 8.
But water-powered millhouses, etc. Beit din does not send an agent to close such a mill until the debt is redeemed, because land is not seized as collateral. Rather, if the creditor sues in beit din, beit din orders payment from the land where there is no other property. If the lender himself closes the mill, he does not violate the prohibition against entering the debtor's house for collateral, because this is not classified as taking a pledge, and he need not return the mill during working hours. Therefore the ruling concludes that if he actually enters and takes something, then it becomes ordinary movable collateral.
Nevertheless, if the lender, etc. This is also the Tur's wording. Nimukei Yosef, however, writes "if he transgressed and entered," implying that even with attached items there is an initial prohibition against entering.
They have the law of food-preparation utensils and he returns them at the time of work. This proves that if one unlawfully took any food-preparation utensil as collateral, the seizure nevertheless takes legal effect. He need return it only when the borrower needs it for work, and afterward the borrower must return it to the lender, repeatedly. The consequences include that the pledge is not treated as ordinary movable property in the hands of the borrower's heirs and that Shemittah does not cancel the debt. I explained at length that even when the lender entered the borrower's home illegally, or a beit din agent took food-preparation utensils, or one took a widow's garment, the pledge need be returned only at the time of need and may then be taken back.
