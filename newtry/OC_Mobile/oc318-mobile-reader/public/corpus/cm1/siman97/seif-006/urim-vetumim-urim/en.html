He violates a prohibition. Scripture states, 'You shall not enter his house.' 'His house' is not literal only, but includes his domain, teaching that the creditor may not forcibly take collateral from the debtor's hand. Tosafos Yom Tov explains the verse as meaning: do not perform an act that will cause you eventually to enter his house, for if you seize an article from him in the marketplace you may ultimately come to enter the house. See Tumim. I wrote that Sema's explanation appears correct and reconciled the Gemara accordingly. This is the view of Tur and Rambam. Tosafos, Nimukei Yosef, and apparently Rashi disagree and hold that taking outside the house is prohibited only rabbinically as a decree lest one enter the debtor's house. See Tumim.
To enter the debtor's house. The Torah was particular about the entry itself, as it says, 'You shall not enter.' This is Rashba's explanation.
To take, etc. The prohibition is to take forcibly. If the debtor refuses to allow an agent of beit din to take the collateral from his hand, the agent may strike him if necessary, and if the agent injures him while performing his duty he is exempt. The creditor himself, however, may not do so. If the collateral is already in the creditor's possession and the debtor seeks to snatch it back, see above, siman 4, concerning the rule that a person may sometimes execute judgment for himself.
Not at the time of the loan. This means that the time for repayment has arrived and the debtor delays payment, so the creditor seeks collateral through beit din. At the time of the loan, however, where the debtor voluntarily gives collateral, and likewise afterward whenever the debtor voluntarily gives it, even utensils used to prepare food may be accepted. This excludes Rambam's view that utensils used for food preparation may not be taken even at the time of the loan and with the debtor's consent. See Tumim for my reconciliation of Rambam.
An article with which one performs, etc. See Tumim, where I discussed at length the source for Tur's ruling that even an agent of beit din may not take, outside the house, utensils used for food preparation, since the verse 'You shall not take as collateral' speaks of the creditor and appears to add a second prohibition.
Like a millstone, etc. Scripture says, 'For he takes a life as collateral.' All utensils used to prepare food are included. Upper and lower millstones are mentioned only as examples of movable utensils with which food itself is prepared.
They are like land. According to the view above that detached property later attached is treated as movable property, another reason is needed. Ramban, cited by Baal HaTerumos, explains that the creditor does not actually take the millstones but merely closes the door so that the debtor cannot grind with them, and this is not included in the prohibition of taking collateral, which applies to physically taking the objects. Accordingly, if he detaches and takes them, he is liable. Tur, who holds that they are treated as attached, uses the language that they are like land, but that reason is not actually necessary here.
For beit din does not take them as collateral. This means that beit din does not send an agent merely to lock the mill so that the debtor cannot grind until he pays. Just as beit din does not prevent a debtor from plowing his field until he pays, it does not prevent him from using his mill. Ramban writes this in Baal HaTerumos, gate 1.
If the creditor entered. This means that he transgressed by doing so, for entering is forbidden, as Nimukei Yosef explains.
The upper millstone. The same law applies to the lower stone. It is mentioned differently only because the lower stone is attached to the ground and difficult to remove. If it can be removed, the law is identical. There was no need for the forced explanation of Gidulei Terumah.
