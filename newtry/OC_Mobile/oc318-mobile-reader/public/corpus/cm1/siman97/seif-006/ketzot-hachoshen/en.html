One may not seize collateral on his own. I wrote in siman 4 that people act improperly when, before coming to beit din, they grab an object from the opposing party merely to create a migo and strengthen their litigation. This violates the Torah prohibition against entering the debtor's house to take collateral, and Rambam indicates that even forcible taking in the street can fall under Torah restriction. There is a distinction where one actually takes property as collection of his own object or debt rather than merely as collateral. Nimukei Yosef, citing Rabbeinu Tam, distinguishes the rule that a person may sometimes enforce his own rights from the prohibition against self help seizure of collateral. Yet I find the category of full collection without beit din difficult, because who appraises the property and determines what counts as payment? Without judicial appraisal the act ordinarily remains only collateral seizure. The Tur cites Rif that where the debtor is violent, evades payment, and there is no other practical path of collection, beit din may enter and investigate his hidden property, even through a gentile agent. One might argue that fear the debtor will deny the debt is similarly a situation in which ordinary collection is impossible. But Baal HaTerumot sharply limits such departures from the Torah rule. He says they are used only under great necessity and only by beit din acting for the sake of Heaven, under the general emergency power of courts to punish beyond ordinary law in order to preserve Torah. Accordingly, even the opinions permitting exceptional seizure speak about beit din, not the private creditor. Fear of monetary loss does not authorize a person to violate the Torah. One must leave the loss to Hashem rather than stretch out his hand against the prohibition.
