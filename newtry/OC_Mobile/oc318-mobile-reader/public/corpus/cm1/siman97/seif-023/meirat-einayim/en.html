We arrange necessities for a creditor's debtor, etc. This is learned by a verbal analogy from the laws of valuations.
They give him food from everything for thirty days, etc. If he has food in his house, they leave him enough for thirty days and the remainder goes to the creditor. If he has no food, some movable property is sold to provide him the money for thirty days' food, as stated in seif 27.
Even if until now he ate as a poor person. Until now he may have restricted himself so that the surplus could support his family. Now, since nothing is set aside for them, he need not starve himself for the creditor. Conversely, if he formerly ate as a wealthy person, his standard is reduced now to an intermediate level. Rambam's expression, "food according to his manner," must therefore be understood as the manner properly fitting him, namely the moderate middle path.
Rather, they remove it from him. Not only do they refrain from giving him money to clothe himself in such expensive garments; even if he is currently wearing them, they take them from him.
And other bedding needs. This means pillows, cushions, and sheets. When merely taking collateral they leave him only a mat and bedding, while pillows are taken during the day and returned at night. But when arranging his needs before selling the remainder, they also leave pillows and sheets in his possession.
Not for his wife and children, etc. From the verse regarding valuations, "according to what the hand of the one making the vow can attain," Chazal derive that the arrangement is made for him alone, not for his wife and children.
They do not purchase lacking utensils from the proceeds of excess utensils. If he has more utensils of one type than he needs and too few of another type, they do not sell the excess to fill the deficiency. One might have argued that until now he lent his excess utensils to neighbors and borrowed from them the type he lacked, and once the excess is taken away he can no longer do so. The ruling teaches otherwise.
Not his yoke nor his donkey. "His yoke" means the oxen with which he plows, analogous to his donkey.
They do not leave him his books. Tefillin are left because they are worn as clothing and are a personal mitzvah.
Even a Torah scroll is taken. Although the Rosh writes that nowadays buying other holy books is an even more practical mitzvah than owning a Torah scroll, since Torah scrolls are kept in the ark and not used for ordinary study, the Tur and Mechaber quote the older formulation. The Mechaber himself holds that the Rosh meant only to add that buying other books is also a mitzvah, not to uproot the positive mitzvah of acquiring a Torah scroll.
