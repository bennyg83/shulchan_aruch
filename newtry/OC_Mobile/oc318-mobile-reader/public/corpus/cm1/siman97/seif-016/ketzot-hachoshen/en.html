With the borrower's consent. See what I wrote in siman 72, seif katan 2.
The pillow at night. See the Shach in siman 72, where he discusses Ramban's objection to Rashi and Baal HaMaor, who hold that a creditor acquires a pledge to the extent of liability for accidents. Ramban asks: a borrower is liable for accidents because all benefit is his, whereas a creditor may not use the pledge and, if he does, must pay the borrower for that use. The Shach answers that this is not a proof. Who says the creditor may not use it when the pledge is in his hand, such as a pillow by day or a plow at night? Since it is his as security for payment, perhaps he may use it. The Torah requires him to return it to a poor debtor at the time the debtor needs it only as a charitable obligation. Even if use is forbidden, the creditor can still be liable for accidents because the pledge is fundamentally his; he may be barred from using it only because the Torah obligates him to return it intact for the poor person's use, and using it may wear it out and diminish that charitable return. This is difficult from the end of chapter HaMekabel. The baraita says, 'If he is a poor man, you shall not sleep with his pledge.' The Gemara asks: does that imply that if he is wealthy you may sleep with it? Rashi explains the question: why should a rich borrower's garment be permitted for the creditor's free use, which resembles interest? According to the Shach, there should be no difficulty. If the creditor acquires the pledge and the only bar to use is the mitzvah of returning it intact to the poor, then where the debtor is wealthy and there is no return obligation, the creditor should indeed be able to use it. The Gemara's assumption that he may not shows that the creditor does not acquire a right of use in the pledge.
Whose reward is stated alongside it. This comes from a responsum attributed to Ramban, but it is difficult. Tosafot in Bava Batra 8b says that although the reward for charity is expressly stated, we nevertheless compel charity because it also contains the negative commandments 'you shall not harden your heart' and 'you shall not close your hand.' Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah siman 248 cites this in the name of the poskim, including Ramban. Here too, returning the pledge includes the negative command, 'you shall not sleep with his pledge,' and Rambam writes in chapter 3 of Hilchot Malveh that one who fails to return it violates that prohibition. Although no lashes apply because it is a prohibition without an action, the same is true of charity. Perhaps the responsum is actually Rashba's, and Rashba may hold that even charity is not compelled in the same manner despite the prohibition, as Beit Yosef's presentation in siman 248 appears to indicate. According to our ruling that charity is compelled, however, the same should apply to returning the pledge. In my opinion, where one unlawfully seized a pledge without authorization, failure to fulfill the positive command of returning it places the matter within the structure of a prohibition corrected by a positive command, and every Jewish beit din with power should certainly compel him forcefully to return it and fulfill the mitzvah. Although the Sema, seif katan 15, appears to imply otherwise, the principal law is as I have written. Many people breach this area by sending agents without beit din authority to seize property for debts and failing to return the debtor's pillow at night. Every beit din should supervise this carefully.
