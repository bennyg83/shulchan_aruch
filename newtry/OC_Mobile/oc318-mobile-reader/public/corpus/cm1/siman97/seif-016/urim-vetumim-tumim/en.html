He is not obligated to return it to him. The Sema, seif katan 34, cites the Darkei Moshe in the name of Mordechai in the chapter HaMekabel, saying that an item constantly needed by the borrower, such as a pillow or a plow, must nevertheless be retained until the borrower redeems it. The Sema therefore explains that "he need not return it" means only that there is no periodic return obligation, but the creditor still may not sell it. Their words are not clear, because Mordechai does not state such a rule. Rabbi Kalonymus interprets the Mishnah's dispute between the first Tanna and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, whether collateral may be sold after thirty days, as applying even to items that are not subject to periodic return. From this he inferred that according to the first Tanna, even collateral taken at the time of the loan may never be sold. In his view, whether the pledge was taken at the time of the loan or later makes no difference once the particular item is not subject to return. Mordechai himself disagrees. He explains that the first Tanna and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel do not dispute items that are outside the law of periodic return. Everyone agrees that such items may be sold after thirty days, because once there is no obligation to return them to the borrower, there is no reason to preserve the specific object. Their dispute concerns only items that are subject to periodic return. The first Tanna holds that such an item can never be sold because the creditor must preserve the ability to fulfill the Torah's continuing command of return whenever the debtor needs it. Accordingly, collateral taken at the time of the loan is treated, with respect to this issue, like an item outside the return law, because no continuing return obligation applies to such collateral. It should therefore be saleable after thirty days even if the physical item is one that would ordinarily be needed constantly. Once we rule that the first Tanna's restriction on sale applies only to collateral actually subject to periodic return, as is clear in this siman and explicitly above in siman 73, the Sema's rule has no basis. Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 49, part 5, states this expressly: the ruling against Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel applies only to items subject to periodic return, where the Torah imposed an ongoing act of righteousness upon the creditor. Items not subject to periodic return may be sold after thirty days in accordance with Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. Collateral taken at the time of the loan has no Torah obligation of periodic return, so it certainly may be sold after thirty days through beit din. This is contrary to Rabbi Kalonymus and leaves the Sema's statement difficult.
Beit din is obligated to compel him. This appears from the Rambam's language concerning one who takes a widow's garment or a food preparation implement, where he writes that if the creditor took the item, beit din causes it to be returned against his will. In such cases the court must compel performance of the positive command. The creditor violated a prohibition that would ordinarily carry lashes, but the Torah redirected it to the positive command of return. Beit din must therefore compel fulfillment of that positive command. So long as the command remains unfulfilled, the original prohibition remains operative. This is especially true according to our ruling that liability depends on whether he fulfilled the corrective command. Rashi explains that the prohibition takes effect immediately, which is why the initial warning is not considered doubtful. Only actual fulfillment of the positive command removes the liability. It is therefore obvious that beit din must compel the creditor to perform the command rather than allow him to remain indefinitely in violation. Tosafot in Makkot state this explicitly. According to the authority who focuses on fulfillment, if beit din warns the creditor to return the pledge and he refuses, he can receive lashes. This shows that beit din actively directs him to return it. If he ignores the court, the consequences of the prohibition remain. If the court had no duty to compel, how would its warning have this role? It is true that a responsum attributed to Ramban, siman 88, sounds otherwise. It says that where a creditor entered the debtor's house and took collateral that is subject to the return obligation, beit din does not compel him. This may not be decisive. The wording "entered the house" may not mean that he entered unlawfully. The case could involve an agent of beit din or another circumstance in which entry was permitted. Alternatively, Ramban may follow the authority who holds that lashes depend on active nullification of the positive command. On that view, so long as the creditor has not affirmatively destroyed the possibility of return, the original prohibition no longer remains upon him in the same continuing sense. Beit din might therefore have no obligation to compel immediate performance. According to our ruling, however, and according to what was explained above that the Rambam rules in accordance with the fulfillment standard, the prohibition attaches immediately and remains until the positive command is performed. The Rambam's statement that beit din compels return against the creditor's will is therefore precise. The Sema above, seif katan 15, appears to understand that beit din never compels return, but that does not appear correct.
