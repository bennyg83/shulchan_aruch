Seif 6. (He shall not take a pledge from him.) Concerning the prohibition against taking a pledge in siman 97, if property of the borrower came lawfully into the creditor's possession, one might at first think that there is no prohibition in retaining it as a pledge. This would seem especially plausible because a pledge given at the time of the loan with the borrower's consent is permitted, even with respect to an article otherwise protected by the laws of pledges, so long as it was not forcibly seized from him. If, however, the article came into the creditor's possession after the loan merely as a deposit and not as a pledge, it is clear that he may not simply retain it. Once the depositor demands the deposit back, failure to return it immediately can violate the prohibition against withholding another's property. A deposit is unlike an ordinary loan, concerning which the Sema in siman 339 writes that delay does not constitute that prohibition when the debtor intends to pay. This distinction is also reflected in the rule that one who falsely denies a deposit is not presumed merely to be stalling, and can thereby become established as a denier and suspect. Thus, one may not convert another's deposit into a pledge without lawful judicial authority. Even where beit din authorizes temporary retention until the dispute is adjudicated, as under certain rules of attachment, the retention must remain within the Torah's laws governing pledges. The article needed for sleeping must be returned at night where required, articles needed for food preparation may not be taken, and the protected clothing of a widow may not be seized. The same principles apply even where the borrower's property originally entered the creditor's hands with permission, because once its return is demanded, continued retention requires legal authority and cannot exceed what beit din itself could authorize. The cited authorities state that a person may sometimes retain a deposit in his possession against a debt owed to him, but this is at most an after the fact rule, or applies where he has proper authorization, or where the retention is for unpaid wages, certain forms of guaranty, or an identifiable stolen article under the permitted rules of siman 97. The Sema's explanation that the borrower's "house" means his legal domain does not change this conclusion. A deposited article can itself be regarded as remaining within the depositor's legal domain for certain purposes. According to the alternative explanation, there is likewise no meaningful distinction between taking the pledge in the marketplace and retaining a deposit already in one's possession. A practical consequence concerns liability for accidents. If the creditor wrongfully retains the article as a pledge, he can become liable even for unavoidable loss. If he retains it lawfully, his status is only that of a paid custodian. This can apply even where the owner would not otherwise have demanded immediate return, if he in fact demanded it once he learned that the creditor intended to hold it against the debt. Using a non Jew to seize a pledge raises the rule that a non Jew cannot serve as a halachic agent. Using a Jew raises the rule that there is no agency to perform a transgression, and the Jewish agent himself is forbidden to enter and seize a pledge. Rabbinic agency through a non Jew may also be relevant, although there are authorities who are lenient regarding one non Jew instructing another in rabbinic prohibitions. Taking a pledge for merchandise sold on credit may be somewhat more lenient, particularly in a transaction where persistent demand for payment can sometimes undo the sale.
