But Shabbos clothing, etc. His intent is not to transfer ownership of these garments to them absolutely. Rather, they remain his property, with permission for him to give them to his family members to wear. Therefore a creditor may collect from them. It appears that if the husband says that he gave the garments to them as an absolute gift, he is believed, for he has a migo that he could give them an absolute gift now, in which case the creditor could no longer collect them. This may not apply where the creditor has a lien on movable property together with land, for perhaps the market protection enactment does not apply to a gift made to another at the time of arranging the debtor's necessities, and according to all opinions the creditor could then collect, leaving no migo. This requires study. Aside from that case, he certainly has a migo, because until the property is actually delivered to the creditor he may give it away, and the creditor cannot recover it from the recipient. Mordechai in the chapter Na'arah Shenispattah implies, however, that an oath is required. This requires study, for he has a migo. Perhaps Mordechai follows the opinion that a migo is not used to exempt from an oath.
If there were rings, etc. Here even the dissenting opinion agrees, because it is not ordinarily assumed that such articles were given as an absolute gift. But if it is known that he did give them as a gift, even if they are rings, the creditor has no claim to them. See the preceding seif katan concerning whether the husband is believed through a migo.
She is believed. Tur writes that she is believed with an oath, because he holds that since the articles are presumed to belong to the husband, she is considered one who collects from another and therefore requires an oath. Alternatively, she takes hesset where the husband denies her claim. Otherwise, he himself would be believed through the migo explained above, and therefore her definite claim rests upon the husband's statement.
If the dowry is still, etc. See Tumim. This ruling requires study and applies specifically where she has no right to support at all. See there.
Even if he was taken captive, etc. But if the money is in his possession, he may redeem himself with it, and the wife cannot prevent him on the ground that doing so diminishes her ketubah and dowry rights. See Tumim.
