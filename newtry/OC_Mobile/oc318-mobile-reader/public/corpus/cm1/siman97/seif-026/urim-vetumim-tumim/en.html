The dowry is in her possession, etc. The wording implies that she may retain the dowry even toward payment of her ketubah. This ruling requires study. Mahari Weil, responsum 78, gives no proof for it. I do not understand how it can be correct, because every moment the wife is obligated to deliver these assets to her husband. They are his property as nichsei tzon barzel. With money there is no concept of retaining the specific ancestral property for the honor of her father's house. If the husband owes another creditor, the creditor should therefore be able to collect these assets through the rule of Rabbi Natan. The responsum of the Rosh, rule 38, siman 2, concerns a woman who brought into the marriage, in place of a cash dowry, a mortgage document on land. The document was held by a scribe. Before his death the husband directed that the document be returned to his wife. An earlier creditor of the husband sought to collect from that mortgage. The Rosh writes that the creditor has no right to it either way. If the arrangement is treated as though she brought actual land into the marriage, a creditor of the husband cannot collect it. If it is merely an ordinary debt, the husband could have forgiven it. Moreover, because she owes the husband the dowry while the husband will owe her the ketubah when he dies, once he has died she may retain what is under her control toward payment of her claim. The Rosh's language shows that her right to retain the assets for the ketubah arises specifically upon the husband's death, when the time for payment arrives. During his lifetime, before the ketubah is collectible, the husband's creditor can apparently reach property that she presently owes him. Otherwise there would have been no need for the Rosh's lengthy analysis. The asset never came into the husband's physical possession and she remained its holder. If mere possession were enough, the husband's death would be irrelevant. Mahari Weil did add another factor in his responsum: the husband was unable to support her, which gave her a status resembling a wife in rebellion from whom the dowry was not forfeited. But where the husband can support her, for example because she has nichsei melog whose fruits are sufficient for her maintenance, or because her earnings support her, the rule that she may retain the dowry toward a ketubah that is not collectible during the marriage requires study. Mahari Weil provides no proof for such a proposition, and the Rosh's responsum appears to refute it.
Even if he was taken captive, etc. This implies that where the dowry is already in the husband's possession, he may use it to redeem himself. From here the Rama, in responsum 93, correctly proves that a wife cannot demand that her husband provide security for the value of her dowry. There is nevertheless a question. The reason a ketubah cannot be collected during the husband's lifetime is that its due date has not arrived. Yet above in siman 73 we rule that when a debtor is dissipating his assets, beit din may restrain those assets even before the debt's due date. According to the Maharshal's view explained there, this presents no difficulty. Maharshal limits early restraint to a debtor who maliciously and improperly squanders his assets. Because he acts wrongfully, beit din departs from the ordinary rule and freezes the property. A person who is simply declining financially through no fault of his own is not subject to such restraint. There is no greater involuntary misfortune than captivity. According to the authorities who disagree with Maharshal, however, one must distinguish differently. Early restraint applies to an ordinary debt whose liability will definitely mature at a fixed and known time. A ketubah may never become collectible at all, and even when it will, its due date is unknown. It would be impossible to immobilize the husband's property indefinitely on the possibility that the ketubah might eventually become due. That differs from the case above, where both the obligation and its maturity are certain.
