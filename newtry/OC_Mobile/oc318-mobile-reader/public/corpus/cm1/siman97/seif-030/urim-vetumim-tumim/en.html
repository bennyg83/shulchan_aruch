And he shall not violate his oath. This ruling is disputed. Rivash writes that where a person swore to pay a debt but has no means to do so, there is no greater circumstance beyond his control. What should he do, steal or rob in order to fulfill his oath? Rivash concludes that beit din nevertheless leaves him the protected minimum of property according to the order set out by the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh for one who claims that he lacks the means to pay. This is explicit: the ordinary debtor's exemptions are preserved even though he swore. At first glance there is a clear proof for Rivash from the law of vows. Why should an oath be stricter than a vow? If a debtor must throw himself into destitution to fulfill his oath, then one who vows, "It is upon me to give one hundred to the Temple treasury," should likewise have to strip himself of all protected property in order to fulfill the vow. Yet we rule that those obligated by valuations and one who says "one hundred is upon me for the Temple treasury" are given the standard protected allowance, as stated in the Gemara and codified by the Rambam in Hilchot Arachin, chapter 3. We do not say that he must impoverish himself to fulfill his vow. Surely a private creditor should not have greater rights than consecrated property. One might distinguish that where a person swore merely to pay without fixing a date, beit din can preserve the protected minimum because he may yet fulfill the oath later. But if he fixed a date, once that date passes he violates the oath, and perhaps he must therefore impoverish himself in order to pay on time. This distinction is strained. Neither the Shulchan Aruch nor the responsum Chazeh HaTenufah, which is the source of the present ruling, makes any distinction between an oath with a fixed date and one without. Moreover, when no date is stated, every moment can itself be considered a time when performance is due. It therefore appears that Rivash is correct as practical law. Once Torah law itself establishes that certain basic assets are not included in ordinary debt collection, the debtor's oath to pay is naturally understood as an oath to perform what legally counts as payment. He did not intend to include property that Torah law removes from the collection process. The responsum of the Rosh, rule 8, law 4, similarly writes that ordinarily a person who swears to pay is not understood to have sworn to sell every possession, sleep in the street, and die of hunger. This is precisely the rationale of the debtor's protected minimum. The oath attaches only to assets legally included in payment. That conclusion is simple if it is certain that the debtor is entitled to the protected allowance. But there is an old dispute whether such an allowance exists in ordinary debt collection. Rabbenu Tam rules that the halachah follows the authority who gives the debtor no such allowance. The Rashba writes that even if a protected allowance applies when taking collateral, it may not apply to actual payment. Asifat Zekenim cites the Rashba and concludes that Rabbenu Tam's position would have appeared correct, were it not that the words of the Geonim are received tradition and should be followed, particularly because money should not be extracted without clear proof. Thus the matter was treated as sufficiently doubtful to prevent collection. Accordingly, Chazeh HaTenufah may have been uncertain on the underlying monetary law and inclined toward Rabbenu Tam, while nevertheless refusing to remove property because of the doubt. That restraint works for monetary law, but an oath raises a possible Torah prohibition, and in a doubt of Torah prohibition we are stringent. If perhaps the halachah is that there is no protected allowance, then those assets are legally included in payment and the oath attaches to them. The debtor would then have to impoverish himself in order to avoid a possible violation of a Torah oath. There is no contradiction from vows to the Temple treasury. There the law of the protected allowance is certain and explicit. The entire doubt is whether ordinary creditors are learned from the law of consecrated valuations. Since sacred obligations themselves unquestionably preserve the allowance, an oath or vow to pay them plainly does not extend beyond that legal obligation. Rivash, for his part, clearly rules like the Rambam and those who hold that ordinary creditors also must leave the debtor the protected minimum. It therefore follows directly that the oath does not attach to those exempt assets, just as a vow to the Temple treasury does not. This explanation is sound. It is true that Chazeh HaTenufah is traditionally attributed to the Rosh, and in Nedarim chapter 9 the Rosh expressly rules that the debtor is given the protected allowance. Still, the responsum may have treated the issue as doubtful, much as the Beit Yosef notes that in Bava Metzia the Rosh cites Rabbenu Tam's position last, and the Tur's abridged rulings there also leave the matter uncertain despite the explicit statement in Nedarim. On that basis the responsum can be reconciled.
