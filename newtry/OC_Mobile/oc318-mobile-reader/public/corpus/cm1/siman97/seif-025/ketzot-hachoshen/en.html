Regarding a wife's clothing, the authorities divide into several approaches. The Mishnah says that one who consecrates or values himself cannot include his wife's or children's clothing, dye used for them, or new sandals purchased for them. In Bava Kamma the Gemara initially explains this through agency: when the husband purchases or has items prepared specifically for his wife, he acts as her agent and she acquires directly. Rabbi Abba instead answers that with consecration the husband is treated as though he had transferred these items to them beforehand. These explanations matter greatly. If the wife truly acquired directly through the husband's agency, the husband never owned the items and neither his creditor nor a purchaser from him can reach them. If, however, Rabbi Abba means only that for purposes of consecration or creditor collection we treat him as though he transferred them, then ordinary ownership may still have remained with him before the special legal fiction applies. Hagahot Ashri cites one opinion that a man cannot sell garments purchased for his wife, even before she physically receives them, by an a fortiori from consecration. Mahari Chayim answers that Rabbi Abba's rule may be special to consecration and valuation, and that an ordinary creditor or purchaser can still reach them until the wife actually acquires them. Another opinion says Rabbi Abba's answer was only a deflection and the real rule remains agency. Ramban, cited by Beit Yosef, appears to follow Rabbi Abba, because he says that where the creditor has an after acquired property clause and movables were subjected together with land, the creditor can sometimes collect from the wife's and children's clothing. If the wife had acquired directly through agency, no lien of the husband could reach them at all. Nimukei Yosef points in the same direction. Rosh likewise appears to accept Rabbi Abba's final explanation. This may explain why the Shulchan Aruch did not simply codify Ramban's stronger collection rule, because the underlying reason remains disputed. Even according to the opinion that Rabbi Abba's legal fiction does not ordinarily protect against every creditor, once the wife actually wore or otherwise physically acquired the clothing, ordinary acquisition is complete. The husband intended to give it to her. The only reason a special legal fiction was needed before delivery was that she had not yet performed acquisition. After she has actually acquired the garments, neither a creditor nor the husband can treat them as his. This also resolves the Gemara at the end of Perek Na'arah, where Rav says a widow's clothing is appraised against her ketubah while Shmuel says what she is wearing is not appraised. The Gemara challenges Rav from the Mishnah of one who consecrates or values himself and answers that when the husband originally gave the clothing he intended her to use it while remaining with him, not necessarily to take it away upon separation. If the special protection applied only to consecration and valuation, the challenge to Rav would otherwise be difficult. The proper understanding is that once she actually wore the garments she acquired them, but the scope of the husband's gift can still be limited by his presumed intent concerning later departure. This also explains why Shmuel's statement focuses on "what she is wearing." Garments merely purchased for her but not yet delivered can still be treated differently. The legal fiction that protects them against consecration or creditors does not necessarily make them hers against the husband himself or his heirs. Once she wore them, however, the actual acquisition is complete. Accordingly, even under Mahari Chayim, a creditor may have rights in clothing merely bought for the wife and not yet acquired by her, while no creditor can reach garments she has already actually acquired. If the husband expressly sells garments that have not yet reached her, the sale can be valid under Rabbi Abba's framework. If one accepts the agency explanation, even that sale fails because the husband never acquired them. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek discussed a ketubah clause saying the wife would take all garments belonging to her person beyond the fixed ketubah sum. After the husband died, ten cubits of silk were found which he had bought to make her a garment. The questioner argued that designation alone is nothing and that the ketubah could not convey future items. Tzemach Tzedek ruled that she acquired the silk because the husband had bought it for her and his ketubah language showed an intention to transfer all such clothing even upon separation. In my view, if the silk had not yet been made into or actually received as her garment, the result depends on the analysis above. Under Rabbi Abba, the husband had not yet made a real transfer to her merely by buying material for her. The legal fiction of prior transfer operates against consecration and creditors, not necessarily against the husband himself. The special ketubah language can show that, once garments were actually transferred to her, he intended a permanent gift even upon separation, but ten cubits of raw silk are not yet "garments belonging to her body." The principle that one buys later property in order to uphold his word applies where he later acquires the very item previously sold. Raw silk later purchased is not identical with a promised garment not yet existing. Therefore the wife does not necessarily acquire the raw silk merely because it was purchased with her in mind. The distinction also resolves a well known difficulty. The Gemara challenges Rav specifically from clothing actually worn by the wife, while Shmuel appears to agree that garments merely prepared for her can be appraised. If the Mishnah's rule that newly purchased clothing for the wife was full ownership in her, Shmuel too would be contradicted. Under Rabbi Abba, however, the pre delivery protection is special against consecration or creditor collection and does not establish absolute ownership against the husband or his estate. Actual wearing changes the law. This is the coherent approach.
The Sema explains the phrase concerning dye used for the wife as though the husband owned the garments, later decided to designate them for his wife, and by dyeing them for her she acquired through the change. I am astonished. The Mishnah says only that the creditor does not take "the dye with which he dyed for them," not that garments owned by the husband become the wife's merely because he dyed them for her. Tosafot's discussion likewise assumes the garment was already the wife's and asks only who acquires the dye or the value added by the dye. A physical change acquires property in the laws of theft and robbery, not as a general gift mechanism. If the husband owns a garment and his wife dyes it for herself, the act of dyeing does not automatically transfer ownership of his garment to her. The same is true if he cuts and sews his cloth for her. Preparation alone is not an acquisition. Rashi and Rambam's language about dyed garments can be understood as clothing ordered or acquired already in dyed form specifically for her. Once the husband instructed the artisan or seller to prepare garments for his wife, the question returns to the ordinary issue whether she acquires through agency or through Rabbi Abba's legal fiction. There is no new acquisition through change. The Tur notably omits a separate rule that dyeing the husband's garment for his wife transfers it to her. He says simply that garments purchased for the wife are not collected by the creditor, even if new and unworn. Had a new rule of acquisition through dyeing existed, the Tur should have stated it. I therefore conclude that a garment owned by the husband does not become the wife's merely because he dyes, cuts, or sews it for her.
