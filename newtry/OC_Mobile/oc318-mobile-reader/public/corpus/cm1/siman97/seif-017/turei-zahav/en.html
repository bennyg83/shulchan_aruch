(See there, siman 98) “If the debtor admits.” This excludes the law of a ketubah stated in Ketubot 104: if a woman waited twenty-five years without demanding payment, we presume that she waived it. The Gemara sought to distinguish there between a case where the ketubah remained in her hand and one where it did not. An ordinary creditor is different. Even without mentioning the debt for many years, he may collect forever. Even if no note is presently in his possession, so long as the debtor admits the debt, the delay itself proves no waiver. Until the creditor expressly states that he waived it, he has not lost it. This applies, however, only where the debtor does not claim an express waiver. If the debtor says that the creditor expressly released him, and the note is not in the creditor’s hand, he has no further claim against him. This is the Tur’s precise wording: “We do not say that he waived,” meaning that beit din does not infer waiver merely from his long silence. If the debtor asserts that the creditor expressly waived, he is certainly believed. This appears simple to me. Sema writes that a claim of waiver is ineffective, and I saw my teacher and father-in-law write likewise on the ground that the debtor lacks a migo of “I paid and tore up the note,” since the creditor may later produce the note and expose him as a liar. They did not examine the matter carefully. No one compels the debtor to say, “I paid and tore up the note.” It would suffice to say simply, “I paid,” and he would be believed when the note is not before us. The same applies to “You expressly released me,” or any other defense, whenever the note is not present, even if it later emerges from another person’s possession. This is beyond doubt. The present law does not concern a factual dispute whether an express waiver occurred. It concerns only an inference from the creditor’s failure to demand payment for a long time, namely whether we presume an implicit waiver as with a ketubah. For an ordinary debt we do not. This appears plainly correct. An error escaped the distinguished authorities because they did not sufficiently examine the source. This is also why the Tur immediately adds the rule concerning all property, another area where a ketubah differs from an ordinary debt, and concludes that these presumptions are stated only concerning a ketubah.
