Even according to the enactment cited by the Sema in siman 97, instituted because of fraudulent debtors, allowing collection from clothing belonging to the debtor's wife, it appears that clothing reserved for Shabbat and festivals should not be collected where the husband has no right to sell it and, according to some opinions, a sale of such clothing would be ineffective. Even under the enactment, her garments should then be treated only as property serving as security for the debt, much like other assets upon which a lender relied. Accordingly, where the creditor can collect from the husband's own clothing, he should not collect from hers, even if he has seized it. Beer HaGolah cites Mordechai that if the husband formally transferred jewelry to his wife, all agree that the creditor does not collect it. Such a transfer must be established by proof, and the burden rests on her. Although the acquisition of the jewelry may initially have been for her and therefore there is not a simple prior ownership presumption in favor of the husband, the ordinary rule is nevertheless that the creditor collects unless an express transfer to her is shown. Moreover, there is some aspect of prior ownership in the husband, because he initially acquires the item without designation and then transfers it to whomever he chooses. If the husband dyed fabric, purchased or took possession of it for his children, and then filled it with feathers, the finished object may acquire a new legal identity. Dyeing for their sake and sewing can constitute an irreversible change. Likewise, if a loan was lawfully made against a woman's garment on condition that the lender's wife may wear it, a creditor of the lender should not be able to collect that garment, because it resembles clothing that was acquired specifically for her use.
