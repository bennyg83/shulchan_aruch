(Seif 14) “A widow, whether she is,” etc. The same rule must apply to every unmarried woman, whether divorced or never married. Scripture speaks of the common case, because a widow ordinarily conducts business, whereas unmarried young women generally do not. This is supported by the Mishnah in HaMekabel: “A widow, whether poor or wealthy, may not be taken as security. Rabbi Shimon says: a wealthy widow may be taken as security; a poor widow may not, because you are obligated to return the pledge and thereby give her a bad reputation among her neighbors,” meaning suspicion of sexual impropriety. That rationale applies to every unmarried woman. If the first tanna prohibited only a widow, there would be two disputes with opposite tendencies: the first tanna, who is stringent regarding a wealthy widow, would be lenient regarding a divorcee or unmarried woman and prohibit only a widow; Rabbi Shimon, who is lenient regarding a wealthy widow, would be stringent regarding other unmarried women. Yet the Mishnah presents only one dispute. Maggid Mishneh uses the same form of proof in chapter 3 of the Laws of Creditor and Debtor. Rambam rules that the prohibition against taking a widow’s pledge applies even at the time of the loan. Maggid Mishneh objects that if so, why does Rabbi Shimon prohibit taking a poor widow’s pledge because of the bad reputation created by repeated return? A pledge taken at the time of the loan need not be returned. One cannot answer that they also disagree on that point, Rabbi Shimon requiring return even for a pledge taken at the loan and the first tanna not requiring it, for then the Mishnah should have stated both disputes. It records only the dispute about a wealthy widow. The same reasoning applies here with even greater force, because the supposed opinions would be reversed. The primary conclusion is therefore that the first tanna likewise draws no distinction between a widow and other unmarried women.
