The Gemara asks against Rava, who holds that an act forbidden by the Torah generally has no legal effect, from the law of collateral. It answers that Scripture says, "You shall surely return," showing that even though the taking was forbidden, an acquired pledge relationship arises. This verse applies clearly to the poor debtor. What of the rich debtor, where the return command does not apply? Should we say the illegal seizure does not acquire? One possibility is that even if the creditor does not acquire the pledge fully for purposes such as Shemittah, he need not immediately return it merely because the prohibited act was ineffective. He may still hold it as security until the debt is paid. Rashba's language that what was done remains done may point to this. Tosafot, however, implies that if the general rule "if he did it, it has no effect" applied, even holding the object for the debt would fail and it would have to be returned. It therefore appears that once Scripture revealed in the same prohibition that unauthorized seizure can create a pledge for a poor debtor, the legal effect extends to the rich debtor as well. The same prohibition cannot be treated as effective in one branch and entirely ineffective in another without a textual basis. Thus the creditor acquires the pledge even from the wealthy debtor despite the absence of the return command.
