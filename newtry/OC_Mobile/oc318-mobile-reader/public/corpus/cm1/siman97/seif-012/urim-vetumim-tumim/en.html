But if the creditor took other items as collateral, etc. It requires explanation where the Rama derived this rule. The Rashba responsum cited by the Beit Yosef says that we rule in Temurah that according to everyone an unlawful act is effective where the Torah itself connected the prohibition to a positive command, "you shall surely return it." Rabbenu Yerucham likewise says only that if the creditor seized the item, the seizure is effective. But with respect to items to which the duty of return does not apply, the positive command of return is absent. We should then return to the general rule that an act done in violation of the Torah is ineffective. Why should the collateral acquisition stand? Perhaps the Rashba's reasoning is as follows. Otherwise, the Gemara could have identified a practical difference between Abaye and Rava in the case of collateral that does not require periodic return. According to Rava, the creditor would have to return it because the unlawful taking is ineffective, while according to Abaye he would not. Since the Gemara does not give this distinction, it follows that the creditor's acquisition is effective in all forms of collateral. Once the Torah reveals, with respect to items that must be returned when needed, that the original taking is effective and the creditor need return the item only at the appropriate times, Scripture thereby reveals its intention that conduct violating this particular prohibition can nevertheless create a valid pledge. We may therefore infer that even where there is no periodic duty of return, the creditor's acquisition remains effective. Now. The Rambam likewise indicates that the creditor does not receive lashes. The Mishneh LaMelech asks why.
If the creditor takes collateral from a wealthy debtor, the positive command to return the pledge when needed does not presently apply, so why is the prohibition considered one redirected to a positive command? It is not enough to say that because the same prohibition is redirected to a positive command in cases involving a poor debtor, it is never punishable by lashes. The Rambam rules concerning substitution of sacred property that an individual who makes a temurah receives lashes, even though Scripture says that both the original and its substitute become sacred. The Rambam's reason is that when a congregation makes a substitution, the substitute does not become sacred, so in that setting the prohibition is not redirected to a positive command. Thus, where one application of a prohibition is not redirected to a positive command, lashes can still apply even in another application where a positive consequence exists. That appears to be the reverse of what would be needed here. The Mishneh LaMelech's question from the case of a wealthy debtor can be answered. If the debtor was wealthy when the collateral was taken but later became poor, the creditor must then return the item when needed. Ultimately, it is now the debtor's garment for his skin, with what else will he sleep? Conversely, according to Rabbi Shimon, who holds that one may take collateral from a wealthy widow but not from a poor widow because returning the pledge repeatedly could give her a bad reputation among her neighbors, if she was wealthy when the collateral was taken and later became poor, the creditor still does not return it, because the repeated visits would now create that same bad reputation. This is the meaning of the Tosefta, also cited in the Yerushalmi: Rabbi Shimon says that one may not take collateral from a poor widow, but may take it from a wealthy widow and does not return it to her because doing so could give her a bad reputation among her neighbors. At first glance this is puzzling. A wealthy debtor is not entitled to periodic return in any event, as the verse says, "and if he is a poor man." Why does Rabbi Shimon need the additional reason of a bad reputation? According to what was explained, the Tosefta means that even if the wealthy widow later becomes poor, the creditor still does not return the pledge, because repeated returns would then create the very bad reputation the Torah sought to avoid. Thus "one may take from a wealthy widow and does not return it" means even if she later becomes poor. Accordingly, there is no difficulty from the wealthy debtor. Even while he is wealthy, the prohibition remains within the category of a prohibition redirected to a positive command, because the collateral is constantly subject to a potential future obligation of return if the debtor becomes poor. By contrast, where a congregation makes a substitution, there is no possibility that the act will ever become subject to the positive consequence that the substitute becomes sacred, because a communal substitution never takes effect. In that case the prohibition is genuinely not redirected to a positive command. This answer does not suffice, however, for collateral that is itself excluded from the law of return, such as implements used to prepare food, if one unlawfully took them. There the positive command of return does not apply at all, so the Rambam should at least have explained that lashes are administered in such cases. The Mishneh LaMelech's basic difficulty therefore remains. It seems that one must distinguish between two kinds of prohibitions redirected to a positive command. Where the positive consequence occurs automatically, as with temurah, there is room to say stringently that because in one application the prohibition is not redirected to a positive command, lashes remain possible even in the application where the positive consequence occurs. There is no contradiction in both effects applying, because no one must tell the offender to perform an act. The original and substitute are automatically sacred through Heaven's decree. Where, however, the positive command requires beit din to tell the offender, "Arise and do this," it is impossible to administer lashes while simultaneously directing him to perform the corrective act. The prohibition therefore cannot carry lashes in the cases where the positive command applies. Once the Torah framed the prohibition in that manner, we do not divide a single prohibition into halves by imposing lashes in some applications where the corrective command happens not to apply. Either the prohibition as a whole is one for which lashes are imposed, or it is not. Here, because in its ordinary application we direct the creditor to return the collateral, lashes are excluded from the prohibition as a whole, even in an instance where no actual command to return applies. Tosafot in Chullin 81a, in the passage beginning "leave it," make this distinction between the rule of temurah and an ordinary prohibition redirected to a positive command. They write that temurah is not a complete example of such a prohibition because the sanctity of the substitute takes effect without any corrective act by the offender. Their reasoning accords with what was explained. In any event, the point stated above is correct: if the debtor was wealthy and later became poor, the creditor must return the collateral at the times of need, as appears from the Yerushalmi.
