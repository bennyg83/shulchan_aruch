A widow. Sema writes that the same applies to a divorced woman, and Taz writes that it applies even to an unmarried woman. See Tumim. Heaven forbid that one add to or alter even the smallest point of the Torah's wording. The prohibition applies specifically to a widow and to no other woman.
Not at the time of the loan. This excludes Rambam's view that even at the time of the loan one may not take collateral from her. The ruling teaches otherwise, because every 'taking of collateral' mentioned in the verse refers to taking it after the loan. See Tumim for what I wrote regarding Rambam.
And if, etc. Necessarily this refers to the time when the creditor would otherwise be required to return the collateral, not immediately after taking it, as explained above.
If she admits, she pays, and if she denies, she swears. Sema asks: if the creditor lacks witnesses that he took the collateral and witnesses who later saw it in his possession, why is the widow believed merely by swearing? The creditor has a migo that he could have denied taking it altogether, as the Gemara says explicitly concerning one who took a food preparation utensil as collateral. Even though he must return it, he has a migo that he could have said, 'It never happened,' or, 'I returned it.' If there are witnesses who saw it as well, then it is obvious that she is believed. Shach cites Rivash's answer: one might have thought that because the Torah had special compassion for a widow and prohibited taking collateral from her, the creditor likewise may not compel payment from her, and certainly may not impose an oath when she denies the debt. The ruling therefore teaches that in all other respects her law is the same as anyone else's. If she owes money she must pay and may be compelled, and if she incurs an oath obligation there is no distinction. Baal HaTerumos and Gidulei Terumah do not explain Rambam this way. See Tumim, where I gave several explanations. All may be correct in practical law. In particular, I wrote that one who took collateral from a wealthy widow and is not required to return it is not believed to say that he returned it, for why should we assume that he returned it when he had no obligation to do so? I also wrote that because he becomes disqualified from swearing through the transgression, he cannot claim, 'She owes me this amount,' through a migo, for he would need to take a Mishnah oath. See there, where the practical law is clear.
If the collateral was lost or burned, etc. He receives lashes and the value of the collateral is credited against the debt even if it was lost through an unavoidable accident. Since he took it unlawfully, he acquired responsibility for it and becomes liable even for accidents. The same applies where he unlawfully takes food preparation utensils or collateral from any widow, according to our ruling that the prohibition applies only after the loan. See Tumim. I wrote that if he himself destroyed or burned the collateral, there is reason to say that he must purchase a replacement utensil with his money and give it to the borrower in order to fulfill the positive commandment of return. See there for my reconciliation of Rambam. Many authorities disagree and hold that there are no lashes. This has no practical difference for us today, when lashes are not administered due to our sins. He nevertheless becomes disqualified even if there are no lashes, because this is a prohibition involving wrongful taking, as explained above in siman 34.
But from a guarantor. Scripture says 'his house,' excluding the house of a guarantor. Rambam gives an additional reason from the verse, 'Take his garment, for he guaranteed a stranger.' This is because one might otherwise interpret 'his house' as excluding wages and similar obligations that never arose from a loan. A guarantor, however, is connected to a loan, and one might think the prohibition applies. Therefore Scripture provides an allusion that even a guarantor is excluded. Once the verse is shown to exclude a guarantor, certainly wages and similar obligations that involve no loan at all are excluded. Gidulei Terumah nevertheless leaves Rambam difficult.
And some say nevertheless, etc. They hold that the prohibition concerning food preparation utensils is a separate prohibition and contains no exclusion for a guarantor. According to them, the same would apply to taking a widow's utensil where she became a guarantor, because that too is a separate prohibition. See Tumim. I wrote that this opinion appears to hold that the positive commandment to return collateral does not apply to a guarantor, because the verse 'you shall surely return it' does not refer to him. Nevertheless, if the creditor unlawfully took a food preparation utensil from a guarantor, he need not return it immediately on the ground that an unlawful act cannot take legal effect. See there.
Not to take a utensil, etc. See Tumim. I wrote that the same applies to a treasurer collecting for consecrated property or for the poor, mentioned above in seif 12. Although the requirement to return collateral does not apply to him, he may not take food preparation utensils or a widow's utensils. If he did take them, however, he need not return them. This requires reflection regarding the practice in some communities of taking collateral for taxes and entering homes. Entry may perhaps be permitted because the collector resembles a treasurer, or because this resembles entering the house of a guarantor. But the practice of taking food preparation utensils and a widow's utensils deserves serious attention. Who permitted what the Torah forbids to everyone? It is strained to say that the collectors act as agents of the king to collect royal taxes, and that royal law allows all property to be seized, comparable to Rosh's responsum permitting seizure of the debtor's person for taxes under the law of the kingdom. Not all taxes are for the king personally, and one cannot compare separate Torah prohibitions without a compelling source in the Gemara and authorities. Due to our sins, these matters are often entrusted to communal leaders who are not learned in Torah and do not seek the guidance of halachic authorities. Distorted judgment therefore results. May Hashem have mercy upon His people and restore our judges as at first and our advisers as at the beginning.
