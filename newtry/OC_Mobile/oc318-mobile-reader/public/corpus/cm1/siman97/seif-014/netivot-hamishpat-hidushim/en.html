If he transgressed and took a pledge, he must return it against his will. This means when the borrower needs the article, not immediately:
If you admit, you must pay. If you deny, you must swear. The Sema asks: if there are no witnesses that he took the item and no witnesses who saw it in his possession, why is she believed only with an oath? He has a migo that he could deny the taking entirely, as the Gemara says concerning utensils used for preparing food. Although he is obligated to return such a pledge, he nevertheless has a migo that he could say, "It never happened," or, "I returned it." And if there are witnesses who saw the item, then it is obvious that she is believed. The Shach answers that one might have thought that since the Torah has compassion upon a widow and forbids taking her pledge, she likewise cannot be compelled to pay, and certainly cannot be required to swear when she denies. The ruling teaches otherwise:
He is flogged. The value of the pledge is credited against the debt even if it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. Since he seized it unlawfully, he acquires responsibility for it and is liable even for unavoidable loss. The same applies if he seized utensils used for preparing food or a widow's utensils, according to our law that the prohibition applies only when he takes the pledge not at the time of the loan:
But to retain it. For it is written, "to his house," but not to the house of a guarantor:
And some say, nevertheless. This is an independent prohibition and contains no exclusion for a guarantor. The same applies to one who takes a widow's utensil as a pledge where she became a guarantor:
Not to take a utensil as a pledge. The same applies to a treasurer collecting for consecrated property or for the poor. Although such a collector need not return the pledge periodically, he may not seize utensils used for preparing food or a widow's utensils. If he did seize them, however, he need not return them. One should therefore consider the practice in some communities of taking pledges for taxes and entering people's homes. Entering the home may perhaps be permitted because the collector is like a communal treasurer or because it is comparable to entering the house of a guarantor. But as for taking utensils used for preparing food or a widow's utensils, one must seriously ask who permitted this, for the prohibition applies to everyone. It is difficult to justify it by treating the collector as the king's agent collecting royal taxes under a law that authorizes seizure of all property.
