A widow. The Sema writes that the same applies to a divorced woman, and the Taz adds that the same applies to an unmarried woman. I say that once we begin adding categories to what the Torah expressly stated, there is no end. I too could say that if a woman's husband traveled overseas and she is left alone, one should not take collateral from her, for all the more so might repeated visits give her a bad reputation among her neighbors while she remains a married woman. But Heaven forbid that we alter even the smallest detail of the Torah's wording. The central point is that a widow's tears are upon her cheeks and she weeps over her broken state. In our sins, her condition resembles the destruction of our glorious House. See Ramban to Parashat Mishpatim on the verse "you shall not afflict any widow," where he explains that the Torah had special compassion for a widow, and Hashem is close to the brokenhearted. Therefore the first Tanna holds that one may not take collateral from any widow. Rabbi Shimon holds that if this special compassion were the sole consideration, we should likewise refrain from collecting from her property altogether. Rather, with respect to monetary collection itself we do not extend the exemption. The prohibition is because the repeated taking and returning of collateral may create a bad reputation among her neighbors, causing renewed anguish and tears. The Torah was especially solicitous of the widow's grief in that circumstance. But Heaven forbid that we add other categories to the verse. This is especially true of a divorced woman, concerning whom the Torah permits the husband to remove the marital bond. How can we invent a new exemption out of compassion that the Torah did not state? Rabbi Shimon himself did not uproot the verse from its plain meaning. He interpreted "the garment of a widow" as referring to a poor widow whose garment is her only covering. Indeed, Riaz holds that only her clothing may not be taken as collateral. In any event, one must not depart from the verse's meaning or add a category that Chazal did not receive and derive through the thirteen interpretive principles transmitted to them, with the spirit of Hashem within them. See Shach, seif katan 1.
Not at the time of the loan. The Rambam writes that taking collateral from a widow is prohibited even at the time of the loan, while many authorities disagree. The Maggid Mishneh brings proof for the dissenting view from Rabbi Shimon, who explains the prohibition as preventing the creditor from giving the widow a bad reputation among her neighbors. That concern does not apply to collateral taken at the moment of the loan, because the law of periodic return does not apply there. From Rabbi Shimon, the Maggid Mishneh infers the same limit for the first Tanna, since they need not be assumed to dispute that separate issue. According to his reasoning, when he states categorically that collateral taken at the time of the loan is not subject to periodic return, this must mean only in the absence of the special prohibition. The Rambam himself writes that if one did take the widow's garment he must return it against his will, meaning at the time of her need, as the Sema explains and as stated above. The fact that lashes apply only if the item was lost or burned likewise shows that the prohibition is redirected to the positive command of return. The Maggid Mishneh's intent must therefore be that since the Torah prohibited taking this collateral, although ordinarily the transaction would have been effective, Scripture imposed a duty to return it when needed. Had the Torah not prohibited the taking in the first place, there would have been no basis for applying the law of periodic return to this collateral. Thus one may ask why the Torah should prohibit it at the time of the loan and thereby create a return obligation when, absent that prohibition, there would be no repeated visits and no bad reputation. This is the Maggid Mishneh's proof. Even so, his argument is not clear to me. The Ramah, cited by the Rama in this seif, holds that one may not take food preparation implements as collateral from a guarantor. This indicates that the exclusion derived from "you shall not enter his house," which allows entry into the house of a guarantor, concerns only the prohibition on entering the debtor's house, while the separate prohibition "you shall not take the millstones" is not excluded and applies even to a guarantor. If so, the same should be true of "you shall not take a widow's garment." Since the permission to enter a guarantor's house does not eliminate other collateral prohibitions, a widow who is a guarantor would still be protected. The difficulty according to Rabbi Shimon remains. He bases the prohibition on the concern for a bad reputation. Yet according to the Tur's position, cited later, collateral taken from a guarantor is not subject to periodic return. If a widow serves as guarantor, Rabbi Shimon's rationale would permit taking her collateral, while the first Tanna would prohibit it. That would create a new dispute between them not mentioned by the Gemara. Likewise, if a woman owes money to another woman, Rabbi Shimon's concern for suspicion of immoral conduct may not apply. The same would be true if a son takes collateral from his mother. Rabbi Shimon would permit these cases while the first Tanna would prohibit them, again creating new disputes. It therefore appears clear that all of these cases are included in the single dispute itself. In his commentary to the Mishnah, the Rambam explains the first Tanna's reason: taking collateral from a widow cannot avoid producing some dispute or interaction that may generate suspicion or other harm. Rabbi Shimon holds that the concern applies only where the collateral must repeatedly be returned. Thus the Gemara's terms "wealthy" and "poor" are classifications of whether the law of return applies. A wealthy widow represents every case where there is no duty of periodic return, including a guarantor or collateral taken at the time of the loan. A poor widow represents every case where periodic return applies. The first Tanna prohibits even where there is no return obligation, while Rabbi Shimon limits the prohibition to situations involving return. It is all one dispute, and this is clear. The Maggid Mishneh's formulation is therefore not fully persuasive to me. The Rambam's own explanation also requires clarification. He writes that the reason is that taking collateral from a widow cannot avoid conflict and dealings that may cause her a bad reputation. Yet the Gemara says that Rabbi Shimon is the one who interprets the rationale of the verse, implying that the first Tanna does not base his ruling upon a stated rationale. The Lechem Mishneh already raises this difficulty, noting that the Gemara compares the dispute to Rabbi Shimon's treatment of the prohibition against a king multiplying wives. It seems that the Rambam understands the dispute about interpreting the reason of a verse in a more precise way. Certainly no mitzvah is without a reason. The Rambam explains in Moreh Nevuchim that the reasons of many mitzvot operate according to the majority of cases. There can be exceptional cases to which the reason does not apply, but the Torah legislates according to the general rule. This is the position of the authority who does not derive legal exceptions from the reason of the verse. Once the rationale applies to the majority, we do not exempt the minority merely because that rationale is absent there. The Rambam's explanation that conflict cannot always be avoided does not literally apply to every widow and every form of collateral. If a person takes a specified item from a modest woman under a clear written agreement witnessed by others, there may be no conflict or suspicion whatsoever. Nevertheless, because the first Tanna does not derive exceptions from the rationale, the Torah's prohibition applies to all widows once its purpose generally applies in most cases. Rabbi Shimon, however, does derive law from the reason of the verse. If he can identify a category to which the stated concern does not apply, he excludes that category from the prohibition. He therefore reasons that Scripture's phrase "the garment of a widow" refers specifically to a poor widow whose garment is her only covering and who is entitled to repeated return of the pledge. In such a case every widow is exposed to the concern of a bad reputation because the creditor repeatedly comes to return and reclaim the garment. A wealthy widow, where no return is required, is excluded. The same model applies to a king multiplying wives. Even righteous women such as Abigail would not turn his heart away, but most wives may do so, and "a woman of valor, who can find." The first Tanna therefore applies the general prohibition to all additional wives. Rabbi Shimon, who interprets the rationale as defining the law, permits the exceptional case where the concern does not apply. This resolves the matter.
If she denies, etc. See what I wrote in Urim explaining the Rivash. His interpretation is strained, and Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 49, part 4, law 26, does not understand the Rambam that way. After proving from the Gemara's case of the man who seized a knife, which was an implement used for food preparation, that the creditor nevertheless has a migo that he could have denied the seizure entirely or claimed that he returned it, Baal HaTerumot proceeds to clarify the law of one who takes a widow's garment. He writes that it appears equivalent to the law of a food preparation implement, since there is no basis to distinguish them. Yet the Rambam writes concerning a widow that if she admits, she pays, and if she denies, she swears. Baal HaTerumot therefore understood the Rambam differently from the Rivash, and that the Rambam stated this rule specifically in the case of a widow. This is difficult, because the Rivash's explanation is exceedingly strained. According to Baal HaTerumot's understanding of the Rambam, perhaps the Rambam is speaking of a wealthy widow, from whom the creditor is not presently obligated to return the pledge. When the Rambam says that he must return it against his will, this means only when it later becomes needed if she becomes poor, as explained above. Before that time there is no obligation to return. The case could therefore be one where the creditor took the pledge in the presence of witnesses but there were no witnesses who continuously observed it. Ordinarily he would have a migo that he returned it. Here, however, such a migo is unavailable, because there is no present obligation to return it and the situation resembles a debt that is not yet due. Since the creditor deliberately violated the prohibition and took a widow's pledge in order to retain security, why should we assume that he returned it when there was no reason to do so? The migo is therefore weak. Even according to the authority who accepts a migo against a chazakah, this may be a particularly strong chazakah. Alternatively, since the pledge remains presumptively the widow's property, the creditor is the one seeking to collect through it and therefore certainly cannot rely on a migo without an oath. This would explain why the Rambam stated the rule in the case of a widow, whereas a food preparation implement is subject to return. In truth, however, all the commentators write that the Rambam began with a widow merely because she was the first case under discussion, but his language governs all the prohibited pledges mentioned afterward. Moreover, the suggested reading that his unqualified statement refers only to a wealthy widow is strained. There is another possible explanation based on the Rambam's general position that one who committed robbery in the presence of witnesses must return the stolen property in the presence of witnesses. The Maggid Mishneh understands this to be the Rambam's view, and the Mabit and his son Maharit explain the rationale: because the offender committed the theft openly and thereby established himself as a wrongdoer, when he returns the property he must do so before witnesses in order to establish that he corrected the wrong. According to this, one who forcibly seizes a food preparation implement or the like is certainly a full robber. As the Shach writes, everyone agrees that in such a case he bears liability even for unavoidable loss. He therefore resembles one who robbed in the presence of witnesses and must return in the presence of witnesses. Indeed, that rule itself is stated concerning the famous case of Rabbi Abba's ingot. The authorities who adopt it hold that even though there were no witnesses who continuously saw the object, the defendant has no migo that he returned it, because the original snatching occurred before witnesses. The same would apply here. There would be no migo unless the pledge was taken at the time of the loan. The Rambam holds that even taking a widow's garment or a food preparation implement at the time of the loan violates a prohibition, but since the debtor voluntarily delivered it, the creditor is not technically a robber. Still, the same rationale for requiring witnessed return may apply. Since he openly violated the prohibition by taking the widow's garment, his eventual return is the act that repairs his wrongdoing, and he should establish that return before witnesses. He therefore lacks the migo of having returned it. The Gemara's case involving Rava's seizure of a knife must then have involved no witnesses at all, so that the defendant retained the migo that the seizure never occurred. The difficulty with this explanation is that the Shulchan Aruch should not have copied the Rambam's ruling without qualification, because as explained above in siman 90 and below in the laws of robbery, the Mechaber does not rule that one who steals in the presence of witnesses must also return in the presence of witnesses. Therefore. One of two explanations must be adopted. The first is that the Rambam is not discussing admission and denial of the underlying debt at all. That is an independent matter, and it is obvious that the creditor cannot collect a disputed debt without proof. Rather, the debt itself is clearly established by witnesses or a document. The Rambam says that the creditor must return the forbidden pledge, and the parties dispute whether he in fact returned it. The creditor says that he returned the pledge, while the widow denies receiving it. Although the creditor holds the debt instrument, because it is known that he took collateral and they dispute its return, the borrower is believed with an oath that the pledge was not returned, and the creditor loses the amount represented by the pledge. Thus, "if she admits" means that she admits he returned the pledge and she must pay, while "if she denies" means that she denies return, swears, and thereby defeats his collection. This accords with the Tur's ruling below in seif 21 in the name of Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat. See what I write there. The second possibility is that the Rambam follows the Geonim who hold that a creditor who asserts, "the pledge is worth only this amount of my debt," and relies on a migo, is one of those who swear and collect. He therefore requires the oath imposed by the Mishnah, as in the case of one who rents utensils and the like. This is well known. The Rambam rules above in siman 84 and siman 92 that whenever a person who ordinarily swears and collects is disqualified from taking an oath, the opposing party swears and is exempt. A creditor who violated a Torah prohibition by taking forbidden collateral becomes suspect and cannot take the Mishnah's oath. He therefore loses the benefit of his migo, and the opposing party swears and is exempt. This is a significant novelty in the Rambam: even though the creditor would otherwise have a migo, if the widow denies liability she herself swears and is exempt because he is disqualified from swearing. As for Rava's case, either there were no witnesses to the unlawful seizure, and a person does not become disqualified merely by his own admission of wrongdoing, or, according to the view above that the opposing litigant may still say, "you admitted that you are disqualified, so I will swear instead," the seizure there was inadvertent. Tosafot explain that the entire incident occurred through error because the creditor did not know that taking a food preparation implement was prohibited. He therefore was not rendered suspect. "Who can understand errors?" This explanation appears correct as practical law.
If the pledge was lost or burned, he receives lashes. The Rambam writes this concerning one who takes a widow's garment and one who takes a food preparation implement, and also concerning a creditor who enters the debtor's house to seize collateral. In the first two cases, however, he does not say that the creditor calculates the value of the pledge and then claims the balance of the debt. In the case of entry into the debtor's house, he does write that the creditor calculates the pledge's value. This is strong proof for the Shach's position that where the creditor entered the debtor's house and forcibly took the pledge, his liability for unavoidable loss arises because he is a robber. He acquires liability for the object and becomes responsible even for accidents. By contrast, the first two cases include collateral taken at the time of the loan with the debtor's consent. There the creditor is not a robber and has no general liability for unavoidable loss, so the Rambam does not say that he deducts the pledge's value from the debt. One may add that according to the view above in siman 72 that Rabbi Yosef's perutah benefit for a pledge does not arise from the duty to air and care for it, but specifically at the moment of the loan when the lender performs a mitzvah and is thereby exempt from giving a coin to a poor person, even that rationale may not apply here. A lender who is simultaneously violating a Torah prohibition by taking forbidden collateral cannot claim the mitzvah benefit as the basis for acquiring greater responsibility. His gain is offset by his transgression. The commentators challenge the Rambam's ruling from the Gemara in Makkot. The Gemara says that because the creditor is liable to pay for the pledge, the prohibition remains one redirected to a positive command, or alternatively that one cannot both receive lashes and make payment. The matter can be resolved according to our text and Tosafot's explanation that the payment obligation preserves the corrective character of the command. Asifat Zekenim cites Tosafot of Sens, who answer Tosafot's question about why one who takes the upper and lower millstones can receive two sets of lashes despite the apparent positive command of return. They write that the payment obligation does not necessarily repair the prohibition. The creditor may simply deduct the value of the destroyed millstones from the outstanding debt. He is not obligated to hand the debtor money with which to buy replacement millstones so that the debtor can again prepare food. Whether the creditor retains the original pledge or merely retains its value through setoff, the debtor has still lost an implement needed for his livelihood. In that sense, the duty of return has not been fulfilled. This is an excellent conceptual point, but the commentators object that the Gemara in Makkot itself says that because the creditor is liable for payment, the prohibition remains corrected through the positive command. The answer must be that according to Rava, who holds that any act performed contrary to the Torah is ineffective, where Scripture commanded return and the creditor deliberately defeats that command by destroying or selling the pledge, his attempt to frustrate the Torah's instruction cannot be legally effective. He must therefore use the value to procure a replacement food implement so that the return command can still be fulfilled. It is possible that even Abaye would agree in this setting, because otherwise every person could evade the corrective command by selling or breaking the pledge. At least according to Rava, however, this is certain. That reasoning applies where the creditor himself deliberately destroys the pledge. If an accident occurs and he merely becomes monetarily liable because, as a robber, he bears responsibility for accidents, he did not perform any new prohibited act designed to defeat the Torah's command. Rava's rule that an unlawful act is ineffective therefore has no application. It is enough that the pledge's value is deducted from the debt, and the creditor need not purchase another implement. Ramban in Milchamot Hashem, in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, writes that the Makkot sugya follows the authority who holds that lashes depend upon actively nullifying the positive command. Even according to the Shach's reading of the Rambam that liability for the pledge can exist according to the authority who focuses on whether the positive command was fulfilled, because the creditor acquired accident liability, the Rambam must in any event interpret the Gemara in light of the distinction between active nullification and mere failure of fulfillment. The Rambam holds that taking a widow's garment or a food preparation implement at the time of the loan also violates the prohibition and is redirected to a positive command. Yet if such consensually delivered collateral is accidentally lost, the creditor is not liable for accidents, as explained above. The Gemara's inability to find a case of lashes therefore necessarily follows the authority who requires active nullification, because an accidental loss is not active nullification. Accordingly, the Rambam can adopt Tosafot of Sens. Where the pledge is accidentally lost or burned and we rule according to the authority who considers failure to fulfill the command sufficient for lashes, the creditor receives lashes because merely deducting the value from the debt is not actual return. By contrast, the Makkot sugya follows the authority who requires active nullification. Its case is one where the creditor himself burns or destroys the pledge. In that case Rava's rule makes the deliberate frustration ineffective, so the creditor must replace the item and the positive command can still be fulfilled. The Gemara therefore says that payment keeps the prohibition within the corrective framework. This resolves Tosafot's question concerning the Mishnah's statement that one can receive two or three sets of lashes for taking forbidden collateral. There is no need to say that the prohibitions are not redirected to a positive command, which would create the earlier difficulty why the Gemara in Temurah does not identify these cases as differences between Abaye and Rava. The Mishnah can follow the authority who holds that failure to fulfill the corrective command generates lashes, and the Rambam understands that standard differently from Tosafot in Makkot. For the Rambam, the person does not receive lashes merely because beit din warned him to return the pledge and he delayed. Lashes apply only when fulfillment has become impossible, such as where the pledge was accidentally burned. So long as return remains possible, there are no lashes. This also answers the Kesef Mishneh's question in Hilchot Shechitah, chapter 13, why the Rambam rules according to the authority who focuses on fulfillment rather than active nullification, since Rabbi Yochanan appears to hold the latter view. The entire dispute between Abaye and Rava about whether unlawful acts take effect fits naturally with the authority who focuses on fulfillment, and halachah follows their analysis, which supports the alternative ruling. It is even more likely, however, that the Rambam had the older text of Makkot recorded by Rivash and other authorities. According to that text, the Gemara answers simply that since monetary payment applies, we do not impose both lashes and payment. That reading is preferable. The version that calls the payment itself fulfillment of the positive command is difficult in light of Tosafot of Sens, because the creditor is not literally required to buy a replacement implement. The older text was apparently also the Raavad's version, as suggested by his gloss. Tosafot ask that if both lashes and payment are available, we ordinarily say that the offender receives lashes and does not pay. But that principle applies where Scripture has not otherwise revealed a preference for monetary restoration. Here the Torah explicitly reveals that it wants restoration by redirecting the prohibition to "you shall surely return it." Once the corrective command can no longer be performed, payment is therefore more appropriate than lashes. This resembles the rule that Scripture specially increased the liability of false witnesses toward monetary payment. Here too Scripture indicated its preference for restoring the debtor's loss. This is especially compelling according to what was explained that the creditor who forcibly seized the pledge is a robber. A robber ordinarily pays rather than receiving lashes, and it would be unreasonable for the borrower to lose the value of his pledge merely because the creditor receives corporal punishment. Now. Rashi explains the dispute between "he fulfilled it or did not fulfill it" and "he nullified it or did not nullify it" as follows. According to the first view, the prohibition is violated immediately, so a warning can take effect at the moment of the act and is not merely a doubtful warning. Although the offender may later perform the corrective command, until he does so the prohibition remains operative and the warning attaches. According to the second view, liability depends upon a later act of nullification. Until the person actively destroys the possibility of correction, the original prohibition does not ripen into lashes. The warning is therefore initially uncertain. This distinction yields another practical difference. Liability to pay for the pledge arises only when the pledge is lost or burned. Tosafot and the other authorities explain this in the discussion of a borrowed and rented animal: the obligation crystallizes at the time of the loss, which is why a person may be uncertain whether the item was borrowed or rented in a manner analogous to one who does not know whether he ever incurred a loan. According to the view that liability to lashes depends upon failure to fulfill the positive command, the prohibition and warning attach from the original unlawful taking, while the monetary obligation arises only later when the pledge is destroyed. Lashes and payment therefore do not arise simultaneously, and the rule that one punishment excludes the other does not apply. The Maggid Mishneh makes precisely this point, that the liabilities do not arise at one moment. According to the view that lashes require active nullification, by contrast, the essential liability to lashes arises only when the offender destroys the pledge and thereby nullifies the corrective command. At that exact moment the monetary obligation for the pledge also arises. Lashes and payment therefore coincide, and the familiar rule that one who pays does not receive lashes, or one who receives lashes does not pay, becomes directly relevant. This explains why the Makkot sugya, which proceeds according to the active-nullification view, answers through the monetary liability. According to our ruling based on failure of fulfillment, the two liabilities arise at different times, so the Rambam can hold that the creditor both pays and receives lashes. This also makes the dispute between Abaye and Rava fit the authority who focuses on fulfillment. Nor is there any difficulty from the Mishnah stating that one who takes the millstones violates two separate prohibitions. According to what was explained, the absence of lashes in the Makkot discussion results only from the principle that payment and lashes coincide. The Mishnah can follow Rabbi Meir, as anonymous Mishnayot often do, and Rabbi Meir holds that a person can both pay and receive lashes. There is therefore no difficulty. This entire explanation is clear, and it removes the need to assume that the Rambam possessed a different text, as the Maggid Mishneh suggested.
Some say that nevertheless, with respect to taking food preparation implements as collateral, etc. This is somewhat difficult. If so, what is the Gemara's question against Shmuel, who holds that even an agent of beit din may not enter the debtor's house? Scripture says, "he shall not take the millstones as collateral," and likewise, "he shall not take a widow's garment." The Gemara infers that other items may be taken. Why not say that these separate prohibitions are needed specifically in cases where taking ordinary collateral is otherwise permitted, such as from a guarantor and the like? If the Maharshal's interpretation were sustainable, that the baraita itself should be read as expressly implying "other items may be taken," the difficulty could be resolved because the contradiction would arise from the baraita rather than from the verse. But Maharsha rejects that reading forcefully, and his position is correct. Suppose the Ramah held that a guarantor and similar persons are not subject to the mitzvah of returning a pledge, because the command of return is attached to the prohibition "you shall not enter his house," whereas that prohibition is relaxed with respect to a guarantor, as the Tur writes later. Then another problem arises. According to Rava, who holds that an act done in violation of the Torah is ineffective, one who takes a forbidden food implement from a guarantor should have to return it immediately. One could not say that the prohibition was redirected to a positive command, because the return command would not apply to a guarantor. Nor can one say that this really is the law, because then the Gemara in Temurah should have listed this as a practical difference between Abaye and Rava. One would have to answer that the prohibition against taking food preparation implements also applies to an ordinary borrower, and in that ordinary case it is connected to the positive command of return. Scripture thereby revealed that an unlawful taking under this prohibition can nevertheless create a valid pledge. Once the Torah reveals that principle for the borrower, it applies to the guarantor as well. Even though the guarantor is not personally subject to the return command, the Torah's treatment of this prohibition as one whose act remains effective extends to every application of the same prohibition. This is the reasoning explained above in seif katan 7. But this works only if the prohibition against taking a food implement is shared by both an ordinary borrower and a guarantor. If one were to say that the separate verse applies only to the guarantor, the original difficulty would return, because there would be no case within that prohibition where Scripture revealed through a positive command that the unlawful act remains effective. One could perhaps say, as Tosafot write in the passage beginning "he takes," that the Gemara in Temurah challenges Rava from ordinary practice. The present Gemara can similarly be understood as challenging from ordinary practice. We see that even with a guarantor the creditor does not have to return the pledge immediately. Why is that so? It must be because the special prohibition also applies to an ordinary borrower, where the Torah's return command reveals that the original taking remains legally effective. If so, the Gemara can properly ask why the special prohibition is needed for an ordinary borrower, since taking collateral is already prohibited there. This would resolve the matter. The difficulty is that the Ramah expressly holds that even a guarantor is subject to the return obligation in this context, so this particular reconciliation cannot be used. In any event, according to the Ramah, although Scripture excludes a guarantor from the prohibition on entering the debtor's house, it does not exclude him from the separate prohibition against taking a food preparation implement. The same should therefore be true of property taken for consecrated obligations or charity to the poor. There we say that the obligation of periodic return does not apply because Scripture says, "and it shall be righteousness for you," implying a private creditor to whom the act of return is credited. But that only excludes the positive command of return. The separate prohibition "he shall not take the millstones" has not been excluded. This is especially compelling because without the verse "and it shall be righteousness for you," we would have thought that even obligations of valuation require periodic return, through the verbal analogy of the word "assessment." If the Torah needed a special verse only to remove the return obligation, what basis is there to remove the independent prohibition against taking a food implement? Why should consecrated or charitable debts be more lenient than a guarantor? The Rama above, in seif 12, writes that this leniency applies specifically to food preparation implements, which at first glance sounds as though the collectors of consecrated or charitable funds may take even such items. That cannot be correct. Rather, the Rama must mean only that although taking the item is prohibited, it is not subject to periodic return because the positive command "you shall surely return it" does not apply there. The unlawful taking nevertheless remains legally effective for the same reason explained above regarding the guarantor: Scripture revealed within this prohibition that the act itself can take effect despite the transgression. The Rama's earlier statement must therefore concern only the absence of a return obligation, not permission to take the pledge initially.
