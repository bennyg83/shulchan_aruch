Rambam says that if the unlawfully seized pledge is burned, the creditor receives lashes, credits its value, and sues for the remainder. Raavad objects that one should not both receive lashes and pay. Maggid Mishneh explains Rambam: this prohibition is not like robbery. The Torah did not prohibit the act because the creditor takes another person's property without entitlement. He takes it on account of a genuine debt. The prohibition is a special decree preventing self help seizure. It is therefore not a prohibition fundamentally given over to monetary restitution like theft or robbery. At the time of seizure the creditor does not intend to steal the item but to hold it for the loan. When it is later lost, the debt is reduced by its value because that is the implied basis on which the pledge was taken. This proves that one who seizes a pledge without authority is not treated as an ordinary robber. Liability for unavoidable loss arises from the pledge relationship and its implied terms, not from robbery. Therefore if the pledge is worth more than the debt, the creditor is not automatically liable for the excess as a robber. This is contrary to the Shach's suggestion in siman 72 that unauthorized seizure creates robbery liability. Scripture itself treats the unlawfully seized object as an acquired pledge, since the law of returning a pledge and the effect on Shemittah apply. If it were stolen property, the creditor would have to restore it under the ordinary law of robbery rather than retain it as collateral.
The Sema asks why lashes apply when the pledge is lost or burned, since a prohibition linked to a positive command is often understood not to produce lashes even if the positive command is later unfulfilled. Rambam expressly rules otherwise in Hilchot Sanhedrin: for prohibitions linked to a positive command, such as taking the mother bird or leaving the corner of the field, one does not receive lashes if the positive command is fulfilled, but if it is not fulfilled he does. Rambam follows the version of the Gemara that applies the criterion of whether the positive command was fulfilled. Even where failure occurred through circumstances beyond one's control, lashes can follow under that version. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 177 likewise notes that Rambam follows this reading.
