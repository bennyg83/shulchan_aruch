(Seif 7, not for the stated purpose) It appears that if the borrower spends the money for the very purpose for which he requested the loan, there is no prohibition even if the investment is lost, because the lender advanced it on that understanding. If he uses it for a different purpose but the principal remains intact, there is likewise no prohibition, because the lender did not condition the loan exclusively upon that venture; he merely assumed that the borrower would ordinarily use it for the purpose disclosed. So long as the money remains secure, the lender does not care that the borrower departed from what he said. The prohibition exists only where the borrower departs from the stated purpose and loses the money in the different venture. “But one may enter the guarantor’s house to take security.” This is difficult. At the end of Get Pashut, cited below in siman 98, the Gemara states that one does not descend to the borrower’s property until first demanding payment from him, because a person’s property serves as his guarantor and a guarantor pays only after the borrower is first demanded. There can be no distinction there between land and movables; a judge surely may not turn movables over to the creditor before demand, and otherwise the Gemara should have distinguished them. How, then, can the verse be expounded, “his house, but not the guarantor’s house,” when the Torah has already prohibited taking security from property, which itself is also a guarantor? One cannot answer that the extra word can be used only for a personal guarantor, because the Gemara gives another derivation, “his house, but not for the wages of a porter or camel driver,” etc. It appears that the verse contains two redundancies. It should have said “to a house,” yet says “to his house.” We therefore derive two permissions: one within the law of a loan itself, namely entry into the guarantor’s house, and another outside the subject of a loan altogether, despite the verse’s phrase “any debt,” namely entry to collect wages owed to a porter or camel driver. This explains why the baraita introduces the second teaching as “the other side” rather than “another interpretation,” as is usual elsewhere. It means that there is a second derivation from a wholly different subject, not from the loan discussed by the verse. It also explains why the baraita did not combine the guarantor’s case with the cases of wages, but presented two separate applications of “house.”
