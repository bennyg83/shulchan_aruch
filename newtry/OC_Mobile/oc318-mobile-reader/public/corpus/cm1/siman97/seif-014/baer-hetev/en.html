Wealthy. The Sema writes that this implies that even a method of taking collateral that is permitted against another debtor, namely through an agent of beit din, is forbidden regarding a widow, even a wealthy widow, because women are generally of weaker disposition. Scripture mentions a widow because she has no helper or support from a husband or from her father, since she has already left his authority. The Sema extends this to a divorcee, excluding an unmarried daughter who remains under her father's authority. The Shach writes that this requires study. A widow is still compelled to pay; only the taking of collateral is prohibited. The reason therefore appears to be that one should not distress her because her heart is broken and anxious, and that reason does not apply to a divorcee. The Taz, however, holds that there is no distinction among a widow, a divorcee, and even an unmarried woman. Scripture speaks of the usual case, since widows commonly conduct business whereas unmarried daughters generally do not. He rejects the Sema's assertion that there is no prohibition where a daughter has a father and remains under his authority, and offers proof.
From him. The Sema writes that here too "against his will" does not mean immediately, but only when the debtor needs the item. See the Taz, who discusses this law at length.
She shall swear. It appears that the case is where the creditor took the collateral before witnesses and the witnesses also saw it in his possession. He therefore has no migo that he purchased it or returned it. Otherwise he would be believed through such a migo. Even though he is obligated to return the collateral, if he wished he could have denied taking it, and we could not have compelled him to return it. The Sema asks why it is necessary to say that if she denies the claim she must swear. The Shach answers that this teaches that although one may not take collateral from her, beit din still compels her to pay. Therefore, if she admits the debt she pays, and if she denies it she swears. Responsa Rivash 488 states likewise.
Receives lashes. The Rambam rules similarly where one took a food preparation utensil as collateral, and where a creditor himself unlawfully entered the debtor's house and took collateral. If the collateral still exists, however, the Rambam holds that he does not receive lashes because the prohibition is linked to the positive command to return it. The Sema writes that in Derishah he explained why lashes apply where the collateral was lost or burned, even though we generally rule that for a prohibition linked to a positive command, lashes do not apply merely because the positive command can no longer be fulfilled. See the Maggid Mishneh, and the matter requires study. The Raavad also objects for another reason, namely that because the creditor becomes liable for unavoidable loss, he should not both receive lashes and make monetary payment. Shach.
Wages. Likewise, if one stole property and the stolen item remains intact, or even if the robber disposed of it but its value was not converted into a loan obligation, the owner may personally seize collateral from him. So writes Sefer Yere'im, siman 273. Shach.
