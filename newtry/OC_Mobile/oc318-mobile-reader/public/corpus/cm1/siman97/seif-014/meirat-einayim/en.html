A widow, whether poor, etc. The verse states, "You shall not take a widow's garment as collateral." This implies that even what may be taken from another debtor through a beit din agent is forbidden from a widow, even if she is wealthy, because her strength is weakened in the manner of women and she has no husband or father upon whom to rely, having already left her father's authority. In my opinion the same applies to a divorcee, but not to an unmarried daughter still under her father's authority.
They return it from him against his will. As I wrote at the end of the previous seif, "against his will" does not mean immediately, but when she needs it.
If she denies, she must swear. In Hagahot Darkei Moshe I explained that this is specifically where he took the pledge before witnesses, so he has no migo that he purchased it, and they later saw it in his possession, so he has no migo that he returned it. In such a case even against an ordinary debtor he would not be believed to claim up to the value of the pledge. Otherwise he would be believed through a migo. Even though he is obligated to return the pledge when she needs it, he could have denied ever taking it and we would not have been able to compel its return. The Tur states this explicitly in siman 72 concerning food-preparation utensils and a widow's garment.
If the pledge was lost or burned, he is lashed. Rambam likewise rules this concerning food-preparation utensils and a creditor who entered the borrower's house unlawfully. The Mechaber mentions it here because Rambam presents this case first in chapter 3 of Hilchot Malveh. If the pledge still exists, there are no lashes because the prohibition is remedied by the positive command to return the pledge. I explained that food-preparation utensils and a widow's garment are included in this command of return. I also explained why lashes apply when the pledge is lost or burned even though, generally, violation of a prohibition remedied by a positive command does not incur lashes even when the positive command can no longer be fulfilled.
But from a guarantor it is permitted. The Gemara derives this from "you shall not enter his house": the Torah warned specifically against entering the borrower's house, not a guarantor's.
If he converted the wage into a loan, etc. Then it becomes an ordinary loan.
