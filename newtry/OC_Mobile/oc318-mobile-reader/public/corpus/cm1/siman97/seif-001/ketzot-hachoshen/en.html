Rashba, in responsum 18, cites Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat's explanation why one does not recite a blessing over several mitzvot such as gifts to the poor, lending, charity, and the grant to a Hebrew servant. The mitzvah is not entirely in the actor's control, because the recipient may refuse. A mitzvah that can be uprooted by another person's choice is not treated as fixed for purposes of a blessing. He derives this from the law of a rapist required to marry the woman, where the positive commandment does not override a prohibition because she can say, "I do not want him," and thereby eliminate the mitzvah. A difficulty arises from honoring parents. The Gemara needs a verse to teach that a father who tells his son to become impure or not return a lost object may not be obeyed. Why is a verse needed? A parent's honor can be waived, so according to the principle just stated the positive commandment should not be strong enough to override a prohibition in any event. The answer is that honoring parents is exceptional because parental honor is compared to the honor of Hashem. Tosafot therefore says there was reason to think it might override even a positive and negative command concerning impurity or returning lost property. That exceptional severity could also have led us to regard the mitzvah as sufficiently fixed despite the parent's power of waiver. Scripture therefore had to teach, "You shall fear every man his mother and father, and keep My Sabbaths," all are obligated in Hashem's honor. Once Scripture teaches that the comparison to Hashem's honor does not give honoring parents power to override these prohibitions, it returns to the ordinary category of a mitzvah that can be waived and therefore does not warrant a blessing. Tosafot similarly says that once the verse denies the stronger implication of the comparison, we do not use it even to override an ordinary prohibition.
