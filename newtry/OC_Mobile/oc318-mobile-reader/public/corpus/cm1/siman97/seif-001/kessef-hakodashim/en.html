Seif 2. (It is forbidden to press the borrower.) Concerning the prohibition, "You shall not be to him as a creditor," although this might appear to create a Torah level doubt whenever one is uncertain whether the borrower has means, there is a sound basis for leniency. The expression "as a creditor" denotes a creditor who presses severely, as in the verse describing the creditor coming to take the children captive, and similar verses. One might initially have thought that the essence of the Torah prohibition is only what is stated explicitly at the end of the passage, namely entering the borrower's house to take a pledge, while the Sages extended the idea and warned even against demanding payment when one knows that the borrower cannot pay. On that approach, their warning would be analogous to a rabbinic safeguard derived from the apparent reason of the verse. Yet this approach cannot stand, because all authorities agree that the prohibition of "You shall not be to him as a creditor" is itself one of the Torah prohibitions, distinct from interest and distinct from the prohibition against entering the borrower's house to take a pledge. Therefore, when the creditor knows, based upon a reliable assessment, that the borrower has nothing with which to pay, demanding payment is forbidden by Torah law. The further warning that the creditor should not even pass before the borrower in a way that causes him distress may be rabbinic, although perhaps that too is included in the Torah prohibition, since by doing so he presents himself to the borrower as a pressing creditor. In Torah prohibitions, the intent of the heart is material. Nevertheless, the prohibition applies only when the creditor knows with certainty that the borrower lacks means. This is indicated by the verse, "If you lend money to My people, to the poor person with you, you shall not be to him as a creditor." The expression "with you" implies that his poverty is established and known to you. Thus one need not treat every uncertainty as a Torah doubt requiring stringency. Had uncertainty itself sufficed, the word "poor" would have been enough. The addition of "with you" indicates that the prohibition is triggered only once his poverty is known. There is also a presumption that the borrower still possesses property or its value. A borrower is forbidden to dissipate his property in a manner that leaves him unable to pay, as Rambam rules. He therefore retains a presumption of proper conduct, together with the presumption that the property that came into his possession, or its equivalent value, remains available. Accordingly, the prohibition applies when the creditor knows that the borrower has no property at all, neither land nor movable property anywhere, according to the rules governing the debtor's order of payment. If, under those rules, he presently has nothing from which collection can be made, the creditor may not press him. The creditor may, however, bring him before beit din in order to conduct the formal debtor procedure. If the borrower then swears that he has nothing, the creditor may also require the continuing oath that he will pay when he later acquires means. This is not pressing him as a creditor, because he is not being forced to produce money at a time when he has none, nor is he being forced into harsh labor, and the demand is not mere harassment. It merely preserves the obligation to pay when he becomes able. The prohibition in its strict form appears to concern a loan. Debts arising from wages or similar obligations may not fall under this particular prohibition, although it is still proper to avoid causing needless distress.
