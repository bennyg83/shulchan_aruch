It is a positive commandment to lend to the poor, etc. The verse states in Parashat Mishpatim, “If you lend money to My people, to the poor person with you, you shall not be to him as a creditor.” Rashi, following the Gemara, explains that the word “if” here has the sense of “when” and imposes an obligation. The meaning is: when you lend your money, lend it to My people rather than to a gentile; among My people, give precedence to the poor; among the poor, give precedence to the poor person who is with you. In all cases, after lending, do not press the borrower forcefully if you know that he has no means to pay. Since the verse says simply “My people” and excludes only the gentile, it follows that the positive commandment applies even to lending to a wealthy person who temporarily needs funds, although the poor person takes precedence. In Parashat Re’eh the verse says, “If there will be among you a poor person, from one of your brothers, in one of your gates,” and the Sages likewise derive that the poor of one’s city take precedence. From “one of your brothers” they derive that a paternal brother takes precedence over a maternal brother, from which it follows that one’s relatives take precedence over others. This is not, as the Ir Shushan writes, derived from the verse in Yeshayahu, “Do not hide yourself from your own flesh,” because the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber are discussing the positive commandment of the Torah itself.
And it is a greater mitzvah than charity. So write the Rambam and Tur. A recipient of charity has already reached the point of needing to ask, meaning that if one does not lend to him he will have to turn to the charity administrators. A borrower, however, has not yet reached that stage. See the Perishah.
