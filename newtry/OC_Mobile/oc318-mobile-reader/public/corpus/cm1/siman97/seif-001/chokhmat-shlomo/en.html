Seif 1: It is a positive mitzvah to lend to the poor of Israel, etc. Note: See Rashi to Shevuot 39b, s.v. “it rests upon both of them,” who explains that both litigants are punished because the lender should have entrusted his money only to a trustworthy person. This implies that one is obligated to lend only to a person whom he knows to be trustworthy; otherwise there is no obligation to lend. One cannot say that Rashi’s explanation applies only to a deposit. If so, in a loan the oath would not be said to “rest upon both of them.” Yet the baraita states generally that in every judicial oath they say this warning, and Shulchan Aruch siman 87 rules accordingly. If the reason differed in loan cases, there would be no reason for Rashi to give the deposit rationale as the general explanation. It follows that the same principle applies to loans and that there is no obligation to lend to one whom the lender does not know to be trustworthy. In truth, Rashi’s explanation itself requires study. Why was he compelled to this reason? He could have said that the lender should have lent before witnesses, or, where repayment was the concern, should have lent by written document or stipulated, “Do not repay me except before witnesses.” The Sema in siman 87, seif katan 61, gives a different reason, namely that the parties should have reached an accommodation. It requires study why he did not cite Rashi’s explanation. Moreover, Rashi’s explanation remains difficult in cases of damages and similar claims, where there was no voluntary entrustment at all. Why then does the baraita include all judicial oaths in the warning that the oath rests upon both parties? The Sema’s rationale works more broadly. This matter requires study. Further in the same seif: Bach writes here, and his view is cited in Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 90, and in Ketzot HaChoshen here, that where there are several creditors, the creditor whose loan or property was the source of a particular asset takes priority in that asset. Bach derives this from Baal HaTerumot, cited by Beit Yosef here, concerning a married woman who borrowed, etc. In my opinion this requires great study. The two cases are not comparable. Baal HaTerumot speaks about a husband, who is not merely another creditor. His right is that of an heir or derives from the rule that what a wife acquires her husband acquires. The Sages gave him only what is truly hers, not property that still belongs to others. Property that belongs to another and remains identifiable is not inherited by him and was never granted to him by the marital enactment. His hand is no stronger than hers in this respect. Since she herself must return that property, he too must return it. Likewise, Bach’s citations from the Tur in simanim 108 and 280 concern an heir who seeks to inherit, not competing creditors. An heir does not stand in a better position than the debtor himself and cannot inherit that which is not truly the debtor’s. Where a debtor owes several creditors, however, money borrowed by him was given for expenditure and becomes his property. If he used that very money to betroth a woman, the betrothal would not be invalid on the ground that the money remained the lender’s. Once the money belongs to the borrower, it becomes subject to all creditors equally. There is therefore no comparison between the cases. With respect to creditors, the creditor’s lien can be stronger than the debtor’s own freedom to designate the property. The same question arises with land. If one sold land and the buyer owes him the purchase price, and the buyer also owes several other creditors, does the seller take priority in that land or its price because the land came from him? Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 104, seif katan 14, seems to understand Bach as applying his rule even to land, since he challenges it from the law of improvements. In truth, however, the cases are different. Bach’s ruling and his proof from Baal HaTerumot concern a loan of movables or money, where there is no specific lien or creditor reliance on the particular object. In such a case one might say that the original owner has a special priority in the very property that came from him, especially because in a loan he did not convey the underlying value as a sale but advanced it as credit. One view in the Gemara even regards a loan, while the identical coins remain in existence, as still in the lender’s possession for purposes of return. Although we do not rule that way generally, perhaps that residual relationship suffices to give him priority in collection. But where the transaction is a sale of merchandise, and certainly where it is the sale of land or a house, creditors ordinarily rely upon the debtor’s land and the land is legally subject to all of them. In that setting all creditors stand equally. Ketzot HaChoshen’s difficulty from improvements therefore falls away, because improvements concern land and an actual sale, where even Bach can agree that all creditors are equal. Since Bach’s rule is itself novel and lacks a clear proof, we should not extend it beyond its proper scope. In land and ordinary sales, the law should certainly not be expanded in that fashion. This appears correct as practical halacha.
