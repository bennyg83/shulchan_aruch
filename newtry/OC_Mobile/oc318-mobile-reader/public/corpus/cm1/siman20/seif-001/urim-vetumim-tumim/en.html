Because they were not available to me. Kenesset HaGedolah asks that Tur appears to contradict his father Rosh in responsum, klal 13, siman 19. There, a community claimed that it possessed evidence located with one of its members overseas. Rosh answered that they should have raised this before beit din. A heir is different, because he may genuinely know nothing of his father's affairs and have no knowledge of what evidence exists. But a communal matter is public and well known. It is impossible that the community knew nothing of the circumstances they later asserted. They should therefore have said at the time, "We have a right and evidence elsewhere." This seems to imply that only an heir receives the leniency, not an ordinary litigant. Kenesset HaGedolah gives a strained answer. But he himself should have noticed a broader point. If Rosh held that everyone other than an heir automatically loses the right to produce later evidence, why did he need to emphasize that communal affairs are public and well known? He could simply have said that anyone who is not an heir loses the claim. The matter should instead be explained from Rambam's wording. Rambam gives the reason: "I said I had no evidence because it was not available to me." At first glance, even then the litigant should have said, "I do have evidence overseas, but it is not presently available." Why should silence be excused? The answer is that such a statement may not help him if he has no external basis for it. Beit din may simply disbelieve him and regard it as an empty excuse. A person who wishes to lie can always send his alleged evidence to a distant place. He may therefore think silence is preferable until he can actually produce the proof. That reasoning applies where no witnesses presently establish that such evidence exists elsewhere. But if witnesses can already testify that he possesses evidence overseas, then he certainly should say so before beit din. Why force himself later to overturn a judgment? If the court knows there is relevant evidence in another country, it will not rush to issue a final ruling but will wait a reasonable period for the evidence to be obtained. Therefore, if the litigant fails to raise the matter when witnesses are available to confirm the existence of the distant proof, he loses that right. This explains Rosh's responsum. A communal matter is by nature public and well known. It is highly unlikely that no members of the community know of the relevant facts. The community should therefore have told beit din of the evidence and brought local community members who could testify that such material existed. One might object that those witnesses themselves are members of the community and therefore interested parties. But in that very responsum the community claimed that local custom permitted testimony from townspeople regarding tax matters in which they had an interest. According to their own position, they therefore could have produced testimony confirming the existence of the evidence. Rosh was thus correct to emphasize that communal matters are public and well known. His ruling does not establish that every non-heir automatically loses later evidence. It concerns a case where the litigant could have credibly informed the court of the evidence at the proper time. This is the correct practical distinction.
