Urim VeTumim instead says that 'he hired them' is not literal; rather, his earlier statement amounts to an admission that these witnesses must be mistaken, and against his own admission we treat them as mistaken rather than intentional liars. In my view that cannot be maintained. A signed document is likewise treated as testimony already examined in beit din, and if we merely attributed contradiction to innocent mistake there would be no distinction. The rule in Ketubot proves otherwise. Urim VeTumim asks that if Reuven sues Shimon and says he has no proof, later produces witnesses and loses because of his statement, then if Shimon later sues Reuven in another matter using those same witnesses he should also lose: either the witnesses are truthful, in which case Reuven's first claim was false, or they are hired liars. Yet no authority states this. The answer is that a litigant disqualifies witnesses against himself only when his own statement was against his interest. An admission against interest is like one hundred witnesses, and therefore witnesses contradicting that admission in his favor are disqualified as against him. But where he made a self-serving denial and witnesses contradicted him to his detriment, he does not thereby disqualify them. This is exactly like a borrower who says, 'I never borrowed,' while witnesses say he borrowed and repaid: despite his denial of both borrowing and repayment, we believe the witnesses that he borrowed and obligate him, because his self-serving statement does not disqualify them. Therefore, if a lender claims two hundred and witnesses prove only one hundred, he collects one hundred. But if he claims only one hundred and his own later witnesses testify to two hundred in a way contradicting his prior self-damaging admission, the effect can be different. The same principle explains why Shimon in the hypothetical may later use the witnesses against Reuven. Reuven's prior statement that favored himself did not disqualify them.
