Or evidence, etc. Even if beit din fixed a time for him to come and present his arguments, and he failed to do so at that time but came afterward, he has not forfeited his claim. Beit din has no power to extinguish his claim merely because he missed the deadline unless he made a formal kinyan accepting that consequence. Sema, citing Darkei Moshe and Rashba.
Even if he later found it, it is nothing. Rashi explains that we suspect he hired the witnesses. According to that explanation, if Reuven sued Shimon for one hundred and said he had no proof, then later produced witnesses, Shimon remains exempt. Moreover, if Shimon later brings another claim against Reuven using those same witnesses, he should also be unable to collect through them: if the witnesses are truthful, Reuven was already owed the first hundred; if we say they were hired to lie, they are disqualified witnesses. Yet the decisors do not state such a sweeping consequence. It therefore appears that "he hired them" is not literal. Rather, by his own earlier admission he has acknowledged that these witnesses are mistaken and speaking from an inaccurate impression. Ordinarily we do not suspect witnesses of such error, but his own statement forces us to treat their present testimony as mistaken without disqualifying them generally. This also resolves the cases where he later produced proof from his pouch, or said, "Let such and such persons approach and testify." It is difficult to say that he hired them within that brief moment. Under the present explanation, there is no difficulty.
And needless to say if they said to him, etc. Bach distinguishes the two clauses. In the first case, if the litigant supplies a credible explanation for his earlier statement, the later proof may be accepted; in this second case, even an explanation does not help. He therefore explains why Tur and the Shulchan Aruch stated them separately. This appears to depend upon an early dispute. In Temim De'im there is a case of a daughter in an estate of limited assets who challenged one holding her father's land. He first said, "It is false; this land belonged to my ancestors." When he saw that this plea would not help him, he produced a deed showing that her brother had sold it to him. The father-in-law of Raavad held that the deed was lost because his first plea denied such a purchase. Raavad held that if beit din sees that the first plea resulted from error and foolishness, he has not forfeited his deed. This is precisely an excuse recognized by beit din. Raavad's position therefore disagrees with Bach's rule that in the case of producing proof from his pouch no explanation can ever help. Raavad was a preeminent authority, and his view is compelling.
But if he did not say, etc. The same applies if he presented some arguments and beit din never asked whether he had any additional claim. He may later add another claim and even overturn the prior ruling, provided the new claim does not contradict the earlier ones. Sema, citing a responsum of Rosh. Shach adds that even if beit din asked whether he had any further proof and he remained silent, so long as he did not expressly say that he had none, he may later produce evidence.

But an heir, etc. In the case of an heir, even if the documents were deposited in the same city and he could have obtained them, he has not forfeited the proof if he did not know they were there. The same should apply to any person where there are reasonable grounds to believe he was unaware that another person held the evidence, even if, had he known, it would have been readily available. In Tumim I concluded that the explanation, "I said I had no proof because it was not available to me," is accepted only where his inability to access it cannot itself be disproved. If that point can be clearly investigated and disproved, his explanation does not help.

Or witnesses came and said, etc. With an adult litigant, he ordinarily must bring proof that the documents were in another person's hands. With a minor heir, we presume they were elsewhere unless witnesses show that they were actually in his possession. Rama's source in Mordechai merely says that if the documents were in his hands he cannot later rely upon them, but does not explicitly require witnesses that they were in his hands. I therefore understand Rama as speaking of one who was still a minor at the time of the litigation. In that situation the natural presumption is that his father's documents were not entrusted to him, and therefore witnesses are required to establish that they were actually in his possession. But if he was already an adult at the time of litigation, although he had been a minor when the ancestor died, the ordinary presumption is that the decedent's deposits had been delivered to him once he matured. He must then prove that they were not in his possession. This distinction is clear.
