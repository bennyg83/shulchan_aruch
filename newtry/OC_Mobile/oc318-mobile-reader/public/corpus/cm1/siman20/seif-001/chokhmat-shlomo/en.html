Seif 1. “Even if he has already paid, so long as he brings proof he may overturn the judgment.” Note: A newly printed work called Shaar HaMishpat proposes that where a person was sued for money, answered that he did not know, and was held liable on the basis of witnesses, and afterward produced witnesses who disqualified the first witnesses by hazamah, or where heirs were made liable by witnesses and later produced witnesses of hazamah, the money may nevertheless remain with the recipient if the payer had already despaired of recovering it. He seeks support from Keritot, where, concerning an ox sentenced to stoning whose witnesses were later shown to be zomemim, Rabbi Yochanan says that anyone who takes possession of the ox acquires it. Rava explains that this is reasonable where the owner was told that his ox had been used for bestiality, so that the owner had no personal knowledge contradicting the testimony and abandoned hope of the ox. My heart does not incline to this conclusion. We rule that despair alone does not transfer title from a robber, whereas despair can permit acquisition of a lost object. The Gemara explains the distinction: a lost object came into the finder’s hand permissibly, while stolen property came into the robber’s hand unlawfully. This remains true even where the robber is so violent that the owner despairs immediately. The property did not reach him only after a preexisting despair. If the despair arose because of and simultaneously with the taking, it is still considered to have come into his possession unlawfully, and despair does not acquire it for him. The condemned ox is different. Once its judgment was concluded, it became prohibited in benefit even while still in the owner’s possession. The owner therefore despaired of it before anyone else took possession. When a third party later acquired it, his acquisition was comparable to finding an ownerless object after despair. But where a judgment orders Reuven to pay Shimon, the specific money in Reuven’s possession remains Reuven’s until he actually transfers it. It remains at his risk, and if it is lost he must pay other money. He therefore does not despair of that money while it is still in his own possession. If it is later established that he never owed Shimon, then when Shimon received the money it was in truth Reuven’s property. If Shimon knew the claim was false, he was certainly a robber. Even if Shimon relied innocently upon the witnesses, once their testimony is shown to have been false, the money is retrospectively found to have been received without legal right. The owner’s despair occurred only because the money entered Shimon’s hand. It is therefore like despair after theft, or like a lost object picked up before despair, where the finder already became obligated to restore it and later despair does not eliminate that duty. Moreover, an abandonment directed only to one individual and not to everyone is not a valid hefker. This does not contradict the Rosh at the end of HaGozel Batra, who rules that if a person sends money with an agent to pay a non-Jew and the non-Jew forgets the debt, the agent may acquire the money because the owner had despaired of it. There the agent received the money permissibly for the purpose of payment and had no part in causing the owner’s despair. He is therefore like a finder who received property permissibly. Here, by contrast, the despair is caused by the claimant’s acquisition itself. Even if payment is sent through an agent, this remains analogous to a robber who uses an agent. We do not say that because despair occurred before the stolen property reached the robber personally, the robber acquires it. The taking on behalf of the robber is still an unlawful acquisition whose very process generated the despair. The case of money sent to pay the non-Jew is different because the underlying debt was genuine and the agent’s receipt was entirely lawful. Nor can one prove the opposite from the Sema in siman 105, since there the payer did not initially know that there was an excess amount, and any supposed despair would be despair without knowledge. Here the payer knows precisely what he is paying and might consciously abandon hope. Nevertheless, for the reason explained, even such despair cannot transfer ownership to one who received money only through an erroneous judgment. The recipient must return what he received, just as in any mistaken compromise or mistaken judgment. A strong proof appears from the Yerushalmi cited by the Rif at the end of HaKones, and by Hagahot Ashri and the Shach in siman 175, concerning the deposit in the hands of the sharecroppers of Bar Zuzai. Rabbi Yishmael initially instructed the heirs to pay half, and Rabbi Chiya later held that the other half belonged to the sharecroppers’ heirs. When they asked to recover the first half, he answered, “The elder has already ruled.” The implication is that only because Rabbi Chiya could not conclusively demonstrate Rabbi Yishmael’s error did he refrain from ordering the first half returned. Had he been able to establish an actual judicial error, he would have ordered restitution, despite the fact that the first heirs had already received the money and the others had apparently despaired of it. The Rosh, Tur, and Shach in siman 25 likewise indicate this. Thus, in my view, there is no basis to treat payment under an erroneous judgment as a final waiver through despair. The money must be restored once the error is established.
