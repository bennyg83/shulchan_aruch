Even though he later brought proof, etc. The same applies if beit din fixed a time for a litigant to come and present his claims and he failed to appear. He may still later present them. Beit din has no power merely by fixing a deadline to prevent him from later returning with claims, unless a kinyan was made from him. See below, siman 21, the end of siman 24, and siman 207:15. Darkei Moshe cites this from Rashba.
And he said, "I have none." See Rambam, Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 7. The text there has an obvious omission and must be corrected so that it reads: if they said to him, "Do you have witnesses?" and he answered, "I have no witnesses; do you have proof?" and he answered, "I have no proof," and the case was adjudicated, then if he later finds proof it is ineffective. This is compelled by the mishnah and all the poskim. The same correction is required here in the Mechaber's wording, which follows Rambam.
A.A. who later found nothing. I don’t have a false witness, I have another salary I’ve seen that I’ve been told that I’m not going to give a call if it’s great and said first I don’t have any evidence of it
This is of no effect. Since he knew of these witnesses and proofs and nevertheless said, "I have none," the later evidence appears forged or false. At first he did not wish to rely upon the fabricated material so long as he thought he could succeed without it, but after seeing that he lost the case he attempts to use it.
But if he did not say, "I have none," etc. This is not limited to witnesses and documentary proof. The same applies where he advanced one claim and beit din did not ask whether he had any additional arguments or rights. He may later raise other claims that do not contradict the first and thereby overturn the judgment. So writes the Rosh, rule 13, siman 19, cited by Darkei Moshe.
When does this apply? Where the proof was with him. Rambam and Tur use this language. In Perishah I demonstrated that "with him" is not literal. The same applies if the document was deposited with another person who was in the same city when he said, "I have none." Such evidence is treated as effectively available to him, since he can retrieve it whenever he wishes. Witnesses differ because they are independent people and not under his control. That is why the law speaks of witnesses who are "with him in the province" rather than "with him." This is also proved from the Mechaber's concluding rule concerning an heir who had been a minor and later says, "My predecessor deposited this proof with another." If an adult could always overturn the judgment merely because the proof had been deposited with someone else, there would be no novelty in the rule regarding a minor heir.
Or his father's document case, etc. The Gemara, Rif, Rosh, and Rambam use the example of his father's document case. I demonstrated in Perishah and Derishah that according to Mordechai and Tur this is not limited to the father's papers. The same applies to his own documents, so long as the deposit holder was not in the city when he said, "I have none." The Gemara mentions the father merely because that is the usual case. Rama also says so in Darkei Moshe. Rambam and Tur explain the reason: he can say, "When I said I had no witnesses or proof, I meant that none were then available to me." That explanation applies equally to his own documents. If the rule concerned only his father's documents, they should have said, "because I did not know of them." Therefore Rama should not have presented this as merely "some say," for there may be no dispute.
Deposited with another, and this person came, etc. As I explained in Perishah, this too concerns a case where the deposit holder came from overseas and then produced the documents. Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber did not combine witnesses and documentary proof into one clause because the details differ. With witnesses, they speak of witnesses who were not in the country at all when he said, "I have none." With documentary proof, even if the document was physically in the same city in another's custody, he can still overturn the case if the custodian himself was not then in the country. This explains the wording "and he came" and "and produced." The Tur's language implies that even if the particular proof document stood alone in the custodian's possession, one may say, "It was not available to me." Mordechai, however, speaks of the proof document being among other papers in the custodian's hands, and Darkei Moshe and Rama cite it this way. The Gemara's term "document case" also suggests a collection of papers. Perhaps if the proof document was held separately and known, he should have mentioned it before beit din.
Because he can claim, etc. So too is Rambam's wording. It appears the litigant himself must offer this explanation; beit din does not automatically plead it for him. The continuation, "whenever he can claim," supports this. The Tur's wording can sound as though beit din supplies the explanation, but it may simply mean that his original words, "I have no proof or witnesses," are capable of this interpretation, and therefore we accept it when he himself explains his words that way.
Because a minor heir does not know, etc. This is Rambam and Ramah's view. The Tur cites the Rosh as disagreeing. The Rosh holds that once the heir became an adult before the trial, his law is like any other adult. Presumably he investigated his father's affairs before appearing, or at least after the claim was filed, and therefore should not have said, "I have none." If he did say so, later evidence is suspect. Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh's objection is not decisive, because one who was a minor at his father's death may never have known which people had information concerning his father's rights, and once the opportunity was missed he may no longer know whom to investigate. The Mechaber therefore rules like Rambam and Ramah. Tosafot, however, agree with the Rosh. See Darkei Moshe and Derishah for reconciliation of these views with the laws of possession against one who was a minor when his father died.
Or witnesses came, etc. Meaning that in the case of an heir who was a minor and later grew up, where we ordinarily allow him to produce new proof because he did not know his father's affairs, witnesses later testify that he actually knew of this proof. Mordechai remains uncertain where witnesses say the evidence was physically in his hands but do not know whether he knew of its contents. Perhaps we say he should have known because it was in his possession. Darkei Moshe concludes that if he was already an adult at the time of the case, everyone agrees we say he should have known, and he cannot later overturn the judgment. Thus Rama's word "knows" means that knowledge is presumed from the circumstances.
