According to the custom of the locality. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 103. Concerning a maidservant who fled claiming that excessive burdens had been imposed upon her, the law depends on local custom. If families in the city ordinarily impose that burden upon their servants, the employer may do so. If some do and some do not, but the majority practice is known, we follow the majority. If the majority practice cannot be determined, the employer may not impose a burden that is not clearly customary. Similarly, if the mistress locks the bread chest, and she is publicly known to do so while the maid is a local girl who accepted employment with that knowledge, she accepted the arrangement. Otherwise we follow the custom of women of comparable standing. If the practice is contrary to custom, the mistress may not impose it even if she treats her own children similarly. As to deducting from the maid's wages for minor breakage or loss of household utensils, strict law supports the mistress because the maid is like a paid custodian and is liable for theft and for accidental breakage caused through mishap, unless the utensil broke through ordinary work or it is known that another member of the household broke it. Nevertheless, as a matter of custom, most women do not insist upon small losses. Although monetary law generally does not follow a simple majority, that principle applies where a meaningful minority follows the opposing practice. If only one or two women in the city are unusually strict, their view is disregarded as an insignificant minority. See there.
