Seif 1. “We attend first to the claimant.” Note: I saw in Shaar HaMishpat another interpretation, cited from an authority whose name I do not presently recall. If the claimant sues the defendant for money and the defendant thereby becomes obligated to take an oath, while the defendant has a counterclaim against the claimant that likewise obligates the claimant to swear, then the defendant is made to swear first. This too, he says, is included in the rule that the court attends first to the claimant, and he concludes that this is the practice of halachic decisors. In my view this is not correct. First, the Shulchan Aruch itself appears not to accept this interpretation. Below, siman 72, seif 28, where a lender and borrower have opposing monetary claims, both are required to swear. If each wishes to reverse his oath onto the other, and each says, “I reverse mine,” the matter remains pending until they reach agreement. Why do we not apply the rule that the court attends first to the claimant, thereby allowing the claimant to reverse his oath onto the defendant first? Indeed, if the defendant must swear first, then since he is already swearing, the claimant should be able to say: include my claim in your oath as well and I will pay you. It therefore appears that the Tur and Baal HaTerumot did not understand the rule in this way. Second, even if one were to include this within the rule in Talmudic times, the practical situation has changed. In the time of the Gemara there was no universal practice that one who imposes an oath on another first accepts a cherem. Today, however, under the later enactment cited in siman 87, one who causes another to swear must first accept the appropriate cherem. If the defendant must swear first, the claimant may have to accept the cherem and afterward swear on the counterclaim, while the defendant may be able to discharge his side with a single oath that also includes the claim that he is not needlessly causing the claimant to swear. If the claimant swears first, the reverse may occur. Sometimes it is advantageous to the claimant to swear first, and sometimes advantageous for the defendant to swear first. There is therefore no universal benefit that can be identified as “attending first to the claimant.” The rule applies where the court itself can identify an ordinary advantage that claimants generally receive from having their matter addressed first. But where the court cannot know which sequence is advantageous for ordinary people, a particular claimant cannot insist that one sequence happens to suit him. His individual preference is not enough to define the rabbinic rule. Therefore this case should follow siman 72, seif 28: the matter waits until both sides agree which oath comes first. If they agree to cast lots to determine who swears first, that is also effective. The same applies in siman 72 regarding reversed oaths. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch did not need to state this separately, because agreement to use a lottery is itself included in the concept of mutual agreement. In practice, a halachic authority should initially suggest that they cast lots in both cases. This appears clear to me.
