We attend first only to the claimant, etc. Many explanations have been given for this rule. With God's help, I will also state my understanding. The primary explanation is certainly that we first attend to the claimant by hearing his words before those of the defendant. According to the other explanations, the wording should have been, "We rule first for the claimant." Why say, "We attend"? The proof cited in the Gemara, from the verse, "Whoever has a matter, let him approach them," interpreted as, "Let him bring his words before them," also indicates that the issue is whose claims are heard first. So too the Tosefta in Sanhedrin, chapter 6, says, "One who brings a claim against his fellow speaks first." Mordechai in chapter HaParah and Hagahot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 57, already explain this in the name of Ri. Their reason, however, that the claimant can greatly embellish his words by speaking first, is strained. Their further explanation that the defendant's property may become cheap because people will see that he is a defendant and pressure him in their dealings with him, whereas if the defendant speaks first people will think he is the claimant, is also remote. In my humble opinion the meaning is as follows. The parties come to beit din. The claimant wants his allegations heard first, while the defendant wants to speak first. There is a practical difference if the defendant admits part of the claim. A partial admission creates a Torah oath only when made after the claimant first asserted the claim. If the defendant first admits fifty and the claimant then demands one hundred and the defendant denies the remainder, he is exempt from the Torah oath, as stated below, siman 88, seif 15. One should not object from siman 87, seif 5, where if the judge sees that the defendant is strategically offering immediate payment in order to evade a Torah oath he remains liable, or from siman 88, seif 14, where an apparent stratagem likewise does not exempt him. Here we discuss a case where the judge sees no stratagem, but only that the defendant finds even a truthful oath difficult, or alternatively those cases concern a claim already asserted, whereas here the parties are disputing at the outset about who must speak first. Therefore we attend to the claimant by hearing his allegations first. Even if the defendant then admits part, he remains the defendant so long as he has not already paid what he admits, and the opposing party is called the claimant. He is obligated in the Torah oath because he should have paid the admitted amount before being sued in beit din. But if the defendant's property is presently depressed in value, or he would have to sell quickly at a loss to pay and for that reason did not pay before he was sued, we attend first to the defendant. If he admits part before the claimant states his claim, he is then exempt from the Torah oath, in accordance with our ruling below in siman 88 that a Torah oath arises only when the claimant's demand preceded the admission. This appears correct to me. Examine it carefully.
In a manner that he cannot say, "I paid." For example, he lent him money by written document, or a comparable case. See below, siman 85, seif 7.
More than thirty days. Thirty days are granted even where his property has not depreciated in value. Tosafot and the Rosh write that there is another practical difference regarding personal injuries, for no extension of time is ordinarily granted for payment of damages for injury, as stated below, siman 420, seif 27. Nimukei Yosef writes that according to this explanation, where the defendant's property is depressed in value, time may be granted even for injury damages. The Bach challenges him, arguing that the categorical statement that no time is given for injury damages means under all circumstances, even where the property is depressed. The challenge is unjustified. Nimukei Yosef's conclusion is compelled by this interpretation. If the sugya concerns injury damages, how can it then say that if the defendant's property has depreciated we attend to him first? The general rule that no time is granted for injury damages, even where property values are low, applies when the defendant seeks additional time merely to make payment. Since he indisputably owes the money, we do not consider the depressed value of his property. Here, however, he says, "I have witnesses who will exempt me." In that situation, if his property is depressed, we grant him time to bring them. Tosafot initially argued a fortiori: If beit din ordinarily grants thirty days for payment, surely time should be granted to bring witnesses. They answered that injury damages are different, because no payment period is granted. This is why the ordinary thirty day period cannot prove the point. But the final distinction is sound: a request for time to bring exonerating witnesses is stronger. This is clear, contrary to the Bach.
He must pay him immediately. The Bach writes at length that we do not require the claimant to swear that he is not holding property belonging to the defendant, because the defendant says he has witnesses and we are concerned that the witnesses may later arrive and contradict the claimant, producing desecration of God's Name, as discussed below in siman 72. His words are incorrect in light of what I write below at the end of siman 75, seif katan 77 and 88, and siman 87, seif 33. Clearly, this passage does not address the law of oaths at all. The claimant is required to swear whenever the general law calls for it. The only effect of attending first to the claimant here is that the defendant must pay immediately and we do not wait for his witnesses, even when we know that he has such witnesses. Examine this carefully.
That they hear his words first, etc. See what I wrote above, seif katan 1. See also Agudat Ezov, page 76b.
Because he does not want him to swear to him now, etc. See below, siman 16, what I write concerning this.
And he does not wish to state his claim now, etc. Although below, siman 87, seif 24, the Rama writes that we tell the claimant to state all his claims, he concludes there that if the claimant does not wish to do so, the defendant swears and is discharged from all claims. Here the claimant says that he does not care if the defendant swears now, because even an oath will not discharge him from all claims. He may have additional claims to which an oath is inapplicable, or he may later bring witnesses. The Sema and Bach do not explain it this way and discuss the matter at unnecessary length.
We heed the claimant, etc. There is another practical difference even if the defendant's witnesses later die or travel overseas. This applies only when they are not presently ill or preparing to travel, but such an event might unexpectedly occur. Otherwise, there could be no greater depreciation of the defendant's position than the imminent loss of his evidence. This resolves Tosafot's and Maharshal's difficulty with Ri's explanation. See Bach. There are other interpretations of this rule as well, brought by Maharshal in chapter HaParah, siman 4, who writes that all of them are true as practical law. See his lengthy discussion. See also Responsa Rama, siman 108, and Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, part 3, siman 25. Because these rules depend greatly upon the judges' assessment of circumstances, I have been brief.
