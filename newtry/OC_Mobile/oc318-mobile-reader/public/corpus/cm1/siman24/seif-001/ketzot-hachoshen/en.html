Shiltei Giborim writes: if Reuven sues Shimon with a document showing Shimon owes him a maneh, and Shimon in turn claims that Reuven owes him a certain object or a certain field, beit din tells Shimon, "First pay the document that you admit, and afterward bring your separate claim in beit din." If Shimon's counterclaim itself is something directly capable of satisfying the document, for example he says Reuven owes him a certain sum of money from another transaction, then it is as though he claims the document has already been paid and the matters form one dispute. If he demands that Reuven swear, Reuven does not collect until he swears. This implies that where the counterclaim concerns property not directly suitable as repayment, such as movables or land, the lender need not take the serious oath even if the borrower says, "Swear to me." We address the documented claimant first, and afterward the lender need only take hesset on the separate counterclaim. One might object from Perek HaKotev, where Tosafot says that if a debtor would otherwise suffer loss he may satisfy a creditor with land even though a creditor ordinarily collects money. Here too the borrower suffers a procedural loss, because by being forced to pay money first he loses the stronger oath he could have imposed had he been able to offset land. One must say that this is not considered sufficient monetary loss, because the difference concerns only the form of oath rather than actual money. See what I wrote in siman 72:27.

The Tur writes: "I had collateral in your possession and its value depreciated," and even if the claimant admits depreciation but they dispute its amount, we still attend to the claimant's demand first. The Sema cites Darkei Moshe from Mordechai. Tumim argues that Mordechai follows his own view that where one is sued for a specific object or a maneh, lacks witnesses, and the defendant admits the claim but says he has an offsetting claim, the defendant must first pay what he admits and then sue separately. Therefore in the collateral case, even though the defendant has a migo, Mordechai does not allow him to retain the money. According to the majority of authorities who accept migo to retain property, Tumim says the same should apply here: if the lender admits depreciation, he thereby admits an offsetting liability, so why should the borrower not be allowed to clarify the amount before paying? Tumim adds that Mordechai's two rules are intertwined and the Sema failed to notice this. The objection is unnecessary. Mordechai indeed denies the defendant's migo in his own general case, and therefore similarly says that even where the lender admits some depreciation and disputes the amount, the borrower must first pay. But authorities who accept migo will indeed allow the borrower to retain where he actually has a migo. The present ruling, however, concerns a case where no migo exists, for example a documented loan, as Rama states. In such a case we certainly first collect the note and afterward adjudicate the dispute over depreciation. Whatever depreciation the lender admits is deducted. If the lender admits fifty and denies the rest, the borrower must still pay the remaining fifty, just as if he asserted an ordinary counterclaim of one hundred and the lender admitted fifty. There is no distinction between total denial and partial admission in this respect. This is straightforward.
