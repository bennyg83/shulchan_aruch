More than thirty days. Beit din always grants the defendant thirty days even where his property is not depreciating. More than thirty days is not granted unless there is concern that his property will be sold at a loss, Sema:
And he must pay him immediately. This means that if he has cash and is not seeking time to obtain money, but only time to establish his counterclaim so that it can be deducted from the loan, he must pay immediately. Since he cannot bring proof within thirty days that the plaintiff owes him an offsetting amount, and he will not be granted more than thirty days, there is no reason to delay payment needlessly for the first thirty days. If he has no money, he is not granted more than thirty days to obtain funds by some means, Sema. This applies when he specifically requests more than thirty days. If he merely says, "I have witnesses," he is granted thirty days to seek them. If those thirty days pass and he then has no money, he is granted another thirty days, because during the first period he was occupied seeking witnesses, Urim VeTumim. Nevertheless, when he pays he may immediately require the lender to swear, even though he claims that he has witnesses. We do not withhold the oath out of concern for desecration of God's Name, Shach, unlike the Sema:
That his property will depreciate. Meaning, if he lacks money and prospective buyers know that he must sell assets in order to pay, they pressure him to sell cheaply. If the defendant's counterclaim is true, he may not have to sell at all and suffer that loss. In such a case, the defendant's claim is heard first. Rashi gives another explanation in Bava Kamma. The defendant claims a pledge. If the plaintiff returns it now, he can sell it at a high price because present market conditions are favorable. If it is returned only much later, the merchants may have gone away and he will no longer obtain that price. We therefore hear the defendant first, because perhaps the plaintiff will be obligated to return the pledge and the defendant will avoid the loss. The same applies where a rumor that he owes money prevents him from obtaining a suitable marriage or causes people to refuse to transact with him on credit. Such consequences are likewise included in depreciation of property. It appears that the same applies where there is concern that a violent party will meanwhile gain power and resort to non Jewish courts in a place where Jewish authority is weak. This too is included in the obligation to support the oppressed. The rule that more than thirty days may be granted where property is depreciating applies where we know that the defendant actually has witnesses concerning the matter. Likewise, the rule in siman 16, seif 1, that more than thirty days is granted for witnesses who have traveled far away also concerns a situation in which the defendant's property would otherwise depreciate, Sema. The Urim VeTumim answers differently: where witnesses are simply absent and we do not know that they are far away, no more than thirty days is given. If it is known that they are distant and cannot arrive within thirty days, the necessary time is granted:
He wants his words heard. One practical difference is that he can first refine and arrange his claims. Where the defendant's property would depreciate merely because the public knows he is being sued, the defendant is heard first so that people will think that he is the plaintiff. The Shach writes that another practical difference concerns a partial admission. If the defendant speaks first and admits part of the claim in a manner that is plainly sincere and not contrived, but simply because he does not wish to swear falsely, he may be exempt from an oath. Therefore, the rule that the plaintiff is heard first causes the plaintiff to state the claim before the defendant and thereby may create an oath obligation. In the case of depreciating assets, the Shach explains that the defendant's prior failure to pay did not result from negligence but because collection could not previously be made without a loss, such as where no merchants were available to purchase his movable property. In that situation the defendant is heard first even if this causes him to be exempt from an oath. His explanation is very strained. If the plaintiff asks that his witnesses be heard first and the defendant asks that his witnesses be heard first, then if the two groups are equally situated, either neither group is at risk of dying or traveling overseas, or both are equally at risk, the plaintiff's witnesses are heard first and depreciation of property is not relevant. If one group is at risk and the other is not, it is obvious that the testimony at risk of being lost is received first:
Or if he requests. Perhaps today or tomorrow he will establish his claim by proof or the other party will admit it. This applies even if he already showed his documents to beit din. Even if a kinyan was made from him concerning this, he may still say, "I do not wish to argue now." This implies that once he actually stated his claim, he may not retract it, Sema:
That he swear to him now. The Shulchan Aruch's statement above in siman 107 that after thirty days the defendant swears even against the plaintiff's wishes concerns a case of depreciating assets, where nevertheless thirty days are allowed. Therefore, the word "now" here is not literal. Thirty days are always granted, even where property is depreciating. Alternatively, where the defendant does not claim that he has witnesses, then if his property is depreciating not even thirty days are granted, Sema:
And anything similar. The same applies if the defendant says that he will bring his proof within thirty days. Beit din may issue a ruling immediately, subject to reversal if the proof is later produced. If no proof is produced, the plaintiff gains the benefit that the subsequent period granted by beit din begins from the date of the immediate ruling. Likewise, if one sues for bodily injury and Shimon says, "I have an offsetting claim against you and will bring witnesses," no time is granted, because no delay is granted in injury cases. The Yam Shel Shlomo writes in Bava Kamma, chapter HaParah, that where a claimant is entitled to swear and collect but says, "I will neither swear nor collect," the defendant cannot force him to swear and collect. But if the defendant reversed the oath onto him, requiring him to swear and collect, then although the defendant may retract that reversal, he may say, "Either swear to me within the time fixed by beit din, or I retract and will myself swear and be exempt, because I reversed the oath only on that condition and will not remain bound to you indefinitely":
That he has many claims. The Sema asks from below, siman 87, seif 24 in the gloss, where the plaintiff is required to present all claims together, and if he refuses, the defendant swears that he owes him nothing and is thereby discharged from all the claims. He answers that here the present claim does not itself generate an oath obligation. Therefore, the defendant cannot argue that otherwise he will be forced to swear repeatedly. If neither the present claim nor the other claims the plaintiff still wishes to raise would generate an oath, the plaintiff cannot be forced to present them together, since there is no concern of multiple oaths, Sema. The same applies where the plaintiff agrees that the defendant may now swear generally in beit din that he owes nothing on any of the claims, but the plaintiff nevertheless wishes to raise the individual claims later when circumstances are favorable because he believes he will then have proof. The plaintiff cannot be forced to present all claims at once, because the defendant will not swear twice. He has already sworn generally, and if the plaintiff later cannot establish proof, the defendant is already discharged, Shach.
