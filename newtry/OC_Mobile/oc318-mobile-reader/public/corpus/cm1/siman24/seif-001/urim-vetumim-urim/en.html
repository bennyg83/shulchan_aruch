Regarding a case where he cannot say, "I repaid." This concerns a loan evidenced by a document, or by witnesses where the lender told him, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," or a debt still within its fixed term. But where the defendant would be believed to say that he repaid, he is also believed to say, "I have a counterclaim against you for property of mine in your hands," by virtue of a migo, and he need not bring witnesses or other proof. Sema. This is obvious where the claimant demands money, for the defendant's response is equivalent to saying, "I do not owe you." Even where the claimant demands a specific object, though one might argue, "Who authorized you to assess it for yourself?" and the object itself remains in the claimant's possession, the conclusion of most poskim is that the migo is effective, contrary to the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia. See there.
You have so much of mine, etc. The Tur writes: "I had a pledge in your possession and it depreciated in value," etc. Even if the claimant admits that it depreciated and they dispute only whether the depreciation was large or small, the claimant's case is nevertheless heard first. Sema, citing Darkei Moshe in the name of the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia. See Tumim, where I wrote that it appears that most poskim disagree with the Mordechai on this point, and the law does not follow it.
More than thirty days, etc. Beit din always grants the defendant the ordinary thirty day period even where his property is not falling in value. More than thirty days is not granted unless his property would fall in value. Sema.
And he must pay him immediately, etc. Meaning, if he has money available but asks for time to establish his proof, then once the ordinary thirty days have passed, since his property is not falling in value he receives no additional time and must pay. If he has no money, he receives time as stated below in siman 100. But to bring proof he is given no more than thirty days. Sema. The Bach writes that although he must pay the claimant, the claimant is not required to swear that he holds nothing belonging to the defendant, because the defendant claims that he will establish his position by witnesses, and perhaps witnesses will come and contradict the claimant, with the result that the Divine Name would have been taken in vain. The Shach rejects this and writes that the Shulchan Aruch is not discussing the law of the oath here, but merely teaches that no further time is granted and he must pay. It is nevertheless obvious that the claimant must swear that the defendant's allegation is false. His words appear correct.
That his property is falling in value. Meaning, if he has no cash and buyers know that he must sell property in order to pay, they pressure him to sell cheaply. If the defendant's counterclaim is true, he need not sell at all and need not sacrifice the value of his property. In such a case the defendant's claim is heard first. Sema. Rashi in Bava Kamma gives another explanation: the defendant claims a pledge from the claimant. If the claimant returns it now, the defendant can sell it at a good price because the present season is favorable, whereas if it is returned only after many days the merchants may have departed and he will no longer be able to sell it for that amount. We therefore hear the defendant first, for perhaps the claimant will be required to return his pledge and he will avoid the loss. As a matter of law, both explanations are correct. Likewise, where the mere public report that a person owes money causes others not to offer him a suitable marriage match, or not to conduct business with him on credit, this too is included in the category of his property falling in value, as Kenesset HaGedolah cites from Rashach. It appears that the same applies where there is reason to fear that meanwhile a violent person may gain the upper hand and resort to secular courts in a place where Jewish authority is weak. This too is included in property falling in value, and belongs to the duty to "render justice to the oppressed," as I wrote in Tumim. The rule that the defendant is heard first where his property is falling in value applies where we know that he has witnesses who know the relevant facts. If we do not know this and he merely asserts that he has witnesses, then even where his property is falling in value he receives no more than thirty days. Tosafos and Sema, seif katan 4. This is clear. See Tumim, seif katan 3, where I distinguished this case from siman 16.
And there is a loss to the defendant through this. See Tumim, seif katan 1, where I established that if the claimant is not known to be wealthy, so that there is concern that the defendant later may find no assets from which to collect his own claim, the defendant's case is heard first. This too is included in property falling in value.
He wants his words to be heard first, etc. The Mordechai explains that this allows him to present and clarify his position to beit din more effectively, and therefore the claimant is heard first, unless the defendant intends in any event to sell his property and fears that once it becomes known that he is a defendant in beit din people will think he is compelled to sell and will depress the price. If he presents his own claim first, people will think that he is the claimant and his property will not fall in value. Mordechai and Sema. The Shach, seif katan 1, gives another practical difference: if the defendant presents his claim first and admits part, he will not incur a Torah oath because the admission preceded the claim, whereas if the claimant first demands payment he would incur the Torah oath. The reason is that he should have paid the portion he admits. Yet if his property is falling in value and he would have been compelled to pay first, then the claimant is not heard first. These words are strained. Why mention specifically falling property values? The same is true if the admission concerns a debt still within its term, or if he has no means at all and could not have paid. Moreover, who can stop the defendant when he enters beit din from immediately saying, "I owe so and so this amount"? If he is not acting deceptively, as the Shach himself conditions the case, it is obvious that he is exempt from the Torah oath. What relevance is there then to hearing the claimant first? If the claimant asks that his witnesses be heard first and the defendant asks that his witnesses be heard first, then where the two groups are equally situated, either neither group is in danger of dying or leaving overseas meanwhile, or both groups face that danger, the claimant's witnesses are heard first, and falling property values are irrelevant. Tosafos in Sanhedrin. If only one group is at risk and the other is not, it is obvious that testimony is taken first from the group whose testimony may otherwise be lost. Bach.
Or he asks, etc. Because perhaps today or tomorrow he will clarify his claim by proof, or the other litigant will admit. Even if he has already shown his documents to beit din, and even if he made a kinyan concerning this, he may still say, "I am not presenting my claim now." Darkei Moshe in the name of Rashba, and Sema. It follows that if he has already stated his claim, he cannot retract it, and beit din rules according to the law.
That he swear to him now. The Shulchan Aruch wrote above in siman 16 that after thirty days the defendant is required to swear despite the claimant's objection. There the case concerns falling property values, yet even there thirty days are allowed. Accordingly, the word "now" here cannot mean literally now, for thirty days are always granted, even where property values are falling. Alternatively, where he does not claim that he has witnesses, then when property values are falling not even thirty days are granted. Sema. One might ask regarding the earlier phrase that he "became obligated to swear": why state that an oath had already been imposed? Even if no ruling had yet been issued, since his property is falling in value he must immediately present his defense. The Bach therefore writes that "became obligated to swear" is not precise. This is difficult, however, because the Shulchan Aruch above did not mention falling property values at all, and other strained explanations are also required. It therefore appears that above, where the claimant says, "Do not let him swear to me because I have witnesses," no more than thirty days are granted. There is no loss to the claimant, because either way the defendant presumably will not swear falsely when he too knows that the claimant has witnesses, since his disgrace would later be publicly exposed, he would have taken the Divine Name in vain, and would become disqualified from testimony and oath. Even if despite all this he swears falsely, if witnesses later arrive and testify as the claimant says, the claimant does not lose his claim because of that oath. Therefore only thirty days are granted. Here, however, the claimant asks for time because perhaps after many days the defendant will reconsider and decide to admit rather than swear falsely. The claimant himself concedes that he presently has no proof, as is clear from the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. In that situation he is granted the time he requests if the defendant's property is not falling in value. There is therefore no difficulty. Examine the texts: there the claimant says that he has additional proof, while here the concern is merely that perhaps the defendant will reconsider and admit. This resolves the matter. But where the defendant's property is falling in value, no delay is granted even for thirty days before administering the oath. The Shulchan Aruch's word "now" is thus precise, without the Sema's strained reading.
And similarly in every comparable case. The same applies if the defendant says he will bring his proof within thirty days. Beit din rules immediately, and if he later brings proof the judgment may be overturned. If he does not bring proof, the claimant gains because any further time ordinarily granted by beit din begins to run from the judgment that was issued immediately. Tur and Rosh. Likewise, if one claims that another injured him and the defendant says, "I have a counterclaim against him and will bring witnesses," he receives no delay, because no extension is granted for payment of damages for bodily injury. Sema in the name of Tosafos, Rosh, and Mordechai. This must concern a case where there are witnesses to the injury, for otherwise the defendant would be believed by a migo that he could have denied injuring him entirely, as explained above. If so, however, this teaching seems unnecessary. Even where one claims money and the defendant says he has witnesses, and his words appear credible to beit din, he receives no more than thirty days, the ordinary period beit din grants even without such a claim. Certainly, then, in a bodily injury case where no extension is granted, there is no special period. If the point is that even thirty days are not granted for bodily injury, the reason is simply that bodily injury obligations receive no extension, a rule already stated by the Tur and Mechaber below in siman 420. Where property values are falling, on the other hand, time can be granted even in bodily injury cases, and there is no distinction between such a claim and any other, as Nimukei Yosef writes and the Shach concludes in seif katan 2. The Tur therefore properly omitted this separate law of bodily injury, since it follows a fortiori from other claims, and the Bach's difficulty is removed. Yam Shel Shlomo in Bava Kamma, chapter HaParah, writes that if a claimant is entitled to swear and collect but says, "I do not wish yet to swear or collect," the defendant cannot compel him to swear and collect. But if the defendant transferred the oath to the claimant, so that the claimant must swear and collect, although the defendant cannot simply retract the transfer, he may say, "Swear to me within the ordinary beit din period, or I retract and will swear myself and be exempt, because I transferred the oath only on that understanding and will not remain bound to you indefinitely." See there. His words appear correct, especially since many poskim hold that the defendant may retract before the other party actually swears.
That he has many, etc. The Sema asks from siman 87, seif 24 in the gloss, where the claimant is ordered to state all his claims together, and if he refuses, the defendant swears that he owes him nothing and is exempt from all those claims. He answers either that the claim now being advanced carries no oath, in which case the defendant cannot say that otherwise he will be forced to swear repeatedly, or that the claimant's additional claims also carry no oath, so there is no reason to compel him to state all of them at once. Sema. Likewise, if the claimant does not object to the defendant swearing generally in beit din that he owes him nothing on any claim, but nevertheless wishes to sue him later at a more opportune time because he believes that he will then have evidence, he cannot be forced to state every claim at once. The defendant will not swear twice, since he already swore generally, and if the claimant later cannot establish his case by proof the earlier oath has already exempted him. Shach. This too is correct as a matter of law.
The claimant is heard first. If Reuven sued Shimon, Shimon refused to respond and is a violent person whom beit din cannot compel, and afterward Shimon sues Reuven on unrelated claims, Reuven need not answer until Shimon first responds to Reuven's original claim. Sema in the name of Mahari Weil. This is clear.
