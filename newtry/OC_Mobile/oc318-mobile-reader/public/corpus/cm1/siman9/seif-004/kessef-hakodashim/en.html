Seif 2. And he sent a gift to the judge. Maharik, roots 16 and 21, discusses benefits received by a judge where it is not explicit that they are connected with the litigation and presents several grounds for permission. Regarding the horse mentioned there, one might initially think that the benefit was of the type where one person benefits and the other loses nothing, because otherwise, if the benefit was received between hearing the claims and rendering judgment, there would be reason for Torah-level stringency. A gift need not be verbally identified as a bribe for bribery to exist; otherwise the prohibition could rarely be enforced except where both parties expressly stated their corrupt intent after warning. The better understanding, however, is that Torah bribery requires that the payment be accepted as a bribe in connection with the case. The fact that an item normally has rental value does not by itself determine the matter. As with interest, even a benefit below a perutah can matter where people are ordinarily particular about it, while something genuinely treated as ownerless and insignificant, like a splinter taken from a fence to pick one's teeth where nobody objects, is outside the concern. Thus one cannot simply say that Maharik speaks only of an item with no rental value. There is no independent Torah prohibition upon the giver of a bribe parallel to "you shall not take a bribe." The giver's prohibition ordinarily arises through placing a stumbling block before the judge. Therefore, if the judge knows with certainty that he is accepting something he would have accepted even without the litigation and not as a bribe, the giver's hidden corrupt intent, even if it existed, does not make the judge transgress, and consequently does not create a stumbling-block prohibition for the giver. In addition, we do not presume people wicked without evidence, and when the gift is one normally given regardless of litigation we should not assume a corrupt motive. This explains Maharik's rejection of suspicion against the judge. We retain his presumption of fitness and do not assume that he abandoned a permissible gift in favor of a forbidden bribe. This may also explain the ruling in Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 9, that a gift regularly given to the judge is not bribery even if he accepts it after learning that the giver's case will soon come before him. If the judge nevertheless feels that the gift has awakened special affection, pious conduct may require withdrawal. Maharik distinguishes ordinary affection, which does not legally disqualify a judge, from the unusually intense friendship that does. The lesser emotional leaning therefore belongs to pious caution, not strict disqualification. I have elsewhere suggested that the authority permits this because it is an advance bribe received before the claims, which would be only rabbinic. That explanation is insufficient. An advance bribe is still prohibited rabbinically, and where the judge already knows that the litigation is immediately forthcoming the situation is very close to receiving it during the case. If the item were not something ordinarily given, its timing would itself reveal a connection to the case. Even a doubt whether it would otherwise have been given should seemingly be treated as a Torah doubt, since the giver's conduct then appears to signal bribery. The end of Ketubot 105 likewise shows that an unexplained payment to a judge can be full bribery, and the only permission there is where both sides pay equally as compensation for lost time. We do not reinterpret an unexplained payment as a random gift merely because the judge subjectively does not intend to be influenced. Accordingly, the sound reading is that the permitted case concerns something the giver regularly would have given regardless of litigation. In such a case no defect attaches to the receipt itself. I have written elsewhere concerning gifts that there may sometimes be a double doubt during an ongoing proceeding, for example with Purim gifts or a customary fee for writing a summons: perhaps the giver would have given this amount anyway, and perhaps even if the pending case influenced him, he gave it only because he expected the judge to attribute the gift to the ordinary custom rather than to bribery. That second doubt alone is not enough, because even a bribe given with only a hope of influence can be a bribe. But where a fixed social custom determines the amount, such as an established writing fee or customary arbitration fee, there may be no reason even for pious withdrawal. The same is true where arbitration fees are customarily equalized between the parties. If one party places his fee first, perhaps the other would have chosen that same amount on his own, and the first party would then have matched it. Where proceedings have not yet begun and there has only been an attachment to preserve property, with no known dispute over the merits, the situation is even more like a gift before summons, since the attachment itself is not the commencement of adjudication. Still, if a real contested case later develops and the rabbi must engage in substantial deliberation, the preferable course is to return what was received from both sides at that time. Doing so removes the concern according to all views.
