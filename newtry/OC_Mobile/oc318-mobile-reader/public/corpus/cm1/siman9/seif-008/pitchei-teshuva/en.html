To support their judges and sages. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 164, discusses rabbinic stipends. He writes that the Torah was given equally to all Israel, who are obligated to fulfill “You shall meditate in it day and night.” Ideally, people should work only enough to obtain their daily necessities and devote the remainder of the day to the service of Hashem, or work during particular seasons needed for their livelihood, such as the days of Nisan and Tishrei, while the remaining times are holy to Hashem. A person should therefore not seek luxuries; the more he separates from excess, is satisfied with little, and thereby gains time for Torah and service, the more holy he is. During the time designated for Torah and service, if someone comes to learn from him, to ask him to judge a dispute, or to seek a halachic ruling, he must do so without payment even though this diminishes his own learning, because in this respect his fellow's need takes precedence. During the time in which he works to support himself, his wife, and his dependent young children, however, his own needs take precedence, and he need not leave his work unless another person takes his place or he is paid the livelihood he would have earned that day. This is the recognized “compensation for evident lost time.” The community, however, is obligated to appoint someone who will remain ready and available at all times so that anyone who seeks the Torah of Hashem can find him. The community must provide that person's livelihood generously and honorably. Although if he were supporting himself he would be expected to live frugally, that obligation is his choice and not theirs. Certainly, if he has independent means and can support himself without taking communal compensation, he should perform the work without charge; but not everyone merits two tables. On the other hand, if the community does not provide him adequately and comfortably, it is not proper for him constantly to refuse and demand increases, thereby burdening an already burdened public. Concerning this, Ketubot at the beginning of the chapter Shnei Dayanei states that if the judges' support is insufficient, the public increases it for them even if they do not wish to accept more. The Gemara's expression cannot mean that they simply refuse proper support, as though dealing with wicked people, but that their support has become insufficient. Chatam Sofer explains elsewhere, simanim 22 and 166, that “it became insufficient” does not merely mean that food prices rose, for then it would be obvious why they cannot refuse after accepting appointment on condition that their bodily needs be met. Rather, their households increased. From this Rambam derived in Hilchot Shekalim chapter 4 that the support includes the judges themselves, their wives, children, and household members. Nechemiah praised himself, “I did not eat the governor's bread,” and contrasted himself with earlier governors who burdened the people. Although the Sages say he was punished for speaking disparagingly of those earlier officials, we nevertheless see the pious trait of not burdening the public and of being content with little. Yet because of the pressures of the times, there are situations of “It is a time to act for Hashem.” Just as the prohibition against writing the Oral Torah was relaxed for the preservation of Torah, certainly if we impose excessive stringencies of this sort upon Torah scholars there is danger that Torah study itself will be abandoned. In siman 166 Chatam Sofer sharply reproves the leaders of a city who wished to reduce their rabbi's fixed salary because of a change in currency. A Torah scholar appointed over the public must be provided with his livelihood, and if his support becomes insufficient it is increased even against his reluctance; all the more so it may not be reduced.
