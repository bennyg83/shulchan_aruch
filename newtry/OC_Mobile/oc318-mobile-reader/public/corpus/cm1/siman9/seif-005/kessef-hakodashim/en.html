See Sema, seif katan 7. The ruling cited in the name of Mahardach requires study whether its concern is Torah-level or rabbinic. It may fall under the verse that bribery "distorts the words of the righteous," meaning that even where the judge knows he has not actually been biased, the known receipt of a benefit may inhibit the other party's presentation. Yet this is not literally the legal category of bribery. If the opposing litigant knows that a gift was sent but does not know whether it exceeded the giver's normal custom, then even if an excessive gift could involve Torah concern, the opponent should judge the matter favorably rather than treat the unknown as a Torah doubt. We do not presume wickedness, and the judge's willingness to continue adjudicating itself supports the presumption that he recognized no improper deviation from the ordinary pattern. This is not because the judge's subjective sense that he is unbiased can erase the opponent's concern. The Torah itself teaches that bribery can blind even the wise. Rather, where the factual premise of an unusual gift is itself doubtful, the other party must preserve the presumption of fitness. The Torah's concern reflects the human tendency to feel gratitude toward one who benefits us. Even a person deeply devoted to Hashem may not exempt himself by saying that all causes are ultimately in Hashem's hand and that he therefore feels nothing toward the human agent. The Torah commands gratitude toward the agent of good, and bribery can create a sense of unity with the giver so that the judge no longer sees his fault. The same psychological mechanism can inhibit the opposing litigant's arguments. Nonetheless, where it is genuinely doubtful whether any unusual benefit was given, one must judge favorably. If a litigant sent something as a bribe and the judge refused it from the outset, there is plainly no concern. If he accepted it, spent it, and later returns its value in order to remove the bribery concern, it requires study whether return is fully effective, since the giver could refuse repayment and the judge may feel gratitude for the original benefit. My inclination is that repayment does help, though the point requires further study. I once had a dispute brought before me and the parties left intending to compromise. I completely put the matter out of mind. Months later one of them sent a beverage on Shabbat, at a time when other householders sometimes did the same, and I had no thought whatsoever that it related to litigation. Two months later the old dispute unexpectedly revived, with no other judges readily available. When I began analyzing the case, I first saw grounds favoring the sender and later a clear basis favoring the other side. I then worried that perhaps the very desire to avoid suspicion about the earlier gift could itself bias me against the sender. Before the matter concluded, however, the opposing party independently modified his claim after hearing arguments from those with him, and the case did not reach final judgment, so with Hashem's help I escaped the practical concern. The possibility of consciously leaning against the sender merely to avoid suspicion is itself something one need not ordinarily fear, by a fortiori reasoning from siman 9, but pious self-scrutiny remains proper. For many years I have arranged that I do not personally inspect the details of Purim gifts sent to my household. My wife receives them, and another person records them only so that reciprocal gifts can be sent. This goes beyond the strict law. Where there is a double doubt, there is generally no concern: perhaps the sender would have sent the gift regardless of litigation, and perhaps the larger amount resulted from a different reason, such as unusual prosperity or a desire to fulfill tzedakah obligations. Siman 9 likewise indicates that where the judge senses no bias from a regularly given gift, there is room for leniency even when he knows litigation is forthcoming. The concern is stronger between the commencement and completion of a case, particularly because the opposing party may feel inhibited if he learns of the gift. Therefore, where it is easy, one should take pious precautions, especially in a small town. In one case involving a townsman and a villager connected by marriage, I attended a brit meal during the pending litigation and warned the father not to give me the customary honorarium. Before the matter was resolved and Purim arrived, I likewise asked the townsman not to send a gift, and instructed my household to return it if he did. Strictly speaking there were several grounds for leniency because his annual custom was known and I do not review the individual gifts. Still, since the villager did not himself have a custom of sending, he might hear that the other party had sent something and wrongly assume I knew of an unusual gift. Because Mahardach is concerned with inhibition of the opponent's claims, avoiding the gift was the preferable pious course where easily possible.
