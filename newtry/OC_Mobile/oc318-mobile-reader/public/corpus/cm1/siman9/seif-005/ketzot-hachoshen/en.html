Give me compensation for my labor. If no fee was stipulated, the Sema holds that the litigant must pay the amount for which a person would agree to sit idle from the work he abandoned, as with one who returns a lost object. Taz disagrees and says that without a stipulation nothing is owed. He compares it to paying another's debt without authorization, where the debtor can say he would have persuaded the creditor without payment. Here too the litigant could say, "I would have gone to another judge." Taz adds that one who voluntarily benefits another without authorization is ordinarily treated like one who merely drives away a lion, whereas the finder of a lost object receives compensation because the loss was certain. That distinction between certain and uncertain loss is indeed stated by Tosafot. Nevertheless, the Sema is correct in the present case. The rule exempting one who paid another's debt concerns a benefit conferred without authorization. But if a person tells another, "Throw away your donkey and save mine," or, "Stop your work and lift my found object," he must certainly compensate him. If a debtor tells another person to pay his debt, he must reimburse him. Here the judge stopped his own work and attended to the case at the litigant's request. The benefit was therefore conferred with authorization, and compensation is due. If the judge adjudicated against the litigant's will, however, no compensation as a worker is owed. This is explicit in Yoreh Deah regarding a sage who examines firstborn animals. It is surprising that Taz himself rules there that in judicial work one may receive compensation like an idle worker. Even so, the Sema's measure appears too high. In Yoreh Deah, the Tur rules that where no fee was stipulated for examining a firstborn, the sage receives only the wage of a completely idle worker, not the amount one would demand to leave his particular occupation. Returning a lost object is different because that itself involves some labor and is not complete idleness. Judicial work is treated like the examination of a firstborn. Therefore the judge is entitled to payment, but only according to the rate of complete idleness.
