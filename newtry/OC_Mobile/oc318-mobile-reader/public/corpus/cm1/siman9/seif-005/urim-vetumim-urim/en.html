One who takes payment to judge. See what I wrote at length below in siman 34.
Unless it is known that he took no payment. Then that particular judgment remains valid even if the judge took payment for other cases. This is unlike a person disqualified by ordinary wickedness, whose disqualification extends broadly. The invalidity here is a rabbinic penalty, and the Sages applied it presumptively only where payment may have been taken. If it is known that no payment was taken for this case, they did not penalize it. Sema. Likewise, if it is established that today he took payment for judging, earlier judgments and testimony are not invalidated retroactively. We do not presume deterioration backward in time. We say the defect arose now. Knesset HaGedolah.
And he said to the litigants, give, etc. According to Sema, he must have stipulated in advance that they compensate him for actual lost work. If he made no stipulation, judged the case, and only afterward asks for compensation for his time, he is entitled at most to ordinary compensation for lost labor, like one who returns a lost object, as explained below in simanim 264 and 265. Taz disagrees and says that even this is not necessarily recoverable after the fact. The litigant can say, "Had you told me in advance that you wanted payment, I would have gone to a judge who was available and would judge without charge." This differs from returning a lost object. Taz's reasoning appears correct.
But if the loss is not evident, etc. There are three levels. If the judge was actually engaged in his work and stops it in order to judge, everyone agrees that compensation for that demonstrable loss may be stipulated and taken initially. If he was not working at that precise moment but is known to have a regular occupation and says that because of the case he is losing the opportunity to engage in it, taking payment is improper, but if he took it, the judgment is not invalid. If he has no known work at all and merely says that perhaps, if he were free, he might find some profitable occupation, then even if he already took payment and judged, his judgments are invalid. Sema and Perishah. In my view, this provides the basis for the accepted practice concerning full time rabbinic judges in our era. Because their occupation is judging disputes, they do not engage in commerce or other labor. Were they not carrying the public burden of judgment, they would plainly need some other means of livelihood. Their lost earning capacity is therefore comparable to a recognizable occupation. Moreover, where the accepted communal custom is to pay judges, litigants come to them on that understanding, as though the condition had been stated in advance. This is the basis of the common permission.
