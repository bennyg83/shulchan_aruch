Who borrowed an item. Birkei Yosef, letter 11, cites Responsa Devar Shmuel, siman 54, which was asked about a physician who habitually treated a judge without charge and later appeared before him in litigation. He answered that if the physician treats him for free there is an element of bribery, like the judge who habitually borrows items. He considered whether one should distinguish between a physician who had already been treating the judge before he ever needed to litigate before him and one who began treating him during the litigation, shortly before it, or afterward. If the practice of free treatment existed previously and only later the dispute arose between the litigants, perhaps continuation of his normal treatment does not disqualify the judge because of bribery. Perhaps the same would apply where the judge had previously been accustomed to borrow utensils and later became a judge; continuing that prior practice might not disqualify him. He sought support from Yoreh Deah, siman 160, where a benefit the borrower had already been providing before the loan is permitted. Nevertheless, because of the friendship that existed between the judge and the physician who treated him free of charge beforehand, the judge should not hear his case initially. If the physician charges a fee, there is room to say that as an act of piety the judge should withdraw, but the opposing litigant has no power to disqualify him. Birkei Yosef questions this conclusion. He writes that the suggestion that prior free treatment, or a prior habit of borrowing utensils, does not create a bribery disqualification does not appear correct. The Tur says that any judge who needs to borrow utensils from his neighbors is disqualified, implying that even if no borrowed item is presently in his possession during the case, the very fact that he has borrowed and continues to need to borrow disqualifies him. Rashi similarly indicates this. The law of interest in Yoreh Deah, siman 160, provides no proof, as is clear upon examination.
