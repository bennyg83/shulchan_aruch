All the judgments he rendered, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah cites Maharit that the rule invalidating the judgments of one who took payment for judging applies where the judgment depended upon shuda d'dayanei, a matter Chazal entrusted to the judge's discretionary assessment. In such a case, once the judge took payment, the sages' entrustment of discretion to him has failed and the result is invalid. But where he ruled in accordance with clear Torah law, truth, and justice, why should the correct legal result become void merely because he improperly accepted payment? These are persuasive words. Kenesset HaGedolah discusses at length an apparent difficulty from the Gemara's question concerning Karna, who took an istira from the party who prevailed and an istira from the party who lost. The Gemara asks how Karna could do this, since the Mishnah says one who takes payment for judgment has his judgments void. Why not answer that Karna accepted payment only in cases that did not involve shuda d'dayanei? There is no question. Maharit does not say that initially one may accept payment for judging a case whose law is clear. Heaven forbid. The prohibition against taking payment for Torah and judgment is learned from "just as I taught gratuitously, so too you must teach gratuitously," and there is no distinction in that prohibition between discretionary and nondiscretionary cases. Maharit addresses only the rabbinic penalty imposed after the judge violated that prohibition. Chazal voided his acts as a sanction. Maharit says that where his ruling was determined by clear law and did not depend on his personal discretion, there is no basis to undo a substantively correct result. The Gemara's question concerning Karna is therefore straightforward: how could Karna initially accept payment at all? The Mishnah teaches the penalty for violating the Torah-based prohibition, but the prohibition itself applies equally to every judgment. Rama's words in Even HaEzer, however, appear to disagree with Maharit. There Rama labors to uphold a get arranged by a rabbi who accepted payment. A get does not involve shuda d'dayanei, yet Rama discusses whether the get itself could be invalidated. His discussion appears directed not merely to the initial prohibition against taking money, but to the after-the-fact validity of the act. This suggests that Rama does not accept Maharit's limitation. Parenthetically, Rama writes there that a get does not require a beit din. That is indeed the implication of the Gemara. Tosafot in Kiddushin 9, however, write that the husband writes the get in beit din. I also found in Targum Yonatan on "and he shall write her a bill of severance" the phrase that it is written at the gate of beit din. This may be the source of the custom to arrange a get before a beit din.
