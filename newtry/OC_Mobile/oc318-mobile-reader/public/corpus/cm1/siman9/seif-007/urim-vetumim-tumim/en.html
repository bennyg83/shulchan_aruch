Distance yourself from a false matter. In Sanhedrin the Gemara derives the rule from "you shall not fear any man", meaning that a judge should not hold back his words out of fear. In Shevuot the rule is derived from "distance yourself from a false matter," and Shulchan Aruch follows that formulation. We must say that where the student actually knows an injustice is being done to the poor litigant, the prohibition of "you shall not fear" obviously applies. The case here is subtler. The student believes that according to strict law the poor litigant should lose, yet he also feels certain that, as a matter of factual truth, the wealthy litigant must be lying. Why would a wealthy person, surrounded by possessions, sue a destitute person who has nothing? Conversely, poverty can tempt a person to deny a debt in order to satisfy hunger. The student may therefore think: I will remain silent. Perhaps the poor man is legally unable to prove his case, but truth is surely on his side. For this Torah says, "Distance yourself from a false matter." There is no truth more absolute than the truth of Torah law. Human assumptions are vanity. Wealthy people sometimes lie, deny, and oppress. Poor people sometimes guard even the smallest coin with complete honesty and refuse to wrong another. Therefore the student may not suppress the legal truth because of social assumptions about rich and poor. This explains why the example speaks specifically of a poor litigant. The same principle certainly applies in reverse to a wealthy litigant as well, as Maharshal writes. See Urim.
