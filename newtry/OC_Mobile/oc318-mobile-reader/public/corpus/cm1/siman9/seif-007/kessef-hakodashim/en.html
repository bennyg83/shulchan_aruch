Seif 5. From both of them equally. I have written elsewhere concerning arbitration or judgment fees where one party gives more than the other, and the second then increases his own payment so that the sides become equal. Under the common practice, the judges first finish deliberating while the litigants are outside, agree upon the ruling or compromise, and designate the senior judge to announce it. Only afterwards are the parties called in and the fee is given. At that point there is essentially no bribery concern, because the substantive decision has already been fixed. This is unlike late interest, where an unusual later payment naturally appears to compensate for the loan. Here everyone knows that the judges will not reconsider the agreed ruling after receiving the fee, so the money is compensation for their work rather than an inducement. If one party whispers to the other, "Give twice what I gave," and the second does so, after which the first adds enough to equal the amount, there is no basis for suspicion, since both intended the same equal fee and the first was not embarrassed to acknowledge the need to add. More generally, where any remaining concern is only rabbinic, there are several doubts: perhaps the giver's purpose is not to influence at all; even if it is, perhaps the recipient does not accept it as a bribe; arbitration itself does not always involve the Torah prohibition of distorting a legal judgment; and perhaps the small benefit of prompting a larger fee is no more than the slight favor for which only pious judges withdrew in the Gemara. The best practice, however, is plainly to receive fees only after the compromise or judgment has been stated and accepted, when no possible influence remains. This does not create even an appearance of impropriety according to ordinary social understanding. People may say that greasing a wheel makes the wagon move faster, but here justice must precede the payment: "Righteousness shall go before Him, and He shall place his steps upon the way." Once the judgment has already been fixed, the later fee is judged at its own time and does not retroactively taint the deliberation. This is especially true in compromise, where the matter is already inherently dependent upon consent and estimation. I have also written that when one party places a generous judgment fee on the table before the other and, by the known custom of equal contribution, the other then gives the same amount, there is at least a double doubt. Perhaps the second would have chosen that amount even had he paid first, and perhaps neither giver intends bribery at all, but merely compensation for lost time or effort. Public payments placed openly on the table normally do not create emotional influence. Torah bribery requires a payment given as a bribe; the broader rabbinic concern may still remain, so pious conduct is to avoid receiving the money before the ruling has been announced and even before final deliberation. In one such case I told the parties I did not wish to take the money. They insisted, placed it on the table, and left while further investigation was still needed. I instructed another person to hold the money as a deposit, and it remained with him. There is no analogy here to stealing in order to annoy or in order to pay double, because the money was voluntarily given. Even if I had taken it into my own possession with the express intent to return it before continuing deliberation, the concern of theft would not apply. Still, leaving it with a third party is the preferable course because it prevents even a remote sense of personal closeness until the case is over. One might argue that the claimant is the person who initiates the case, thereby causing both sides to pay the judges, so the judges receive an indirect benefit from him even when the fees are equal. This might seem similar to the benefit enjoyed by craftsmen because a customer chose them rather than others. The concern could be greater when the claimant selects a judge from another city, or in a town where the defendant has little practical choice but to appear before the local judge. Nevertheless, the established Jewish judicial custom provides a strong defense. Once equal fees from both sides are a fixed public institution, they are payments the judges would receive in the ordinary course regardless of a particular claimant's motive. Everyone knows they are not given as affection or inducement. Moreover, the summons and the expected fee arise together, so it resembles a customary gift before litigation rather than a special payment prompted by bias. The present practice is still better when the judges first finalize the entire ruling, then bring in the parties, and only then both place equal fees. At most this resembles a possible late bribe, which is a rabbinic concern and subject to leniency in doubt, while the essential duty remains to guard the heart from bias. In reality a claimant almost never files suit in order to enrich the judges; his purpose is to recover what he believes belongs to him, and the fee is usually an incidental consequence that barely enters his thoughts. Bribery is fundamentally connected with awakened personal affection, and that emotional mechanism is absent where the payment is routine, equal, and institutionally expected. Even so, it would be preferable to establish communal systems that remove the question entirely. Rabbinic salaries should be arranged honorably and predictably through community revenue. In some countries, small fixed levies on widely distributed commercial production can supply part of the support. Other communities can use systems suited to their local economy. In smaller communities where fixed salaries are impossible and judges must be supported from case fees, a separate collector or court attendant can collect the fees, or the parties can place them into a designated receptacle. The judges can receive a fixed salary while another person is assigned the income from case and compromise fees according to a published schedule based on the value or type of case. Similar fixed assessments can be attached to weddings, engagements, appraisals, contracts, compromises, judgments, gittin, chalitzah, estates, britot, communal meals, derashot, Purim gifts, Chanukah, festivals, and other community events. The precise method should be supervised carefully and adapted to each place. Although the practices inherited from earlier generations have sound defenses, later generations should improve the system where possible, following the ideal described by Maharam Alshich and removing every opening for suspicion. One cannot judge the first settlers of communities harshly, since exile and economic pressure may have made better arrangements impossible, but later generations can add higher standards of piety where circumstances permit. Where the parties place unequal amounts on the table but the judges do not know which party gave more, there is likewise no meaningful concern of early or late bribery, even if the different coins reveal that the amounts were unequal. So long as the judges do not intend to inquire and cannot know who gave more without asking, no personal leaning arises. The litigants likewise know that the judges cannot identify the larger giver, so their arguments should not be inhibited. Payment to a beit din for drafting a written record of testimony also need not create witness-interest merely because only one side pays the writing fee. The judges' hearing of the testimony can be viewed as the substantive judicial act, completed before the later fee for writing. Even where the subsequent pleading has not yet occurred, their reception of testimony determines what the testimony is and can be written by two of the three if necessary. Where the record will be verifiable by the court itself, the payment is for the later writing service. If, however, the beit din later forgets the substance and would rely only on a paid written record, the matter may require further study.
