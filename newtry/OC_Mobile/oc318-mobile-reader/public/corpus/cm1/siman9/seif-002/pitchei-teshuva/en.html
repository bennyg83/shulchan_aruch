When the giver demands it from him. See Baer Heitev, who writes that without a demand, etc. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 135, explains that even if the giver did not demand repayment for a long time, so long as he did not expressly waive it he may demand it whenever he wishes. Until he makes a demand, beit din does not act on his behalf. If, however, the judge wishes to fulfill his obligation before Heaven and repent, he must return the bribe even without a demand. Regarding the statement that this differs from interest, see the Taz, who disagrees with the Sema and holds that repayment of interest likewise requires a demand; he writes the same in Yoreh Deah, siman 161, seif katan 3. The Bach, Urim VeTumim, Shaar Mishpat, and Shevut Yaakov agree. The Taz further records a case where a debtor had nothing from which to pay except money that another person had taken from him as fixed interest, and the creditor sought to collect that interest from the recipient by virtue of Rabbi Natan's rule. The Taz rules that the recipient need not pay it. Panim Meirot, part II, end of siman 74, disagrees. In his view, once the debtor's assets are encumbered to his creditor, every right that comes into the debtor's hands is likewise encumbered, and the debtor cannot now waive it. Although he may have waived the matter when paying the interest, the Torah did not recognize that waiver, and since he retains the right to demand repayment, the creditor is effectively the interested party and the debtor must pursue the claim. Moreover, the Torah's concern for the debtor's physical sustenance certainly implies concern for his soul, so that he should not remain within “the wicked borrows and does not repay.” Shevut Yaakov likewise writes that in Mishpetei Yaakov he rejected the Taz's ruling. Birkei Yosef, letter 1, writes that according to these later authorities, the same applies where a person gave a bribe to a judge and afterward owns nothing except the claim to recover that bribe, and his creditor seeks to collect it under Rabbi Natan's rule. The judge must pay the money, and the debtor should be compelled to demand it from the judge, because payment of a debt is a mitzvah and compulsion is used to enforce it. He brings some support from Sefer Chasidim, cited by the Shach below in siman 366. Just as one who must repay a creditor is required to accept back stolen money or interest even where ordinarily the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with one who takes it back, so too where the creditor can collect only from a bribe or interest, the recipient must return it and the debtor or bribe giver must demand it. This is contrary to the Taz. Birkei Yosef adds that all this is where the debtor or bribe giver makes no further claim. If he asserts that after giving the bribe or interest he fully waived his right to recover it, privately and sincerely, it requires consideration whether his statement is believed so as to cause loss to his creditor. He then cites Shevut Yaakov's view that until a demand is made beit din does not intervene, though one who wishes to satisfy Heaven must return the bribe even without a demand. Lechem Yehudah similarly explains Rambam's words, “he is obligated to return it when the giver demands it,” as meaning that when the giver sues for the bribe it is recoverable through beit din, analogous to interest; without a demand, beit din does not remove it from him, although he himself is forbidden to retain it if he seeks to avoid enjoying forbidden gain. Birkei Yosef, however, argues that the plain language of Rambam and the Mechaber implies that even one who wishes to satisfy Heaven need not return a bribe voluntarily without a demand, because in most cases the giver has waived it. This differs from interest, where a waiver at the time of payment is ineffective. In the case of a bribe, the giver's waiver at the time of payment is effective, and moreover a bribe is more commonly given with full willingness than interest; therefore they wrote that it need be returned only when demanded. He notes that on this approach one might somewhat reconcile the dispute between the Sema and the Taz and those who agree with him. Birkei Yosef further investigates whether, in the law of bribes, the rule resembles interest in Yoreh Deah, siman 161, where there is no lien upon the recipient's property, and whether a judge's heirs would likewise be exempt from repayment. See further Ketzot HaChoshen, Netivot HaMishpat, Meshovev Netivot, Shaar Mishpat, Divrei Mishpat, Divrei Chaim, Hilchot Dayanim siman 2, Beit Yehudah, Ateret Tzvi, and Tiferet Yaakov, and what I wrote in Pitchei Teshuva to Yoreh Deah, siman 161, seif katan 5. Shevut Yaakov also discusses a case where an agent demanded from a judge the return of a bribe that he had delivered on behalf of another person. The judge denied receiving it and also argued: even if your account is true, you are not my litigant; if the principal sues me I will know how to answer him. Shevut Yaakov examines whether this is a valid defense, discusses Terumat HaDeshen, siman 308, cited by the Shach in siman 121, seif katan 42, and concludes as follows. The principal certainly cannot impose an oath on the judge because he does not know whether the judge received anything. The agent's report cannot supply that knowledge because the agent is an interested party and is not believed for this purpose, as explained in Terumat HaDeshen and in the Rama to Shulchan Aruch, siman 75, seif 23. Therefore the principal cannot impose an oath except in the agent's presence. For the same reason he cannot impose an oath upon the agent alone merely because he does not know the facts, unless the judge first contradicts the agent, as explained in siman 121, seif 10, and the Shach there, seif katan 58 and 59. The agent likewise cannot sue the judge unless the principal first makes a claim against the agent, because perhaps the principal waived the bribe. So long as the principal makes no demand, beit din cannot compel the judge to return it, as implied by Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in siman 9, which state that the judge must return it when the giver demands it. If, however, the principal makes clear that he did not waive what was given to the judge through his agent, the agent may require the judge to swear; afterward the agent himself swears that he delivered the money and is thereby discharged from liability toward the principal.
