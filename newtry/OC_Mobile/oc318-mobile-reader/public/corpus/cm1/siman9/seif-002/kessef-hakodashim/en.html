I further found. The Sages describe judges who, as a matter of piety, withdrew after a litigant performed a slight favor such as removing a feather. In the laws of interest, however, we rule that an advance greeting or other courtesy is problematic only where it is done because of the loan; something the person would have done anyway is permitted. Yet the feather incident appears to involve the sort of ordinary kindness one would commonly perform even without litigation. This suggests that judicial piety is stricter. Still, there is certainly no concern where the same conduct would occur identically regardless of who performs it. For example, if a congregation customarily waits for its av beit din before beginning prayer, and a litigant who happens to be leading the prayers waits for him in the ordinary manner, there is no personal benefit attributable to that litigant. The same would have occurred with any prayer leader. Moreover, mitzvot were not given for personal benefit, so there is no basis even for a pious disqualification in such a case. The feather incident may have involved unusual speed or eagerness that revealed the pending case as the reason for the favor. If, however, the Sages intended pious withdrawal even where the favor would certainly have been done anyway, the distinction from interest may be that the concern in judging is not the objective value of the benefit but the subtle psychological effect it may create. Even where reason can afterwards dismiss the benefit as something that would have happened regardless, the initial sensation of gratitude may disturb the judge's thoughts. This resembles the rule that even the effort of suppressing distracting thoughts can itself disturb prayer. Perhaps, therefore, pious conduct requires avoiding a case whenever the favor actually awakened even a fleeting sense of closeness. On the other hand, it is possible that even in the Gemara the favor was one that would not have been done but for the litigation, in accordance with the rule governing interest. This requires further study.
