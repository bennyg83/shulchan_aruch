If he did not know of it, she has no ketubah from the third husband. This cannot mean that at the time of marriage he did not know and after marriage he learned of it but wishes to keep her, for then it is obvious that he must write her a ketubah, since one may not keep a wife without a ketubah. Rather, the case is that immediately upon learning he wishes to divorce her, or he died without ever learning of it, and the heirs do not pay her ketubah. In truth, Rambam, chapter 21 of Hilchot Issurei Biah, writes simply that if she married she need not leave, which ordinarily means that the marriage remains fully valid and she is not sent away without a ketubah. Rambam himself explains in a responsum that the status of a woman whose two husbands died is not a formal prohibition but a matter of ominous sign and fear, affecting weak constitutions. He records that some sages advised her not to marry, but if she found someone willing to betroth her they did not require divorce, and courts would sometimes arrange the kiddushin and then write her a ketubah and bring her to chuppah. This was the practice of the beit din of Rav Yitzchak Baal HaHalachot, of Rabbi Yosef HaLevi his student, and later authorities, and Rambam says they did so in Egypt as well. Rosh, however, holds that he is compelled to divorce her, as the Tur cites here, because danger is treated stringently. According to the Rosh the husband could certainly claim that the marriage was entered into by mistake. The Mechaber has combined the Rambam and Rosh in a way that is not compelled. Especially according to our ruling that a husband cannot ordinarily compel his wife to accept a divorce because of Rabbeinu Gershom's enactment, it is difficult to allow him to expel her against her will merely because he did not know she was a katlanit, according to Rambam, who does not treat this as a complete defect. Indeed the Rama below, siman 117, rules that only epilepsy or another major defect permits compulsion, but not ordinary defects.
