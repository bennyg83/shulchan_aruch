When the giver demands it. The Sema writes that a judge who took a bribe need return it only if the giver demands repayment. This differs from fixed interest, which must be returned under the verse, "your brother shall live with you." Taz cites Rashi in Bava Metzia, who also speaks of returning fixed interest where the borrower demands it. A practical question arises where a debtor has no assets except fixed interest another person took from him, and his creditor seeks to collect that interest through Rabbi Natan's lien. Taz concludes that the recipient need not pay because until the borrower demands return, there is no collectible property. In my view, however, repayment of debt is a mitzvah and beit din compels the debtor to fulfill it. If he has the power to demand money that would enable him to pay, beit din can compel him to say, "I want to demand it," so that he will not remain a wicked borrower while able to pay. Mishneh LaMelech similarly rules that where collection of a gift depends upon the woman's demand, beit din may compel her to demand it so that her creditor can collect. There is a difficulty, however. If the demand is forced, perhaps it is not legally voluntary. Coercion to sell can create a valid transfer because one who is compelled to perform a mitzvah ultimately says, "I want," but coercion to acquire is different. Rama, citing Baal HaItur, rules that coercing someone to buy does not create the same resolved intent. Here compelling him to demand repayment resembles compelling him to acquire. This also explains why those liable for sin offerings are not generally subjected to ordinary collateral enforcement, despite the principle that one can be coerced until he says, "I want." With an olah he is transferring property to consecration, while a sin offering requires his affirmative desire for atonement. Coercion cannot create such acquisitive consent. Knesset HaGedolah nevertheless rules that a borrower can be compelled to demand fixed interest so that he can repay his creditor. Yet if the borrower already waived the interest or bribe, the matter changes. Before a demand, Rabbi Natan's lien has not attached because according to this approach no debt exists until demand. Once valid waiver occurred, beit din cannot cure the matter through coercion. Regarding interest, Rambam holds that waiver can be effective, while the Geonim disagree on the ground that interest is always taken with consent and the Torah nevertheless requires return. According to the Geonim, waiver is ineffective and beit din can still compel a demand. If waiver occurred after demand, then with bribes Rabbi Natan's lien has already attached and later waiver is ineffective against the creditor. With interest, even after demand, the obligation derives from "your brother shall live with you" and resembles charity or severance gifts, to which Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply in the same way. Ultimately, however, the more fundamental point is that fixed interest does not require a demand at all. The Gemara in Bava Kamma says that during the rabbinic enactment encouraging repentance, robbers and interest takers who returned what they took should not have it accepted from them. The very fact that they were expected to return it even when the borrowers were not demanding it proves that the obligation exists without demand. Tosafot explains that according to the opinion fixed interest cannot be judicially recovered, there is likewise no obligation to return it even to satisfy Heaven. The sugya therefore follows the opinion that fixed interest is judicially recoverable, and it nevertheless assumes return without demand. This also fits the Geonim's position that waiver does not help because the Torah did not accept the borrower's consent. Knesset HaGedolah likewise concludes that fixed interest requires no demand. Thus, regarding a bribe, a demand is indeed necessary. Before demand the creditor of the giver has no lien on the bribe, and coercion to create the demand is problematic as explained. If the giver waives before demand, the waiver is effective according to Rambam and no later coercion can restore the right. After demand, however, the judge's obligation is fixed and Rabbi Natan's lien can attach, so a later waiver cannot prejudice the giver's creditor. Regarding interest, by contrast, the obligation exists even without demand.
