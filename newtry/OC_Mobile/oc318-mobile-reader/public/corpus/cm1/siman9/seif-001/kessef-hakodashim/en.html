Seif 1. Bribery through words. Regarding the pious conduct of judges who disqualified themselves after a litigant performed a small service for them, such as brushing a feather from their clothing, it appears that this applies when the service benefits the judge alone and gives no corresponding benefit to the litigant. Although the benefit is worth less than a perutah, it creates personal closeness. It is not necessarily a Torah prohibition as a half measure, because the rule of half measures applies where the small amount can combine into a prohibited measure, whereas an ordinary favor for which people do not demand payment may not even be forbidden to receive without the giver's explicit consent. Certainly, if the same person would have performed the favor even had no litigation been pending, the ordinary prohibition of bribery is absent. Nevertheless, the Sages teach that pious judges should withdraw, because a momentary closeness of mind may still arise. Perhaps, absent the pending case, the person would not have acted with the same speed or attentiveness, and that added promptness itself is a benefit. Where the litigant merely does something that primarily benefits himself, such as removing soot from a lamp on the judge's table so that the lamp burns better for everyone, there may be even less reason to see this as a favor to the judge, though because the benefit is immediate there remains room for pious caution. Handing bread to the judge at a meal appears still more remote from bribery when the bread belongs to the host and the person is merely passing it. If he passes bread equally to all diners there is plainly no issue. Even if he selects a cleaner piece for the judge, this is less significant than the acts of piety in the Gemara, especially where the host or another server might have given the judge the same piece in any event. If the judge accepted it without realizing that he alone was favored, then even if he later learns that it was especially fine and he enjoyed it, the matter remains at most a doubtful issue of pious conduct, in which there is substantial room for leniency.
