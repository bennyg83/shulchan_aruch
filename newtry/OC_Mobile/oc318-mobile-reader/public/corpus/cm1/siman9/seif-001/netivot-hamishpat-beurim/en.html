He must return it. There is room to ask whether one who hired false witnesses and they actually testified falsely must return the fee when sued, as one must return a bribe. It appears he need not. Interest and bribery are different because the Torah says 'do not take,' and an act the Torah says not to do is legally ineffective according to Rava. Thus the prohibited taking itself never validly transfers title, and the money remains the giver's. Regarding interest Abaye derives repayment separately from 'your brother shall live with you.' False testimony, however, is prohibited in the act of testifying, not in receiving a fee. Payment for the sinful act resembles the wage of prostitution, which need not be returned merely because the act purchased by it was forbidden. There is some support from Rashi in Rosh Hashanah, who implies that where the hired witness actually completed the condition, he may keep the payment as a matter of law. This appears clear.
When he demands it from him. See Taz, who says that interest likewise requires a demand. At first glance this is difficult, because bribery and interest are negative prohibitions corrected by restitution, and failure to fulfill the corrective command can produce lashes. Why should repayment depend on demand? One must say, as Urim VeTumim writes, that because the giver paid voluntarily, if he does not demand return we presume he waived it. Ketzot HaChoshen therefore is incorrect in saying beit din should compel the giver to say, 'I want to demand it.' There is a presumption of waiver. His proof from Mishneh LaMelech is also inapposite because there the liability already arose and only the timing was tied to demand. Indeed Mishneh LaMelech says beit din can compel the obligor himself, which clearly does not happen with a judge who took a bribe or a lender who received interest absent a demand. According to the Geonim who hold that waiver does not help concerning interest, no demand should be required, because the only reason to exempt absent demand is presumed waiver. Ketzot HaChoshen also tries to prove from the rule that one who repays interest is not accepted back for repentance but must still return it to fulfill his heavenly duty. That case can simply involve a prior demand that he refused and a later return after repentance. As for his answer to the early authorities' question why those obligated in sin offerings may be compelled if coercion would undermine consent, the proper analogy is to a coerced sale, not a coerced purchase. A person ordinarily wants atonement but his desire for money prevents him from dedicating the animal. Once coercion weakens that impulse, the atonement itself functions like consideration, just as Tosafot treat fulfillment of a mitzvah as consideration. Since the coercion is upon giving over the animal while he receives the benefit of atonement, he completes the act under pressure, like one coerced to sell.
