Not to take a bribe. Chavot Yair discusses whether it is permitted to give a bribe to a non-Jewish judge. A non Jew is commanded regarding civil justice as one of the seven Noahide laws. If so, perhaps "you shall not take a bribe" is included in the obligation, and a Jew who offers such a bribe violates the prohibition against placing a stumbling block before another. Chavot Yair does not reach a definite conclusion. Nevertheless, the longstanding practice is to permit giving such payments, and people generally do not treat the matter as prohibited. The basis can be explained as follows. The reason bribery corrupts judgment is that once the judge receives money from a litigant, the litigant becomes psychologically close to him. This disqualifies a Jewish judge because relatives and comparable personal closeness create judicial invalidity. But among Noahides, relatives are permitted to judge one another. There is no greater personal closeness than a father toward his son, and everyone knows that a father's inclination toward his son exceeds the effect of a gift of a thousand dinars. Yet a Noahide father can judge his son. If so, giving money in order to secure a correct judgment, to vindicate the innocent and condemn the guilty, can be no more disqualifying than kinship itself. It is true that the Gemara explicitly states only that a Noahide relative may testify. Perhaps judging is different because the reason for disqualification is not a Scriptural decree alone but the concern that the judge will not perceive liability impartially. However, Bach, responsum 111, and the Gaon Mahartz, responsum 55, in discussing an animal violated by a non Jew, conclude that the non Jew himself can serve as both witness and judge in the relevant proceeding. The principle that a person is related to himself does not operate among Noahides in that manner. If a Noahide can judge even a matter involving his own property interest, there can be no greater bribe than that direct self-interest. This appears to provide a straightforward basis for the longstanding permissive practice.
