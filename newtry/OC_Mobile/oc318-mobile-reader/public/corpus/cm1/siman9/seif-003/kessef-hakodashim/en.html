Who borrowed an item. Regarding the pious rule that a judge who borrows an object from a litigant should not judge his case, it appears that returning the object does not cure the problem once the benefit was enjoyed between the beginning and end of the litigation. This is especially so where the other party knows of the benefit and may therefore feel inhibited in presenting his claims, as the Shach writes in the name of Mahardach. The benefit of borrowing cannot be "returned" merely by giving the object back undiminished, since the use itself had value. In the case of a monetary loan, one cannot reimburse the lender for the benefit of the use, because payment for the use of money would itself be interest. The judge should therefore withdraw from the case. Perhaps a pure loan is different from borrowing an object, because an object can lawfully be rented and thus has a defined use-value, whereas the Torah forbids charging for a loan of money. One might therefore say there is no legally recognized benefit of the kind relevant here. Nevertheless, if there is reason to think that absent the pending litigation the person would not have lent to the judge at all, or would have lent the money to someone else, or would have invested it through an iska rather than make a free loan, the judge has received a real practical benefit. The prohibition of interest does not erase that benefit as a psychological matter. Where there is a clear basis to conclude that the litigant would have lent to him in precisely the same way in any event, however, there appears to be no need even for pious withdrawal. The central duty is for the judge to examine himself honestly and guard against bias.
