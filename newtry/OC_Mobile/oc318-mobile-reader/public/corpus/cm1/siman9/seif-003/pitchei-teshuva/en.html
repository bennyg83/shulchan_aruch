The giver transgresses “You shall not place a stumbling block.” Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 136, discusses whether one who gives a bribe to a non-Jewish judge also violates “You shall not place a stumbling block,” since a Noachide is commanded concerning laws and courts. I cited this in Pitchei Teshuva to Yoreh Deah, siman 151. See what Urim VeTumim writes on the matter. Divrei Mishpat questions his discussion and later cites Ramban to Parashat Vayishlach, who writes in the name of the Yerushalmi that even a Noachide is warned against accepting a bribe; according to this there is no general permission. Likkutey Responsa Chatam Sofer, part VI, siman 14, likewise discusses this and concludes that there is absolutely no basis to permit giving a bribe to a Noachide judge in order that he pervert judgment. Apart from the wrongdoing of the giver himself, who seeks to commit injustice against his opponent, the giver also violates “You shall not place a stumbling block” by causing the judge to accept the bribe and distort just judgment. Even if the money is given to another person, such as the judge's friend, so that he will act in the giver's stead to influence the judgment, the giver likewise violates this prohibition; this is not considered merely placing a stumbling block before someone who then places another stumbling block. There are, however, circumstances in which giving money could be permitted. In earlier generations, or in distant countries where justice was not properly administered, a Jew might know with certainty that the law is on his side and that his opponent admits the debt, or that the debt is otherwise clearly established, yet it is difficult to collect from a non-Jewish debtor because the judge delays and favors the debtor as a member of his own faith, believing it virtuous not to press him on behalf of a Jew. In such a case it was permissible to give the judge money so that he would render a just judgment. Not only would the Jew not violate “You shall not place a stumbling block,” he would perform a mitzvah, for without his payment the judge would pervert the Jew's judgment and violate one of his seven commandments, whereas the payment saves him from that transgression. Similar cases are the subject of Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat, siman 176, seif katan 8, and certainly of Gittin 28b. See Chavot Yair further for why the Torah did not state a separate prohibition upon the giver of a bribe as it does regarding interest. Birkei Yosef, letter 3, discusses a non-Jewish judge appointed by a non-Jewish ruler who exercises arbitrary power. Reuven and Shimon came before him, and he postponed their case until the next day. Reuven, a God-fearing man, learned that Shimon had offered the judge a bribe to tilt the judgment and that the judge had agreed. Reuven asked whether he might preemptively bribe the judge so that the rightful party would prevail, since it was clear that if he did not pay the judge would take Shimon's bribe anyway. He argued that there would therefore be no prohibition of placing a stumbling block, because in this very case the judge would take a bribe regardless, and his payment would also save the judge from perverting justice and save Shimon from theft. Birkei Yosef discusses the matter at length and concludes that there is no permission to do so. See also what I wrote in Pitchei Teshuva to Yoreh Deah, siman 160, seif katan 1.
