And he sent a gift to the judge. The Taz, Yoreh Deah, siman 246, seif katan 7, writes that his father-in-law the Bach concludes that an av beit din may accept gifts in order to become wealthy, so that his words will be heeded by the public. The Taz objects that the gain is outweighed by the loss, because this creates a stumbling block: one who expects to have a case before the av beit din will send him a gift well in advance in order to win his favor, and there is no earlier bribe than this; moreover, the judge thereby receives payment for his Torah. Birkei Yosef responds that a responsum of a Gaon cited by the Tur and ruled upon by the Mechaber and Rama explicitly states that if a plaintiff sent a gift before litigation, the judge need withdraw only as an act of piety if he perceives that his mind has been drawn closer to the giver. Thus there is no inevitable stumbling block. If the judge knows that his mind has not been influenced, there is not even a pious reason to withdraw. Birkei Yosef further cites Responsa Rei'ach Sadeh, siman 19, that a judge who also serves as a scribe of legal documents and ketubot should charge a fixed fee, not increasing it for the wealthy or reducing it for the poor; if he accepts personal gifts from individuals, he is disqualified from judging them. Birkei Yosef notes that Baal HaIttur, in the section on choosing judges, cites Rav Nachshon Gaon that a document scribe who later becomes a judge may still receive writing fees, though perhaps the Gaon's case concerns a fixed writing fee. Birkei Yosef further writes that if Reuven is a generous wealthy man who, on festivals, Purim, the birth of a child, or other family celebrations, distributes gifts to the Torah scholars of the city, and later needs to litigate with another person, none of those who regularly partake of his bounty may judge him, and they are disqualified even through a selection procedure, as Maharikash writes in a manuscript responsum, siman 131. Birkei Yosef reports that some great scholars of his generation, including the author of Machaneh Ephraim and the author of Chazon Nachum, were careful not to accept gifts from wealthy people, whereas other great scholars, even avot batei din, were not careful about this. Perhaps they relied on the principle that gifts given neither at the time of litigation nor because of the litigation do not create a legal disqualification. Nevertheless, he considers Maharikash's stricter view primary. Pitchei Teshuva notes that it is unclear why these gifts are more severe than the gifts discussed by the Bach above, which he permits an av beit din to accept in order to become wealthy, a ruling Birkei Yosef himself defended against the Taz. Perhaps the distinction is that here the gifts are regular and recurring several times each year, creating a greater concern that the judge's mind becomes close to the giver, similar to a judge who habitually borrows, who is legally disqualified if the practice is regular. The matter requires study.
