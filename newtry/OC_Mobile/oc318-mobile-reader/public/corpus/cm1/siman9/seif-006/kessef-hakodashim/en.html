Seif 3. They were accustomed to establish a fund for the beit din. Maharam Alshich, responsum 52, discusses payment of the town scholar's livelihood so that there be no concern that contributors create personal closeness and judicial bias. He writes that the best arrangement is for the scholar to be supported from the communal charity fund. In these countries, however, ordinary charity collections often cannot provide even a third or a quarter of the needs of a town scholar in small and medium communities, and sometimes not even in larger ones. Many donors also designate their gifts for particular charitable purposes that cannot simply be diverted. We may nevertheless learn an important principle from Maharam Alshich. A charity fund necessarily has collectors and administrators, since collection requires at least two and distribution three. Yet their involvement in providing the scholar's support does not automatically create disqualifying personal affection. Their work is a mitzvah that could readily have been performed by others, and if they had not undertaken it, others would have done so, often gladly. Thus the scholar's personal livelihood is not dependent on these particular individuals in a manner that should bias judgment. Any affection he feels toward them for their involvement in a mitzvah is itself love of mitzvah, which should reinforce rather than weaken his fidelity to Torah law. This resembles the Torah's command not to favor a poor person or distort judgment because one litigant is righteous and the other wicked. Love of righteousness and hatred of wickedness are themselves Torah values, yet the Torah commands that those values not distort the judgment. Precisely because the affection or dislike is not a private personal interest, it can be subordinated to the Torah's judicial command. The same applies to communal charity administrators. We presume their intention is for the mitzvah rather than for personal attachment, and the judge knows that if they did not perform the task, others would. Direct private benefactors are different. They possess the power to increase, reduce, or discontinue the scholar's support, and their contribution could have been directed to many other Torah scholars or mitzvah purposes. Consequently the scholar experiences a direct personal benefit from their choice, and there is greater concern for closeness of mind and bribery. The fact that an eminent person can create benefit for a giver merely by accepting the gift does not reverse the analysis. The Torah prohibited bribery even for the greatest sages, because once the recipient accepts a gift, personal closeness can arise. In my own community, the money given for my support is collected separately from compulsory communal assessments. I did not wish to take from funds collected without each contributor's full consent. Instead, approximately ten or twenty householders contribute voluntarily through a designated collector. Most of the contributors are unknown to me. Even regarding those I know, there is a strong basis to say there is no judicial bias, since absent this voluntary collection they would be assessed through the communal leadership anyway. Their contribution therefore does not depend upon personal favor toward me and is analogous to a collection that would simply be performed by another collector if not by this one. If Maharam Alshich preferred a charity fund because the small number of known collectors could easily be avoided in litigation by having other local scholars judge their cases, the same can largely be done in the present arrangement. The contributors can remain unknown, and where a contributor becomes known there are only a few such cases, from which the scholar can withdraw. Strict law, however, appears more lenient, because the support is a gift he would certainly have received regardless of the litigation and is not given during the proceedings. Any stricter conduct is a matter of piety. The matter nevertheless requires further study.
