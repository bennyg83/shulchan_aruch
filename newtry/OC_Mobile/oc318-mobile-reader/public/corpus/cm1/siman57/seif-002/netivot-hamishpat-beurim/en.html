The apparent difficulty in Ran can be resolved by understanding the basic prohibition against holding an accommodation or paid document. The evil lies not merely in possessing another person's paper. A debt document affects purchasers and prior creditors. Even if the borrower agrees that the lender may keep it, third parties who did not consent can be harmed. This is why Ran distinguishes an accommodation document from a conditional document. In an accommodation document no lien ever took effect and the witnesses did not create publicity; retaining it creates a false appearance of an encumbrance and may harm purchasers. In a conditional document, however, the lien exists from the time of writing and the witnesses publicize it. If the creditor later fulfills the condition, collection reaches back to the original date. Purchasers who ignored that publicity caused their own loss. Therefore retaining such a document is not the same wrongful maintenance of a false lien. Ran compares this to a partially paid document. Where a balance of the principal still remains due, everyone agrees that the creditor may in proper circumstances retain the document, even against the borrower's will, because purchasers know that the instrument remains potentially enforceable. If payment occurs before the final due date, the creditor may have to write a receipt because he cannot compel the borrower to pay the remaining balance immediately. If payment occurs after the due date and the creditor could demand full payment, the borrower who chooses partial payment cannot necessarily compel a receipt; purchasers who buy despite the outstanding instrument assume the risk. Accordingly, Mechaber's language is precise. He first prohibits retaining the document merely for a scribal fee, but later permits retaining it when a real part of the debt remains, provided a receipt is written where the borrower is entitled to one. If the creditor has a right to insist on full payment and is retaining the document to secure the unpaid balance, a receipt may not always be required immediately. But where he has no right to retain the instrument against the borrower, leaving it outstanding without a receipt can be an injustice to purchasers even if the borrower personally consents. This resolves Shach's objections. Urim VeTumim suggests Ran speaks only with the borrower's consent. In my view Ran's comparison to scribal fees shows that in the relevant cases even the borrower's objection does not always require immediate surrender, because the remaining debt itself supports retention. The key distinction is whether third parties are justifiably entitled to rely on the apparent extinguishment of the lien.
