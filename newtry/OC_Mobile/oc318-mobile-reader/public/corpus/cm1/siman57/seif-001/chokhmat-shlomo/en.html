Seif 1. Provided that he writes a receipt upon the document indicating what has been paid. See the Shach, who objects that once a receipt is written, or the payment is recorded on the document itself, there is no prohibition in retaining it. Rashi in Shabbat 78, however, regarding one who carries a paid document, indicates that even where a receipt is written there remains a prohibition. The Gemara itself also seems to support this. It explains the dispute between the Sages and Rabbi Yehudah as depending on whether it is permitted to retain a paid document. According to the Sages it is forbidden, and therefore carrying it is exempt because it has no legitimate use. If merely writing on the back of the document made it permissible to retain, then even according to the Sages the document would still be useful, since after Shabbat it could be marked and used to cover a small vessel. One might answer that because it is Shabbat he cannot write on it now, and every moment he retains the paid document he violates the prohibition against keeping wrongdoing in his home. He therefore must return it to the debtor immediately on Shabbat and may not retain it until a weekday merely so that he can later write upon it. Once returned, it has no further use to him. Even though a document is muktzeh, since the creditor has already picked it up, the rule is that one who has muktzeh in hand may carry it to the place he wishes, so he can return it immediately. Nevertheless, Rashi’s wording remains a strong proof against the Shach. The Gemara also presents difficulty if a mitzvah-related need counts as a constructive purpose for Shabbat liability. If he is not obligated to return the document that day, it is still useful because after Shabbat he can write the payment upon it and then retain it for use. If he is obligated to return it immediately, carrying it is itself for the purpose of fulfilling that obligation and should count as purposeful carrying. One might say that a rabbinic mitzvah need does not create biblical liability for carrying. Yet the matter remains difficult from either side: if we assess according to rabbinic law, returning it is a legitimate need, while if we assess according to biblical law and there is no such obligation, the document remains fit for later use. See Tosafot in Shabbat, chapter HaOreg, concerning destructive acts and the dispute whether a mitzvah need is considered constructive, and see also Tosafot in Pesachim and Ketubot. At minimum, Rashi clearly supports the position contrary to the Shach. I later obtained Shaar HaMishpat and saw that he too noticed Rashi, though he did not address the remainder of these considerations.
