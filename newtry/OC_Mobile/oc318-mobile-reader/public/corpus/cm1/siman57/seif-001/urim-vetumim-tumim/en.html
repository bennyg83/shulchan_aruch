The same applies to a document whose lien has been waived. Although this resembles a paid document, the case is where only the lien upon land was waived while the underlying debt remains collectible from unencumbered property. Sema nevertheless says that it is forbidden to retain the document. Shach disagrees and holds that since the debt remains collectible from the borrower personally, the document is not included in the prohibition against keeping an unjust instrument. His view appears more correct. Rosh in Nedarim, in the case of one who repaid part of his debt, writes that the unpaid half remains collectible from unencumbered property. To prevent the creditor from using the original document to collect the entire amount from encumbered property, even the remaining half is treated as collectible only from unencumbered property. If retaining such a document were itself prohibited, how could the third party holding it ever return it? It would be forbidden for him to facilitate keeping an unjust document. If the case is that the third party writes on the document that half was paid, or provides a written record of the terms of his deposit as explained above in siman 56, then there is no concern that the creditor can collect the full amount from encumbered property. But if that concern is removed, why should the unpaid half lose its original lien on encumbered property? The two sides cannot both be maintained. We must therefore say that so long as the underlying debt remains collectible from unencumbered property, retaining the document is not considered keeping an unjust instrument. The third party may return it without any prohibition. Shach answers Sema's difficulty by saying that the law teaches two separate cases, partial repayment and waiver of the lien without repayment. This is strained, because once the lien is waived the document is still a mere shard with respect to its original land lien. The actual source of this ruling is Responsa Rosh, rule 74, concerning a document that passed through a Sabbatical year in a place where the remission of debts was observed. Its lien is treated as waived. Rosh there does not mention a prohibition against retaining the document. He discusses only whether the borrower can force its removal from the creditor. Rama added that it is also included in the prohibition against retaining an unjust document. One might otherwise argue that there is no injustice in retaining a document whose date itself shows that a Sabbatical year passed and that the debt was extinguished. Rama teaches that it is nevertheless prohibited, because we rule that a creditor is believed to say that he had a prozbul and lost it. If he dies and the document falls before his heirs, they may claim that perhaps their father had a prozbul and collect money improperly from the borrower when in truth he had none. For that reason it is forbidden to retain the document.
It is proper to place him under a ban, etc. Shach below, siman 81, seif katan 17, asks that there is an independent reason to place the creditor under a ban. The borrower demands, "Return my document, lest you collect from me a second time." Shach answers that the creditor can give him a receipt, and nevertheless we place him under a ban because the document remains an unjust instrument, since the receipt may be lost. This requires study. Tosafos in Bava Metzia 17 writes that according to the view that a receipt may be written, even where the woman still holds her ketubah she may retain it and give the husband a receipt initially. Although we do not rule entirely like Tosafos there, how can we go so far as to say that retaining the document constitutes an outright prohibition from the words of the Prophets and merits a ban? The original question can be answered differently. The Mechaber follows Ran, who holds that the creditor may not withhold the document merely because the borrower has not yet reimbursed him for the scribe's fee. This is because of the prohibition against retaining an unjust instrument. But based only on the borrower's private demand, the creditor could say, "I will not return the document until you pay me the scribe's fee. If you are afraid I will collect twice, then pay what you owe me and remove that concern." There would be no reason to place him under a ban merely because he refuses to return it before receiving his fee. Indeed, until one places the creditor under a ban, the borrower himself should first be compelled to pay what he owes. The novelty is therefore that because of the independent prohibition against keeping an unjust document, we place the creditor under a ban despite this argument. The difficulty disappears.
Provided that he writes a receipt. Shach raises a powerful question. The source is Ran in the second chapter of Ketubot. Ran asks why witnesses who say, "it was a document of trust," are not believed because they participated in an injustice, while witnesses who say, "there was a condition," are believed even though a condition might likewise create an unjust document. Ran answers that a conditional document resembles a document for one hundred on which fifty has been paid: such a document may be retained and contains no injustice. But if even such a document may be retained only where a receipt is written, Ran has not answered the question, for in his condition case no receipt or notation was written. It would then be just as improper as a document of trust. This strongly implies that even without a receipt such retention can be permitted. Yet if Shach's reading of Ran is accepted, a serious difficulty arises from Perek Eizehu Neshech in Bava Metzia. There the creditor added expected profit to the principal and trusted the debtor regarding the amount. The Gemara asks that although such reliance may work for the creditor himself, what happens if the document falls before heirs? We rule that the arrangement is forbidden because the heirs may collect the full amount even if the profit never materialized. But that arrangement is also a conditional obligation. The increased sum is owed only if profit is earned. If an ordinary conditional document may be retained without a written notation, why was this forbidden? All the more so should we fear in an ordinary condition that the document may fall before heirs, who will collect absolutely and ignore the condition, producing actual Torah theft. Baal HaTerumot, gate 53, likewise writes that a document whose payment is doubtful may not be retained because perhaps it will fall before heirs. The distinction is this. Where a document is conditional from its inception with the borrower's knowledge and agreement, and the witnesses wrote it in that form with his consent, it may be retained. The borrower knowingly accepted the risk that if some later mishap occurs and heirs collect without applying the condition, the obligation will be treated accordingly. This resembles the law that where a person says that an item was entrusted to a third party under agreed terms, he is understood to have accepted the consequences of the third party's administration. His consent gives the condition its legal framework. The profit arrangement is different because it is forbidden interest. The borrower's consent cannot validate an interest prohibition. Every borrower receiving interest bearing funds may be willing to the arrangement, yet Torah law or rabbinic law forbids it despite that consent. Therefore, if the document may fall before heirs and the profit was never realized, their collection would be prohibited interest. The borrower's willingness cannot cure that concern. Accordingly, the Shulchan Aruch here can be understood. The case throughout is one where the borrower does not consent to the continued possession of the partially paid document. The creditor keeps it against his will. In such a case it is permitted only if the payment is written on the document or a receipt is delivered, because otherwise it may fall before heirs and produce wrongful collection. Ran, by contrast, discusses a conditional document created with the borrower's consent. There it is permitted even without a receipt or notation, because the borrower accepted the instrument in that form. This also resolves Shach's question why the Shulchan Aruch ruled like Ran that the creditor may not retain a document merely to secure the scribe's fee, when Tosafos and Rosh disagree and Bedek HaBayit itself notes their position, and the Gemara's phrase about withholding for the scribe's fee seems to support them. The case of withholding for the scribe's fee is comparable to one who has been paid only part of a larger debt. A perutah and one hundred are alike in principle. Tosafos and Rosh discuss the analogue of a trust document that came into existence with the borrower's consent. In that setting they permit retention, because the borrower accepted the arrangement. The Shulchan Aruch here addresses retention against the borrower's will, and therefore forbids it unless there is a receipt or notation. Thus the authorities are not necessarily in disagreement. One may still ask what novelty there is in saying that if the payment is written upon the document it may be retained. The receipt is then attached to the instrument. Perhaps the Mechaber follows the authorities who explain the fear of a document falling before heirs as concern that dishonest heirs may deny even a known condition. One might therefore fear that they will erase or scrape away the notation. Since the Mechaber follows Rashba above, siman 42, that an erasure or scraping does not automatically invalidate a document, such fraud might appear possible. The ruling teaches that we do not extend our concern so far. Likewise, if the receipt is handed to the borrower, one might ask what novelty exists, since the possibility that the receipt will be lost already appears above in siman 54, where a borrower who paid fifty on a document for one hundred may be required to rely upon a receipt. There, however, the loan had already matured. The creditor can say, "If you fear losing the receipt, pay the remainder now and take back your document." Here one might have thought that where partial payment is made before maturity, and the borrower cannot yet be required to pay the balance, retaining the original document would be unjust unless it were replaced. The ruling teaches that a separate receipt is nevertheless sufficient.
