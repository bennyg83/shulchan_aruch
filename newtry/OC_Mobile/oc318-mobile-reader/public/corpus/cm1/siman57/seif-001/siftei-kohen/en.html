The same applies to a note whose lien has been waived. The Sema explains that although every paid note loses its lien, a note may lose its lien even before payment, such as where the creditor waived recourse against the debtor’s land and permitted him to sell it without collection from purchasers. Alternatively, it may refer to a note that was paid and then reused for a new loan, as discussed in siman 48. The first explanation does not appear correct to me, because such a note may still be collected from the debtor’s unencumbered property and therefore does not violate the prohibition against retaining an instrument of injustice. Certainly this follows from what I write below, that even a note for one hundred upon which fifty was paid is not prohibited. In truth, no such explanation was needed. The ruling teaches that when the creditor voluntarily forgives the debt, even without payment, he may not retain the note. See also siman 61, seifim 11 and 12.

He may not retain the note because of the scribe’s fee. Tosafot in Ketubot 19b and the Rosh there state expressly that he may retain the note for the scribe’s fee. The Beit Yosef cites them in Bedek HaBayit and adds that the Ritva rules likewise. It therefore requires study why the Shulchan Aruch rules here without qualification in accordance with the Ran. In any event, it appears to me that if the creditor wishes to tear the note, or to write upon it that it has been paid, all authorities agree that he may retain it for the scribe’s fee. This is unlike the implication of the Mechaber, as explained below.
And in a place, etc. The Rama appears to understand the preceding ruling, that the creditor may not retain the note for the scribe’s fee, as applying even if he tears it, because the borrower pays the fee and the physical document belongs to him. The Ir Shushan implies this even more strongly. In my view this is incorrect. The present discussion concerns the prohibition of retaining an instrument of injustice. The Ran’s ruling addresses a case in which the borrower was obligated to pay the fee, the creditor advanced it, and the creditor now wishes to retain the fully valid paid note until reimbursement. Although the creditor may recover the fee from the borrower, he may not keep an intact paid instrument. The responsum of the Rashba cited in siman 61 and in Darkei Moshe to siman 48 addresses a different issue, namely ownership of the paper for incidental use, not the prohibition against retaining an enforceable paid note.
But it is permitted, etc. The Mechaber requires that the creditor write a receipt upon the note. This is difficult. The Beit Yosef cites the Ran as permitting retention of a note for one hundred when only fifty remains due, but the Ran plainly permits this even without writing a receipt. The Ran explains that a note issued upon a condition differs from a note issued in trust. In a trust arrangement no present obligation has arisen, whereas in a conditional note the lien takes effect immediately to some extent because the creditor can fulfill the condition. Such a note may therefore be retained, just as a note for one hundred may be retained when fifty remains owing. The Ran never mentions writing a receipt, and his comparison to a conditional note necessarily concerns a document bearing no notation. This is also evident from the Ran’s proof from the opinion in the Gemara that a fully paid note may sometimes be kept for the scribe’s fee. Had he meant a note bearing a written receipt, he could simply have cited the settled rule that a receipt may be written, including for partial payment. Even Rabbi Yehudah, who objects to a receipt because the debtor must guard it, does not regard the creditor’s retention of the note as a violation of the prohibition against keeping injustice in one’s house. Moreover, if the note expressly stated that it was retained only for the scribe’s fee, there would be no injustice, and there would be no reason for the opposing opinion to prohibit it. The Gemara compares a paid note retained for the scribe’s fee to a trust note. The comparison shows that the discussion concerns a note on which nothing has been written. The permissive opinion says that a paid note differs because it may be retained for the scribe’s fee; it does not say that it differs because a receipt is written upon it. The correct distinction is that a wholly paid note belongs entirely to the debtor and may not be held merely for an unrelated claim, according to the stringent opinion. But when part of the sum written in the note remains due, the creditor necessarily has a legitimate interest in the instrument. Retaining it is not maintaining injustice in his house, and failure to write a receipt is not itself wrongful, since parties frequently trust one another. Thus Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Ritva certainly permit retention even for the scribe’s fee without any notation. Even the Ran, who prohibits retaining a wholly paid note for that fee, permits a partially unpaid note without a receipt. The ruling of the Mechaber, that a note for one hundred after payment of fifty may be retained only if a receipt is written upon it, is therefore unsupported. The principal ruling is that it may be retained without such a notation.
