One who causes damage, etc. The Rambam writes at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Chovel: “One who damages another person’s property through damage that is not perceptible is liable. How so? If he rendered another person’s ritually pure food impure, mixed terumah into his ordinary produce, or mixed a drop of wine used for idolatrous libation into his wine, thereby prohibiting all of it to him, and similarly in every comparable case, the loss is assessed and he pays full damages,” end quote. From his formulation of a general rule, “one who damages another person’s property,” and also from his words, “and similarly in every comparable case,” it is evident that he rules like Rav in the chapter HaNizakin, at the beginning of Gittin 52. Were he to rule like Shmuel, the Gemara clearly implies that liability applies specifically to these three cases and no others, because one does not derive one penalty from another. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes there, regarding the Rambam’s subsequent statement that one who mixes libation wine is liable to pay, that this follows Rav, who states this in HaNizakin. The Rambam’s words here, “and similarly in every comparable case,” demonstrate even more clearly that he rules like Rav. Note this carefully. However, in the Ramban’s treatise on dina de-garmi I saw that he writes that the law follows Shmuel in monetary matters and that one does not derive one penalty from another. According to him, a person is liable only for rendering food impure, mixing terumah into produce, and mixing libation wine, but not for other forms of imperceptible damage. I likewise saw in Rav Hai Gaon’s Mishpetei Shevuot, page 19b, that he holds that one does not derive one penalty from another. This is also the position of the Rif at the end of the chapter HaChovel, concerning the incident in which one person preempted another and covered the blood of a slaughtered animal, and Rabban Gamliel required him to pay ten gold coins. The Rosh there also holds this view. A reason may be given for the Rambam’s ruling like Rav. In the Gemara in Chullin, at the end of the chapter HaShochet, 40a, it is evident that this is the accepted law, because the Gemara challenges from the case of one who mixes libation wine. That challenge necessarily follows Rav, as Tosafot state there. Why should the Gemara challenge if it might hold like Shmuel? It must be that the law follows Rav. See there. It further appears to me that Rabban Gamliel’s requiring the offender to pay ten gold coins was certainly a penalty, in accordance with the unnamed opinion cited by the Rif at the end of HaChovel. The Shiltei Gibborim in HaChovel, page 34a, likewise writes in the name of the Riaz that it appears to have been a penalty. See there. He does not accept the Rif’s view that it was strict law, for if it were strict law, why is it nowhere taught that one who snatches another person’s blessing or mitzvah must pay him ten gold coins? Moreover, if this were strict law, from where was the fixed amount of ten gold coins derived? It therefore appears to be a penalty. The Rif asks that, if so, how could Rav derive from that incident the law regarding an ox and a tree, since one does not derive one penalty from another? In my humble opinion, this is no difficulty. Rav follows his own position in HaNizakin that one does derive one penalty from another. The unnamed authority cited there by the Rif may hold that it was a penalty, while ruling in practice like Shmuel in monetary law, that one does not derive one penalty from another. This appears to me to resolve that authority’s view. At first glance the Rif’s position is difficult. According to the Rambam, one may say that on this point he agrees with the unnamed authority that Rabban Gamliel imposed a penalty, for the reason I wrote. It is then evident that the law is that one does derive one penalty from another. In the chapter Kisui HaDam, 87a, after the Gemara reports that Rabban Gamliel required ten gold coins, it asks whether this was compensation for the mitzvah or for the blessing, and explains that the practical difference concerns Birkat HaMazon. Why would the Gemara ask this? Even if it were compensation for a blessing, one should not derive one penalty from another. It must be that the anonymous Gemara holds that one does derive one penalty from another. See what I wrote further regarding the Rambam and the Mechaber above, siman 382, concerning one who preempted another and covered the blood. They hold that Rabban Gamliel’s ruling was a penalty and that one derives one penalty from another, as explained there. This is also evident from the authorities I cite below, siman 386, seif katan 1, who hold that dina de-garmi itself is a penalty and nevertheless apply one such penalty by analogy to another. See there.
From the best of his property. Meaning from his superior-quality land, as is the law of one who causes damage, below in siman 419. Although this liability is only rabbinic, the Sages enacted their law in a manner resembling Torah law and required payment from superior-quality land. So writes the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Kamma.
They did not impose the penalty upon his son after him. The Sema explains that this applies even if their father had already stood in judgment. He writes that the Mechaber rules similarly above, siman 108, seif 2, unlike the case of an informer who died, as stated in siman 388, seif 2. His words do not appear correct to me. The Rambam and the Mechaber below, siman 388, who rule that when an informer dies collection is made from his heirs even if he had not stood in judgment, do so for the reason I wrote there, namely that they hold that dina de-garmi is strict law and not a penalty. The alternative opinion there, which holds that collection from the heirs applies only if he stood in judgment, maintains that dina de-garmi is a rabbinic penalty and that the Sages did not penalize his son after him. He therefore is not liable unless he had stood in judgment. Accordingly, here too, if he stood in judgment the heirs are liable. This is explicit in the Mordechai in the chapter HaChovel in the name of Maharam, whose view is the alternative opinion cited by the Rama below, siman 388, seif 2. He writes: “One cannot infer that, since the payments imposed upon an informer are a penalty, we should say that the Rabbis penalized him but did not penalize his son, as stated in the chapter Mi SheHafach. I likewise heard that the Riva ruled regarding an informer that this applies only where the informer informed and the victim did not manage to bring him to judgment before the informer died. If, however, the informer stood in judgment and was found liable, the property became obligated, and the heirs no longer inherited it. Peace, Meir son of Baruch,” end quote. The same appears in Maharam of Rothenburg’s responsa, Prague edition, siman 599. He thus expressly invokes the principle that “they penalized him but did not penalize his son,” as stated in the chapter Mi SheHafach, where the discussion concerns one who renders food impure, mixes terumah into produce, or mixes libation wine. What the Mechaber writes above, siman 108, seif 2, “but if they obligated him in a penalty, they did not penalize his son after him,” refers to a case in which he was obligated to pay more than the actual loss. On the contrary, at the beginning of that seif the Mechaber writes that one who was held monetarily liable by a beit din because of informing, and then died, may be collected from his heirs. The correct explanation is therefore as I have written. See also what I wrote there in siman 108, seif 2.
