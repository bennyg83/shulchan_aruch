In the Tur. “And so it appears from the words of the Rambam,” etc. Rabbeinu uses this formulation rather than writing, “And the Rambam likewise wrote,” because Rabbeinu's principal novelty is that extraordinary measures may be taken even where there is no complete testimony, but only strong circumstantial indications. The first clause of the Rambam does not mention this. There, complete testimony exists, but the strict law would not impose lashes, and the Rambam rules that the court may nevertheless administer lashes. One may infer Rabbeinu's point only from the Rambam's latter clause, where he writes, “Likewise, a person of evil reputation may be flogged,” implying that there is no clear testimony. Even that clause, however, is not decisive proof, since it mentions only lashes. One might say that capital punishment requires clear testimony. Rabbeinu, however, writes that even where the evidence is not of the kind upon which liability would have been imposed when capital cases were adjudicated, extraordinary judgment may be rendered, implying that this applies even to death. Nevertheless, throughout the passage it appears that the Rambam also holds this way. In the first clause he explicitly makes no distinction between death and lashes. Presumably the same applies to the latter clause, since he writes, “Likewise, one may flog,” indicating that its rule parallels the first. He mentions lashes because the first clause mentioned them first, where the Rambam proceeded from the lesser case to the greater.
