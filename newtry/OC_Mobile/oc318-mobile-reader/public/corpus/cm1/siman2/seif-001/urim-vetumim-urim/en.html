When the people are sexually lawless. This does not necessarily mean the entire populace. Even a single offender can be punished beyond the strict law when the needs of the hour require it. When the public is broadly lawless, communal regulations can be established for everyone and violators can be punished even if a particular violator was not previously notorious. Sema. Perishah objects that extraordinary punishment must always be tied to an immediate public need. If only one person is sinful and the public is not, what need justifies departing from Torah law? When the majority are lawless, the need is clear because others must be deterred. This resolves Nimukei Yosef's question in the chapter Arba Mitot regarding the incident in which Rav Chama bar Toviyah wrapped a woman, a daughter of a kohen who had committed adultery, in bundles of branches and burned her. The Gemara says he erred because capital jurisdiction does not operate without ordained courts. Nimukei Yosef asks why this could not be justified under the rule that beit din may punish beyond the strict law when the hour requires it. The answer is that the generation was not generally lawless in that sin, Heaven forbid. She alone had transgressed, so there was no public necessity for an unordained court to impose death. Sema's intent can therefore be understood to mean that even before widespread lawlessness actually develops, if leaving a particular offender unpunished would encourage others to become lawless, that prospective danger itself constitutes the need of the hour. Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchot Rotzeach implies that when there is merely a need to frighten the public so others will not imitate the offender, but not an immediate emergency, beit din should use a punishment short of death. Death is reserved for a genuine public emergency. Accordingly, "the people are lawless in this offense" is the more precise formulation.
Whether death or money. Bnei Shmuel cautions strongly against assuming an unordained court may impose capital punishment and spill Jewish blood. Since we are ordinary judges and do not act as agents of the ordained courts for such matters, there is serious reason to refrain. Nimukei Yosef speaks of punishments close to death, such as cutting off a hand, rather than definite execution. Rivash brings proof from the case of Bar Chama, who killed a person, and Rav Huna ordered that he be blinded. That does not necessarily establish authority to execute. Blinding may create danger, but causing a possible danger is not the same as deliberately taking a life. Moreover, there were methods of blinding in which sight could be removed without endangering the body. Therefore great caution is required nowadays. Even according to Rivash, the strongest case is a deliberate murderer like the case there. Since a blood redeemer has a mitzvah or permission to kill the murderer once lawful judgment has been completed, extraordinary communal jurisdiction can arguably restore that role where needed to protect society. For other offenses, however, spilling blood is far harder to permit. See Rambam, Hilchot Rotzeach 4:9, where he distinguishes murder from other transgressions. The same distinction applies here.

He gives forty gold coins. One who sat on the ground to receive lashes, but the appointed official did not come to flog him and he was released, is exempt from the lashes. Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 17, cited by Sema. This applies where the local practice does not require the person to be bound to a post. Sitting in readiness is then equivalent to being bound, and once the restraint is released and he departs he is treated like one who escaped after being tied for lashes. Where local practice requires actual binding and he was never bound, the same exemption would not necessarily apply.
