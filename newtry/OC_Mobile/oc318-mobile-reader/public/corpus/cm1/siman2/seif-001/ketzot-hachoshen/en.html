Including all punitive laws. The Urim writes that even a blood redeemer has authority to kill only after the killer's case was concluded by beit din, citing Rambam. This is incorrect. Rashi in Perek Nigmar HaDin holds that even in accidental killing the blood redeemer may kill in some circumstances. Rambam indeed disagrees as to ordinary accident, but expressly rules that where the accident is close to deliberate wrongdoing and the killer therefore does not go into exile, if the blood redeemer finds him and kills him he is exempt. Rambam further writes that anyone who killed deliberately but cannot be executed by beit din, for example because there was only one witness, no warning, or a similar defect, has no blood if the blood redeemer kills him. These cases plainly have no completed capital judgment. Rambam's statement in Hilchot Rotzeach chapter 1, that the witnesses and observers may not kill a deliberate murderer before he comes to beit din and is sentenced, is not speaking about the special law of the blood redeemer. That law is explained in chapter 6. In our era, however, it requires study whether the law of the blood redeemer can operate, because at minimum testimony must be received to establish that the killing occurred, and we are lay judges without authority to receive capital testimony. Rambam's reference to killing with one witness is explained by Kesef Mishneh as special testimony that cannot combine for capital punishment, or perhaps testimony contradicted in the investigative details. Since Rambam's essential source is that such a killer is no better than one who killed without intent, one might argue that the same principle should apply in our time as well. This requires study.
