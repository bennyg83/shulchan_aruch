A document containing interest. Shach cites Tosafot's question that the witnesses should themselves be disqualified. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that they become disqualified only after completing their signatures, comparing one who slaughters on Shabbat and becomes an apostate only after the act. He therefore disagrees with Maharit, who proves from Bava Kamma that testimony can be invalidated retroactively when the witnesses' own wrongdoing occurs in the testimonial act. This comparison is incorrect. Becoming an apostate requires an established status of rebellion, according to many authorities through repeated conduct. Becoming a wicked witness is different. At the very moment a person knowingly performs a disqualifying transgression, he is wicked. Indeed Maharashdam himself discusses Tosafot in this manner and draws support that the disqualification arises during the act. There is also a direct difficulty from the laws of gittin. Witnesses who sign a get on Shabbat can render it biblically invalid. Yet a get signature need not contain the full name in every case, and some sages signed with a single recognized letter. Rambam rules that writing two identical letters is liable only when they form a meaningful unit. Thus the precise moment of the transgression can be at completion of the signature, and nevertheless the get is invalid. This shows that completion of the sinful signature can itself disqualify the testimonial act. Even if one assumed disqualification arose only after the last stroke, the document could still fail. Rif writes regarding a witness who became a son-in-law after signing but before the document left the witnesses' hands that the later relationship can matter. In ordinary non-acquisition documents all agree that delivery is part of the evidentiary completion, as Ran explains: the signature witnesses function as the delivery witnesses through the document's emergence in the recipient's hand. Here it is known that the interest-bearing instrument was delivered after the signers had already become disqualified. Who then proves lawful delivery? If the same now-disqualified persons are needed to establish the delivery, their testimony cannot be relied upon. Ketzot HaChoshen also accepts Maharit’s lenient opinion in a case where a community decreed that only specifically designated persons should serve as kiddushin witnesses, and other people nevertheless witnessed the kiddushin. He reasons that they were not disqualified until after the kiddushin. Maharit himself asks: even if they were qualified at the moment of kiddushin, they are disqualified when they later testify. More fundamentally, because a person cannot make himself wicked through his own statement, we can divide their words. If two say, 'She was betrothed before us, but we were wicked witnesses,' their self-accusation is disregarded and we accept only the statement that she was betrothed before witnesses. This is not an artificial addition to their words. The law simply gives no testimonial force to a person's statement disqualifying himself. Ketubot proves the point. Witnesses who say, 'This is our handwriting, but we were disqualified by transgression,' can under appropriate circumstances be believed to weaken the document, and Ran discusses dividing their words concerning the transgression. The validity of the document depends directly on their status, just as kiddushin depends on valid witnesses, yet the principle of divided testimony still applies. Maharit's own proof from the case of witnesses to theft and slaughter who are later disproved on the slaughter is also not decisive. There the earlier theft testimony is invalidated not merely because the witnesses committed a later transgression. Rashi explicitly says the reason is that when part of one joined testimony is invalidated, the whole is invalidated. If the earlier and later testimony concerned different claimants and different defendants so that they were not one testimonial unit, the earlier testimony would remain valid even though the witnesses committed a disqualifying act immediately afterward. This proves that ordinary later wickedness does not automatically retroactively nullify earlier valid testimony; the special result in the theft-and-slaughter case follows from the unity of the testimony.
