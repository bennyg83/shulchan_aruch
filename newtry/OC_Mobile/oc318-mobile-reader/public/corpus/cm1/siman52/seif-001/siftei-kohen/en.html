[" But he collects the principal with it. This is so even where Torah prohibited interest is explicitly written in the document, as Tosafot, the Rosh, the other poskim, and the Tur and Mechaber in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 161 write. The signing witnesses are not disqualified merely by signing such a document, because ordinary people understand the prohibition ‘you shall not place interest upon him’ as directed to lender and borrower, as explained above in siman 34, seif 10. This is contrary to Taz in Yoreh Deah there, who did not accurately understand the Mechaber. I have already challenged him in Nekudot HaKesef there. Ramban, however, holds that the witnesses are disqualified. He explains the Gemara as discussing a document that does not explicitly state interest, where the witnesses did not know that the transaction contained interest. The creditor may then collect the principal even from encumbered property. We do not decree here lest he also collect the interest, as we invalidated a predated debt document above in siman 43 even for collection from the later valid date, lest he collect from the earlier date. There, collection from the earlier date would make the entire seizure from purchasers unlawful. Here, even if he collected the interest together with the principal stated in the document, that does not make the principal itself prohibited, and we do not penalize the permitted amount because of the prohibited amount. Nimukei Yosef cites Ramban’s view as his conclusion; Ran in Responsa, siman 80, cites it as one opinion, see also siman 33; Maggid Mishneh, chapter 24 of Hilchot Malveh, cites Ramban and rules it primary; Bach and Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 46, part 2, likewise conclude so. Gidulei Terumah argues that when Tosafot say ‘perhaps’ people understand the prohibition only as applying to lender and borrower and not witnesses, their uncertainty cannot overcome Ramban’s certainty, especially for purposes of extracting money. This requires study. Tosafot use ‘perhaps’ only in Eizehu Neshekh, while in HaManiach and HaMokher Peirot they state the answer without that tentative language. The Rosh, Mordechai, Tur, Hagahot Ashri, Shiltei Giborim, and other poskim also appear to hold that the witnesses remain valid, as I discuss in Nekudot HaKesef to Yoreh Deah 161. The matter therefore appears to be a doubt in law. Gidulei Terumah himself seems ultimately to agree, as indicated by his phrase ‘especially for purposes of extracting.’ The practical difference is that if the holder seized, we do not remove the property from him. Netivot HaTerumah there further writes that when rabbinic interest is included together with the principal, everyone would agree that one may collect from encumbered property, because Tosafot’s decree regarding principal because of interest was said only of Torah prohibited interest, and Ramban would not disqualify the witnesses where they violated no Torah prohibition. This assumes it is known that the signing witnesses knew the transaction contained interest, for otherwise they remain valid even according to Ramban. See above in siman 34, seif 3, gloss. Sema writes that the prohibition ‘you shall not place interest’ applies only to lender, borrower, and guarantor. Some poskim, however, treat a guarantor like the witnesses. Beit Yosef cites them above in siman 34, and the Tur there cites the Rosh to this effect. The Mechaber also appears to agree, both in Beit Yosef and in Shulchan Aruch, siman 34, seif 10, where he lists lender and borrower as disqualified but does not include guarantor and witnesses:"]

This means that once its invalidity was exposed before beit din, it has no remedy. But if the creditor writes such a limitation on the document before it has appeared before beit din, that is effective, as above in siman 45, seif 14. I wrote similarly above in siman 43, seif katan 11, regarding a predated document. See there:"]
[" However, if the obligor admits. The same applies if two independent witnesses testify concerning the principal, as Maharik writes there. According to the poskim who hold that the signing witnesses themselves were not disqualified, those witnesses too are believed regarding the principal. See Agudat Ezov, p. 79b:"]
[" And some say, etc. The Rama’s presentation of this as an alternate view requires study. In my opinion there is no authority who holds the position as he states it, as I will explain. Sema says Tosafot in the third chapter of Bava Kamma likewise hold this, and that they distinguish the interest document from a predated document. This is not correct. Tosafot make that distinction only according to Rabbi Meir, who penalizes the permitted portion because of the prohibited portion. According to the Sages, whose view we follow, Tosafot explicitly imply that an interest bearing document is like a predated document: the document itself is invalid, but the underlying loan is not forfeited. The Sages impose no penalty upon permitted money merely because of prohibited money. They invalidate the document only where there is concern that the interest might improperly be collected, as a protective decree. Where no such concern exists, no penalty is found in Tosafot or the other poskim. To the contrary, all the poskim compare this to a predated document, and everyone agrees that on a predated document one may collect the underlying debt when the obligor admits it, as above in siman 43, seif 7. Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 2, beginning of part 4, states this explicitly: where interest is expressly written in the document, the principal may not be collected from encumbered property, and according to some explanations it cannot even be collected from unencumbered property, except where the obligor admits or independent witnesses establish the debt. Thus he expressly permits collection where the obligor admits or other witnesses testify. The Rama in Darkei Moshe cites Hagahot Mordechai to Eizehu Neshekh as saying that even the principal is forfeited, but no such conclusion follows there. When Hagahot Mordechai says in the name of Rabbenu Yitzchak that predated debt documents are entirely invalid and cannot be used even against unencumbered property though the borrower admits, the meaning is that the borrower admits the document was written at his direction, not that he admits a presently unpaid debt. This is compelled by the source, which is copied word for word from Tosafot in Mi SheMet in the name of Rabbenu Yitzchak. Tosafot compare predated documents to interest documents and say they are wholly invalid. But Rabbenu Yitzchak plainly agrees that the principal may be collected from the borrower himself when he admits the present debt or independent witnesses prove it, as is evident from Tosafot in HaManiach and HaMokher Peirot, and as I wrote above in siman 43, seif katan 14, in the name of the later authorities. Rivash, siman 382, likewise implies this. Thus the phrase ‘even though the borrower admits’ means that he admits only that the document was written with his authorization. The continuation of Tosafot and Hagahot Mordechai proves this too. They bring proof from Reish Lakish in Eizehu Neshekh, who interprets the Mishnah about predated documents according to Rabbi Meir, who penalizes an interest document so that neither principal nor interest may be collected, and cite the Tosefta that a person who produces an interest document must tear it, and if he comes to beit din, beit din tears it. This proves only that the document itself is to be destroyed and cannot be used for collection, not that a debtor who presently admits the underlying debt is exempt from paying it. Their formulation, ‘even though the borrower admits,’ therefore necessarily means admission that the document was issued at his direction. One cannot infer otherwise from a later passage in Hagahot Mordechai asking how the principal is collected and answering that the borrower admits. The careful reader sees that those are two separate explanations. The first words are Rabbenu Yitzchak’s interpretation of the Gemara as discussing a document that explicitly states the interest, and in that explanation collection is from the document itself. The later answer that the borrower admits is not part of Rabbenu Yitzchak’s explanation. Mashat Binyamin, siman 34, writes that where principal and interest are combined, there is a dispute whether even the Sages agree that neither may be collected, even when the obligor admits the principal. He was apparently following the Rama, as Sema and Ir Shushan did. In my view there is no opinion that forfeits the principal when the obligor admits it, for all the reasons stated. I also found Responsa Mahari ibn Lev, book 3, end of siman 21, questioning a ruling of the Rosh on this basis. In my view Maharik, shoresh 17, is primary; his reasoning is clear. The Rosh’s use of the word ‘penalized’ in Responsa, klal 108, siman 22, is not literal. It means that where there is a concern of improper collection, the Sages penalized the holder by invalidating the document. It does not mean that they penalized permitted principal because of prohibited interest so that the borrower himself need not pay a debt he admits. Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber use the same language regarding predated documents above in siman 43, seif 7, saying the Sages ‘penalized’ him so he may not collect with the predated document except from unencumbered property, even though collection from the borrower remains available. The language of penalty therefore refers to the protective invalidation of the document. The same is true here. This appears to me clearly the practical law:"]
[" However, etc., through an agent, etc. This distinction applies only according to the latter view. Maharik there expressly discusses the case where the obligor admits. According to the first view, there is no distinction whether the loan was made through an agent: the principal is always collectible from unencumbered property where the obligor admits, while from encumbered property, or where the obligor does not admit, the principal cannot be collected by means of the invalid document even if the loan was made through an agent. The document remains invalid:"]

Nimukei Yosef in Get Pashut writes in accordance with Ran, and Bach cites him without qualification:"]
[" Or it is torn lengthwise and crosswise. It appears that the intersecting tears must run through the entire written portion of the document. Otherwise the law would have no definite measure, and it is unreasonable that any tiny cross tear should invalidate it. Such a small cross tear may naturally occur at the folds of a document through handling and repeated folding:"]
[" It was torn, etc. Sema writes that this applies only when the tear is not along the folds, but if it is torn along its folds, even completely, we attribute it to ordinary handling and the document is valid. Bach similarly understands Responsa Rosh to validate a tear along the folds even where it passes through the witnesses, date, and essential text. Their words are not compelled. It appears that a formal beit din tear invalidates even along the folds, because neither the Gemara nor the poskim distinguish, when defining a beit din tear through the witnesses, date, and essential text or as intersecting tears, between the folds and elsewhere. The same is certainly true when the document is torn completely into two pieces, which is worse than an ordinary beit din tear. What Responsa Rosh implies, that a tear along the folds may be valid, refers to a tear that does not have the form of a beit din cancellation. The reason is that ordinary handling would not usually tear the document fully through its folds all at once. It ordinarily deteriorates little by little. The holder should therefore have gone to beit din before it became torn in the formal manner or split into two. His failure to do so indicates intentional cancellation. This appears correct to me:"]
[" But otherwise we are not concerned. For example, if a large hole is found, we do not say that there had originally been a beit din tear and the holder removed the surrounding material. We do not invalidate a document because of doubt. So writes Rashba there:"]
