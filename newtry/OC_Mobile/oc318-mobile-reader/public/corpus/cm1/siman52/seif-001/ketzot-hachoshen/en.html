A document containing interest. Tosafot asks why the witnesses are not disqualified, since by signing they violate the prohibition, "You shall not place interest," and even one who violates a prohibition without personal financial gain can be disqualified, as Abaye rules concerning one who eats nonkosher food in defiance. Mishneh LaMelech asks whether this violation carries lashes. Rambam says a wicked witness is one who violated a transgression punishable by lashes, and the witnesses to an interest bearing loan apparently receive no lashes because the prohibition itself is not punishable by lashes. Knesset HaGedolah rejects the premise and argues that Rambam does not literally require a lash liable prohibition. He cites theft, robbery, and Shabbat. This is mistaken. Rambam himself expressly explains that thieves and robbers are disqualified even though they are subject to repayment and not lashes because they take property that is not theirs and Scripture separately disqualifies a violent monetary wrongdoer. He likewise says that those liable to capital punishment are certainly disqualified, because Scripture calls one condemned to death wicked. Thus Mishneh LaMelech's question is real. Gidulei Terumah answers that the lender does not receive lashes because the interest can be returned, but the witnesses and guarantor have no corrective repayment available to them. Their prohibition therefore remains subject to lashes. Bnei Shmuel at first similarly seeks to infer that witnesses receive lashes, but asks that if they merely speak and do not perform an act, this is a prohibition without action. Mishneh LaMelech responds that when they sign a document there is an act. Even where they do not sign, perhaps the violation remains lash liable because this prohibition can be violated through an act in some cases, comparable to muzzling an animal by voice according to Rambam. He further cites Lechem Mishneh, who says that plotting witnesses can sometimes testify in writing and therefore their category can involve action. Yet this is difficult under Rambam himself, who treats testimony in documents as rabbinic in ordinary monetary law. It cannot transform the biblical core of the offense into an action for lashes. Nor, according to Baal HaMaor, can hazamah be based upon written testimony at all. Perhaps Tosafot simply disagrees with Rambam and holds that even a transgression not punishable by lashes can create biblical testimonial disqualification, or perhaps Tosafot assumes that signing creates sufficient action for lashes in the interest prohibition. There is, however, a more fundamental answer to Tosafot's question why the document is not void because its witnesses became disqualified through the very signing. Tosafot in Chullin asks why one who slaughters on Shabbat intentionally has a kosher slaughter when he thereby becomes an apostate for Shabbat desecration. Tosafot answers that a single violation does not necessarily make him an apostate. Ran gives another answer: he does not acquire the status of apostate until the slaughter is completed, so this very slaughter is not invalidated retroactively. The same principle applies here. The witnesses do not violate "You shall not place interest" merely by witnessing an oral transaction where collection may never occur. Their violation arises specifically through the signed instrument, as the early authorities discuss. Therefore they become disqualified only through the act of signing. The signature produced while they were still legally qualified remains valid; only testimony they give afterward is disqualified. This becomes especially important in the broader discussion of when a sinner's disqualification begins. Maharit, discussing communal bans against serving as kiddushin witnesses without the local rabbi and communal leaders, reports an argument that the witnesses were still qualified at the moment of kiddushin and became disqualified only afterward. Maharit objects from cases of plotting witnesses and from the Shabbat slaughter. The objection must be refined. There is a fundamental difference between a monetary wrongdoer or false witness, whose disqualification rests upon suspicion of lying, and one disqualified merely by the Torah's decree against a wicked witness. Nimukei Yosef writes that a person who swears falsely about a stone pillar is disqualified although he has no financial motive. According to Abaye, this is not because every sinner is assumed to lie in unrelated matters, for one suspected in one matter is not automatically suspected in another. Rather, Scripture itself says, "Do not place a wicked person as a witness." Such a person resembles a relative or a woman: his disqualification is statutory, not evidentiary suspicion. By contrast, a robber or false witness is disqualified because he is suspected of lying for gain or in that very matter. Therefore a nonmonetary sinner becomes disqualified only from the point at which the transgression is completed onward. His prior act of testimony is not retroactively tainted. A robber or plotted false witness can be treated more severely because the very conduct under examination shows unreliability in that testimony itself. This explains many sources. Meisharim says that when a witness signed while valid and only later became a robber, his signature must have been established before he became a robber because afterward he is suspected of forging it. But if he later became related, no such concern exists. Meisharim is uncertain about one who later became wicked through a nonfinancial transgression, precisely because such wickedness is a statutory disqualification and does not necessarily create a suspicion of lying. I have argued elsewhere that such a witness should resemble the relative more than the robber. Maharit further attacks the position defending the kiddushin witnesses by saying that in Shabbat slaughter the animal is already invalid because Shabbat desecration occurs before the completion of slaughter. But this is exactly Rambam's own explanation in his commentary to Chullin: the desecration begins when he first wounds the neck, before the kosher slaughter is completed. Ran disagrees and says Shabbat liability arises only at the completion because before then the wound is destructive. According to Rambam and Tosafot, a destructive act done in order to repair can already be liable, so he becomes disqualified before the slaughter's completion. According to Ran, he becomes disqualified only once the slaughter is complete. Thus both sides agree to the general rule: a statutory sinner is disqualified only from the moment the transgression occurs onward, not retroactively within the same event. They disagree only about when the Shabbat violation begins. The same applies to our interest document. Even if the witnesses become disqualified by violating the interest prohibition, the document is not invalidated unless the disqualifying status precedes the testimony. Here the transgression is completed through the very signing, so their disqualification operates only afterward. This also explains Tosafot in Sanhedrin concerning one who is voluntarily violated sexually and whether his presence invalidates the group. If he is considered a wicked person because he sinned for pleasure, his status at the later testimony can invalidate him. According to the view that a disqualified observer nullifies only when he later comes to beit din and testifies, he is already wicked at that time. Even according to the view that seeing alone can nullify, a person who commits a sin for personal pleasure can be treated like a wrongdoer suspected in that very matter rather than merely a statutory sinner. The guiding distinction is therefore between a wicked person disqualified only by Scripture's decree and a person whose conduct itself gives reason to distrust the relevant testimony. As an allusion, the verse says, "Do not place a wicked person as a witness," placing wickedness before witness status, while concerning a violent witness it says, "When a witness of violence rises against a man," without requiring the violence to precede the testimony. This is a sound and important distinction.
