Seif 1. A document in which explicit interest is written, etc. The Gemara in the fifth chapter of Bava Metzia teaches: “A document that contains interest.” Rashi explains there that the document states that a particular person borrowed from another person at a specified rate of interest for a specified period. At first glance Rashi altered his wording from his explanation in Bava Kamma, where he wrote more briefly that a person borrowed one maneh at interest and did not mention the amount of the interest or the period. It appears that Rashi intended a precise distinction. According to the initial assumption of the Gemara in Bava Kamma, Rabbi Meir’s reason is that the Sages penalized the permitted principal because of the prohibited interest. Under that approach there would be no reason to distinguish whether the precise amount of interest was written. Once the document states that the loan was made with interest, Rabbi Meir could penalize the principal because of the interest. However, according to the Gemara’s conclusion there, and likewise in Bava Batra, Rabbi Meir’s reason is that at the time of writing the document the prohibited gain has already been assessed. Accordingly, the document must be capable of serving as a basis for collection of the interest. That is so only when the document specifies how much interest is due and for what period, for then the document itself reveals how much interest will ultimately accrue. The lender can collect that interest by means of the document, so already at the time of writing there has been an assessment of the prohibited gain. If the document merely says in general terms that the loan was “with interest,” without stating the amount or the terms, the lender still cannot collect any definite interest through that document. In such a case the principle that the amount was assessed from the time of writing does not apply. This explains why Rashi, when explaining the conclusion, specifies the amount and period. Rashbam in Bava Batra similarly explains a case in which one maneh was lent for two hundred by a fixed date. This yields a practical difference even according to the Rabbis, whom we follow, that the principal may be collected. Rashi and Tosafot necessarily understand that a document may explicitly state the interest without automatically disqualifying the witnesses. According to the view that the witnesses are not biblically disqualified because the prohibition involved does not carry lashes, but are only rabbinically disqualified and require public proclamation, they become disqualified for future testimony after such proclamation. Tosafot initially suggest that witnesses are disqualified only when the principal and interest are combined into one sum, whereas if the interest is stated separately, and beit din will not collect it, the witnesses do not violate the prohibition. Tosafot reject that distinction because the prohibited amount is already assessed at the time of writing. It follows, however, that this rejection applies only where the amount of interest is actually specified. If the document merely states that the loan bore interest, without specifying the amount and without combining principal and interest, there is no assessment at all. The witnesses therefore are not disqualified on account of the writing. This is especially clear according to the Mishneh LaMelech in the name of Mahara’sh, that witnesses do not violate the prohibition merely by an oral arrangement. If even orally the witnesses did not know the amount of interest, it certainly cannot be called an assessment, and they did not violate the prohibition. This is clear in my opinion. One might argue that even such a general document could support collection of at least one perutah of interest. This can be answered in several ways. Perhaps the expression “with interest” referred even to less than a perutah, so no definite perutah can be collected from the document. Or perhaps the interest was prepaid, so the lender cannot presently collect any interest through this document. By contrast, when the document states that a particular sum is to be paid for a specified period, the lender can collect it at the end of the period, for payment is ordinarily due only at the end. Moreover, since in truth the agreed interest exceeded a perutah but the witnesses did not state that amount, they did not record the actual assessment, and such writing is not treated as testimony establishing the prohibited assessment. See also what I wrote at length in Yoreh Deah, siman 162. There, in the same seif 1. Ketzot HaChoshen explains that the witnesses who signed an interest-bearing document are not disqualified because they do not become wicked until after completing the signature. Although I too suggested this reasoning in my youth, I later retracted it. The reasoning cannot stand with respect to witnesses. For slaughter, the question is whether the slaughterer was valid at the moment of the act, and therefore one may say that he did not become an apostate until that slaughter was completed. Testimony is different. A person bears the status of a witness only if he is fit to come before beit din and testify regarding the event. If from the outset it is known that by performing this very act he will immediately become disqualified and therefore will never be fit to testify in beit din regarding it, the designation “witness” never properly rests upon him. The very word “witness” denotes one who is fit to testify in beit din. Thus, even if he was valid when he observed the event, where it was already certain then that he would be disqualified before he could testify, he does not have the status of a valid witness. I discussed this in my novellae to Even HaEzer, siman 35. Therefore the Gemara properly asks that such witnesses are disqualified. Examine this carefully.
