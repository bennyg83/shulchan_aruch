But he collects the principal, etc. Tosafos writes that the witnesses are not disqualified, because the prohibition of "you shall not impose" does not appear to them to refer to witnesses. In Perek Eizehu Neshech Tosafos writes, "perhaps 'you shall not impose' sounds to ordinary people as though it applies to the lender and not to witnesses." Gidulei Terumah infers that Tosafos itself was uncertain about this rule. Shach answers that although Tosafos expressed uncertainty there, elsewhere it treats the matter as clear. His answer is correct. I initially thought that Tosafos's word "perhaps" did not reflect doubt concerning the legal interpretation, but only doubt concerning these particular witnesses. Even if we generally say that ordinary people do not understand the verse to apply to witnesses, that is not absolute. The witnesses could be Torah scholars who know that the prohibition applies to witnesses as well, and who knowingly violated what the Sages taught. Since it is uncertain whether they knew, we preserve their presumption of fitness, as I explained at length above, siman 34. But I retracted this explanation because of Tosafos's wording. Tosafos says, "perhaps 'you shall not impose' sounds to ordinary people as though it applies to the lender," when it could have said simply that it does not sound to witnesses as though it applies to them. Since the phrase expressly refers to ordinary people, Tosafos's uncertainty concerns the substantive assumption whether ordinary witnesses are included among people who do not know the prohibition. It is therefore clear that Tosafos itself remained uncertain on the point.
It is invalid, etc. Ramban's view is that the document remains valid for collection of the principal, because the defect was not created in the principal itself. This differs from an antedated document, where the defect affects the entire document. See Shach, who rules that a party in possession may say that he relies upon Ramban. Ramban's position requires explanation. The Gemara asks concerning an antedated document why, according to Rabbi Yehudah, the reason for invalidity must be a decree lest one collect from the earlier improper date. Why not simply say that such a document was not permitted to be written? The Gemara answers that although it could not be written from the first date, it could have been written from the later proper date. One would have expected an even stronger question concerning a document in which principal and prohibited interest are combined into one total amount. Why do the Sages permit collection of the principal? Such a document could not be written in that form at all and is apparently worse than an antedated document. Even if the final answer could be that the principal itself was permitted to be written, at the initial stage, before the Gemara introduced that distinction, this should have been the stronger question. If principal and interest are stated separately in the document, there is no difficulty, because the principal is independently written in a permissible manner. Asifat Zekenim explicitly cites Ramban himself to this effect. But if principal and interest are included in one total sum, the document was not permitted to be written in that form and appears entirely comparable to an antedated document. Nor can one say that because the witnesses did not know the sum included interest, the writing is treated as permissible. If that were enough, why does the Gemara challenge the rule concerning improvements upon stolen land? We could say that the witnesses to the sale did not know the field was stolen and wrote innocently. Perhaps the answer is that Amemar transmitted Rava's distinction between material that may be written and material that may not be written, and Ravina challenged Rava specifically from the antedated document. He could not challenge him from the interest document, because Rava could have answered that the baraita concerns a document in which the interest is stated explicitly. If one then objects that the witnesses are disqualified, according to Ramban, that objection is not decisive under Rava, because Rava holds that disqualification requires a witness motivated by unlawful gain, and one who eats nonkosher meat merely to satisfy his appetite is not thereby disqualified. Ravina therefore chose the antedated document. According to our ruling like Abaye, which does not require unlawful gain, Ramban was compelled to say that the interest case concerns principal and profit combined in a way that does not disqualify the witnesses. Even so, once the Gemara reaches its conclusion, there is no longer a difficulty from the rule that the document was not permitted to be written, because the principal itself was permitted to be written, just as an antedated document could be written from the proper later date.
And some say that he is exempt, etc. The cited source is Hagahot Mordechai to Perek Eizehu Neshech, copied from Tosafos in Bava Batra 157. Rabbeinu Yonah explains that when the Mishnah says antedated documents are invalid, they are completely invalid, even for collection from the borrower himself, although the debtor admits the debt. His proof is that Reish Lakish explains the Mishnah there according to Rabbi Meir, who penalizes the lender with respect to a document containing interest, even where the debtor admits and even against the borrower personally. The later Mishnah's phrase "invalid" therefore appears likewise to mean total invalidity. Hagahot Mordechai then says this may be rejected because there the lender actively assessed the prohibited interest, whereas here he did not, though the wording still implies complete invalidity. Rama learned from this that in the interest case the borrower is exempt even if he admits, because the lender actively created the prohibited amount. Shach labors to reinterpret Hagahot Mordechai and says that "the debtor admits" does not mean that he admits owing the debt, but only that he admits instructing the witnesses to write the document. This is plainly forced. The phrase does not bear that meaning, and if the document was executed by witnesses who were not disqualified, why should it matter whether the debtor admits that it was written at his instruction? The witnesses will not lie in either event. Nevertheless, I do not understand Rama's proof from Hagahot Mordechai. Hagahot Mordechai sought to prove that antedated documents are invalid even where the debtor admits from Reish Lakish, who understands that Mishnah according to Rabbi Meir and compares it to an interest document. We, however, rule like Rabbi Yochanan, who says that the Mishnah can follow the Sages, who permit collection of the principal in an interest document and invalidate the antedated document only because of a decree. Hagahot Mordechai must therefore understand that Rabbi Yochanan does not disagree with Reish Lakish about what "invalid" means, namely invalid even where the debtor admits. Thus, if an antedated document is invalid because of the fear that one might collect from the earlier date, the penalty must extend even to the principal against an admitting debtor. All the more so should that be true where principal and interest are combined. The disagreement between Rabbi Meir and the Sages with respect to an interest document would then concern a document in which the interest is separately stated. But once Hagahot Mordechai rejects the analogy and says that the interest case differs because the lender actively assessed the prohibited amount, the proof reverses. Even Reish Lakish could then concede that an antedated document is invalid only against purchasers and that where the debtor admits, the principal may be collected, because the defect in the date is not the same active wrongdoing as assessing interest. If so, according to Rabbi Yochanan and the Sages, an antedated document can be invalid where the debtor denies but still collect where he admits. Then there is no basis to say that principal and interest combined should cause the principal to be forfeited when the debtor admits. The rejection in Hagahot Mordechai is itself accepted as practical law above, siman 43, so it does not furnish a proof for Rama's rule. There is, however, a proof for Rama from Tosafos themselves. Tosafos asks: if the interest is expressly written in the document, the witnesses should be disqualified, while if it is not expressly written, how can the Sages permit collection of the principal when there is concern that the lender may collect the interest as well? Why did Tosafos not answer that the document indeed contains express interest and the witnesses are disqualified, but the case is one where the debtor admits the debt? The Mishnah could then use the language "collects" and "does not collect" rather than "valid" and "invalid": the document itself is invalid, but the dispute would concern whether the lender may nevertheless collect based on the debtor's admission. Rabbi Meir would impose a penalty, while the Sages would not. One could not dismiss this by asking why the Mishnah mentions a document at all rather than simply a loan with interest. The novelty could be that even where principal and interest are included in one written sum, creating a danger that the lender may later demand the entire amount and the borrower may not be believed to say part was interest, the Sages nevertheless do not penalize him. In an oral loan the borrower could always assert that the excess claim was interest and would have a migo that he never borrowed or that he repaid. Thus the written case would teach much. It follows that Tosafos must hold that where principal and interest are included together in a manner that creates a danger of collecting the prohibited interest, even the Sages penalize the lender and prevent collection of the principal notwithstanding the debtor's admission. This resembles Rav's rule in Perek HaManiach that a person may be penalized with respect to the permitted component because the permitted component itself enabled the prohibited gain. Therefore Tosafos could not establish the dispute in the manner just suggested. The earlier passage of Hagahot Mordechai also supports this. It asks how the lender acquires the principal if the witnesses are disqualified, and says that according to the view allowing division of a person's statement, one can understand that the witnesses signed concerning the principal but not the interest. According to the view that we do not divide the statement, however, how can he acquire the principal? It answers that the case is one where both borrower and lender admit that the principal is owed, so it is collected from unencumbered assets as an oral debt. Shach correctly notes that this is not the earlier answer of Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who gave reasons why the witnesses are not disqualified. But the question itself still requires explanation. If the interest is not stated in the document and the witnesses innocently signed a facially proper document, why should the principal not be collected? The fact that beit din refuses to allow the lender to collect interest would not turn the document into a mere shard, and the issue of dividing testimony from Sanhedrin would be irrelevant. It is therefore clear that Hagahot Mordechai understands the case as one where the interest is expressly stated. The question is that the witnesses are disqualified, unless one can say that they signed only concerning the principal and not the interest. Ritva, as cited by Beit Yosef, likewise says that the witnesses can say, "for this we signed and for that we did not sign." According to the view that does not divide the statement, however, the document is ineffective and collection depends solely upon the debtor's admission. Now, once that is the case, why did Hagahot Mordechai not simply establish the baraita as one where interest is not expressly stated, so the witnesses remain fit, and then say that the debtor admits the debt if there is concern that the lender might collect interest? Necessarily it understood that whenever there is a realistic concern that the lender can collect the interest, the Sages themselves penalize him and do not allow collection of the principal even from an admitting debtor. This is precisely the rule derived above from Tosafos. Responsa Rosh, rule 108, siman 52, also indicates this. As Mahari ibn Lev notes, the Rosh writes that where witnesses establish that prohibited interest was included in the total stated in the document, the lender does not collect the principal at all. He does not distinguish whether the borrower admits the principal. He also holds in principle that a denying borrower would owe a heset oath, except that in the particular case the lender was a disqualified person and was therefore exempt from the oath on a separate ground. It is therefore clear that the Rosh holds that even where the debtor admits the principal, the Sages penalize the lender and he collects nothing. This is contrary to Shach's interpretation that the case concerns only a borrower who denies. I have written all this to defend Rama and to show that he has substantial support. For practical law, however, the primary ruling appears to follow the majority of authorities that the borrower must pay the principal. Tosafos's familiar objection that the sinner should not profit is strong, for the borrower also violated a prohibition, and Tosafos's answer is strained. Accordingly, one cannot say that the borrower may simply plead that he follows the minority view, because the majority of authorities disagree.
Provided that it is torn, etc. Ran, responsum 75, holds that if the document is torn in any one of the three significant places it is invalid. Gidulei Terumah objects that the Gemara's language implies that the tear must involve all three. Rivash, Ran's student, appears to disagree with his teacher. In responsum 383 he invalidates a ketubah whose first half had been cut away because perhaps that half had originally contained a crosswise tear and the holder cut it away to conceal the tear. He does not say more simply that perhaps there had been a tear merely in the place of the date, which indicates that a tear in one such location is insufficient. It also appears that the questioner in Ran's responsum was Rivash, and his position there was that all three places must be involved. The questioner's proof was from the Gemara in the second chapter of Gittin, which says that one may cut away the date and give the get. This supports Rivash, for according to Ran, how could the get be valid? Perhaps there had been a disqualifying tear in the date and it was cut away to hide the defect. Ran answers that there is no comparison. If it were known that the cutting was unrelated to repayment or another event that invalidates the document, the instrument would not be invalid. But when a document comes before us torn at the date, we fear that it was torn because of payment or another invalidating event. His answer is difficult. In the case of the get whose date has been cut away, who knows why it was cut? Perhaps there had been a tear and the holder cut away the place of the tear to conceal the defect. If witnesses testify to the cutting, they would also know when it occurred, and the entire concern of shielding the husband's relative would disappear because the witnesses could establish whether the relevant right arose before or after the cutting. Rashba, in his novellae, asks concerning the authorities who hold that cutting away the date leaves a get valid even initially: who says the get ever contained a date at all, perhaps it merely contained a tear? He therefore concludes that initially such a get should not be used, although after the fact it is valid. This shows that Rashba's case does not involve witnesses to the cutting. Yet he still validates it after the fact, which is difficult according to Ran if we must fear that the date contained a disqualifying tear. Ran himself writes in Perek HaMegareish that according to Rambam a torn get is valid where there are witnesses to delivery, while according to Rashba it is invalid. In the case where the date was cut there are no witnesses to delivery, for otherwise they could tell us when the operative act occurred and the date problem would not arise. This indicates clearly that Rashba does not regard a tear merely at the date as sufficient to invalidate. One possible explanation is that Ran follows the position toward which he inclines in Perek HaMegareish, like Raavad, that a get which has been authenticated remains valid despite tears. In the case of shielding the husband's relative, the husband may use every stratagem to protect her. He can write a get, send it through a woman to be authenticated by beit din, whose judges may authenticate without closely reading the date, and after authentication cut away the date. There is then no reason to fear that a prior tear invalidated it, because an authenticated get remains valid despite tears. This would preserve the concern of shielding. Yet Ran's wording in the responsum is very obscure if this was his intention, and according to Rambam, who disagrees with Raavad and invalidates even an authenticated torn document, the rule would not hold. The matter therefore requires study. Tosafos in Bava Metzia 7 also supports the view against Ran. They write that where removing a portion would invalidate the document, valuation is not made by assuming that portion removed, but they do speak of removing the date, implying that loss of the date itself does not invalidate the document. They do not fear that perhaps a tear had been there and was cut away. One cannot say the date is removed before witnesses, because then their question against Rashi from an erased document would not arise. Likewise, Tosafos and the Tur below, siman 65, write that in the presence of witnesses a holder may cut away the witnesses' signatures. Why is the document then valid? Perhaps it had been paid and torn at the place of the witnesses, after which that portion was cut away. Again, one cannot answer that the cutting itself is witnessed in a way that resolves the matter, for then Tosafos's question loses its force. This strongly indicates that all three significant portions are required. It would be forced to say that where there are witnesses to delivery everyone agrees a tear at the witnesses' signatures is irrelevant because the holder can say he relied on the delivery witnesses.
Or it is torn lengthwise and crosswise, etc. Shach, seif katan 7, writes that the crosswise tearing must extend over the entire written document. Otherwise the rule would have no defined measure, and it is unreasonable to invalidate for a tiny crosswise tear. Rivash's responsum cited above does not appear to agree. He invalidated a ketubah whose first half had been cut away on the ground that perhaps there had been a crosswise tear there and the holder cut it away, both because the cut passed across the document and because perhaps there had been a crosswise tear. This indicates that the original crosswise tear need not have traversed the entire written text. Accordingly, Rivash disagrees with Shach. Still, even Rivash must agree that a tear involving only a few letters does not invalidate, for otherwise the earlier difficulty from cutting away the date would return. A very small tear is not the type of crosswise and lengthwise cancellation tear discussed here.
