If he has money, etc. The Shach in seif katan 1 writes that the same applies to a woman's ketubah: if the heirs possess cash, she may demand it, as he attributes to the Tur, Even HaEzer siman 100, and to a responsum of Maharshal in the name of Rabbi David Kohen. What is obvious to him is very doubtful to me. First, below in siman 107 it is explicit that where heirs possess both movable property and land, a creditor cannot insist on movable property. The Beit Yosef, citing Rabbenu Yonah, writes that the same applies to money and land. If the heirs can therefore turn him from cash to land, why could they not turn him from cash to other movable property, which is superior to land and is deemed better payment? I searched the Tur in Even HaEzer and found no such statement. There is also an error in the citation to the responsum. The source is not Rabbi David Kohen, but the Tashbetz. Indeed, in the relevant responsum it is stated explicitly in the name of Rabbenu Shimon ben Tzemach that if the husband left cash, the widow collects her ketubah, its additional amount, and her dowry from that cash. Yet in Tashbetz, part 3, siman 99, he rules regarding a widow who wished to collect cash from the heirs while they wished to give her land, where the land was property she had brought into the marriage, that because she had originally brought that land she could not demand cash. This implies that had she not brought that land, they would have had to pay her cash, apparently contrary to the authorities cited above. It must therefore be that in the Tashbetz's locale ketubot were customarily written to bind both movable property and land. In that case the heirs' movable property was encumbered by law, not merely by the Geonic enactment described below in siman 107. The reasons offered by the authorities for permitting heirs to turn a creditor from cash or movable property to land then fall away, because those reasons rest on the fact that movable property of heirs was liable only by Geonic enactment while land was liable by Torah law. Where movable property itself was expressly encumbered, the Tashbetz's ruling is coherent: when cash is present, she cannot be turned to land. But in a ketubah that does not contain such a clause, where the law permits the heirs to turn her from cash to land, it follows all the more that they may turn her to movable property, which is considered better payment. Even so, I remain somewhat doubtful. Rabbenu Tam's entire reason that a creditor who lent money receives money when available is that he gave money and should receive money, and even Rabbenu Chaim Kohen disputed that. This rationale applies to a dowry that she actually brought in cash, but not to the basic ketubah, for which she gave no money. On this point, however, I defer to the Tashbetz. It is nevertheless clear that his ruling concerns a place where the ketubah expressly binds movable property and land, as demonstrated above. Where it does not, she has no special right to cash.
And he cannot turn him to land, etc. The Gemara in Ketubot 92 states that Reuven sold a field to Shimon with warranty and converted the purchase price into a loan. Reuven died, Reuven's creditor seized the field from Shimon, and Shimon appeased that creditor with cash. Reuven's heirs could then say that their father left movable property in Shimon's hands, and movable property of heirs is not encumbered. Rava says that if Shimon is clever, he first transfers land to the heirs in payment and then collects it back from them, under Rav Nachman's rule that when heirs collect land, their father's creditor may collect that land from them. Tosafot ask that a creditor's legal payment is cash, so how can Shimon pay the heirs with land when he has cash? They answer that because Shimon would otherwise suffer a loss by losing his claim, he may pay with land despite possessing cash. The Tur accordingly rules that in a case of loss a debtor may turn the creditor to land. The Beit Yosef, in Bedek HaBayit, objects that this rule has no practical application after the Geonic enactment permitting collection from heirs' movable property, and therefore omitted it from the Shulchan Aruch. The Bach in siman 107 labored to defend the Tur. According to what is written below in siman 107 in the name of Shaarei Rav Hai and other authorities, however, where two creditors exist, one whose document expressly binds movable property together with land and one whose document does not, the former has priority against heirs because his claim is effective by law, while the latter collects only by enactment. Accordingly, the case can readily occur where Shimon's warranty document did not bind movable property, while a later creditor of Reuven did have such a clause. If Shimon pays cash, the later creditor will seize it because his lien is legally effective. Shimon is therefore clever to transfer land, against which his earlier lien gives him priority, and then collect it. Thus the Tur's rule remains fully applicable. However. The Rosh rejected Tosafot's answer and explained that the case is different because the debt itself arose from the land. The Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 4, likewise cites the Raavad as explaining that "if he is clever" means that if he lacks cash he need not pursue cash, but may pay with land. Maharam, in the Prague edition of his responsa, explains that Shimon should pay the heirs before they learn that his field was seized, when they will willingly accept land. According to all these explanations, the Bach asks from the end of Ketubot 111, where a rule of reciprocal collection is established in a case involving heirs, and the Gemara asks why one party should not transfer land to the heirs and then collect it back. If a debtor who has money cannot pay land even to avoid a loss, what is the Gemara's question? The Baal HaTerumot already asked this against the Raavad. The resolution appears as follows. The Mishnah there concerns Reuven who lent to Shimon in Nisan until Iyar, and Shimon then lent to Reuven in Iyar. When Reuven later seeks to collect, Shimon argues that Reuven's document must be forged or paid, for had Shimon truly owed him an already matured debt, Reuven would not then have borrowed from Shimon. Admon accepts Shimon's argument, while the opposing view says that each collects from the other. The Gemara establishes the case with heirs: both claims are legally collectible, but one claimant cannot in practice collect because movable property of heirs is not encumbered. The Gemara then asks why land should not be transferred to the heirs. Those authorities can hold that the rule barring payment with land where cash is available applies only when the debtor is known to own the cash. If he says that the money visible in his possession belongs to others, whether deposited by a Jew or a non Jew, it cannot necessarily be taken from him. The Shulchan Aruch, following the Hagahot Maimoniyot, rules that even visible funds may be claimed to belong to others. Although some authorities presume that whatever is in a person's possession is his, many dispute this, as explained above in siman 99. Moreover, where the creditor will be paid in any event and the only issue is whether payment is in cash or land, all should agree that the debtor's claim that the money belongs to others is sufficient, since the alleged true owners can say that the creditor loses nothing, as he is receiving payment from the debtor's land. A relatively weak claim and migo suffice where collection itself is assured. This also resolves the Giddulei Terumah's question on the Baal HaTerumot, who derives from the rule concerning one who attributes his money to a non Jew that where everyone knows he has cash but he claims it belongs to others, beit din compels him to sell land and bring money to his creditor. The Giddulei Terumah asks why this trouble is necessary, since money in one's possession should be presumed his. The Shach answers that the case concerns a violent non Jew whose funds cannot be touched, as the Ritva indeed writes, but the Baal HaTerumot speaks generally and not specifically of a violent person. According to the foregoing, his formulation is correct: since the creditor can be paid from land, the ordinary possession presumption does not justify taking money that the debtor says belongs to others. We may now understand the Gemara. Reuven must be the one who died. If Shimon, the second lender, had died, Shimon's estate would collect from Reuven while Reuven could not collect from them, and that would already amount to Admon's result. Rather, Shimon is alive and Reuven died. Admon says Shimon need not pay, while the other view says that Shimon must pay and Reuven's heirs need not pay him. The Gemara therefore asks why Shimon should lose. He can transfer land to them. If one objects that he has money, he can say it belongs to others. Nor can one say that we are dealing with a wicked man who would lie before beit din and perhaps swear falsely, for he already claims that he owes Reuven nothing. He is believed under oath through a migo: he could have said that the money was not his, in which case he would pay land and then collect his own debt back. Thus the Gemara's question is sound. By contrast, in the earlier case of the clever purchaser, if he actually has cash, his only way to avoid paying it would be to lie to beit din and risk an oath or cherem. There Tosafot's question remains. In practical law, however, Tosafot's ruling is correct: where payment in cash would cause the debtor a loss, he may pay with land. The Rosh's alternative was offered as dialectical explanation rather than a rejection of the law, as is also indicated by his rulings and the Tur. Thus the rule is clear.
