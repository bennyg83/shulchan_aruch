We do not listen to him. Bach writes that this applies where the debtor wishes to pay the Jewish creditor nothing and use all available money for a gentile creditor. But if he is willing to pay the Jew with land and pay the gentile in cash because the gentile refuses land, we listen to him. Where the debtor would otherwise suffer loss, he may satisfy the Jew with land even though he has cash, and there is no greater loss than this. This is correct. Everything depends on beit din's assessment that no sham is involved. Maharshal further writes that this applies where the Jewish creditor's debt is already due. If the Jewish debt is not yet due but the gentile's debt is due, the debtor may pay the gentile even though doing so may leave nothing for the Jew when his term later arrives, provided there is no sham. Nevertheless, if it is possible to preserve some property for the Jewish creditor, for example by placing assets in the wife's name or the like, the judge should impose a serious ban upon the borrower to do so in order to protect the Jew's money.
