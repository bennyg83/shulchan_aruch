The creditor cannot say, etc. The Sema explains that if the creditor wanted money the debtor would have to give it to him, and therefore the reverse is also true. He further notes that even among different items of movable property the debtor has the choice. I do not know why this length is necessary, for there is a more fundamental difficulty with the authorities cited by the Baal HaTerumot and Tur who say that the creditor may insist specifically on land. The Baal HaTerumot rejects them from passages in Ketubot and Pesachim, yet we rule that an appraisal may later be reversed. If land already remained in the creditor's hands for a long time, the debtor may later redeem it with money. All the more should he be able initially to give money instead of land. Why then was proof needed at all? It is strained to answer that an appraisal is reversed only where the creditor initially wanted money and received land solely because the debtor lacked cash, while if the creditor initially chose land the appraisal would not be reversed. Aside from the difficulty of this distinction, the Gemara in Bava Kamma and Bava Metzia says that a purchaser who possesses money can remove a creditor with cash even though the creditor has clearly sought the land. As Tosafot explain, the relevant dispute is in Ketubot 91, and the conclusion follows Abaye and Rava that cash can displace the creditor. Surely a purchaser cannot have greater power than the debtor himself. Likewise the rule concerning brothers who divided an estate and a creditor seized one brother's share, where the other gives one quarter in land and one quarter in money, explicitly shows that a creditor may be paid money even if he wants land. It therefore appears that these authorities agree that by rabbinic enactment, founded on the requirement to act uprightly and well, a creditor can ordinarily be removed with money. Their claim concerns the underlying legal lien. By strict law, if the creditor demands the land upon which his lien rests, the debtor must give it and cannot substitute money. The distinction has practical consequences. If there are two creditors, one earlier and one later, and the debtor has enough money to satisfy one and land to satisfy the other, while the earlier creditor wants the land, he may take it against the debtor's wishes. The equitable enactment does not apply, because in any event the land will go to a creditor, and there is no equitable reason to prefer the later creditor over the earlier one. A further case is where Reuven lent in Nisan, Shimon lent in Iyar, and Reuven later lent again in Sivan. If Reuven collects land, the debtor retains cash, as to which the Iyar and Sivan loans have no priority and share equally. If the earlier debt is paid in cash, land remains and Shimon's Iyar debt precedes Reuven's Sivan debt. Although an appraisal that is later reversed is treated by many authorities as a new acquisition, and the returned land would then be after acquired property subject to both, there is still no equitable basis to deprive the earlier creditor of the land initially. The Baal HaTerumot's proofs are therefore precise. The Gemara says that a creditor is given cash "according to his law," implying a legal entitlement to money, not merely an equitable enactment. Likewise in Pesachim, where a creditor consecrated property and Rava held that the consecration does not take effect because the debtor can remove him with money, if Torah law did not permit such removal then the creditor's consecration would be effective by Torah law. How could the Sages' equitable enactment uproot a Torah consecration and the prohibition of meilah? That question depends on an ancient dispute concerning the power of rabbinic enactments in matters carrying Torah prohibitions. It also appears that these authorities spoke specifically of a written debt in which the land lien is expressly recorded, enabling the creditor to say, "This land is encumbered to me." An oral loan, although we rule that its lien is by Torah law, works through the general principle that a person's property guarantees his obligation. If the debtor pays cash, the guarantee terminates. This approach may also answer the classic question how, according to the view that liens are by Torah law, the Torah can ever have a case of an oath on a loan when a written loan is treated as a claim against land and oaths do not apply to land. Where the debt is written and the creditor cannot by strict law be removed with cash, the essence of his claim is land and it is a land based claim. An oral loan lacks that feature and therefore is not treated in the same way. From all this. The Bach's ruling is rejected. He holds that where the debtor disclosed that if the creditor accepts the land at its future higher value he is willing to give it, the creditor may force him to do so, because otherwise there would be a closing of the door before borrowers. He equates this with the case where the creditor asks for slightly more inferior land. But the cases differ. There, the creditor says that if he had money he could buy land at today's price, and because he lacks cash he should not be forced to accept it at the future price. That indeed raises concern for access to credit. Here the debtor is returning cash, and the creditor can buy land wherever he finds it. What closing of the door can there be when the lender receives exactly the money he advanced? The Bach's position therefore has no foundation.
