In the sale of merchandise. Even without an express stipulation, we assess the seller's intention that one who sells merchandise seeks money and is therefore treated as though he stipulated for cash payment. Ritva writes that the buyer must pay specifically with money, like the law of a worker where payment follows the agreed terms. The Shach says Tosafot also writes this later in siman 336, but I found Tosafot discussing only a worker, not a seller of merchandise. Indeed Tosafot appears to imply the opposite. In Bava Metzia they ask, concerning one who bought land without responsibility and later faced a claim against it, why the seller cannot say, "Take back your land in payment of the money," according to authorities who allow a creditor to be paid with property even when he has cash. This indicates that even a seller can sometimes satisfy the buyer through appraisal of property. The matter can be resolved as I wrote above. A seller may satisfy the claim with the very merchandise that was sold, just as a worker can sometimes be told to take the product of his own labor as wages. Maharam of Rothenburg allows a worker to be paid with the very thing he produced. For a worker, because he labors for his subsistence, the substitute must be something suitable for consumption. A seller of merchandise has no such special subsistence requirement. Therefore, although he ordinarily expects money rather than unrelated property, he may be paid with the very merchandise involved in the sale. Tosafot's question can therefore refer to returning the same land that was sold.
He does not lower him to appraisal. The Sema cites Ir Shushan's inference that an iska document specifying a profit share requires payment in money, because the investor entered for his own benefit. I do not see why the distinction should depend on whether he acted for his own benefit. Ritva's reason is that a seller of merchandise is understood as having stipulated for money, because a seller ordinarily does not intend to exchange his merchandise for some other appraised property. Money itself, however, is usually given in order to obtain merchandise. A person who sold property and later discovers the transaction was mistaken is treated like an ordinary creditor, even though the original sale was plainly for his own benefit rather than charity. Rama rules so in siman 232. Nor can one say that modern arrangements where the borrower regularly agrees to give a fixed weekly profit make the iska equivalent to a sale of merchandise. If so, at most the deposit half of the iska would resemble a sale, not the loan half. Even the deposit half is not truly a sale, because the arrangement is structured primarily to avoid prohibited interest, not because the investor actually intends to sell merchandise. The same is true of other standard heter iska formulas. They remain debt arrangements for this purpose. Given that Taz himself questions the cash only rule even for merchandise sales, we should not extend it beyond its clear source.
