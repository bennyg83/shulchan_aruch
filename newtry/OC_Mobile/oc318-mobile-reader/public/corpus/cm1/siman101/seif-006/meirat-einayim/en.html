He does not give him land through appraisal, etc. The Ritva writes that the debtor must pay him in money, as they agreed, and that this is the law because it is self evident that a merchant did not sell another person movable goods and merchandise intending to receive land in payment. Beit din therefore compels monetary payment. When the Ritva says “as they agreed,” he does not mean that there was an explicit formal condition at the time of sale, because if there were such a condition the same would apply to an ordinary loan. Rather, he refers to the ordinary practice of merchants, who sell goods with the understanding that a specified amount of money will be paid in return. Therefore, even if they set a payment date and that date arrives when the buyer has no cash, he must pay specifically in money, because every merchant is presumed to have intended that result. An ordinary loan differs, because the lender’s primary purpose was to perform kindness for the borrower. For this reason Rabbi Mordechai Yaffe ruled in the Ir Shushan that where a loan document states that one hundred gold coins were given as an investment on a profit sharing basis, since the investor’s intent was his own benefit and profit, the recipient must repay him in money when the term arrives. In my view, this applies specifically where the instrument is structured as a profit sharing investment and it is known that the provider intended that the recipient return cash at the fixed time. But if one simply gives another a sum of money to trade with for shared profit, ordinary commerce involves exchanging merchandise for merchandise and earning profit through the goods taken in place of the goods sold. If the recipient acted in that ordinary manner, the investor must wait until the recipient sells the merchandise for cash, or accept the merchandise itself together with the profit accrued upon it. This is straightforward.
