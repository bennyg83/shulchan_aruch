Some say that one who sold merchandise is not allowed to pay by appraisal at all, etc. These are the words of the Ritva cited by the Beit Yosef. He derives that one who sold merchandise cannot be paid merely with appraised property, but must receive money as stipulated, and in the very currency stipulated, like a worker. We presume that a merchant did not sell his goods intending to receive land or bran in payment. The Sema and Ir Shushan understand this even without an express condition. If merchandise was sold for a stated monetary price payable at a later date and the buyer then says he has no cash, only property of equivalent value, he must sell that property and bring money, as in the case of a worker. A loan, by contrast, was advanced as an act of kindness, so equivalent property may suffice when cash is unavailable. From this they further derive that money placed in an iska for profit likewise must ordinarily be repaid in cash, unless the merchandise offered is itself the product of that iska and was contemplated by the arrangement.
Now. This derivative rule is difficult in light of the Shiltei Giborim, who cites Riaz that when an iska lender must collect land, the half treated as a deposit is collected from superior land because it falls within the categories of damages involving a guardian, while the loan half is collected from intermediate land. According to the above view there would scarcely be any case of land collection at all. The manager would be obligated to sell assets, even at a substantial loss, in order to pay cash. Certainly there would be no logic in improving the lender's land grade in order to facilitate sale while at the same time allowing the loan half only intermediate land. This points against the proposed extension. More fundamentally, I do not find the interpretation of the Ritva compelling. His wording, particularly "the currency he stipulated," suggests an express stipulation. His proof is from Bava Metzia 45b, where one who specifically stipulated payment from a new purse may not be paid from an old purse. Since that case expressly contains a condition, it cannot prove the rule when no condition was made. If the Ritva's rule followed independently from the nature of a sale, there would be no reason to cite that passage. It is also difficult in logic to say that a person who lends cash without profit must accept bran and trouble to sell it, while a merchant who sold goods at a substantial profit, or one who invested money in an iska and earns from the capital, is spared that inconvenience and may insist on cash. The order of justice appears reversed. Rabbenu Tam lists three categories of payment. A lender who has available cash must give cash, but if he lacks it he may give equivalent property. A tortfeasor may pay equivalent property even if he has cash. A worker must be paid money even if the employer must sell property to obtain it. If a merchant automatically had the same rule as a worker, Rabbenu Tam should have listed a fourth common category, namely a seller of merchandise. His omission is significant. There is further proof from a buyer who purchases an ox and then discovers a defect invalidating the sale. The Gemara indicates that if the seller lacks the buyer's cash, he may repay with equivalent property and need not sell assets to obtain cash. Yet the seller was not engaged in kindness at all. The buyer wanted an ox, not a loan. If a sale for profit inherently requires later cash repayment, this case should be treated like a merchant sale. Even if one distinguishes that in the defective ox case the seller never expected the sale to be reversed, whereas an ordinary credit seller anticipated eventual payment, a further difficulty remains. The Gemara has competing readings whether, where the original purchase money is gone, the seller must give other cash if he has it. Tosafot in Bava Kamma, in the case where a buyer says he intended to purchase a barrel rather than pitchers, ask why differing prices would matter. If the sale is void, the seller could simply say, "Take back your pitchers in place of your money." They answer with difficulty that the buyer had conveyed the value of the pitchers to the seller together with land. According to the alleged rule that any seller of merchandise must produce cash, Tosafot's question would disappear entirely. Their discussion therefore strongly indicates that they do not accept such a rule. Accordingly, a seller of merchandise has no greater right than an ordinary monetary lender. This is also the implication of the Darkei Moshe, who writes on the Ritva that the seller's law is like that of other creditors: if the debtor has cash, he must give it, but otherwise equivalent property suffices, unlike obligations where land may be given even when cash exists. The Darkei Moshe is not disputing the Ritva, since that is neither his language nor his method there. Rather, he understood the Ritva to concern an express stipulation. Even in a sale one might have thought such language was mere surplus because no cash was actually advanced, so the Ritva teaches that the stipulation is effective, just as a condition specifying one currency rather than another is effective, and like the implicit cash expectation of a worker. Even this deserves consideration, because Tosafot's answer in the pitcher case could seemingly have been simpler: say that the seller stipulated cash. Their resort to another mechanism may imply that a condition designed merely to impose extra trouble to obtain cash does not create a binding monetary obligation. The Ritva's proof from currency can be distinguished, since money itself acquires the particular coin by Torah law in that setting, whereas a mere promise to exert effort to obtain cash may be only words. This requires study. Still, where an express monetary condition is made, the Ritva's view is persuasive, since monetary conditions are generally valid. Without such a condition, however, it is clear that a merchant has no preference over an ordinary creditor.
