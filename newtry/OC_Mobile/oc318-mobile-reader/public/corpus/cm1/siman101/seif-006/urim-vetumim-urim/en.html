As he stipulated. Sema and Ir Shushan understand "stipulated" broadly. Even if a seller merely sold goods for a specified amount of money payable on a fixed date, once the date arrives and the buyer lacks cash, he must sell property of equivalent value and pay the seller cash. This differs from an ordinary money loan, where the lender performed a kindness without compensation and therefore is understood to accept either money or equivalent property if no cash is available. A seller of merchandise expects cash, not property. From this they infer that one who gives money to another as an iska for half the profits, if merchandise derived from that iska remains in the recipient's hands, the investor must wait until that merchandise is sold. But otherwise the investor need not accept property; the recipient must exert himself to sell and pay money. See Tumim. I concluded that this does not appear correct. Such parties are no better than an ordinary money lender. If the debtor has no cash, the creditor must accept property of equivalent value. The Shulchan Aruch and Ritva speak where the debtor expressly stipulated that he would pay money. Then he must follow the condition, and we do not dismiss it as empty wording. Ritva in Ketubot further writes that if one lends money and expressly stipulates, as is commonly written in documents, that repayment must be in money, the condition is valid and the borrower must exert himself to sell land and produce money. But if the borrower dies, the heirs are not compelled to fulfill that personal condition. See there.
