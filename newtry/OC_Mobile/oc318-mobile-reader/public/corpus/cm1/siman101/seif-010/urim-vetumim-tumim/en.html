And not like the view that says, etc. The Shach argues at length in favor of the opinion that the debtor must swear that he has no money. His proofs are not compelling. Even if the Rashba and Rosh disagree and require an oath, the Shach himself concedes that the Baal HaIttur and Baal HaTerumot, citing an early responsum, hold that no oath is imposed. Their reason is that this would be an enactment built upon an enactment. The rule requiring a debtor who has money to pay money rather than land is itself not Torah law. By Torah law the primary lien is on land, while cash is not specifically encumbered. The Sages enacted cash payment because the creditor originally gave cash. The further oath that he has no cash is only a Geonic enactment. We do not ordinarily add one enactment upon another. Maharam in Mordechai, chapter HaMafkid, also agrees. Although Maharam holds that the oath of insolvency itself has Talmudic roots, analogous to the oath that a bailee no longer possesses the deposited object, his conclusion only strengthens our point. A later Geonic stringency cannot exceed the original rabbinic law. If according to the law of the Gemara no oath is required where the debtor has land available for collection, the Geonic enactment should not extend farther. The Ritva in Ketubot 85 explicitly writes that in such a case the Geonic enactment calls only for cherem, not an oath. Since the matter is entirely one of enactment, practical law should follow the lenient side. The Baal HaIttur, Baal HaTerumot, Maharam, and Ritva all stand together. The fact that the Rosh and Rashba disagree does not outweigh them. One may explain the Rosh consistently with his teacher Maharam. If the oath of insolvency is already Talmudic, the Rosh could ask what the Geonim added and answer that they required an oath even when land remained. But according to the majority who regard the entire insolvency oath as Geonic, we should not extend the enactment beyond a debtor from whom no property can otherwise be collected. This also explains the Rosh's statement that without an oath the Geonic enactment would accomplish nothing. It still plainly accomplishes something where a debtor has hidden or transferred all assets and claims to own nothing. According to the Rosh, however, that basic case already carried an oath under Talmudic law, so the Geonic enactment had to add the case where land remains. The Rama's ruling is therefore sound. I also found that the Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma chapter 1, siman 22, rules that the debtor is not sworn in such a case, but is merely placed under cherem. Who can come after the joint determination of these authorities? Maharam in Mordechai may imply that if the debtor was initially known to possess money and later claims that it was lost, he must swear. But that follows his premise that the oath is Mishnaic. According to our ruling that it is only a Geonic enactment, there is no reason to distinguish. The Hagahot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 53, likewise appears to hold explicitly that even in such a case no oath is imposed, and the Rama's unqualified wording supports this. Where the creditor asserts with certainty that the debtor currently has cash, however, he may impose a shevuat heset, as explained in Urim.
