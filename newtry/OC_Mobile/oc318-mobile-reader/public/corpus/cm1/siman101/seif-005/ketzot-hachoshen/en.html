A debt instrument belonging to the borrower. Rashba, responsum 929, disagrees with those who treat debt instruments as ordinary items available for judicial collection. He proves this from the case of a married woman who injured another. The Gemara asks why she cannot sell her ketubah to the injured person, but answers that she would certainly waive it to her husband and the court would have labored for nothing. If debt instruments themselves could be seized and transferred by beit din as payment, the court could simply award the ketubah instrument directly to the injured person. Judicial collection is a Torah mechanism and should then remove the seller's later power of waiver. Ramban and Ran similarly treat debt instruments as outside ordinary seizure. The conceptual basis may be that debts are not themselves money or money equivalent. Kiddushin with one's own loan is ineffective, which suggests the debt is not presently money in the ordinary sense. Where one betroths a woman with another person's debt, Ran explains that she is betrothed through the benefit she receives from the transfer, not because the debt itself is literal money. Tosafot, however, explains kiddushin with one's own loan differently. It fails because the woman receives no new benefit, since the loan money was already hers for use. With another person's debt there is a new benefit. That approach allows one to regard the debt as monetary and therefore potentially subject to creditor collection. Rambam also explains that one's own prior loan cannot serve as kiddushin money because the money is already with her. This too suggests a debt can have monetary status. Therefore the authorities who permit judicial collection from debt instruments can be sustained. Rashba's proof from the married woman is still answerable. Even if the court can collect the property lien embedded in the ketubah, the husband's personal obligation is not itself transferred in a way that eliminates waiver. Rabbeinu Tam explains that a sold debt can be waived because the seller retains the debtor's personal obligation, something intangible that is not sold. The same can remain true after judicial transfer. Thus the injured party might receive the ketubah's property rights yet still be vulnerable to the woman's waiver of the personal obligation. The Shach writes that if debt instruments are subject to judicial collection, ordinary writing and delivery should not be required. I find this surprising. Land is certainly subject to judicial collection, yet actual judicial transfer requires the appropriate order and delivery before the creditor takes fruits. So too with debt instruments: until the documents are actually delivered and the transfer language effectuated, no completed collection exists. Even if the borrower refuses, beit din can write for the creditor the assignment language, "Acquire it and all its liens," and deliver the documents on the borrower's behalf. That is the equivalent of judicial transfer. In an oral debt where no document exists and a three party transfer cannot be forced, there may be no practical mode of completing such a collection.
