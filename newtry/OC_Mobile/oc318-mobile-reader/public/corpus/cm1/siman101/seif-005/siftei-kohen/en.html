If the debtor has promissory notes, etc. The rulings of the Mechaber and Rama do not appear correct to me. They understood the Rashba and the Tur in the name of the Rosh to disagree with Baal HaTerumot. In truth there is no dispute. A creditor generally cannot be compelled to accept promissory notes instead of cash, movable property, or land, because collection should be arranged for his benefit. He should not be forced to litigate against third-party debtors with whom he has no direct dealings, and the notes may ultimately prove uncollectible. The Rashba's responsum stating that notes are subject to judicial collection concerns a debtor who has no other means of payment. In such a case beit din may compel the debtor to sell the notes properly, with written assignment and delivery, or may appraise and transfer them to the creditor if he wishes. The same is relevant when the debtor is absent or has died leaving only notes, when partners must divide notes, or when a later creditor argues that he left notes available for an earlier creditor. The Tur's cited dispute also concerns a case where notes are the debtor's only property. One view requires the debtor himself to pursue collection because the documents are not tangible money and are prone to failure. The Rosh rules that beit din may appraise them according to the debtor's solvency and the quality of the obligors and may transfer them as payment. His ruling that partners may be compelled to divide notes proves at least as much regarding payment of a creditor. Thus, where other suitable assets exist, the creditor may insist upon them. Where notes are the only assets, they may be appraised and transferred.
The practical ruling regarding collection from promissory notes. The principal ruling follows the leading teachers who hold that promissory notes are subject to judicial collection. If the creditor wishes, beit din immediately appraises the notes and transfers them to him without requiring a separate written assignment and delivery. The same applies if the debtor is absent or deceased, and there is no need to wait until the notes mature. The proofs advanced by the Rashba and Ran against collection from notes are not decisive. Judicial collection cannot confer greater substance upon the note than a voluntary sale by its owner. Without Rabbi Natan's lien, the original debtor could still assert that the note was issued in trust, was paid, or could waive it, because the collecting creditor would come only through him. Rabbi Natan's rule instead makes the first creditor a direct litigant against the second debtor, preventing such an admission from harming him. The case of two persons holding notes against one another likewise proves nothing, because there each seeks collection at face value rather than by discounted appraisal. Although some language of the Rashba suggests that judicial collection is stronger than sale and prevents waiver, in my view the leading teachers held only that, when the debtor lacks other property, beit din may stand in his place and effect what amounts to a compulsory sale. After the note has been appraised as payment, the original creditor may still waive it unless Rabbi Natan's lien independently applies. This also explains the Gemara's treatment of a married woman's ketubah and the language of Baal HaTerumot. Accordingly, notes are collectable by appraisal when necessary, but their transfer does not inherently become stronger than an ordinary sale.
For those who possess only promissory notes and wish to sell them and pay, etc. If they wish to return the notes to the original debtor in exchange for money, the holder of the note may not object merely because he wishes to preserve his ability to collect from that debtor. When the debtor lacks cash and the notes are his only realizable assets, they are appraised or sold for the creditor's benefit. The precise arrangement depends upon whether the creditor accepts the notes themselves or insists upon proceeds obtainable from their sale, as explained above.
