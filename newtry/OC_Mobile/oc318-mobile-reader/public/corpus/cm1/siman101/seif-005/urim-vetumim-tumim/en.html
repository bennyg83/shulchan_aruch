Even if he has other movable property, etc. Know that many great authorities hold that promissory notes are not subject to judicial collection at all, even where the debtor has no other property. Although in such a case the debt owed to him is subject to Rabbi Natan's lien, one might still have thought that beit din merely compels him to sell the notes to others and pay his creditor the proceeds, or to transfer them by the required written conveyance, but that the notes themselves cannot be assigned to the creditor by way of judicial collection. The Rashba, however, agrees with the leading authorities who hold that they may be collected, and the Shach likewise accepts this in principle. In my view there is decisive proof for these authorities from Bava Kamma 14b. The Gemara explains that where the tortfeasor dies, the injured party may collect from property that is equivalent to money and not subject to onaah, meaning land, but not movable property of the heirs because it is not encumbered. The Gemara asks that slaves and documents likewise are not subject to onaah. If documents were not a form of collectible property at all, this question would have no place. The Mishnah would concern only assets that could have been collected from the tortfeasor during his lifetime, while after his death movable property becomes unavailable. Documents, by contrast, would never have been collectible even during his lifetime. Nor should death improve the creditor's position. Likewise, when the Gemara later explains the phrase as property acquired with money and asks that slaves also are acquired with money, it could simply have said that the Mishnah speaks of the tortfeasor while alive and excludes documents because they are not acquired with money, as Tosafot note. The fact that it does not do so shows that documents themselves are within the law of collection. A further proof is Ketubot 100b, which states that documents may be sold without public announcement. Tosafot explain that the creditor had seized them during the debtor's lifetime, because otherwise the heirs' movable property would not be encumbered. If documents could not be collected at all, what sale could be made? The heirs would not be obligated to execute a written assignment, and beit din would have to wait until the debts were paid in cash. It follows that documents can be transferred through a judicial appraisal, and only announcement is unnecessary. The Ritva explicitly derives this from that passage. Indeed, once beit din can effect collection without the ordinary written assignment, even an oral debt may be transferred in collection. Beit din appraises the value of that receivable and, by judicial authority, gives the creditor the right to collect it. In Ketubot 84, Rabbi Tarfon says that where the deceased had money owed by others, the creditor receives it, while Rabbi Akiva disagrees only because the heirs need not swear. Tosafot explain that this applies even without Rabbi Natan's rule. Had the heirs first collected even land, the creditor would have had no prior right in it, yet while the debt remains in a third party's hands he may collect it. The Rosh likewise rules so in Bava Batra. This is possible only if debts and documents are themselves collectible property. There was therefore no need for the Shach to infer the Rosh's disagreement with the Baal HaTerumot merely from a responsum stating that documents may be divided between partners. The Rosh's discussion in Yesh Nochalin proves directly that even an ordinary receivable is collectible, all the more a written note. In responsum 39:4 he likewise writes that documents are property and whatever assets of the debtor are found, whether land, movable property, or documents, are given to his creditor. The Ritva limits this to a debtor who has no other assets, while the Shach also says that if other property exists the creditor cannot insist on documents, for he can say, "What use have I for a doubtful note? Give me tangible property." Yet the Baal HaTerumot answers the case of two parties who hold claims against each other differently: a note collectible only from inferior land is worth less than its face value. That answer would be unnecessary if documents could not be collected whenever other assets exist. Maharit cites the same question and answer in the name of the Rosh, proving that the Rosh recognizes collection of documents even when other assets exist. The Shach's true concern must therefore be that where other assets exist the creditor can object that the receivable may never be collected. But this concern is already reflected in the judicial appraisal. The note is valued at its market worth in light of the risk of nonpayment, just as a ketubah sold for its contingent value is appraised according to the possibility of death or divorce. Thus the concern supplies no reason to exclude documents from collection altogether. Maharam of Rothenburg also writes that a woman collecting her ketubah may receive all forms of property, even bran, because all are deemed suitable payment, except that she ordinarily need not accept promissory notes because collecting from debtors, especially non Jewish debtors, is burdensome for her. This itself implies that an ordinary creditor may collect notes even where other assets exist. The Rosh followed the approach of Maharam his teacher. We also learn that even after a receivable has matured, there is no rule that the debtor must first collect it himself and then pay cash. The discussions of reciprocal collection concern matured debts, yet the authorities still speak of appraising and transferring the notes themselves. Thus documents always remain capable of judicial collection.
However. A difficulty remains in the case where one party has intermediate land and the other only inferior land. It is understandable to answer that the owner of the intermediate land cannot pay the other with the latter's own note if that note is collectible only from inferior land and therefore worth less than its face amount. But the reverse question remains. A note secured by intermediate land is certainly not worth more than the amount written in it. Why should the holder of the intermediate land not collect first from the other by demanding the note that the other holds against him? Since that note is movable property in his possession, a debtor who possesses movable property cannot ordinarily turn his creditor to land. He could simply say, "Give me my note in payment," leaving matters exactly where they stood. It would be strained to say that the Gemara's later establishment of the case with heirs was already assumed. The Tur in siman 85 cites the reciprocal collection rule without limiting it to heirs, and after the Geonic enactment even movable property of heirs is collectible. The correct distinction therefore appears to be this: the authorities who recognize collection of documents mean that the debtor may choose to give the notes in payment even against the creditor's preference, just as he may choose among other movable property. But if the debtor wishes to pay with other property, even inferior land, the creditor cannot compel him to hand over the notes. The second debtor can say that the creditor has no direct standing against the person who owes the receivable. The underlying reason is that a receivable is considered property not presently possessed, even if evidenced by a note. A firstborn does not take a double portion in it, and Tosafot, the Rosh, and Ramban explain that a creditor has no ordinary lien on such receivables. His right comes only from Rabbi Natan. Where no other assets exist, Rabbi Natan's lien attaches, and the creditor can compel transfer of the receivable. Even apart from that, the mitzvah of repaying a debt permits beit din to take whatever value the debtor has. But where the debtor has other collectible property, Rabbi Natan's special lien does not attach in the same way, and the creditor has no basis to force him to surrender the receivable. By contrast, the debtor may elect to pay with it, just as heirs may voluntarily satisfy their father's creditor from their own movable property even though that creditor has no lien upon it. This also clarifies the passage in Ketubot 81 concerning a man who was owed money by his deceased brother while the brother's widow was awaiting yibbum. The Gemara asks how the widow could ever collect that receivable as part of her ketubah, since the yavam can say, "You are not my litigant, I borrowed from my brother, not from you," unless Rabbi Natan's rule applies. If any promissory note could be forced upon the debtor as judicial payment, she could simply demand that note. Maharam therefore holds that a woman is not ordinarily paid her ketubah with debts because collection is burdensome for her. More fundamentally, however, the authorities' rule of collection through documents rests where the creditor has a substantive lien in them, namely through Rabbi Natan. Without that lien, documents differ from ordinary movable property because their substance is not money and the creditor has no direct right against the second debtor. For this reason, according to our law, where Rabbi Natan applies even to a woman's ketubah, she may in appropriate circumstances receive debts as payment. This may explain why the Mechaber and Rama omitted Maharam's limitation. Still, according to the distinction just stated, a creditor cannot force the debtor to pay specifically with documents when the debtor has other property, and the second debtor may say that he is not that creditor's litigant. A further issue is whether, once a note has actually been transferred through beit din, the original creditor can still forgive the debt. The Shach concludes that he can. The Rashba and Ran indicate otherwise. In the case of a woman whose tort liability is difficult to collect because any sale of her ketubah may later be forgiven by her to her husband, the Rashba asks why beit din cannot transfer the ketubah directly to the injured party. Since judicial collection is effective by Torah law, she should no longer be able to forgive it. His responsum 1111 likewise implies that only because documents lack a law of direct collection can forgiveness remain possible. According to the leading authorities who recognize such collection, forgiveness should no longer work. The Ran similarly asks why Rabbi Natan's rule is needed where beit din can itself transfer the debt, indicating that judicial collection cuts off the original creditor's power to forgive. Although this question can be read as directed to the opinion that even under Rabbi Natan forgiveness remains possible, the Ran's adoption elsewhere of the Rashba's question still shows that he regards completed judicial collection as stronger than an ordinary assignment. The Baal HaTerumot, in Shaar 51, cites the French sages that one who says a note is merely a trust document is not believed when this harms another creditor, and a migo that he could forgive it does not help where the note had already come before beit din. Once beit din is ordered to extract from one debtor for another, it is regarded as already collected, and the original creditor cannot forgive. Thus even sages who permit forgiveness under Rabbi Natan recognize the stronger force of an actual judicial transfer. The Shach finds support only in the Baal HaTerumot's discussion of the opinion that documents are not collectible when the debtor has other assets, because the creditor could receive the note and then the original lender could forgive it. But that passage concerns a situation where Rabbi Natan does not attach. When no other assets exist and Rabbi Natan does attach, we rule that the original lender cannot forgive. Judicial collection does not extinguish Rabbi Natan's lien. This is also explicit in Chavot Yair siman 80. Even where the debtor has other assets, the Baal HaTerumot stands alone against the French sages, Rashba, and Ran. Perhaps those who permit forgiveness follow Rabbenu Tam, who holds that even though sale of notes is effective by Torah law, the underlying personal obligation remains with the original creditor. But according to most authorities, forgiveness works after an ordinary sale only because such sale is rabbinic. Judicial collection, by contrast, is of Torah force, as the Rashba writes and as the Shach himself indicates by holding that no written assignment is required. It therefore follows that after beit din has transferred the note the original creditor cannot forgive it. The conceptual explanation is that when beit din assigns the debt without a written transfer and forgiveness is no longer possible, the second debtor becomes directly obligated to the first creditor by force of beit din, while the middle creditor's right is extinguished. It is as though collection had already occurred. This is how the French sages cited by the Baal HaTerumot describe it. In the ketubah case, that would leave the woman effectively without a ketubah, more seriously than where she merely wrote a note in another's name subject to the condition of later widowhood or divorce. This fully answers the difficulty.
