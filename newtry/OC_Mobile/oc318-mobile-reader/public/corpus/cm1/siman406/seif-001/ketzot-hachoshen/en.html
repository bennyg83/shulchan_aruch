An ox of a gentile, and likewise an ox of consecrated property. Rosh was uncertain whether a firstborn animal in the hands of a kohen today counts as the owner's money. Rabbi Yosei HaGelili holds that lesser sacred offerings are privately owned, while the Sages disagree. Later authorities, including Chelkat Mechokek and Lechem Mishneh, understand Rambam as ruling like Rabbi Yosei HaGelili. There is strong support from the laws of susceptibility to impurity. Rambam rules that liquid makes food susceptible only when it comes with the owner's consent. Chullin explains a case involving a peace-offering cow that crossed a river while moisture remained on it. If lesser sacred offerings had no private owner, whose consent could make the moisture effective? The case therefore fits naturally if a peace offering remains the owner's monetary property during life. The same principle appears in Zevachim: according to Rabbi Yosei HaGelili, a person can create a prohibition in lesser sacred offerings because they are his property, whereas according to the Sages he cannot prohibit something that is not his. This also explains why the Gemara specifically uses a peace offering that crossed a river before slaughter. After slaughter and the completion of the sacrificial service, the owners receive their portion from Heaven's table rather than through ordinary private ownership. Therefore the owner's consent for the liquid must exist while the animal is still alive and privately owned. The case is thus strong evidence that we follow Rabbi Yosei HaGelili regarding lesser sacred offerings.
Rambam's rulings still require reconciliation. He says a peace offering that damages another can be collected from its meat, which follows Rabbi Yosei HaGelili's view that lesser sacred offerings are private property. Yet he also rules that one who steals sacred offerings, whether higher or lesser sanctity, is exempt from double payment, and that one who sells his burnt offering or peace offering accomplishes nothing. The theft rule can be explained by the special verse, "and it is stolen from the man's house," which provides an independent exclusion in theft law. The sale rule requires a different distinction. Rabbi Yosei HaGelili's private ownership extends to the meat or economic benefit of the peace offering. The owner can transfer the right to consume the meat after the valid sacrifice. But he cannot transfer the sacrificial identity itself so that the animal will be slaughtered and offered in the purchaser's name. The sacrifice remains designated for the original owner who consecrated it. Just as one may not change one type of sacrifice into another, one may not change the name of its owner. This is why Rambam says that a victim collecting from a damaging peace offering collects by eating the corresponding amount of its meat in sanctity. The animal is still sacrificed for the original owner's obligation. The victim acquires only the economic meat right. Accordingly, the baraita that "one who sells his burnt offering or peace offering accomplishes nothing" concerns sale of the sacrifice for the purchaser's atonement, as Rashi explains. Even Rabbi Yosei HaGelili agrees that such a sale fails. This resolves the apparent contradiction in Pesachim as well. The penalty discussed there concerns the purchaser's money after he attempted to buy another's sacrificial atonement. The underlying sale of atonement is invalid even according to Rabbi Yosei HaGelili. The Gemara's difficulty is then how the purchase money could become ordinary money when the sacred status itself never transferred. There remains a substantial question from Kiddushin, which implies that a woman can be betrothed with lesser sacred offerings while the animal is alive because they are private property. Before slaughter, however, sacred meat is prohibited for ordinary benefit. How can something forbidden in benefit serve as kiddushin money? Tosafot elsewhere says even a future permitted use may not suffice if the item cannot currently serve as ordinary purchase money. Likewise, if lesser sacred offerings are prohibited in benefit before their service, why does Rabbi Yosei HaGelili treat denial of them as denial of money and why does an owner pay for their damage? This question remains difficult and requires further study.
