And he declared it ownerless before standing in judgment. My brother, Rabbi Yehudah Kohen, asked from Bava Kamma. If an ox killed a person and afterward caused monetary damage, when the court first adjudicates the capital case the Gemara asks why it cannot afterward adjudicate the civil case. But once the ox was sentenced to stoning, its owner surely despaired and abandoned it. Keritot says that when the witnesses against a sentenced ox are later discredited, whoever first takes the ox acquires it because its owners had abandoned it. Why then can the civil case still proceed after the death sentence, if the ox was abandoned before standing in monetary judgment?
The answer is that one cannot effectively declare ownerless property that has become prohibited in benefit. Pesachim shows that after chametz becomes prohibited a person no longer has legal control to nullify or dispose of it. Rashi likewise explains that after an ox is sentenced to stoning, its owner cannot consecrate it because it is no longer in his legal possession. Property prohibited in benefit can still have an owner. Rivash proves that such property can be acquired, and Rashba says an orlah etrog can count as "yours" despite its prohibition of benefit. The owner's problem is not lack of title but lack of legal control. Because it is outside his legal domain, he cannot consecrate or declare it ownerless. Accordingly, the rule that an ox declared ownerless before standing in judgment is exempt rests on the Torah requirement that liability belong to an owner from beginning to end, but the ownerless declaration must occur while the owner still has power to make it. Once the ox has become prohibited in benefit by sentence, an attempted declaration is ineffective. Why then, when the witnesses are later discredited, does whoever first takes the ox acquire it because the owners had abandoned it? Once the testimony is discredited, it becomes clear retroactively that the ox never truly became prohibited in benefit. The owners' abandonment therefore operated upon property that, in truth, had remained within their legal power. Despair is different from formal ownerless declaration. Despair can operate even where property is outside the owner's legal possession, as with stolen property. But despair does not itself remove title until another person acquires. Thus even if the owner despaired after the death sentence, the ox did not automatically cease to have an owner. This preserves the possibility of the later civil adjudication.
