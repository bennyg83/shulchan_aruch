He must pay for its damage and cannot, etc. Beit Yosef attributes this rule to Rambam in Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, chapter 5, but I searched the Rambam and found no such statement. Rambam’s ruling that an owner pays when his animal damages other fields proves nothing, for the verse itself speaks of grazing in another field, and the Gemara limits liability to a case where the owner fenced the animal in and it burrowed out, or the fence fell at night. Beit Yosef’s assertion that the Rosh is inconclusive is also surprising, since the Rosh ultimately cites Rabbeinu Chananel and rejects Tosafot, so the Tur correctly states that the Rosh concludes like Rabbeinu Chananel. Maharshal rejects Rabbeinu Chananel because shepherds are presumed robbers and disqualified from testimony for failing to guard against entry into other fields. He argues that if every landowner had to fence his field, all fields would ordinarily be fenced. I do not agree. Even if the animal’s owner is exempt where the field was unfenced, people generally do not fence all fields, because damage by stray animals is uncommon and one cannot fence the entire world. Shepherds are disqualified because they intentionally graze in others’ fields. Nor may we decide against Abaye merely because Rav Yosef was his teacher. From Abaye and Rava onward, the law generally follows the later authority, and the Gemara’s anonymous discussion appears to accept Abaye’s answer that liability for tooth applies where the animal burrowed through or the wall fell at night. Halachot Gedolot and Raavan explicitly rule like Abaye. Since Halachot Gedolot, Rabbeinu Chananel, Raavan, the Rosh, and the Tur all rule that the owner is exempt where the field was not fenced, that is the practical ruling, especially since their reading of the sugya is primary.
