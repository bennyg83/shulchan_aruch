'Buy this particular item for me.' The text carefully says 'this particular item,' meaning the specific merchandise mentioned by the Tur and Mechaber at the beginning of the siman.
And he bought it without specification, he acquires it for him, etc. The Tur reads, 'presumably he acquires it.' In the Perishah I wrote that 'presumably' has the same force as 'without specification': since he did not reveal a contrary intent at the time of purchase, we presume he certainly acquired it for Reuven.
Even though Shimon paid with his own money, etc. We say that Shimon lent his money for the purchase and will later be reimbursed. Ordinarily a person keeps his word after promising to buy for another. The Tur explains: once he said, 'Buy for me,' he intended a genuine purchase on the other's behalf, like saying, 'Acquire for me.' This applies specifically where he said, 'Buy for me.' If he said, 'Bring it to me so that I may buy it,' and the intermediary then bought it, he can acquire it for himself, because he was appointed only to bring the opportunity and can retract. This is the Mechaber's distinction at the end of the siman.
But if Shimon retracted before meshichah, etc. The Tur explains that since money does not itself complete acquisition of movable property, Reuven had not yet acquired. At the moment of meshichah Shimon can acquire for himself. There is not even a mi shepara sanction between agent and principal, because mi shepara was instituted between seller and purchaser, not between agent and principal. Here there is no need for the reason I wrote in siman 182:7, because the underlying sale remains effective to the purchaser in any event. Mi shepara was instituted only against one who withdraws from the sale after money has been given.
In Reuven's name, etc. The Tur says, 'with Reuven in mind.' In the Perishah I wrote that this is not literal. Rather, the seller gave it without specification, and ordinarily intends to transfer ownership to the owner of the money. This excludes where Shimon expressly tells the seller that he is buying for himself. Otherwise, why should it matter that the money belonged to Reuven? Even if the agent's own money were used, if he expressly gave it to the seller in Reuven's name, Shimon could not later retract. Indeed the opening clause itself speaks of Shimon's own money and says he can retract before meshichah only where he gave it without specification, implying that if he expressly gave it in Reuven's name he could not retract.
And he says, 'I am buying for myself.' The Tur says that before meshichah Shimon retracted and bought it for himself. In the Perishah I explained that he immediately designated other money of his own to reimburse the principal, and this is what is called buying for himself. He did not merely seek to convert Reuven's money into a loan against himself. Thus, when he informed the seller before meshichah, he acquires for himself. Otherwise it would conflict with Rambam and the Mechaber's earlier ruling that even if he merely treats the principal's funds as a loan against himself, the acquisition remains the principal's.
Even if he initially agreed, etc. This appears difficult from the end of siman 269, where Rama concludes that according to all opinions if he initially agreed to acquire for both, both acquire and he cannot retract. Rama also concludes here that if he purchased with Reuven's money he must divide with him, whereas at the end of siman 269 he says that if Reuven had available money, then even where Shimon actually bought with his own money both acquire. One can reconcile the rulings. Here Shimon claims: although I initially agreed to acquire for both, at the moment of purchase I expressly retracted and acquired for myself. Therefore his earlier consent does not help once he explicitly revoked it at the transaction. For the same reason, even Reuven's having available money does not matter when Shimon bought with his own funds after express retraction. At the end of siman 269, by contrast, Shimon does not claim an express retraction at the moment of acquisition. He simply purchased without specification and only later sought to retract. The context there compares it to one who lifts a found item for another, which likewise concerns an act done without contrary declaration. Therefore, if there is one favorable factor, either he initially agreed to buy for both or Reuven had money available, both acquire. This appears to reconcile Rama's rulings, both of which are from Mordechai. See what I write there.
The principal cannot retract, etc. This is a separate point referring back to the preceding discussion. Everything until now concerns retraction by the agent. The principal, however, cannot retract in the same manner.
