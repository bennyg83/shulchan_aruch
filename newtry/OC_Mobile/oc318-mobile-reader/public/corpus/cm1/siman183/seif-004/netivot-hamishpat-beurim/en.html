In Reuven's name. See Sema, seif katan 11. The result of his discussion is as follows. If the agent pays his own money without qualification, he may retract before meshichah. If he pays in the principal's name, he cannot retract. If he pays the principal's money, even without naming the principal, he cannot retract before meshichah unless he informs the seller and designates other money in its place. The reason can be explained this way. When the principal's money is paid, even without naming him, a d'oraisa money acquisition arises between seller and principal, because the seller intends to transfer to the owner of the money. Rabbinically either side can retract until meshichah, subject to mi shepara. So long as no retraction occurs, the d'oraisa acquisition stands. The agent is now an outsider because the money has left his possession. He cannot unilaterally cancel the principal's acquisition. He must inform the seller that he is withdrawing from the agency and intends any future meshichah only for himself, and must designate other money so that he is no longer a robber of the principal's funds. Once he does this, the seller, by delivering the item to the agent on that new basis, is effectively retracting from the principal's money acquisition and transferring to the agent through meshichah. The seller has reason to do so because he fears the principal may himself retract, since only a money acquisition existed. Additionally, once the agent substitutes other money, he can become the effective owner of the purchase money. Where the agent pays his own money without qualification, he may retract before meshichah because he does not become the principal's creditor until he actually receives the purchased item. If the money is lost in the seller's hands before meshichah in circumstances where the seller is exempt from returning it, or if the seller is violent and refuses to deliver, the principal is not liable merely because he had told the agent, "Go buy for me." This is no better than the rules concerning a debt instrument or pledge where payment was conditioned on obtaining the designated object. Therefore, before meshichah the agent may withdraw. But where he says explicitly, "Here is the money, and transfer the field to Reuven," he can no longer withdraw. He has effectively caused the seller to acquire the money on Reuven's behalf. If a lender promises a loan and actually transfers the loan money to another person for the borrower's benefit, the lender cannot thereafter retract; if the borrower later accepts, the loan is treated as having vested retroactively. The borrower himself may still reject it upon learning of it, like a beneficiary who protests against a gift acquired for him through another. So too here: once the agent paid in Reuven's name, he is like an outsider and cannot retract, while the principal may still refuse once informed.
Reuven knew of inexpensive merchandise. See Sema, seif katan 13, who questions this from the end of siman 269. In my view the cases can be reconciled. More fundamentally, why should the agent be believed at all here? Rama above in seif 2 says that with specified land, because acquiring it for oneself after accepting agency is deceitful, we presume he originally bought for the principal. Mechaber in this seif likewise rules that one who was told, "Buy this item for me," is not believed to say, "I bought it for myself." Why then should inexpensive merchandise be different? The answer is that the "deceitful" rationale applies specifically to ordinary purchase transactions. One who interferes with another's pending purchase is called wicked. An agent who accepted the mission and then appropriates the same transaction for himself is therefore acting deceitfully, and this supports the presumption that he performed his agency. With a found object, however, one who acquires it for himself rather than another is not called wicked, as explained in siman 237. Bargain merchandise that is exceptionally cheap resembles a found object in this respect. This also explains why the poskim give a different reason in siman 269. There the rule is based on the agent's silence being acceptance of agency and on the chazakah that an agent performs his mission. In a found object, no "deceitful purchase" rationale exists. If the agent had expressly retracted before lifting it for himself, he would not be a deceiver. In an ordinary purchase, by contrast, even if financing issues make the agency less certain, the special concern of deceit remains. Yet where the principal has no money and the agent would have to lend him his own funds, the chazakah that the agent performed his mission is weaker. Perhaps he decided not to make the loan because he learned the principal was not creditworthy, or perhaps he simply lacked available funds. Even if the principal gave him money but the agent left it unused and instead purchased with his own money, the agent demonstrably changed the method of performance, and the usual chazakah of exact fulfillment is weakened. Therefore, where the merchandise is exceptionally cheap and resembles a found object, both grounds for disbelieving him disappear: there is no deceit in acquiring such an opportunity for oneself, and there is no strong chazakah of performance where he had to advance his own money or visibly changed from the principal's funds to his own. He is therefore believed to say he bought for himself. In siman 269, by contrast, the principal sent him only to perform the act of acquisition, such as meshichah or hagbahah, while the principal himself would later pay the seller. The agent did not need to lend any money. The chazakah that an agent performs his mission therefore applies fully. Nor can he claim fear that the principal might not pay and that the seller would then hold him liable, because he could simply have told the seller he was acquiring in the principal's name. This distinction is reflected in Rama's wording: here he says the agent "bought it with his own money," whereas in siman 269 he merely says "bought it," omitting any statement that the agent financed the purchase. Beit Yosef cites Rashba concerning shopkeepers who, when they want to buy garments, sometimes tell each other they are buying for one another. Rashba allows the buyer to say that he was merely joking so the other would not raise the price. This appears to concern a case where the other shopkeeper had not first instructed him, "Buy this particular item for me." If such a direct instruction had been given and the item was not exceptionally cheap, Tur and Mechaber rule that the agent cannot say, "I bought for myself." Rashba's broader wording can be understood where the buyer expressly retracted before witnesses prior to the purchase, or Rashba may disagree with Rama. Our ruling follows Mechaber.
