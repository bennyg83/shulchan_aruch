But if Shimon retracted. The Tur cites Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia: if Reuven told Shimon, "Buy this item for me," and Shimon bought it, Reuven ordinarily acquires from the time of meshichah. Since Reuven said, "Buy for me," Shimon is understood to have intended a full purchase for Reuven, as though Reuven had said, "Acquire it for me." Even if after meshichah Shimon says, "I intended to buy it for myself," he is not believed. This is so even if Shimon paid with his own money, because the money he advanced is treated as though he lent it to Reuven. If, however, Shimon retracted before meshichah and said he now intended to buy for himself, he can acquire for himself. Money alone does not complete the purchase, so Reuven had not yet acquired. When meshichah occurs, Shimon can acquire for himself. The curse of "Who exacted payment" does not apply, because that enactment was made between seller and purchaser, not between principal and agent. This is specifically where Shimon gave the seller his own money. If Reuven gave Shimon money with which to buy, and Shimon handed that money to the seller for Reuven, then before meshichah Shimon cannot simply reverse course and buy for himself unless he informs the seller and the seller now intends to transfer to Shimon. The seller initially intended to sell to the owner of the money. At first this appears difficult according to what I wrote above, that Rabbi Yochanan does not rely upon the theory, "he carried out the agency," where there is no principal's money. How then can Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia say that where the agent uses his own money, the principal acquires without seller notification? The answer is that where the principal expressly told the agent, "Go buy for me," the principal is treated as owner of the money once the purchase is completed. The agent expended the money at the principal's direction, and the principal must reimburse him. The advance is therefore only a loan to the principal, and the principal becomes the owner of the money for purposes of the purchase. This differs from Rabbeinu Yerucham's case. There, the buyer acted entirely on his own and bought with his own money intending to benefit another. The intended recipient is under no obligation to reimburse him and can refuse the transaction. The buyer remains the true owner of the money. Likewise, when a husband pays the dyer and intends the result for his wife, the wife is not obligated to reimburse him and is not the owner of the money. Why, then, can the agent retract before meshichah if, when the principal actually supplied the money, the agent ordinarily cannot retract without notifying the seller? Because the agent's own money becomes like a loan to the principal only after the purchase becomes irreversible. Before meshichah, when the transaction can still be rescinded and the money can in principle be recovered, the agent has not yet definitively expended anything at the principal's command. The principal has therefore not yet become indebted to him, and the money is not yet treated as the principal's money. Ritva states an analogous principle concerning one who says, "Place a maneh on a rock in a place of loss and I will become obligated to you." If the other person places it there at his direction and does not recover it, the obligor becomes liable. If he immediately takes it back, nothing was ultimately done at the obligor's direction and no obligation arose. So too here. After meshichah, when the purchase can no longer be retracted, the agent's expenditure is like money placed beyond recovery at the principal's direction and is treated as a loan to him. Before meshichah, it is not. There is also strong reason to say Rabbeinu Yerucham speaks of a buyer who gives his own money purely as a gift for the intended recipient. Were the buyer expecting reimbursement, the purchase would not necessarily be a pure benefit for the recipient, since he would incur an obligation to pay. Rama in siman 195 says that a purchase made for someone in his absence is not automatically a benefit because perhaps he does not want the purchase. This supports reading Rabbeinu Yerucham as dealing with a gratuitous purchase using the buyer's own money. Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia further implies that the seller's ordinary intent is to sell to the owner of the money, even where no express statement was made to the seller. This provides a clear explanation of Rabbi Yochanan. Seller notification is unnecessary where the principal is the owner of the money because the seller's presumed intent already follows the money owner. Rosh's formulation appears similar but not identical. Rosh emphasizes the agent's intent to acquire for the owner of the money. Under that theory, if the agent purchased without telling the seller for whose benefit he was acting and retracted before meshichah, he could acquire for himself because the seller's intent runs to the agent while the ultimate attribution depends on the agent's own intent. But if he had expressly told the seller that the purchase was for Reuven, he would need to inform the seller before switching the purchase to himself. Still, Rosh's formulation is difficult. If the seller intends primarily for the agent, and everything depends upon the agent's intention to benefit the owner of the money, why should the presence of principal's money make the principal acquire more readily? If intention alone is sufficient, the same should be true when the agent uses his own money. His final words concerning the dyer and the wife are especially difficult, because if the husband initially intends that the wife acquire directly from the dyer, the husband never first owns the garment. The statement that one cannot transfer one's own property to another then seems inapposite. This requires study. The approach of Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia is clearer. The seller's ordinary intent is to transfer to the owner of the money. Therefore, where the principal's money is used, no notification is needed. Where no principal's money exists, notification is required. Shiltei Gibborim and Taz objected that Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia's need for seller notification before the agent can switch the purchase to himself sounds like the rejected scholars of the West. According to what I have explained there is no difficulty. Rabbi Yochanan rejects the need for notification only where the person for whom the purchase is intended is already the owner of the purchase money. Where the agent uses his own funds or changes the intended buyer so that the seller's presumed attribution no longer fits, seller notification can be necessary. This also resolves a question from Bava Metzia concerning a robber who later buys the stolen field from its true owner. The Gemara says the first buyer can sometimes benefit from the robber's later acquisition because the robber wants to preserve his trustworthiness. Why not simply say that when the robber buys from the true owner, he intends to acquire for the earlier purchaser, and one may acquire for another in his absence? The answer is that the earlier purchaser did not supply the money for the later acquisition. Where there is no money of the intended recipient, seller notification is required. The true owner was not told to sell to the earlier purchaser. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ritzba giving essentially this question and answer. The Shach also asks from the Gemara concerning one who buys land in the name of the Exilarch. He understood the Gemara's question to be why the Exilarch does not acquire despite not supplying the money. But the passage means something else. The baraita says one who buys land in another's name cannot compel that other person to write him a deed of proof unless he stipulated so. The Gemara asks: if the actual purchaser acquired the field, why should this conflict with the scholars of the West, who require seller notification? The answer is that he informed the seller that the field was truly for himself, while using the Exilarch's name for another purpose. The Exilarch never acquired at all. This passage in fact supplies strong proof that according to our law, a person who buys with his own money while mentioning another person's name can still acquire for himself, because he is the owner of the money and does not intend to acquire for the named person. Therefore, if an agent uses his own money and before meshichah withdraws from the agency, he can acquire for himself even if he had initially mentioned the principal's name, so long as the seller's actual intent is not fixed otherwise. Sema writes more stringently that if the buyer gave his own money but stated to the seller that the purchase was for Reuven, he cannot acquire for himself. In my view the Gemara of the Exilarch indicates otherwise. Taz also disagrees with Sema, though he requires both that the buyer expressly state his own intention and that the money be his. I think the decisive element is that the seller's ordinary intent follows the owner of the money. Thus where the buyer is the owner of the money and does not intend to acquire for the named person, he acquires for himself. The consensus of the decisors is that the law of the scholars of the West is rejected. Maharshal likewise inferred this from Rif's omission of any general requirement to notify the seller. A final question arises from Rif's citation of the episode of Rav Kahana and flax. Rav Kahana advanced money for flax. The flax rose in price. The owners then sold it to others intending to give the proceeds to Rav Kahana. Rav ruled that if they told the purchasers, "This flax belongs to Rav Kahana," Rav Kahana could take the money; otherwise he could not. Rif explains this according to Rav's own position that one may make a forward agreement in produce but not in money, while the practical halachah follows Rabbi Yannai that there is no difference between the produce and its value. The Gemara initially asks whether Rav's ruling follows the scholars of the West, who require notification. It then answers that Rav Kahana had not actually performed meshichah of the flax, so the arrangement was only a forward agreement. This distinction is exact according to what I have written. Had Rav Kahana already acquired the flax, he would have been owner of the produce. When the purchasers paid for that produce, their intent would naturally be to pay the owner, Rav Kahana, and according to our law no special notification would be needed. But once the conclusion is that Rav Kahana never acquired the flax and had only a forward claim, he was not the owner of the produce. Even according to our law, the purchasers' payment cannot become his merely because the flax owners intend that result. The purchasers must know for whom they are transferring the money. This is why Rif's citation remains necessary.
The conclusion follows from the distinction just explained. The requirement of seller notification is the position of the scholars of the West only where the intended recipient is already the owner of the purchase money. According to our law, in such a case no notification is needed. But where the intended recipient is not the owner of the property being transferred and did not supply the relevant money, notification remains necessary even according to our law. Thus, under the Gemara's initial assumption that Rav Kahana had performed meshichah and already owned the flax, there would be no need to inform the later purchasers that the proceeds were for him. Their payment would naturally go to the owner of the flax. Under the final conclusion, however, Rav Kahana never acquired the flax. His arrangement was merely a forward agreement. He was therefore not owner of the produce. Even though the flax owners intended the later sale proceeds for him, the purchasers had no reason to know they were transferring money to Rav Kahana. Since he was not the owner of the flax, seller or payer notification was required. This is fully consistent with Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia, Rosh, and Rabbeinu Yerucham as explained above.
