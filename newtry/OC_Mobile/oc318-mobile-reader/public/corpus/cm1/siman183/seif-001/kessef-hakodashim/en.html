Even if he agreed. A dispute arose between partners. One claimed that before the other traveled to buy merchandise, the latter promised that they would be equal partners; after returning from the purchase he said he would give him only a small share. Before the trip he had expressly said that he expected to buy for a sum substantially larger than what he ultimately paid. The purchasing partner claimed that he had only said generally that he would give the other some share. We ruled that an oath was required. According to Bach as well as Taz, Shach, and Sema in siman 183, where there are no witnesses that the buyer withdrew from the partnership before the purchase, the original partnership understanding creates a definite claim. The fact that they had not fixed the exact purchase price does not necessarily defeat it, because agreeing to participate even at a higher contemplated price may make the subject sufficiently defined. Ordinarily, an unspecified partnership means equal shares, as at the beginning of siman 176. Since the purchaser is in possession, however, he may plead that he relies upon the Mechaber's view in siman 176 rather than the authorities cited by Shach. The goods were divisible produce, and exact physical equality is not always required. The later dispute concerning whether a partner must make good losses from his own pocket also bears on whether profit and loss are necessarily proportional or whether profit is always shared equally. This requires study. A non-Jew offered to exchange the meat of a ram for a ram hide owned by a Jew. Another Jew said to the hide owner: 'I will reimburse you for half the value that you now say the hide is worth, and we will take the deal in partnership.' The hide owner remained silent. Later he stated that only if the non-Jew gave them a certain additional amount together with the meat would the meat be valued at a specified price. The proposed partner then bought grain and went home. While he was away, the hide owner completed the exchange with the non-Jew and brought the ram home; the absent man afterward seized the ram. I arranged a compromise with their consent. There were several grounds for uncertainty. Under Bach's view cited by Shach in siman 183, when there was no fully settled appointment, the purchaser may claim that he bought for himself, even if the other had funds available. Here, moreover, the non-Jew was willing to exchange only for the actual hide belonging to the buyer. Sema distinguishes a case where the buyer expressly says he purchased for himself, and here the buyer entirely denied agreeing to partnership and claimed he had answered that he did not want it. Thus at minimum he is effectively asserting an express purchase for himself. In addition, waiting until the other person left before completing the deal may be conduct revealing an intention to purchase independently. Sema in siman 269 distinguishes a bargain purchase, which resembles lifting a found object and creates stronger rights for the person who requested the purchase. Here it was uncertain whether the transaction truly was a bargain because the ram might not prove kosher and the commercial value depended on that risk. According to one version, the partnership proposal also contemplated a precise additional payment from the non-Jew. At completion the non-Jew in fact gave two gold coins more. If the proposed partnership was conditioned on the earlier price, then purchasing at the improved bargain might fall outside the agreement. Under Bach's approach, where even a less definite arrangement may fail to create partnership, there is strong reason not to extend partnership beyond the specific terms discussed. There is another difficulty. The principle that a purchase follows the owner of the money is important. Where the principal gives money to an agent, agency can operate normally. Where the buyer uses his own money in a transaction with a Jew, there can sometimes be a simultaneous loan and acquisition on behalf of the principal. But here the seller was a non-Jew and the consideration was the buyer's own hide. The hide remained the buyer's until transferred to the non-Jew, and the non-Jew conveyed the ram to the owner of that consideration. A prior understanding that the other Jew would later reimburse half the hide may not itself make him owner of half the consideration at the moment of sale. This requires study. It also requires study whether the rules of simanim 183 and 269 assume that the person requesting the purchase supplied information or access to merchandise that the buyer otherwise lacked. There is a strong moral and legal basis to hold a buyer to his word when he learns of an opportunity only because his fellow disclosed it in reliance on partnership. Here, however, the buyer was already negotiating with the non-Jew before the other person proposed partnership. His silence therefore does not clearly establish assent, especially if silence merely served to prevent competitive bidding that would raise the price. His immediate later conduct may confirm that he still intended to buy for himself. Even according to the authorities who hold that an unspecified statement 'buy for me' or 'buy for us in partnership' can bind the buyer when he then purchases without further statement, if he initially explained that he was willing to buy for the other only because he lacked money, and he later obtained his own funds and purchased silently, that changed circumstance may reveal that the original reason ended. Yet if he might still need the other's money afterward, the original arrangement is not necessarily cancelled merely because he advanced his own funds. The other party may therefore at least be entitled to an oath or ban, because the original discussion creates some evidentiary basis. If statements of intent can be inferred from conduct, an express private intention to buy only for oneself may overcome the earlier general arrangement, particularly where the surrounding circumstances do not clearly reveal continued agency. If the seller was present throughout the discussion and remained continuously with the buyer until the purchase, the earlier promise may be reflected in the visible conduct more strongly. Perhaps the same applies where witnesses heard the arrangement and remained continuously present until the silent purchase, even if the seller himself did not hear it. These issues require study. Although people sometimes change their minds, one does not ordinarily wish to present himself to his fellow as one who breaks his word. Thus an earlier clear arrangement supplies some presumption of continued intent, stronger than the later fact that he now denies it. A prior unrelated instance in which he retracted from another agreement does not establish a general character for retraction, because the Sages disapprove of one who does not keep his word and we do not presume a person acts improperly without proof. If there was a change in market price between the discussion and the purchase, however, the inference may weaken, because halachah itself distinguishes retraction after a material change in price from ordinary failure to keep one's word. Two partners wished to lease revenue rights in several villages and agreed with a third person that one village would be acquired jointly. If the partnership discussion occurred before the lease became legally complete, the rule of siman 183 concerning a known purchase may give the third person a share. If the landlord had already written all details and delivered a memorandum, perhaps even after payment and signatures, but local practice requires a complete formal deed and none had yet been written, the transaction may still be regarded as incomplete. Neither governmental law nor money alone necessarily completes it where custom requires the formal instrument. Whether a non-Jew's property is acquired by money may depend on full payment and on local legal custom. If the parties also deposited a penalty note against withdrawal, the note should be construed narrowly and may not necessarily extinguish the underlying partnership right. If beit din enforces continuation of the lease, perhaps the penalty never becomes payable. Yet the fact that the parties expressly limited the consequence of withdrawal to the penalty amount could weaken the inference of an irrevocable partnership. If one partner delivered a moda'ah, perhaps only his own half of the penalty is implicated unless the instrument expressly allows collection of the whole from either party. This requires study. Noda BiYehudah writes that a migo can be effective in the cases of simanim 183 and 269 where one said he would acquire for his fellow, because an express retraction before witnesses would itself be effective. By the same logic, even in the rule of siman 209 concerning one who labored and purchased relying on another's good faith, an express declaration before witnesses that he is buying for himself may be effective. I do not presently recall any authority in siman 103 who rejects such a declaration. The Talmudic formulations can readily be understood as cases where witnesses heard the undertaking and no valid withdrawal was made. An unspecified promise to buy for another may also include use of the buyer's own money where the principal has money immediately available and merchants would not object to the temporary substitution, because it is effectively as though the principal's money had been given. If the principal lacks available funds or there is real risk, delay, or expense before reimbursement, an unspecified promise should not automatically be read so broadly. If the principal has money but the seller will not accept that currency, while the buyer possesses acceptable currency, the buyer's contribution may be substantial. Still, where without the promise there was some risk that the purchase would be delayed, the location was unknown, a broker might interfere, or the price might rise, the general promise may reasonably include advancing equivalent funds, because one benefits and the other loses nothing. Similar reasoning may apply even where the merchandise is readily profitable and liquid. Finally, one bought animals from a non-Jew with money and situmta, and afterward told his fellow that he would admit him as a partner so that the fellow would not obstruct delivery by bidding up the price. He later retracted. If the purchase was already complete before the partnership discussion, the later promise does not prevent retraction even in cases where earlier discussions can create rights under siman 183. If the claimant says he does not know whether the money and situmta occurred before or after the discussion, the uncertainty may be treated like 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' depending on possession. Later authorities hold that meshichah is the primary acquisition from a non-Jew, while situmta can also acquire from a non-Jew according to Magen Avraham 448; Chok Yaakov questions this and was lenient there specifically regarding chametz. Perhaps if local non-Jewish law recognizes the situmta as a binding acquisition, all would agree. I did not know the local law and therefore arranged a compromise acceptable to both sides.
