He has against him only a grievance. This rule is also stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 177, seif 10: one who gives money to another to buy produce on a profit-sharing basis, and the recipient says that he did not buy, creates only a grievance. At first glance this is difficult from Bava Metzia 73. There, one gives money to another to buy wine, and the agent negligently fails to buy it. One opinion requires him to pay according to the market at the inexpensive port. Rav Ashi disagrees, and the Gemara asks why this differs from the rule, "If I leave the field fallow and do not work it, I will pay the best expected return." Shitah Mekubetzet explains that the question is: since the Sages imposed such liability in the field case even without an express written stipulation, why not impose the same here? The Gemara answers that in the field case the expected yield is in his control, while the favorable wine price is not fully in his control. Tosafot explain that the inexpensive port price is not a fully established predictable market. Sema in siman 207 likewise says that where purchase at a particular established market is reasonably assured, it can be considered within his control. It follows that if one sends a person to buy wheat at a stable ongoing market, the agent fails to buy, and it is later clear that all other merchants who purchased there earned profit, there should be liability for lost profit under the same principle as "If I leave it fallow." I therefore understand the Yoreh Deah case differently. There the recipient accepted money for a profit-sharing venture for a period. A worker may retract, and the same is true of an iska recipient in the relevant sense. Since he can withdraw from acting as worker, he does not owe the anticipated profit merely because he did not enter the transaction. Here too the agent is unpaid and can withdraw from the agency, as explained in siman 333, seif 5, in the gloss. Therefore the principal has only a grievance. But a paid agent hired as a contractor specifically to buy at an established market, who failed to buy, can be liable for the preventable loss of profit measured according to what ordinary merchants earned there, under the rule of "If I leave it fallow." I have similarly written in simanim 176 and 306 regarding a partner or worker who causes a preventable loss of profit.
