Bava Kamma discusses one who gives money to an agent to buy wheat and the agent instead buys barley, or vice versa. One baraita says losses and gains belong to the agent, while another says losses belong to the agent and gains are shared. Rabbi Yochanan initially explains the contradiction through Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah on whether a deviation acquires. Rabbi Elazar instead distinguishes between goods intended for eating and goods intended for trade. The Jerusalem scholars challenged Rabbi Yochanan according to Rabbi Yehudah: "Who informed the wheat seller that he should transfer the wheat to the owner of the money?" Rav Shmuel bar Sasarti asks that if so, even where wheat was ordered and wheat was bought, the principal should not acquire. Rabbi Abahu answers that where the agent followed instructions he acts as the principal's agent and is like the principal's hand. The Gemara compares this to clothing dyed for one's wife and children, which is not included in an owner's later consecration because the worker acted for them. Tosafot explains that if, where the principal fails to acquire, the agent himself does acquire, Rabbi Elazar could still have established both baraitot according to Rabbi Yehudah: in the eating case the deviation would prevent agency and the agent would acquire for himself, while in the trade case there would be no meaningful deviation. Tosafot therefore entertains a second reading. Perhaps when the principal does not acquire, the agent also does not acquire because he never intended to buy for himself. The transaction would then be a mistaken sale and return to the seller. This raises difficulty under the Shulchan Aruch's rule that if an agent deviates without informing the seller he is an agent, the agent must acquire for himself. If so, why should Tosafot say the transaction returns as mistaken? Even if agency failed, the undisclosed agent should simply be the buyer. The same question arises from Tosafot's discussion of the dyer. If neither the wife nor the husband acquired the dyed goods because the worker was not properly informed, Tosafot says the transaction should be reversed and the dyer should return the money and demand his wage from the wife. Yet where the intermediary never disclosed agency, ordinary law can treat him as buyer rather than create a mistaken sale. A further difficulty concerns the money in a mistaken sale. Nimukei Yosef says that money paid in a void sale becomes an ordinary loan debt, and Rama rules similarly at the end of siman 232. If so, even if the sale is undone, the original owner cannot consecrate the money because an oral loan outside his possession cannot be consecrated. Tosafot's proof from consecration would fail. It appears Tosafot follows their own view at the beginning of Perek HaMokher Peirot that money in a mistaken sale remains a deposit rather than a loan. A true deposit can in principle be consecrated. Even so, I wrote in siman 354 that a deposit whose custodian will not return it without litigation can be treated as property outside the owner's possession. A mistaken sale, though technically a deposit according to Tosafot, may still not be fully in the owner's control. This leaves the precise consecration proof requiring further study.
