The Mishnah in Perek Almanah Nizonet teaches: a widow whose ketubah is two hundred who sold property worth one hundred for two hundred, or property worth two hundred for one hundred, is treated as having received her ketubah. The Gemara asks: in the case where she sold property worth two hundred for one hundred, we tell her, "You caused the loss." But where she sold property worth one hundred for two hundred, why can she not say, "I generated the profit"? Rav Nachman answers: here Rabbi taught that the entire gain belongs to the owner of the money. A baraita records a dispute where a seller gives an extra unit to an agent. Rabbi Yehudah says the extra belongs entirely to the agent; Rabbi Yosei says they divide. Another baraita says Rabbi Yosei gives the whole to the owner of the money. Rami bar Chama resolves the contradiction: where the item has a fixed price, they divide; where it has no fixed price, the extra belongs entirely to the owner of the money. Rav Pappa rules accordingly. Rashi explains that land has no fixed price, since one parcel can sell cheaply and another expensively. Therefore the widow's excess belongs to the estate. Rabbeinu Tam was asked about Reuven who sent Shimon to collect money from a gentile and the gentile made an error in the calculation. He initially answered that they should divide, because money is an item with a fixed value. He later retracted and ruled that any gain produced by a mistake, whether through a transaction or a counting error, belongs entirely to the owner of the money, because the mistake arose through the principal's money. This is difficult. If so, why does the Gemara ask that the widow should say, "I generated the profit"? Selling property worth one hundred for two hundred appears to be a mistake, as the Jerusalem Talmud cited by Tosafot indicates when it asks that the sale should be reversed. If every mistake belongs entirely to the principal even with a fixed price, the Gemara's question is difficult. Tosafot addresses the Jerusalem Talmud by explaining that the case can be one in which the property later rose or fell in value. They reject saying the estate retained ownership of the land, because then, in the case of selling property worth two hundred for one hundred, the land should simply return to the estate. Rather, they conclude that in both directions the widow herself acquired the property before passing it to the purchaser. Tosafot elsewhere explains why the widow is nevertheless treated as having received her ketubah when property worth one hundred was sold for two hundred. Although the transaction began as a mistaken valuation and one might think she could undo it against the purchaser and return the land to the heirs, when she undertook to remove the land from the heirs' estate and place it with the purchaser, she is treated as though she first acquired the land herself at that moment. This requires explanation. If she first acquired the property, then she appears to be the owner of the money or property, and the profit should belong to her. Maharsha answers that the acquisition works asymmetrically. For the widow's disadvantage, such as when she sells property worth two hundred for one hundred, she is treated as having acquired it and cannot return the land to the heirs. For her advantage, however, when property worth one hundred sells for two hundred, she remains only an agent and the profit belongs to the estate. This is difficult because Tosafot explicitly says that in both cases she acquired the property, implying acquisition for both benefit and detriment. Beit Shmuel also noticed this difficulty. He explains that although the widow could in theory seek to undo the transaction against the purchaser because the original valuation was mistaken, her original intent when removing the land from the heirs was to take it toward her ketubah. She therefore cannot later withdraw that acquisition against the heirs. If the property later increased, the heirs may still be able to challenge a sale made without beit din because the property remained subject to their rights, but if it decreased, the loss remains with the widow. The conceptual point is that the widow's intermediate acquisition explains why she cannot undo the transaction to escape a loss. It does not necessarily make her the ultimate beneficiary of every excess produced by the principal's property. The law of added value still follows the distinction between items with a fixed price and those without one, and land, having no fixed price, leaves the gain with the owner of the original property.
It appears from Tosafot that the Mishnah concerns a widow who sold with valid authority. If she had sold without the authorization required by the relevant view, then even where she sold property worth two hundred for one hundred the sale would not stand, because she never acquired the property for herself at all. The Mishnah must therefore concern a sale made with the authority required by the halachah. Once she intended to remove the property from the heirs' domain and place it into the purchaser's domain, the transfer is effective in either direction. If she sold property worth two hundred for one hundred, she cannot undo the result by saying the heirs should take the loss, because she herself caused the loss. Why, then, when property worth one hundred is sold for two hundred, does the excess belong to the heirs rather than to the widow? The answer follows Rabbi Yosei's rule concerning an item with no fixed price. Land has no fixed price. One parcel may sell cheaply and another dearly. The additional hundred is therefore not an independent gift to the agent. It is part of the price of the land itself. Since the land belonged to the heirs, the increased sale price belongs to them, just as an increase in the land's value before the sale would belong to them. This also explains the Gemara's question, "Let her say, I generated the profit." At that stage, before distinguishing between an item with a fixed price and one without a fixed price, the widow could argue that she had acquired the property for purposes of the sale and that the excess was an independent profit resulting from her activity. The Gemara answers that Rabbi taught that the entire gain belongs to the owner of the money, and ultimately distinguishes between fixed and unfixed prices. In an unfixed item there is no independent excess at all. The entire amount is the sale price of the property. Rashi's phrase that Rabbi taught, "The entire gain belongs to the owner of the money," is therefore expressed through the general case of an agent sent to market. In the widow's case she herself effectively stood in the position of the money holder for purposes of executing the sale. The reason the heirs nevertheless receive the excess is that, in an unfixed price transaction, the entire amount is attributed to the property itself. Accordingly, the increased amount belongs to the heirs.
