Tosafot, however, can be read as saying that Rabbi Yochanan agrees in substantive law that seller notification is necessary where the seller does not know for whom the purchase is made, and that his disagreement concerns only how the baraita is interpreted. Tosafot explains that the baraita could be established where the agent informed the seller that he was buying for the owner of the money. The Gemara's question, "Who informed the wheat seller that he should transfer the wheat to the owner of the money?" means that the baraita appears to speak generally, even where the seller was not informed. Rav and Shmuel therefore ask that, if so, even where the agent bought wheat instead of wheat, the same problem should exist. The answer is that when he bought exactly what he was instructed to buy, he carried out the agency. According to this reading, Rabbi Yochanan may not disagree with the scholars of the West about the underlying need for notification. He may simply interpret the baraita as a case where the seller was informed. There is nevertheless a difficulty. If the agent told the seller that he was buying as an agent for the principal, but then deviated from the principal's instruction, the sale should be void altogether, as explained in siman 185. Perhaps the case is one where there is no proof that the principal expressly instructed him to buy wheat rather than barley, so the seller can say that no deviation was established. Yet according to Sema in siman 185, where the principal claims he expressly limited the agent to one item, the sale can be void. The matter therefore remains difficult.
The decisors nevertheless make clear that we do not rule like the scholars of the West, but like Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that seller notification is not required when the principal supplied the money. Rosh explains why. Even if the agent deviated, he still does not intend to acquire for himself. His intent remains to acquire for the owner of the money. Although the seller transfers the wheat to the agent, the agent's hand is like the hand of the principal because the agent intends to acquire for the principal. We do not require the seller to know that the acquisition is ultimately for the owner of the money. Rosh distinguishes the case of dyed clothing for a wife. There the husband supplied the money, but the wife was not the owner of the money. The dyer transfers to the husband. Even if the husband wants the clothing to belong to his wife, one cannot transfer one's own property to another merely by intending that it belong to that other person unless an effective acquisition occurs through a third party. Rabbi Abba therefore needed to say that one who consecrates his property is treated as though he had previously transferred his wife's and children's clothing to them. From this it follows that even under our law, which rejects the scholars of the West, the no notification rule applies specifically where the principal supplied the money. Where there was no principal's money, seller notification is required. This is precisely Rabbeinu Yerucham's rule: if one buys with his own funds intending to benefit another, the other does not acquire unless the seller was informed. This also clarifies the structure of the Gemara. The scholars of the West require notification even where the principal supplied the money. Rav Shmuel bar Sasarti objects that, if so, even wheat bought instead of wheat should fail. Rabbi Abbahu answers that where the agent bought exactly what he was sent to buy, "he carried out the agency." The proof is from the law of one who consecrates all his property, where the wife's garments are nevertheless excluded. According to our approach, that proof is compelling. In the dyed garment case there is no principal's money belonging to the wife, and therefore everyone agrees notification would ordinarily be required. The only reason the wife can acquire is because the husband is treated as having transferred the garments to her previously. Accordingly, the expression "he carried out the agency" explains why wheat bought as instructed belongs to the principal, while Rabbi Abba's separate doctrine is still needed for the wife's garments. Baal HaTerumot cites Ramban as proving from the first chapter of Bava Kamma that, under the initial assumption of the Gemara, the wife would have acquired directly from the seller, and under Rabbi Abba's conclusion the husband first acquires from the seller and then the wife acquires from him. Ramban's language at first seems reversed. In truth it is correct according to the distinction just explained. Under our final law, where the principal's money is present, seller notification is unnecessary. But where there is no principal's money, as with the wife, notification is required unless one applies Rabbi Abba's doctrine that the husband is deemed to have transferred the garments to her. The practical difference between saying the wife acquires from the seller or from the husband can matter where the husband had subjected future acquired property to a creditor, as discussed in siman 97. Thus the principal rule is: where the principal's money is used, the seller need not be notified, because the agent acquires for the owner of the money. Where the buyer uses his own money as a gift to acquire for another, seller notification is required.
