Some say he acquired it for himself. The two opinions depend on the status of the principal's money after the agent misappropriates it. According to the opinion that the agent acquires for himself, once he became a robber of the money and is liable for ones, the money stands in his legal domain. The seller intends to transfer to the owner of the money. Since the agent has made himself the legal owner for liability purposes, the seller's intent points to him. The other opinion holds that although the agent became a robber and acquired sufficient legal responsibility for ones, the actual money itself does not become his. It remains the principal's money for purposes such as kiddushin or acquiring slaves and land. The principal therefore remains the true owner of the funds, and the seller, who intends to transfer to the owner of the money, transfers to the principal. Even if the agent tells the seller he is buying for himself, the principal can still acquire according to this view, as implied by Sema, seif katan 12. Merely converting the money into a loan obligation does not help; only actually designating other money as replacement can shift ownership for purchase purposes. Where the agent materially changes the object of the purchase, such as being sent to buy wheat and buying barley, Sema and Shach hold that the agent can acquire for himself, even though we rule like R. Yehudah that ordinary change does not acquire stolen property. It appears that when one intentionally changes in order to acquire for himself, even R. Yehudah concedes that this type of change effects acquisition. Shitah Mekubetzet and Rashba support this. Accordingly, Sema's statement in siman 176, seif katan 37, that deliberate change can allow a partner to acquire for himself concerns partners joined for a specific business, such as a partnership formed to buy wheat, where one instead buys barley. In a partnership covering all of their business affairs, ordinary change of merchandise would not create that effect. A further difficulty remains. If the agent did not change the commodity, why does the principal acquire? The agent has revoked his agency and intends the meshichah for himself. Even if the seller intends to transfer to the owner of the money, namely the principal, the principal himself did not perform meshichah. At most there was a money acquisition, and money ordinarily does not acquire movable property. The answer is that the rabbinic cancellation of money acquisition was enacted lest the seller say, "Your wheat burned in my attic," so that the seller will remain motivated to rescue the merchandise. Here, however, the agent has already drawn the wheat into his own premises. Even though he did not intend that meshichah for the principal, the concern that the seller will fail to rescue the goods no longer applies, because they are no longer in the seller's possession. In circumstances where that concern does not exist, money can acquire, as explained in siman 198. This rule also proves that although one holding entrusted money may sometimes use unsealed funds, an agent entrusted to make payment may not use the principal's money unless he actually designates substitute funds. Sema says so in seif katan 12, and Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 169, likewise indicates that even an agent sent to repay a debt may not use the money. See Shach, seif katan 4, who says Mechaber also holds that where there are witnesses the purchase belongs to the agent. He overlooked Mechaber's explicit ruling in Yoreh Deah, siman 177, that where there are witnesses the purchase belongs to the agent.
