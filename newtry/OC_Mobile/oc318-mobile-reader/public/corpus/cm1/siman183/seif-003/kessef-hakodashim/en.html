And he gave him only one hundred. Partners joined for a single commercial transaction. When one partner paid a non-Jew, the non-Jew made an error in counting the money. See further in siman 183. If one partner paid from his own funds as part of the purchase and the non-Jew later returned money to him after recalculating the account, it appears that the other partner has no share in that returned money. Their partnership concerned the commercial venture itself, not this incidental refund. The partner whose own money was paid and then returned acquires it for himself. This nevertheless requires study. If, however, the partners had first mixed their funds, one can argue that the partnership itself generated the advantage they received. Even if Reuven gave his money to Shimon, who actually handled the payment, we should not say that Reuven never acquired a share in the coins remaining in Shimon's possession merely because coins are not acquired through barter. Just as mixing funds is effective for buying merchandise jointly, and we do not ask 'who informed the wheat seller' that he should convey to both partners because the partners mutually commit themselves to one another, so too the mixed money itself may immediately become partnership property. This requires careful study.
