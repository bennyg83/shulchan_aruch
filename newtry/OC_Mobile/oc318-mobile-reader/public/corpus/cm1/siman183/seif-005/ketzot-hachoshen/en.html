The loss is borne by the agent. The Shach writes that this refers specifically to loss through market fluctuation. If an unavoidable accident occurred and the loss was unrelated to the agent's deviation, such as theft or loss, and the agent receives no compensation from the principal, the agent is exempt, citing a responsum of Mabit. What the Shach calls obvious is surprising. Once the agent deviated from his instructions, he is no longer acting as the principal's agent. The money in his hands should therefore have the status of a loan, making him liable even for unavoidable accidents. Rabbeinu Yerucham cites the Geonim that one who was entrusted with merchandise to bring it to a particular place and instead took it elsewhere becomes responsible for the merchandise, and the property becomes like a loan in his hands so that beit din may descend to his assets for payment. If it becomes a loan, he is plainly liable for unavoidable accidents. Nimukei Yosef likewise says that one may lend money on condition that the borrower invest it only against specified security. If the borrower deviates and lends it through other people, losses are his while gains are divided. Although the arrangement then becomes one in which the principal is close to profit and remote from loss because all responsibility rests on the borrower, it is not prohibited interest. The phrase "all responsibility rests on him" indicates liability even for unavoidable accidents. Mishneh LaMelech cites the responsum of Mabit but notes that Beit Yosef, in a manuscript responsum, disagreed and brought proofs from many authorities that an agent who deviates is liable even for unavoidable accidents. That is the correct practical law.
