The loss is borne by the agent. See Shach, seif katan 9, citing Mabit that if an ones occurred unrelated to the deviation, or if the goods were stolen or lost and the agent receives no compensation, the agent is exempt. I am surprised Shach cites this only in the name of Mabit, for it is explicit in Rashba and in Shitah Mekubetzet on Bava Kamma 102. At first glance the rule itself is difficult. Once the agent violated the principal's instruction and bought a different item, he should no longer be the principal's agent. The money should become a loan in the agent's hands, making him liable even for ones. Ketzot HaChoshen raises this. The intent appears to be as follows. Suppose the agent returns to the principal after buying barley instead of wheat and says, "Either accept the barley for both profit and loss, or take back your money and the barley will be mine." He cannot force that choice. Because he bought the barley for the principal, the purchase vested in the principal, while the agent bears responsibility for correcting the deviation, as in siman 182, seif 6. The purchase therefore belongs to the principal for profit, while the agent bears the loss until it is sold. Rashba and Shitah Mekubetzet say that the rule "if profit arose they share, if loss arose the agent bears it" is a penalty applied to one who deviates, like the general rule that one who changes from instructions has the lower hand. If the rule referred only to the moment the agent returns to the principal, no special penalty would be needed. If the principal is pleased with the altered purchase, it is plainly his; if he rejects it, the loss is plainly the agent's. The penalty must therefore be that the agent can be compelled to leave the goods in the transaction until the expected time of sale, with profit shared while ordinary business loss falls on him. Because the principal nevertheless accepts the transaction for purposes of profit, liability for external ones, theft, or loss passes to the principal. But if the goods were destroyed by ones before the agent returned to the principal, or the principal never accepted the altered purchase at all, responsibility remains with the agent because the principal's money had effectively become a loan in his hands once he deviated.
And if there was profit. See Shach, seif katan 10, who cites many poskim that even in an agency case the parties divide the profit. In my view the halachah should not follow that position. Once the agent failed to buy the wheat, the money remained a deposit in his hands. If he then bought barley specifically for the principal, the entire profit should belong to the principal. Siman 292, seif 7, in the gloss, states that where one invests deposited money on behalf of the depositor, all profit belongs to the depositor even though the custodian's initiative caused the gain. Possibly the authorities cited by Shach deal with a case where the agent deliberately changed the purchase and intended from the outset to acquire on a basis that profit would be divided. Even according to Shach, it appears that once the agent wants to divide profit he must at least return half the principal money immediately.
