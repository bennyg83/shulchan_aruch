The gentile erred and gave him more. The Tur cites Rabbeinu Yonah that in a commercial mistake one distinguishes between an error in the transaction itself and an error merely in arithmetic. If the seller mistakenly thought the goods were worth no more than the money he received, the law follows the rules governing the sale price itself. Where the item has no fixed price, the entire gain belongs to the owner of the money; where it has a fixed price, the parties divide. This requires explanation. According to Rashi, division in a fixed price item rests on the possibility that the seller intended the excess as a gift to the agent. In a mistake there was no gift. According to Rif, division rests on the benefit reaching the agent through the principal, which likewise seems inapplicable when the seller simply erred about the transaction and intended the excess as part of the sale. It therefore appears that Rabbeinu Yonah follows the explanation cited in Hagahot Ashri: where there is a fixed price, the unexpected gain is attributed to the good fortune of both principal and agent, and they divide; where there is no fixed price, the favorable price is part of the transaction itself and follows the owner of the merchandise or money. On this basis, even a mistaken favorable transaction involving a fixed price item is divided because the gain is attributed to both parties' fortune. According to Rashi and Rif, by contrast, where the excess was produced by a true transactional mistake and there was no gift, the entire gain should belong to the owner of the money. This likely explains why the Shulchan Aruch omitted Rabbeinu Yonah's special ruling. The Tur's statement that Rosh agreed should be understood as referring to the separate rule that a mere arithmetic mistake belongs to the agent, not necessarily to the fixed price transactional error itself.
