And he misled the non-Jew in the measure. One leased a village revenue concession and appointed a bartender there, supplying him with liquor. The bartender diluted the liquor with water without the lessee's knowledge and also profited by using undersized measures. Where the vessels are not legally recognized measures, such as ordinary glasses or bottles, there may be no prohibition of false measures as such, though the bartender may also have used an improper vessel bearing the name of an official measure but containing less than the required amount. The question is whether the resulting profit belongs to the bartender or to the lessee. The answer may depend upon the sequence of payment and delivery. If the bartender first poured the liquor and only afterward received payment, then once the customer drank the diluted mixture the lessee may already have acquired the amount customarily due for that serving. Likewise, any liquor saved by the short measure would seemingly remain the lessee's property, comparable to the rule at the end of siman 183. If, however, the bartender first took the customer's money and only afterward became obligated to provide liquor, one could argue that the bartender acquired the excess created by the dilution or short measure. If they were partners, the issue might relate to Shach in siman 176, who writes that found property or theft does not automatically belong to the partnership, and to the discussion in Urim VeTumim. In the case of an agent, however, the first analysis seems more persuasive. The matter requires study, and we arranged a compromise.
