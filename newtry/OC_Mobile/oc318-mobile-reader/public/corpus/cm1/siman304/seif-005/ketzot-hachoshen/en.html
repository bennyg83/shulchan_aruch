The porter who broke the barrel. Rava rules that porters who broke a wine barrel must sometimes compensate according to the higher market-day price. Rashi and Tosafot understand that even if the breakage occurred on an ordinary day, if they return value only after the next market day they may have to pay five, because had the barrel remained intact the shopkeeper could have sold it on market day for five. At first this is difficult. Robbers pay according to the value at the time of theft, and custodians according to the time of negligence. Why should a future known market price increase the damages? The answer is that a known future market price is already a present cause of monetary value. Where it is certain that the barrel will be worth five on the upcoming market day, destroying it now destroys an asset that predictably yields five. Even though we do not generally follow Rabbi Shimon's rule that a cause of money is itself money for every purpose, direct consequential damage can obligate for the certain future loss. Therefore they must restore either a barrel capable of yielding five on market day or its corresponding value. Ramban likewise explains that if an animal without teeth had to be sold before market day, the liable party pays only the present lower price because the owner could not wait. Had the animal been capable of being held until market day, the higher known market price would control. Rambam, however, appears to measure by the value at the time of return. Maggid Mishneh explains that since the porters can restore a barrel of wine, if they instead pay money they pay the value of the barrel at the time return is due. This remains difficult because ordinary custodial damages are usually fixed at the time of negligence, not suit. The matter requires study.
