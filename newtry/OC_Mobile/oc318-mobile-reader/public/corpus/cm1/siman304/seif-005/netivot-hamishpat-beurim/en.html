A porter who broke barrels of wine. Ketzot HaChoshen seeks to explain why the porter sometimes pays according to the higher market-day value, although robbers ordinarily pay according to the value at the time of robbery and custodians according to the time of negligence. He suggests that where the future rise is known, the present object already has a value based on that future increase, like chametz of hekdesh whose post-Pesach value can matter through "causing monetary value." In my view this is incorrect. If land is cheap in Tishrei and expensive in Nisan, one who damages it in Tishrei does not pay the Nisan price merely because the future increase was predictable. Likewise one who damages grain before harvest pays its present market value, not the future seasonal price. Chametz is different because during Pesach it cannot be sold at all. Its practical commercial value exists only for after Pesach. That resembles property in a wilderness that cannot be marketed until brought to a town. In ordinary seasonal markets, however, people do sell before the peak day, especially when they need money. The object therefore has a real present market value and ordinary damages are assessed accordingly. The reason the porter case is different is that it is specifically a hired porter who broke the wine through negligence. Siman 176 rules that a partner who negligently sells before the proper time can be liable for the later lost appreciation. I explained there that this resembles a worker who, after being relied upon to perform an act within his control, fails and thereby causes a nonrecoverable lost profit. Bava Metzia 74, in Shitah Mekubetzet citing Raavad, says that if one gives wine to another to transport for the market day and he fails to bring it, he must pay the loss of that market day. All the more so, if the porter negligently breaks the wine while transporting it specifically to that market, he must pay the profit the owner would have earned. Thus the case is where the porter was hired to carry the barrels to a market at which they would sell for four, while in the local place they were worth only three, and the porter broke them en route. The owner can no longer obtain replacement wine in time for that market, perhaps because he lacks funds to purchase replacement stock. If the porter pays before the first market day, he may discharge by paying three, because at that moment the owner can still replace the barrels at three and suffer no loss. But once the market day passes, the porter was already liable for the four the owner would have received. He cannot reduce that established obligation merely because the market later returns to three. Delaying payment cannot benefit the wrongdoer. This is the proper explanation of Rambam's distinction.
