A porter who broke a barrel of wine, etc. The Rambam, and the Mechaber following him, do not distinguish whether the barrel was broken on market day or an ordinary day, or whether the owner had other wine available for sale when it broke. The Maggid Mishneh explains the Rambam's reasoning. The porters' primary liability is to return a barrel of wine. Therefore, when they instead pay money, valuation follows the day of payment. If they pay on market day, they pay the market day value, because had they returned a barrel then and the owner had no other wine, he could sell it for that price. If they pay on an ordinary day, they pay the ordinary day value, because a barrel returned then would itself satisfy the obligation. Although robbers generally pay according to the value at the time of taking, these porters are not robbers. They are liable only for negligent damage, whose proper repair is replacement of the barrel or its equivalent. The Rosh rejects this and values according to the time of breakage. Rama's gloss therefore distinguishes. If they broke it on market day when it was worth four and come to pay on an ordinary day, they must pay four in cash and cannot merely return a barrel and tell the owner to wait until the next market day. Certainly this is so if the owner had no other wine that day, because he can say, "I needed cash, and had you not broken it I would have sold it then." Even if other wine remained with him that market day, once this barrel bore the value of four when broken they owe four. But if they return an actual barrel of wine, they can be discharged because it can be sold for four on a future market day. Likewise, if they pay on a market day, even after several prior market days passed, and the owner presently has no other wine to sell, paying the current four is sufficient.

And the diminution caused by the hole. The Rambam likewise writes this in chapter 3 of Hilchot Sechirut. The Gemara says that the porter deducts the labor cost and the amount connected with making the opening in the barrel. Rashi explains that one must hire a craftsman to bore a hole in an earthenware barrel, and the Tur follows this. Some interpret the Rambam and Mechaber the same way and say the "diminution caused by the hole" means the expense of making it. That is difficult. Had they meant this, they should have said so directly. More likely the Rambam explains differently. The negligent porter who pays for the wine and barrel deducts from the value of the wine the labor the owner would have expended selling it, and from the value of the barrel he deducts the loss in value caused by the hole. Had the barrel not been broken, the owner could not have sold the wine without opening it, and a barrel with a hole is worth less than an intact barrel, because an intact barrel is suitable for more uses.

But if he has other wine, etc. In the Perishah I wrote that this appears to mean wine he acquired after the time of the breakage. Then, because the broken barrel was worth four at the time of breakage and now at the time of payment he has other wine so that an additional barrel is not immediately saleable at the higher price, the porter must nevertheless pay the four that were due. But if the wine currently in his possession is the same wine that remained unsold from the day of the breakage, the porter can discharge himself by returning a barrel of wine, as explained above. An old Venetian printing of Shulchan Aruch adds here another rule: when the term of a paid custodian's responsibility ends, his paid custody ends even if the item is still in his house, and he thereafter has only the law of an unpaid custodian. The Krakow printing omitted it because the Mechaber states the law at the end of siman 343, which is its primary source from the law of a borrower.
