(And he is not discharged, etc.) The Sema, seif katan 7, writes concerning wine that remained with him from market day, etc. His words are extremely difficult. Since on ordinary days a barrel of wine is worth only three, how can giving three not discharge him while giving the barrel itself does? It is better for the injured party to receive the barrel's current value. Throughout this law, whenever the Gemara says he may discharge himself by giving the barrel, it means he need not seek cash elsewhere. We never find the reverse. It therefore appears obvious that since giving three is insufficient, as the law expressly states because he owes four, the value at the time it was broken, all the more so giving the barrel itself when it is now worth only three is insufficient. I have clear proof from the Rosh. He cites another version: if he had wine to sell, he should have sold it. The explanation is that the rule requiring four on an ordinary day, based on the market-day value when he broke the barrel, applies only where the owner had no other wine to sell. If he did have wine to sell that day, he receives only three, because the tortfeasor can say, "You should have sold the wine you had. Since you did not sell all of it, I did not cause you a loss of four." The Rosh rejects this, reasoning that the barrel was nevertheless worth four on market day, and if the owner did not manage to sell all his wine that day, he can sell it on the next market day. According to the Sema, who says current money value is insufficient but return of the barrel itself is sufficient, why did the Rosh reject the alternative reading? He could have sustained it by saying that on ordinary days he must give four if the owner had no wine to sell on the market day of the damage, but if the owner had wine and failed to sell it, the tortfeasor need only return a barrel of wine itself. Necessarily, once payment of three is insufficient, return of a barrel worth only three is also insufficient. The Sema further distinguishes, in seif katan 8, whether the injured party acquired other wine before or after the damage. There is no basis for this distinction. The Gemara itself distinguishes simply between whether he has wine or not. It does not distinguish within the case where he has wine between wine acquired before or after the damage. The Rosh's rejection of the alternative version likewise proves against the Sema. The clear rule is that if, on the day payment is made, the injured party already has wine, that day is treated for him like an ordinary day, and the tortfeasor cannot discharge himself with another barrel worth only three. Even if the injured party had wine on the day of the damage and did not sell it, that wine was then worth four to everyone else, and liability is measured at the time of the damage according to market value. What matters for return is that after the tortfeasor already owes four, he must return something worth four to the injured party. Wine held at the time of the damage therefore makes no difference.
