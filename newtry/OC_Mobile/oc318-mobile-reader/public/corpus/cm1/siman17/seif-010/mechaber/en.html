A judge may not show compassion in judgment toward a poor person by saying, "This man is poor and his opponent is wealthy and obligated to support him, so I will rule in the poor man's favor and thereby allow him to be supported honorably." Nor may he favor a prominent person. If a wealthy and great scholar and a poor ignoramus appear before him, he may not honor the former or inquire after his welfare in a manner that causes the other litigant's arguments to become inhibited. He may not say, "How can I rule against him and embarrass him? I will instead acquit him and afterwards tell him privately to give the other person what is his." Rather, he must immediately decide the case according to the truth. If one litigant is upright and the other wicked, he may not say, "This man is wicked and presumed to lie, while the other is presumed not to alter his word, so I will incline the judgment against the wicked man." Rather, while they stand before him both litigants should always be regarded in his eyes as potentially wicked, with each presumed capable of making a false claim, and he should decide according to what appears from the evidence. When they depart after accepting the judgment, both should be regarded as upright, and he should judge each favorably.
