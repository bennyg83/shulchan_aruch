One should not allow one litigant to speak all he wishes, etc. This implies that even if the judge did not tell the first, 'Say everything you wish,' but merely told the second, 'Be brief,' the unequal treatment is prohibited. This is the plain meaning of the baraita.
And the other is dressed in shabby clothing. Rambam and the Tur use this wording. The concern is specifically where one party's clothing is degrading. If both are respectable, though one is more expensive than the other, there is no requirement of exact equality. Maharshal comments that in practice we are unable to uphold this standard fully.
One should not sit above, etc. This refers to the earlier rule that if beit din chooses to seat both litigants, one should not be seated higher. If both remain standing, there is no concern of one being higher because beit din did not create the difference.
At the final ruling both should stand. This is learned from 'the two men shall stand,' which refers to witnesses, together with 'who have the dispute,' which refers to litigants. Just as the witnesses' testimony is their decisive judicial act, so the verse's concern for the litigants' standing applies specifically to the final ruling.
Standing is required initially, etc. Only initially. After the fact, if testimony or litigation occurred while seated, the ruling stands. The Tur says this explicitly, and the Rosh and Ran write likewise in the name of Rashba. The verse is an asmachta establishing proper procedure, not an absolute condition. Ir Shushan's contrary claim is incorrect. Rabbi Eliezer bar Shimshon is a lone view permitting sitting nowadays because the verse says 'before God,' referring to ordained judges; even he does not say that testimony while seated in the era of semichah was invalid after the fact.
Standing while leaning, etc. Below, at the end of siman 28, the Mechaber writes that judges receiving testimony must sit, and leaning in this manner counts as sitting. Both rulings come from Responsa Rivash, siman 267. We therefore apply the characterization leniently in each context. Yet if the judges and witnesses in the same case all lean simultaneously, this should not be done, because necessarily one of the groups would then fail its proper posture. This resembles the rules concerning acquisition with a donkey and camel or with the purchaser's and seller's utensils, where contradictory legal characterizations cannot both be used at the same time.
