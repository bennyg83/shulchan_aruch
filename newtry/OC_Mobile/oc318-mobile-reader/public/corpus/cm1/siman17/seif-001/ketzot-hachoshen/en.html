[“From wearing clothes and writing a morale, we also don’t have to put the laws of religion on the slumber,” but the new house wrote and discussed it when it was said to me, ‘Do not look and write honest,’ and we do it.”
Standing, initially. The Ir Shushan writes that in the period of ordained judges, sitting was invalid even after the fact, while today it is valid after the fact. He bases this on Mordechai in the name of Raavan. The Sema objects that Mordechai in fact permits sitting even initially today, because there are no ordained judges, and never says that in the period of ordination sitting invalidated the proceeding after the fact. It appears to me that Ir Shushan is correct. Even Raavan must agree that today the proper initial procedure is standing. There is decisive proof from the case of Rav Huna's wife, who had litigation before Rav Nachman. Rav Nachman wondered how to rise before her, as due to the wife of a Torah scholar, without appearing to favor one litigant. He instructed his attendant to release a goose so that he would have an independent reason to stand. Rav Nachman was not ordained, yet he still required the litigants to stand before him. The standing of witnesses and litigants is learned from the same verse, "the two men shall stand before God," which the Gemara interprets as the witnesses and the parties to the dispute. Since litigants must stand initially even today, the same is true of witnesses. Raavan therefore intended only to validate sitting after the fact in our era. It follows that in the period of ordained judges, sitting would indeed invalidate even after the fact.
