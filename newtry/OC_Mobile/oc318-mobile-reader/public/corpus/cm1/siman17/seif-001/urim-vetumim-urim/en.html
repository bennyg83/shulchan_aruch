Shorten your words. It is forbidden to tell one litigant to shorten his argument even if the judge did not tell the other to say everything he wishes. Sema. Conversely, if the judge says nothing to one party but tells the other, "Explain and clarify all your words," that is also forbidden. Bach. It is permitted to tell both parties to speak concisely so as not to burden beit din needlessly. Knesset HaGedolah.
And he speaks gently to him. Here too, it is forbidden to speak gently to one party while speaking to the other in a neutral manner, and certainly if he speaks harshly to the other. The converse is likewise forbidden. Knesset HaGedolah.
And the other is dressed in soiled garments, etc. Maharshal writes that today we lack the power to enforce this rule fully. Sema. Even so, it is proper to reassure the poorer litigant, "Do not fear, beit din will not show favor to anyone." Bach in the name of Maharshal. Shach, however, cites Ravyah that the extreme case is uncommon today because wealthy litigants generally do not come to beit din wearing garments of extraordinary value, such as a robe worth one hundred maneh. Therefore the issue rarely arises and no special statement is customarily made.
Rather, both stand. If one litigant sat down on his own while the other remained standing, and the judge instructed neither to sit nor to stand, Bach holds that the judge need not intervene. Knesset HaGedolah disagrees and forbids allowing the disparity even in that case.
Nor may one sit above, etc. If both are standing, it is not significant that one stands on a higher level and the other lower. Sema.
What is considered the conclusion of judgment, etc. There is a dispute whether litigants must stand when testimony is received. Tosafos in Shevuos hold that the litigants may sit, because receiving testimony is considered the conclusion of judgment only with respect to the witnesses. Tosafos in Sanhedrin hold that the litigants too must stand, as seen when Yannai was told, "Stand on your feet and they will testify concerning you." Bach follows Tosafos in Sanhedrin, while Knesset HaGedolah agrees with Tosafos in Shevuos. The Mechaber's silence here appears to support that view, because he defines the conclusion of judgment without mentioning receipt of testimony. Ran writes that the Yerushalmi in Zeh Borer implies that the litigants must stand while testimony is received, but he must have had a different text. Our text says that even those being judged must stand "when they receive their judgment," citing "those who have the dispute shall stand before Hashem." It says when they receive their judgment, not when testimony is received.
Standing by leaning is properly considered standing, etc. See Tumim, where I defended the Rama against the objections raised to him. Nevertheless, ideally the parties should stand plainly without leaning. If the judges themselves are leaning, the litigants during adjudication, or the witnesses during testimony, certainly should not be required to stand, because that would create contradictory postures. Sema. Although Bach and Shach object, I wrote in Tumim that there is no difficulty.
