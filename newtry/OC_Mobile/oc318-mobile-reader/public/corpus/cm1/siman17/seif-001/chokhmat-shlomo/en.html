Seif 1. In the gloss: “Leaning is considered sitting for this purpose.” Note: The Sema observes that in siman 28 the Rama rules regarding witnesses that leaning is considered standing. The Sema therefore writes that judges and witnesses should not both lean at the same time, since that would treat the very same posture as both sitting and standing, two contradictory classifications. The Shach cites the Bach, who challenges this from the Gemara’s answer that Rav Nachman “sat like one who loosens his shoes.” It appears to me that the Sema can be defended. Tosafot ask why Rav Nachman did not seat the distinguished woman, since the positive commandment of honoring Torah should override the ordinary requirement that litigants stand. They answer, first, that seating her would cause the opposing litigant’s arguments to become inhibited, and second, that the positive commandment of honoring Torah does not apply to the wife of a scholar after his death. Maharsha asks on the first answer: if honoring Torah is a positive commandment, why did the Gemara later ask from the rule that at the conclusion of judgment everyone agrees that judges sit while litigants stand? Rav Nachman should be permitted to stand out of respect for Torah. There is no difficulty. Ran, citing Ramban, writes that when a Torah scholar comes to court, the judge may rise before him and there is no concern that the other litigant’s arguments will be inhibited, because everyone knows that there is a mitzvah to rise before a Torah scholar. Yet this itself is difficult. We rule that when a Torah scholar and an ignoramus come to court, the court seats the scholar and also tells the ignoramus to sit so that his arguments not be inhibited. If everyone knows the mitzvah of honoring Torah, why is this necessary? It seems that Tosafot disagree with Ramban’s practical conclusion, not because they dispute the fact that everyone knows the mitzvah, but because the very point of the concern is that one litigant sees the court honoring his opponent more than him. Even if he knows that the honor is required by mitzvah, he still sees his opponent treated with greater honor and may become intimidated. Indeed, awareness that the judge is commanded to honor the other litigant could increase the fear that the judge will search for every possible merit on his behalf. Therefore the judge may not openly rise before him. Accordingly Maharsha’s question is answered. Had there been no requirement that judges sit at the conclusion of judgment, Rav Nachman’s device of occupying himself with a bird would have worked, because the opposing litigant could think that he stood only because of the bird. But if at the conclusion of judgment a judge is absolutely required to sit, the stratagem would not help if Rav Nachman actually stood. The litigant might know that a judge is ordinarily required to sit then and would therefore understand that Rav Nachman was standing because honoring Torah permitted him to do so. His arguments could again be inhibited. The Gemara therefore answers that he sat in a posture like one loosening his shoes. That posture was legally sufficient as sitting for a judge, while appearing to the litigant as though he was occupied with something else. Thus there was no contradiction. The Sema is correct that one posture cannot ordinarily be treated simultaneously as standing and sitting for two opposing legal requirements. Rav Nachman’s case was different because the positive commandment of honoring Torah permitted even actual standing, and the unusual posture was needed only to avoid intimidating the litigant. According to Tosafot’s second answer, and according to Ran in the name of Ramban, the matter is even simpler, since the honor owed to a scholar’s widow after his death is only rabbinic. An act of deference of this sort suffices, even if legally the posture is classified as sitting. This appears to me a correct resolution. Examine it carefully.
