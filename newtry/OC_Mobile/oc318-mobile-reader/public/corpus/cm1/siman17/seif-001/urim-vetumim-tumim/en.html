Both should stand initially. After the fact, however, the judgment is valid. Although Scripture says, "the two men shall stand," from which we learn that litigants should stand, this requirement does not invalidate the judgment after the fact. Ir Shushan explains that this is because nowadays we do not have ordained judges, implying that in the era of ordained judges failure to stand would invalidate even after the fact. Sema already objects to this. Bach agrees with Ir Shushan and rejects Maharuakh's approach, while Shach, seif katan 4, writes that Bach's proof is not compelling. I say Bach's position is untenable. Rosh and Rashba labor to explain the passage in Zevachim which says that a seated person is "disqualified" for testimony. They explain that "disqualified" there is not literal, because the word ordinarily implies invalidity after the fact. According to Bach there would be no difficulty at all. The passage is discussing a Scriptural law in the setting of ordained judges, where according to Bach sitting truly would invalidate after the fact. Furthermore, their principal proof that standing is not indispensable is that a Torah scholar may sit even initially. According to Bach one would have to say that before an ordained beit din even a Torah scholar must stand. Otherwise their proof would still establish that sitting does not invalidate even in front of ordained judges. But Tosafot in the second chapter of Sanhedrin and the fourth chapter of Shevuot indicate that even before ordained judges a Torah scholar is exempt from standing. They ask from the case of a Torah scholar against King Yannai, who was required to stand before a court of twenty-three. R. Avraham son of Shmuel in Mordechai holds that before unordained judges there is not even an initial requirement to stand. But after the fact everyone agrees that even before ordained judges the judgment is valid. Anyone examining Mordechai will see this clearly. Sema's practical conclusion is therefore straightforward and correct. Nevertheless, Sema's explanation that "the two men shall stand" is merely an asmachta is itself difficult. If the standing requirement were only rabbinic, then how does the Gemara derive from a seated priest, through a kal vachomer, that a non-kohen invalidates sacrificial service? The Gemara asks what is unique about one who sits, "for he is disqualified for testimony." That argument assumes the testimony disqualification has Torah force. Rashba and Ran do not call the verse an asmachta in this sense. Rather, Torah itself commands "they shall stand" as the proper mitzvah-form of the proceeding, but not as an indispensable condition whose violation nullifies the judgment. Thus the requirement is d'oraisa as a mitzvah, though not me'akev. This is the correct understanding.
Standing by leaning is properly considered standing. Magen Avraham in Orach Chaim siman 141, and similarly in the laws of Rosh Chodesh, challenges Rama. Rama derives his rule from Rivash, responsum 266. But Rivash speaks only of judges, who are required to sit, and says that leaning can count as sitting. Rivash does not say that for litigants or witnesses, who are required to stand, leaning counts as standing. On the contrary, Tosafot in Zevachim 19, discussing "standing from the side," write that one who reads from the Torah must stand and not lean. This suggests that leaning is not called standing. As for the rule that leaning is permitted in the Temple courtyard, Tosafot explain that the prohibition against sitting there is not based upon a verse requiring standing. It is a matter of reverence for the Shechinah, and leaning does not violate that reverence. Magen Avraham writes that Sotah 40 supports this. His point that Rivash itself is not a direct proof is correct. Nevertheless, Rama's ruling should not be dismissed. First, even according to Magen Avraham's own reasoning, leaning is permitted in the Temple courtyard because the prohibition against sitting there is rooted in reverence for the Shechinah and leaning is not inconsistent with that reverence. But the requirement that litigants stand before beit din is itself explained in Sanhedrin as arising from the fact that they are not merely standing before judges but before the One who spoke and the world came into being, as Scripture says that they stand "before Hashem." If leaning is permitted in the Temple, the openly manifest dwelling place of the Shechinah, then all the more so it should be permissible in beit din. Although "God stands in the congregation of God," the requirement of reverence before beit din is surely no greater than that of the Temple courtyard. This is especially strong according to Sema, who regards the phrase "they shall stand" here as an asmachta and sees the primary reason as honor of the Shechinah present above the judges. The situation is then directly analogous to sitting in the Temple courtyard. I once explained along these lines why Chazal decreed after the incident of King Yannai that kings of Israel are neither judged nor sit as judges, while kings of the house of David remain subject to judgment. The failing in Yannai's case occurred because the king was legally required to stand and the judges improperly showed him favor by failing to demand it. Chazal feared that the same corruption could recur whenever a king of Israel came before a court, and therefore removed such kings from ordinary judicial process. But that concern exists only if the king must stand. The requirement to stand arises, as Shimon ben Shetach said, because the litigant stands before Hashem. Kings of the house of David are permitted to sit in the Temple courtyard. If sitting is permitted to them there, then certainly they may sit before beit din, whose reverence is not greater than that of the Temple. Thus the particular concern that led to Yannai's decree does not arise for Davidic kings. They can therefore judge and be judged. Second, there is evidence for Rama from the very Tosafot in Sotah cited against him. The Gemara asks how the king could read while sitting, since sitting in the Temple courtyard is prohibited. Tosafot ask from the Yerushalmi, which permits a Kohen Gadol to sit in the courtyard, and they also ask from Zevachim's discussion of "standing from the side." At first glance it is unclear how the Yerushalmi's permission for the Kohen Gadol answers or even relates to the question, and Maharsha's resolution is strained. I understand Tosafot as follows. They assume that although Scripture sometimes says "to stand," leaning can ordinarily be included within standing. We see this from the fact that in the Temple courtyard, leaning is allowed even though ordinary sitting is prohibited. Why then does Zevachim say that "standing from the side," meaning standing that depends on support, can invalidate sacrificial service according to one Tanna? The answer must be that the verse "to stand and serve" is specifically superfluous. Since sitting in the courtyard is already independently forbidden, the verse cannot merely teach that a priest should not sit. It therefore teaches a stricter form of standing for the sacrificial service, excluding supported standing. On this reading, Tosafot's question from the Yerushalmi is strong. If a Kohen Gadol is actually permitted to sit in the courtyard, then the redundancy disappears with respect to him. The verse "to stand and serve" could simply be teaching that even a Kohen Gadol, though ordinarily permitted to sit there, must stand while performing the service. If so, there would no longer be proof that leaning itself invalidates. Thus Tosafot implicitly accept that leaning normally qualifies as standing unless a special derivation requires unsupported standing. That supports Rama concerning testimony and litigants, where "they shall stand" contains no such special redundancy. Third, the Gemara in Zevachim derives that a non-kohen invalidates the service through a kal vachomer from one who sits: one who sits may eat sacred foods, yet if he performs the service he invalidates it; a non-kohen who may not eat sacred foods should certainly invalidate the service. The Gemara challenges: perhaps sitting is stricter because one who sits is disqualified from testimony. It answers with a seated Torah scholar, who can testify. If leaning were invalid for service but valid for testimony, as Rama holds, why not derive the kal vachomer from leaning instead of sitting? A person who leans may eat sacred foods and yet, according to the Tanna who disqualifies "standing from the side," invalidates the service. A non-kohen should then certainly invalidate it, and the testimony objection would disappear because leaning is valid for testimony. I have heard that a small work called Asifat Chachamim cites an answer in the name of the great R. Avraham Broda: the reason supported standing invalidates the service is only because otherwise the verse "to stand and serve" would be unnecessary, since sitting in the courtyard is already prohibited. But at the stage where one has not yet established that a non-kohen invalidates service, that redundancy can be used differently, namely to teach that a Davidic king who is allowed to sit in the courtyard must nevertheless stand if he serves. Thus there would be no proof that leaning invalidates. Those words cannot be correct. The Gemara derives two verses concerning standing in sacrificial service, one for the initial requirement and one to teach that failure invalidates the service. The Tosafot question from the general prohibition against sitting in the courtyard concerns why one needs a verse for the initial requirement. It does not eliminate the need for a verse establishing invalidity after the fact. Moreover, even before the Gemara proves that a non-kohen invalidates the service, supported standing is already shown to be invalid according to the relevant Tanna. Otherwise one verse would suffice to teach invalidity after the fact, because the initial requirement to stand is independently known from the prohibition against sitting in the courtyard. The suggestion concerning a Davidic king cannot explain this, because we are discussing the initial obligation to stand, and no one would suppose that a Davidic king may intentionally perform priestly service. He is a non-kohen and already violates the prohibition "no outsider shall approach." The quoted answer should therefore be disregarded. In truth, however, the original question against Rama can be answered simply. Whether leaning invalidates sacrificial service is itself a Tannaitic dispute. The Gemara says that the practical difference between the Tannaim is "standing from the side." One validates it and the other disqualifies it. "Standing from the side" means precisely supported standing, where another person or an object supports him, or he leans against a vessel or wall in such a way that he could not remain standing without the support. Rivash says explicitly that leaning against a pillar is not full standing because in Zevachim the first Tanna holds that standing from the side is not called standing, meaning a posture impossible without support. Thus one Tanna actually validates leaning for sacrificial service. The Gemara seeks a derivation that a non-kohen invalidates service according to everyone, because no Tanna is found saying that a non-kohen's service is valid after the fact. It therefore cannot derive from supported standing, whose invalidity is disputed. According to the Tanna who validates supported standing there would be no kal vachomer. The Gemara instead derives from a seated Torah scholar, whose status yields a kal vachomer acceptable under all Tannaitic views. The question against Rama therefore disappears. Accordingly, once the objection is removed, the same passage actually supports Rama. Tosafot explain the phrase "one who sits may eat" as referring to one who eats terumah separated from sacred foods and to a bechor. Their intent is clear. In Yoma, Tosafot ask how a priest can sit and eat in the Temple courtyard when sitting there is forbidden. Therefore here Tosafot establish the case with sacred foods that can be eaten outside the courtyard, where sitting is permitted. But this is itself difficult. How can the Gemara derive a kal vachomer from one location to another? Outside the courtyard, one may sit and eat. But sacrificial service takes place inside the courtyard, where sitting is prohibited even apart from the laws of service. Perhaps the invalidity results from the location rather than from the nature of sitting. Why did Tosafot not instead interpret "sitting" here as supported standing? Leaning is permitted even in the courtyard, yet according to the first Tanna it invalidates sacrificial service as standing from the side. That would create a stronger kal vachomer without shifting locations. One cannot answer that the Tanna of R. Yosi son of R. Yehudah validates standing from the side. Perhaps R. Mesharshiya's kal vachomer was said only according to the first Tanna, whose ruling is the halachah, while R. Yosi son of R. Yehudah could derive the non-kohen's invalidity from the school of R. Yishmael's kal vachomer from a blemished priest. Likewise, the Tosafot gloss there asks that the Gemara should reject the kal vachomer because a seated person is prohibited in the courtyard, unlike a non-kohen. But if the comparison were to supported standing, this objection would disappear because leaning is permitted in the courtyard. Necessarily, Tosafot understood that R. Mesharshiya's derivation must function according to all views. If he meant only the first Tanna, the Gemara could have derived from supported standing and avoided the testimony objection. The fact that it instead uses a seated Torah scholar shows that supported standing is valid for testimony. Otherwise the same objection would remain. Rama's ruling that leaning qualifies as standing for testimony is therefore strongly supported. One should not ask: if leaning counts as standing for witnesses and as sitting for judges, then leaning simultaneously has both legal forms. Why does Megillah, where Scripture says "I stood on the mountain" and elsewhere "I sat on the mountain," not answer simply that Moshe leaned, which could be described as both standing and sitting? The Gemara answers that Moshe was bowing. The reason is that the question there concerns what Moshe actually did for forty days. Why would he remain leaning for forty days rather than either stand or sit? Bowing, however, can be understood, as Maharsha explains, as a posture of submission and reverence before the Shechinah. Finally, Sema's limitation is correct. Even if we say leaning counts as standing for witnesses, it cannot solve the case where both the witnesses or litigants and the judges are leaning at the same time. The same verse "the two men shall stand" distinguishes the postures of the witnesses or litigants from those of the judges. The former stand while the latter sit. If both groups adopt the same supported posture, we cannot simultaneously classify that identical posture as standing for one and sitting for the other under the very verse whose point is to distinguish them. It is enough to say that leaning counts as standing when the witnesses alone lean while the judges are actually seated. Bach's question, cited by Shach, from the law that removing one's shoes can count as standing for purposes of rising before a Torah scholar while also fitting the judges' requirement of sitting is no difficulty. Those are two unrelated legal contexts. Here, by contrast, both requirements emerge from one verse and deliberately distinguish the respective postures of the participants. Rama's rule is therefore correct, subject to Sema's qualification.
