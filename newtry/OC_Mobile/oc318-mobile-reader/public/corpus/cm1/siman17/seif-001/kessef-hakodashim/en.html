Seif 1. Equal treatment of the two litigants. The requirement to treat litigants equally applies during the claims and active adjudication, or when they arrive for judgment and proceedings begin immediately. It does not govern ordinary interactions outside the judicial setting or before it is known that they have come to litigate. Thus court summonses are customarily written in the style of a respectful letter. The addressee may be given titles appropriate to his social standing, even if the claimant carrying the summons is of lesser status. Within a letter one naturally shortens titles after the opening, so the claimant's shorter designation is not disparagement. The summons is addressed to the defendant in a friendly style in order to encourage compliance and awaken his better qualities. This resembles the ethical principle of rebuking a person as a wise man whose conduct is unworthy of his wisdom rather than labeling him a scoffer. Sacred works likewise explain that carefully chosen praise can make rebuke effective. Such praise need not be false, since even those presently deficient possess mitzvot and virtues, and the term "righteous" can describe a person relative to a particular matter. Chazal themselves sometimes used respectful praise to draw people close and taught proper honor even toward the wealthy where social peace requires it. The claimant or messenger carrying the summons need not receive the same extended titles because the letter is not addressed to him. Mentioning him briefly, even simply as Rabbi so-and-so, follows ordinary documentary style. By contrast, the respectful titles given to the defendant are part of the communication intended for him. If he then refuses the summons, those very titles can become ironic and deepen the disgrace of refusal. Outside the actual judicial proceedings there is no prohibition in giving ordinary honor without a corrupt purpose. This follows a fortiori from the laws of bribery, which apply in connection with the case itself. The Torah says, "You shall not show favoritism in judgment," indicating that the equality requirement is during judgment. The separate prohibition against perverting judgment already forbids an incorrect result; the additional command forbids conduct during adjudication that can close one litigant's mouth or create a significant imbalance. As a protective measure one should avoid even lesser favoritism, but the formal requirement is tied to the judicial setting. The incident of Rav Anan, also recorded in Tanna Devei Eliyahu, does not contradict this. The later judge mistakenly believed the litigant was Rav Anan's relative and therefore showed him special honor, which inhibited the opponent's claims. Had Rav Anan simply stated that he withdrew because he feared bias after receiving a gift, without causing the second judge to honor the litigant, there would have been no such concern. A judge's withdrawal from a case because a gift was offered does not itself reasonably silence the other litigant. Nor do we assume without evidence that because a person attempted to give one judge a gift, he must also have bribed another. We do not presume people wicked. Thus the problem in Rav Anan's case was the actual honor displayed by the replacement judge, not the mere fact that Rav Anan had declined to judge.
