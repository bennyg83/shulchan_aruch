A litigant who sued his fellow, etc. Rama supports this law from the incident at the beginning of Bava Batra, where Ravina surrounded Ronya's field on all four sides. The matter came before Rava, who told Ronya: "Go appease him with what he is willing to accept; otherwise I will judge you according to Rav Huna in accordance with R. Yosi." Rama infers: why did Rava not immediately award Ravina the full amount required by law, since Tosafot hold that the halachah follows that view? This shows that a judge may not award a plaintiff more than the plaintiff himself demanded. Sema explains that if Ravina knew the halachah and nevertheless demanded only the value of guarding, then certainly the judge may not award more, because there is no clearer waiver than that. But if the plaintiff demanded less only because he did not know the law, Sema still appears to hold that the judge should not award more. Bach says similarly that Rava assumed Ravina did not know the applicable law, yet still did not obligate Ronya beyond the lesser amount. Shach objects strongly. If Ravina did not know the law, where is the waiver? Why should Rava not judge according to the plaintiff's true legal right? What can an ignorant plaintiff do if he does not know the law in full? The objection is correct. One might try to defend Sema through the position cited by Kenesset HaGedolah from Tumot Yesharim in the name of R. Yehonatan, that the rule "open your mouth for the mute" applies to a defendant and not to a plaintiff. But that does not suffice. First, Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in seif 9 imply otherwise. Second, where a litigant knew a factual argument but failed to make it, there may be room to discuss whether beit din supplies it. But where he simply did not know the law and therefore could not formulate the legal demand, the judge should plainly protect his actual legal right. I therefore prefer Shach and Ir Shushan's explanation. The plaintiff made a general or incomplete demand, and the judge did not know whether the limited amount reflected ignorance of the law or a conscious waiver. Perhaps the plaintiff knew the law and intentionally relinquished the balance, or perhaps he was offsetting an old debt or some other consideration, as Ir Shushan suggests. Because of that uncertainty, the judge cannot extract additional money from the defendant. The burden of proof remains upon the claimant. Perhaps he knowingly waived the excess and now wishes to retract. That is how the incident with Ravina should be understood. Rava did not investigate whether Ravina knew or did not know the law. In the face of uncertainty, he did not obligate Ronya beyond the lesser amount Ravina was willing to accept. Tosafot in Sukkah, Perek Lulav HaGazul, write concerning the phrase "agree and sell, otherwise I will rule like R. Tarfon" that Ravina appeased and waived. Their meaning may likewise be that perhaps he waived. However, there remains a question concerning an unlearned litigant. With a Torah scholar such as Ravina, it is reasonable to entertain the possibility that he knew the law and deliberately waived part of the claim. It is often more plausible that the law was not hidden from him than that he simply erred. With an ordinary unlearned person, by contrast, perhaps the more reasonable assumption is that he simply did not know the law rather than that he knowingly waived his right. This requires study. There is also a difficulty even after adopting Shach's explanation. Rava said, "If he does not appease Ravina, I will judge you according to Rav Huna in accordance with R. Yosi." How could Rava threaten a judgment for more than Ravina demanded if a judge is forbidden to award more than the claim? One cannot say that Rava merely sought to frighten him, because Tosafot conclude that he stated the actual law rather than an empty threat. Sema answers that this was a penalty because Ronya refused to obey the law. This is plainly strained. Why invent a special penalty? If a litigant refuses to comply, beit din has many ordinary coercive measures. Tosafot themselves ask along those lines. Sema gives a second answer: once Ronya refuses to pay the lesser amount voluntarily, the situation is treated as though Ravina had not limited his claim. Ravina then comes before beit din and the judges award the full amount required by law. This too is difficult. Why should the waiver disappear merely because enforcement becomes necessary? A waiver remains a waiver. One might say Ravina waived only on condition that Ronya pay voluntarily and not force him into litigation. Shach appears inclined toward such a reading. But that too is forced. If this were the law, Shulchan Aruch should have stated explicitly that when a defendant refuses to pay the reduced claim without coercion, the plaintiff can revive the full claim and the judge may award more, as in Ravina and Ronya. Bach and Shach therefore answer that Rava said this only to frighten Ronya. Tosafot's statement that Rava was not merely bluffing concerns only the underlying legal position, namely that the halachah itself follows Rav Huna in accordance with R. Yosi. It does not mean that Rava necessarily intended to impose the full amount upon Ronya despite Ravina's lesser demand. This too is difficult. If we reject the idea that Rava would frighten a litigant by misstating the law merely because otherwise one might mistakenly think the halachah follows Rav Huna, why is it better to say that he would frighten Ronya with a judgment Rava could not actually impose because Ravina had demanded less? That is still a false statement in practice. Indeed, the preceding seif says that when a litigant boasts, "I could claim more from you, but I do not wish to," the judge should not remain silent if the statement is legally wrong, lest listeners mistakenly conclude that the boast is correct. All the more so a judge himself should not say something that does not accord with the actual judicial power of the court and thereby create a misleading precedent that judges may award more than a plaintiff claimed. If one wishes to defend Bach and Shach, one must say as follows. If the halachah did not follow Rav Huna and R. Yosi, Rava could not have threatened Ronya with that position, because listeners would wrongly conclude that the halachah followed it. But since the substantive halachah truly did follow Rav Huna, Rava could invoke it as leverage. Listeners would understand that he was merely applying pressure in this particular situation, because if the judge truly had authority to award Ravina more than he demanded, why did Rava not simply do so immediately? A Torah scholar's right should certainly be protected by the judge. Even this answer remains uncomfortable. Moreover, how would the threat work on Ronya? He could infer that Rava was only bluffing. If the law truly allowed the larger award despite Ravina's limited demand, why was Rava showing him special favor by delaying it? Ronya might even suspect Rava of improperly favoring him and diminishing Ravina's legal right. The better explanation, in my view, is one of two possibilities. First, observe the wording of the Gemara: "He came before Rava," not "they came before Rava." This suggests that Ronya alone came to ask Rava the law. There was no factual dispute requiring both litigants. Ronya sought a halachic ruling concerning how much one who was enclosed by another's fences must pay. Rava told him: "Go and appease Ravina with the amount he is presently willing to accept. If you do not, I will judge you according to Rav Huna in accordance with R. Yosi." Meaning: take advantage of Ravina's present willingness. Perhaps if the matter becomes an actual lawsuit, Ravina will no longer agree to the lesser amount and will instead make a general claim for what the law awards. At that point Rava will obligate Ronya in the full amount. There is therefore no false threat and no award beyond a plaintiff's claim. This very incident supports Rama's rule. If a plaintiff's limited demand were always treated as a mistake whenever he did not know the law, then even while Ravina was asking only for guarding expenses, Ronya would still be withholding the balance unlawfully. The judge would have to warn Ronya: "You do not satisfy your obligation until you pay the full amount required by Rav Huna, regardless of Ravina's limited request." Rava instead told him that if Ravina remained willing to accept the lesser amount, he could pay that amount and be free of liability. This shows that the lesser demand operates as a valid waiver and is not automatically treated as a mistake caused by ignorance of the law. If Ravina later withdrew the willingness and filed a new full claim, then the matter could be litigated accordingly. A second explanation follows Shach's own principle. When a plaintiff demands less and the reason is unclear, we do not extract the balance because perhaps he knowingly waived it. One might object: the legal debt is certain and the waiver is doubtful. Should this not resemble "I do not know whether I repaid you," where the debtor must pay if the creditor states with certainty that no payment occurred? The answer is that here the defendant had no way to know why the plaintiff chose a lesser demand. In cases where the defendant could not reasonably know, even a certain claim by the plaintiff may not prevail as ordinary bari over shema. I have discussed this at length in siman 75. Now Rava in Bava Batra 135, concerning one who says "this is my brother," holds that where a claimant says "you owe me a maneh" and the other says "I do not know," the defendant is exempt. Tosafot in the first chapter of Ketubot and elsewhere ask that there the defendant had no reason to know. They explain that Rava does not distinguish between a case where the defendant should have known and one where he had no way to know. For Rava, the question is simply whether bari prevails over shema, without that additional distinction. Accordingly, Rava follows his own view. He tells Ronya: "Go appease Ravina. If he accepts the lesser amount, good. But if he later retracts and says that his earlier demand was based on ignorance and therefore was a mistaken waiver, I will judge you according to Rav Huna." Why can Ravina's later assertion matter? Because according to Rava there is no special protection for Ronya merely because he had no way to know whether Ravina knew the law. Once Ravina states with certainty that the waiver was mistaken, Ronya's uncertainty resembles "I do not know whether I discharged the debt," and Rava can obligate him. For us, however, the rule may differ because where the defendant had no way to know, bari does not automatically defeat shema. Still, the incident teaches a key point: so long as the plaintiff does not retract, we treat the limited demand as a waiver rather than assume it resulted from legal ignorance. Therefore even if the plaintiff later says, "I did not know the law," he is not necessarily believed to undo the original waiver. We say that he may have knowingly waived then and merely changed his mind now. This also answers another possible difficulty. Perhaps Rava did not initially award Ronya the full amount because Rava himself held that a mistaken waiver is nevertheless effective, as one passage in Bava Metzia suggests concerning the trees. If so, the incident would not prove anything for us, since the final halachah is that a mistaken waiver is ineffective. But according to the second explanation, Rava's statement "if not" means precisely that if Ravina later says the earlier waiver was mistaken, Rava may then award the full amount. This itself shows that Rava does not treat a truly mistaken waiver as effective. Thus the incident still proves that absent such a claim we presume conscious waiver rather than ignorance. The practical conclusion is therefore: If a plaintiff knowingly limits his demand, the judge may not award more. If the reason for the limited demand is unclear, the judge likewise does not award more, because perhaps the plaintiff knowingly waived the balance. The judge should not automatically assume the plaintiff was ignorant of the law and enlarge the claim on his behalf. Where the plaintiff later asserts that he limited the demand solely because he did not know the law, the question depends on the rules governing a claimed mistaken waiver and the relative certainty of the parties, as discussed above. This is the most coherent reading of the incident and of Rama's ruling.
