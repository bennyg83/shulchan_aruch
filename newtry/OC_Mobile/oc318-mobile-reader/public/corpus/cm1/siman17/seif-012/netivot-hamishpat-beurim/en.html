The judge should not award more. See Sema and Shach, who ask from Bava Batra concerning Ronya, where Rava said, 'If not, I will adjudicate you according to the law.' Tosafot say this was not merely a threat. How could Rava say he would award more than what Ravina currently demanded if a judge may not award more than the claim? Many strained answers were offered. Urim VeTumim notes that the Gemara says Ronya came before Rava, not that both came, and suggests Rava merely told him that if Ravina later came and demanded more, the law would award it. Rama inferred that Rava did not tell Ronya that the larger amount was already stolen property in his hands. This proves that silence can be treated as waiver. That explanation itself is difficult, because mistaken waiver is ordinarily ineffective where the mistake is within the amount people commonly err. There can be no greater such mistake today than ignorance of monetary law, especially by an unlearned person. Sema goes even further and treats even a certain legal mistake as waiver, which conflicts with the laws of mistaken forgiveness. In my view the distinction is this: where, according to the law as matters presently stand, the defendant is already holding property that belongs to the claimant and would be committing theft by retaining it, beit din must require its return even if the claimant did not ask. We attribute his silence to legal mistake. But in Ronya's case, Ravina had built the wall. According to Nimukei Yosef, Ronya became liable for half only when Ravina intended from the beginning to grant him a share of the wall upon payment. According to Ramban, liability is like one who entered another's field and improved it without authorization, and the Shach explains elsewhere that such liability depends on intent to improve for the other person. Therefore, if Ravina had originally made a legal mistake and never intended to convey half the wall or improve for Ronya, Ronya might owe nothing beyond a guarding benefit. Even if Ravina intended to sell half upon payment, so long as he does not demand payment for half, Ronya is not presently withholding stolen property. The entire wall remains Ravina's until payment. Beit din therefore need not inform Ravina that he has a right to force Ronya to buy half. This is like many communal obligations where neighbors may compel one another to participate, but beit din does not volunteer to assert the claim. Rama's wording is precise: he says the judge sees that the defendant 'would become obligated' for more, not that he 'already owes' more. Where money already belonging to the claimant is in the defendant's hands, beit din must prevent theft. In Ronya, Rava's statement meant: accept the smaller amount for which Ravina is presently willing to settle, because if Ravina again demands payment for half after your conduct showed satisfaction with the wall, I will rule according to the law and require the full amount. Until Ravina actually renews that demand, however, I cannot award him more.
It is a legal error and is reversed. See Shach, who says the reason a judge does not award more is merely that because of doubt we presume the claimant waived the excess. Therefore, if the money has already been extracted, the possessor can say the earlier underclaim was due to legal error. It appears that even before beit din actually transfers money, if the claimant returns and says, 'The smaller amount I claimed was due to legal mistake,' he may amend his claim and the court may then award the excess. This is obvious in a land claim, because even an express gift of land is revocable before an effective kinyan. But even with movables, where an express forgiveness would be irrevocable, here the Shach treats the original conduct only as a doubtful forgiveness. The claimant is therefore believed to say it resulted from mistake. Bava Metzia shows that one who transferred property under an invalid future sale or asmachta can be believed that the resulting waiver was mistaken; only in a completed sale does the Gemara sometimes treat mistaken waiver as effective for special reasons. All the more so here, before the excess has even been paid, a mistaken reduced claim can be corrected. The Shach's statement about money already extracted merely teaches that even if the claimant says nothing, we do not take the money back from him because the original waiver was only doubtful. According to what I wrote in the preceding seif katan, however, the distinction has little practical application.
