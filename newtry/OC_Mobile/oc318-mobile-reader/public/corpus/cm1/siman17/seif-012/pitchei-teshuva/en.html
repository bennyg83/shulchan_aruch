Through an interpreter. Baer Heitev notes the current practice of judging litigants who speak a foreign language through an interpreter. Bach gives another basis: in monetary law the prohibition is rabbinic and applies only initially. The Gemara's challenge to Rava from the Mishna concerned a case where another judge who knew their language was available. But where that is impossible, such as foreign litigants for whom no local judge knows the language at all, the judge may hear them through an interpreter even initially, because the Sages did not institute their restriction in such a case. Radbaz, volume 1, siman 331, similarly justified the contemporary practice. According to most commentators, the Gemara's foreign-language case concerned witnesses, for whom testimony must be 'from their mouths' and not secondhand. Litigants do not present that problem and may be heard through an interpreter. Even according to Rambam and those who read the case as litigants, the restriction is only rabbinic. Where one can hear them directly, one must, as in Rava's case; where no one knows their language, the Sages did not decree because the situation is uncommon and refusal would cause litigants loss. Radbaz further writes that if there is no interpreter at all, the court may hear the parties through signs and gestures and judge accordingly in monetary cases, though not in capital cases. Birkei Yosef, letter 13, discusses at length Radbaz's statement that most commentators understood the Gemara as referring to witnesses. In letter 17 he cites Mabit in Kiryat Sefer that the rule against hearing through an interpreter applies only to one interpreter, whereas two may be relied upon. Birkei Yosef argues from Bach and Radbaz that there is no distinction: if a judge knows the language, that judge must hear the litigants directly and even two interpreters do not help; if no judge knows it, one interpreter suffices. Otherwise they should have required two whenever available. The reason is that direct hearing lets the judge clarify the claims and discern the truth, and even two interpreters may both misunderstand the arguments. Accordingly, just as a defendant may not appoint an advocate in siman 124, appointing two agents would not help either.
