A judge may not award, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 26, who asks why Rava said, “If not, I will rule against you in accordance with Rav Huna according to Rabbi Yose,” when Tosafot write that he said this as a ruling and not merely to frighten him. I found the same question in Maharu's glosses to Darkei Moshe: Either Ravina told Rava the entire history, namely that he had originally demanded a large amount and later agreed to accept a small amount voluntarily, in which case no proof may be brought from there because Ravina himself knew that more was owed and nevertheless compromised; or he did not relate the entire history and demanded only the smaller amount, in which case how could Rava threaten to rule according to Rav Huna when it would be forbidden to award more than was claimed? Tosafot state that Rava spoke as a matter of law, not merely to frighten him. This requires study. In my opinion, this presents no difficulty. Tosafot's statement that Rava did not speak merely to frighten him means only that Rava did in fact rule like Rav Huna, as their words plainly indicate. Even so, it is possible that although he accepted Rav Huna's ruling, he threatened to apply it here without intending actually to award that sum, because Ravina had claimed only a small amount. The Bach writes similarly at the end of his discussion. More fundamentally, the Rama's words are clear. The Sema and Bach understood him to mean that even where a claimant is mistaken or ignorant of the law, the judge may not award him more than he claimed. They therefore ask what proves that the incident of Ravina involved such a mistake. The Bach answers that Ravina must have asked Ronia only, in general terms, to pay the cost of guarding. Had he told Rava that the law entitled him to the full cost of the fence under Rav Huna, but that he was waiving the excess, Rava's threat to rule according to Rav Huna would have been meaningless, since Ravina had expressly claimed only guarding expenses. Thus, says the Bach, Rava must have thought Ravina unaware that the law follows Rav Huna, and yet he initially told Ronia to appease Ravina for the lesser amount. This proves that a judge may not award more than was claimed. In my opinion, this proves nothing. Ravina had originally demanded the cost of the fence, evidently intending to receive whatever the law entitled him to. When he saw that Ronia refused to pay, he offered to settle for the inexpensive cost of reeds or for guarding expenses, in order to avoid litigation. Thus, even when they came before Rava, Ravina's willingness to accept less was voluntary. If Ronia refused to pay anything without compulsion by beit din, Ravina did not intend to waive the balance. Rava therefore said: appease him with the amount he voluntarily accepted before I rule; otherwise, if you insist on paying only under compulsion, I will rule against you according to Rav Huna. There is also a substantive difficulty with the Sema's and Bach's interpretation. Where the judge sees that the claimant is legally entitled to more than he claims because he is mistaken or ignorant, why should the judge not award him everything due under the law? Merely because a claimant does not know that his property is held by another, can the judge decline to restore it? By what right would the defendant acquire another's property? There is no waiver, because the claimant did not know what he was waiving. The Sema's assertion that Rivash, siman 227, concerns a claimant who knew the law and intended to waive part of his claim is also plainly incorrect. Rivash objected to the judges' ruling there even though the claimant made a general demand and offered the defendant an election. Were the Sema correct, perhaps the facts were simply that Reuven offered the election without saying he waived anything because he did not know the law. Rather, the Rama's words should be understood literally. He does not say that the judge knows the claimant is mistaken. He says simply that a litigant claimed a small amount and the judge sees that the law would entitle him to more. When the claimant makes a general, limited demand, the judge may not award more, because he may knowingly be compromising or may have waived it. Money may not be extracted on a doubt. This is precisely the case of Ravina and Ronia. The Gemara records no argument before Rava and no declaration by Ravina that he was waiving the balance. It states simply that they came before Rava, implying that Ravina demanded only guarding expenses. Rava did not award more because Ravina may have known that the law follows Rav Huna and nevertheless chosen to waive it. Rivash likewise writes that since Reuven waived part of his demand before beit din and gave Shimon an election, the judges should have ruled according to his claim. Although Shimon did not accept it, so long as Reuven maintained that claim they could not obligate Shimon beyond it. Rivash means that, since Reuven gave the election and we do not know why he did so, no greater liability may be imposed unless it becomes clear that his limited claim resulted from a legal mistake. I later saw that the Levush states this explicitly: perhaps the claimant has already made an accurate accounting and knows the defendant owes no more; perhaps he is offsetting another debt; or perhaps he waived the excess. If the judge awards him more, the claimant may then persuade himself that his prior calculation was mistaken and take all that was awarded because everyone sees the court ruled for him. This plainly accords with my explanation. The Sema further infers that the judge may, or perhaps must, tell the defendant: “Pay what he claims, or we will place you under ban until you pay the entire amount due under the law,” and may penalize him if he refuses. He bases this on Tosafot's statement that Rava spoke as law and not merely to frighten him, whether as a penalty or because the defendant's refusal to pay even the lesser amount is treated as though the claimant had not limited his claim. This inference is not compelled. As explained, Tosafot mean only to exclude the suggestion that Rava did not accept Rav Huna's ruling as law. Examine carefully.
And if he ruled, etc. Be'er Sheva, folio 115b, writes that a judge may rule in favor of the holder of a document on the basis of a legal advantage evident in the document, even though the holder did not plead that advantage and was entirely unaware of it. He brings proofs; see there. This requires some study.
It is an error of law and the ruling is reversed. This means that the ruling is reversed so long as the judge has not yet extracted the money. Such was the incident in Rivash, where Reuven gave Shimon an election concerning division of rental income, either by money or by years, and Rivash ruled that the judgment must be reversed and the election remains with Shimon. If, however, the judge already extracted the sum legally due and gave it to the claimant, it appears that the ruling is not reversed. According to my explanation, the judge may not award more only because money cannot be extracted on a doubt. Once it has already been extracted, the money should remain where it presently stands. Examine carefully.
