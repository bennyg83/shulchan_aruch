He sees that the other party would become liable to him for more. Darkei Moshe derives this law from the episode of Ravina and Ronia. Ravina enclosed the field and later asked only for the cost of guarding. Rava told Ronia to appease Ravina with what Ravina had offered, otherwise he would judge him according to Rav Huna and obligate more. Rivash similarly ruled where a claimant gave the defendant a choice between two calculations and thereby voluntarily reduced his demand. Since he waived the excess before beit din and gave the defendant a choice, the judges should rule in accordance with the claim actually made. The Shach writes that this applies only where the reason for the waiver is unknown. If beit din knows that the claimant waived because he made a legal mistake, it is a mistaken waiver and ineffective. In my view, Rivash follows his own position elsewhere that mistaken waiver in a sale context is nevertheless a valid waiver. He explains, based upon Rabbeinu Tam, that where one sold the future produce of a palm tree, collected money, and later the sale proved legally ineffective, the recipient's mistaken concession can still be treated as waiver because he knowingly intended to transfer those fruits and merely erred in the law. This differs from a loan secured by a field, where return of both land and produce is influenced by the special law of interest or by the fact that the original arrangement was a loan subject to repayment. Rivash applies the same principle to an asmachta obligation that one later paid or secured with collateral because he mistakenly thought himself legally liable: where the original relationship was not a loan, the mistaken waiver can be effective. Although Tosafot gives a somewhat different rationale in the palm fruit case, Rivash's own view is clear and is also reflected in the Rosh. Therefore Rivash in the present case could rule that once the claimant voluntarily reduced his claim before beit din, the reduction stands even if based on legal error. The same explains Ravina and Ronia. When Ravina built the fence on land used by both parties, he intended the courtyard to acquire the wall for Ronia when Ronia would pay his share. Once Ravina later asked only the guarding fee, he effectively waived his greater claim. Since he knew the stones were his and nevertheless intended to transfer the benefit for the guarding fee, the mistaken waiver remains a waiver. See Maharit, responsa, part 1, siman 112.
