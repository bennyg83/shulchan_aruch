A judge may not award more than was claimed, and if, etc. Darkei Moshe cites the case in the first chapter of Bava Batra: Ravina surrounded Runya on four sides and demanded payment. Runya refused. Ravina eventually asked only for the cost of guarding, and Runya still refused. When they came before Rava, Rava said, "Go appease him with what he was willing to accept; otherwise I will adjudicate you according to Rav Huna." Darkei Moshe derives that if a litigant claims only a lesser amount even though, under law, he is entitled to more, the judge need award only what was claimed. If he awards more, that is a legal error and the judgment is reversed. Rivash, responsum 227, rules similarly. Rama states this here briefly. The proof, however, requires explanation. In the Gemara, Ravina knew that Runya was liable for more but voluntarily chose to waive part. Initially he demanded payment according to the full fencing, then for inexpensive reeds, and finally only the guarding cost. Perhaps Rava refrained from awarding more precisely because Ravina knew the law and voluntarily waived the difference. That does not prove a case where the claimant demands too little because he does not know the law. Perhaps such a claimant falls under "Open your mouth for the mute," so the judge must award the full legal amount, or at least an over-award should not be reversed. One cannot say Ravina waived only to avoid litigation but, once forced into court, wished to claim everything, because Rava nevertheless said, "appease him with what he was willing to accept." If so, Rama should have taught the greater novelty: even though the claimant no longer wishes to waive once litigation becomes necessary, his earlier willingness still limits the award. Nor can one say that Rava did not know the earlier negotiations and thought Ravina himself believed only guarding costs were due. If so, why did Rava threaten to impose the full law? It would be forbidden to award more than the claimant demanded. Rivash's responsum itself involved a case where the claimant knowingly gave the defendant a choice between two alternatives and thereby voluntarily waived a possible advantage. Thus Rivash proves only a knowing waiver, not an ignorant underclaim. Rama must understand the Ravina incident differently: when they came before Rava, Ravina did not reveal that he knew the full law or had voluntarily waived. Rava therefore treated the guarding-cost demand as the formal claim and did not award more. His statement, "if not, I will adjudicate you according to Rav Huna," was in the nature of a penalty against Runya for refusing even the lesser demand. Tosafot explain that Rava did not say this merely to frighten him, but as an actual legal consequence. According to this reading, even Rama's view allows or perhaps requires a judge to tell the defendant: pay the amount claimed, and if you refuse I will sanction you until you pay the full amount due by law. Alternatively, once the defendant refuses the lesser amount, he is treated as though the lesser claim has fallen away and the parties stand before the court for the full legal determination.
