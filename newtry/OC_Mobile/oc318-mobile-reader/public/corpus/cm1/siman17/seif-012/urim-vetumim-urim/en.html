"You would owe me such and such by law." The litigant's statement, "You are speaking falsely," cannot mean that he is presently asserting an additional claim in beit din and the judge calls that claim false. Before final judgment the judge may not cut off a party's arguments by declaring them false, and at final judgment the court will simply decide according to law. Rather, the case is where a party says, "Had I wished, I could have claimed such and such from you, but I am not doing so." In truth, that supposed alternative claim would have had no legal validity because it is unrelated to the case before them. The judge may say, "You speak falsely; you could not have claimed that under the law." If the judge remained silent, listeners might infer that the supposed legal argument was valid and later rule incorrectly because the judge did not object. This was the case in Ketubot, where the parties said, "If we had money, we could discharge your one-tenth inheritance share with money." The Gemara inferred from the judge's failure to say, "Even if you had money, one-tenth of the estate cannot be discharged in cash," that the legal proposition was true. The text of the Shulchan Aruch is terse, so I have explained the point fully.
A small amount. Sema and Bach say that even if the plaintiff claimed only the smaller amount because he did not know the law, the judge may not award more than was claimed. Shach disagrees, and in Tumim I concur, because such a waiver is made in error. The present rule concerns a case where it is unclear why the claimant demanded less. Perhaps he intentionally waived the balance, perhaps he made an accounting concession, or perhaps an old debt was set off. Since the reason is uncertain, the burden of proof rests on the person seeking more than the stated claim. I agree with Shach but add a distinction between a Torah scholar and an unlearned person. An unlearned litigant is presumptively ignorant of a technical legal right, so where the shortfall is readily explained by lack of legal knowledge we should not assume waiver. A Torah scholar is presumed to know the law, and his smaller demand more readily indicates intentional waiver. See Tumim. Sema further says that if the court sees the defendant stubbornly refusing to obey the judgment, it may penalize him by awarding the plaintiff the entire amount actually due by law, beyond what the plaintiff demanded. Shach rejects this, and I likewise conclude in Tumim that the omission of such a power from Rama supports Shach.
