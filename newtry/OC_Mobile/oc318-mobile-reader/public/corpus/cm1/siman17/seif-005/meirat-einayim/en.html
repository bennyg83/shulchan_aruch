Specifically where the judge knows, etc. Meaning that he also knows the other litigant will be compelled to stand before him. The source says this explicitly. Then he should not initially hear one side alone, because perhaps the other litigant will object to being judged by someone who has already heard his opponent's argument. In addition, when the judge knows he will decide the case, the first party's words enter his mind more forcefully. But if he does not know he will be the judge, he may hear the account. Later he should not adjudicate unless the other litigant agrees to be judged before him despite his having already heard one side. This explains Rama's wording, "but if he heard the claims of one side and afterward..." At first glance this is not the direct opposite of the preceding rule; one might have expected, "but if he does not know he will be the judge, he may hear." Rather, the hearing itself is not inherently forbidden. The prohibition arises because he is already the judge before whom the other party will be forced to appear. See Responsa Maharil.
Likewise the litigant is warned. The verse, "Do not bear a false report," is also expounded as, "Do not cause another to bear a false report." Thus the litigant too is prohibited from presenting his claims to the judge in the other's absence.
