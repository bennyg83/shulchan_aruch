He is permitted to act as judge in the matter. This is only if he informs the other party: "I already heard your opponent's arguments," and that party nevertheless consents to have him judge. If he does not disclose this, the other party can later say, "I did not accept you as judge on that understanding." Mahari Weil records that some of his teachers were stringent and required a kinyan on such consent, treating the judge like an otherwise disqualified person whom the parties accept. In our time, unfortunately, people are lenient about this, especially in the system where each party chooses one judge. Yet the prohibition certainly applies there too. If a judge already heard one side, wrote an actual ruling on the matter, and later is asked to sit in the same case, the majority of responsa authorities disqualify him because he has a personal stake in not contradicting his earlier written position. Even if the other party agrees to have him judge, he may be treated like a fully disqualified judge requiring a formal kinyan of acceptance. If, however, in a different case he previously ruled on a similar legal question or wrote generally in a book how such a law should be decided, he may certainly judge a later case involving the same principle. Otherwise all Torah scholars who author halachic works would be disqualified from judging matters they have previously discussed. Knesset HaGedolah writes at length on this point.

If the opponent himself is a Torah scholar, there is less concern that he will suspect improper private preparation. Mahari Weil, responsum 119, discusses an attempt to disqualify a judge because the authorized representative was the judge's stepson and naturally frequented his home. He rejected the objection. Since the representative receives the same fee whether he wins or loses, he is qualified to testify and is not suspected of distorting the arguments. "A person does not sin without benefit." Do not be misled by Rama's responsum 104, which can sound as though the representative's relationship itself disqualifies the judge. There the representative's compensation increased if he won. He was therefore disqualified as a witness, and his relative was correspondingly disqualified as judge. Where the representative himself is qualified to testify, his relative is not automatically disqualified from judging.
