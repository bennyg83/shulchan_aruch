And the defendant should likewise not bring others with him. See Sema, seif katan 8, who writes that this does not depend on the wishes of the plaintiff or defendant; rather, beit din compels both sides to comply. Bach rules that it depends only on the litigants, and if they agree that friends may sit with them there is no basis to object. Urim VeTumim and Netivot HaMishpat regard this as the principal view. Radbaz, volume 2, siman 753, discusses Reuven and Shimon who came to court, with Shimon bringing acquaintances. Reuven said that their presence would inhibit his arguments. Shimon answered that Reuven could also bring acquaintances, but Reuven replied that he wanted only the two litigants present. Radbaz ruled for Reuven, since Shimon's acquaintances might be sharper and signal arguments to him, or might be powerful or intimidating, or the like. The proof is the rule concerning one litigant wearing a fine garment while the other is poorly dressed. Although we tell the latter, 'Clothe him like yourself,' here the response 'bring acquaintances as I did' is insufficient, because garments can be made equal but people cannot. The rule is that if a litigant claims that the presence of others will inhibit his arguments, and beit din sees even a remote basis for the concern, his request is heard. See Birkei Yosef, letter 4.
