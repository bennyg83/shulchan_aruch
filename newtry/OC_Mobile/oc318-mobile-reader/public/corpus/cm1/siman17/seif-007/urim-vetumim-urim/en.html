The judge must repeat the arguments. Sema explains that this serves two purposes. First, perhaps the judges did not fully understand the parties' intent. Second, the litigants themselves may worry that the court misunderstood, and after discussion the judges may become reluctant to revisit the case. According to this rationale, the judges should repeat the arguments in the parties' presence before deliberation, not as is commonly done today, when the claims are reviewed only immediately before announcing the ruling after the deliberation has already concluded. The modern practice can be defended because the first concern is especially relevant to a single expert judging alone. In a panel of three judges it is much less likely that all will misunderstand the same point, especially in the system where each party chose one judge. Bach similarly writes that the concern is reduced in a three-judge panel. As for the litigants' suspicion, if they hear their claims accurately summarized before the ruling is announced, they know the judges had them in mind during deliberation. Who else would have supplied the details afterward? In a panel of three one judge also reminds another, and in the chosen-judge system the concern is still smaller.
