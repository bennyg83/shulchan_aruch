Seif 12. “When one litigant boasts to the other, ‘You will be held liable to me for such and such in court,’” etc. Note: The wording of the Mechaber appears to imply that the judge must rebuke him only where he asserts that this is how the present case will actually be decided. If he merely says, “Had I possessed such a claim I would have done such and such, but what can I do, it is not in my hands,” or something similar, without asserting what the judge will rule now, the judge would not need to rebuke him. Yet Ketubot 69a, the source of this law, seems to prove the opposite. There the brothers said, “Had we had money, we would have removed her with money,” and Ameimar was silent. The Gemara infers from his silence that he agreed with the legal proposition. Rabbeinu Tam derived from there that a judge must correct a litigant who states an incorrect legal conclusion. The brothers in that case were not saying, “This is what you will now rule for us.” Nevertheless, Ameimar’s silence mattered. It therefore appears that even such a statement requires a response. Urim VeTumim attempts to read the Mechaber differently and argues that where a litigant says what the judge will rule in the present case, the judge should not contradict him because doing so may inhibit his arguments. Before seeing Urim VeTumim, I too was troubled by the opposite question: how may a judge conclusively state the law before fully examining the case? We have already learned that even when the litigants have left and the judges have considered the matter, they should not rush to announce judgment until the ruling is completely clear. The Shach also records opinions that a judge who was already asked to issue a ruling on the identical matter may be considered interested and unfit to serve as judge. Here, while the litigants are still before him and he has not fully analyzed their claims, how can he say definitively that the law is the opposite? Perhaps upon further consideration he will find a reason to change the ruling. Once he has firmly committed himself to one side, he may no longer readily see the other side’s merit. The Gemara itself does not prove otherwise, for there Ameimar was not then sitting as their judge in that dispute. He was free to state his view, and if the precise case later came before him he could decline to judge it. I therefore first thought that the judge should not say, “You are lying, the law is the opposite,” but only, “Your claim that this is plainly the law is incorrect. The matter requires consideration and may go either way.” Such a statement would not make him interested, nor would it improperly inhibit argument. But Tosafot quote Rabbeinu Tam as saying that the judge would tell the litigant, “You speak falsely, for you will not be held liable in that way,” which clearly indicates an explicit statement of the opposite ruling. It therefore appears that Rabbeinu Tam and the Mechaber refer to a law so elementary and settled that it requires no judicial consideration, such as a litigant asserting that one who denies everything must take a Torah oath, or that a single witness can directly extract money. In such a case the judge may plainly say that the asserted law is wrong, since no further deliberation is needed. Where the law does require analysis, even if the judge presently inclines to the opposite view, he should not state a definitive ruling. He may say only that the matter is not as obvious as the litigant claims and requires consideration. If the litigant’s statement concerns a hypothetical situation rather than the current case, as in the Gemara, the judge may state his legal view even if it has not yet been fully explored, because he is not then deciding the case. The Mechaber formulated the law using the present case to teach the stronger point: even where concern for inhibited arguments exists, a plainly false legal assertion should not be allowed to stand. This appears clear to me.
