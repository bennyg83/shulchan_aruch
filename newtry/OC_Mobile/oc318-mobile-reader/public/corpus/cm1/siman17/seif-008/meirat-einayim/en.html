A judge who ruled and whose heart troubles him, etc. Through the phrase "the judge shall not serve as an advocate," I cited in Perishah the baraita in chapter Shevuat HaEdut: from where do we know that a judge should not make himself an advocate for his own words? Scripture says, "Distance yourself from a false matter." Rashi explains that if the judge realizes he erred, he should not defend his earlier ruling. Rambam explains instead that he should not serve as an advocate for one litigant's claims, but should state only what appears correct. The Mechaber adopts both interpretations as practical law: first Rashi's, and then Rambam's.
Rather, he shall say what appears, etc. This is Rambam's language in chapter 21. Meaning that the litigant should state his own argument and the judge should remain silent rather than construct it for him. In Perishah I explained the wording differently, with the entire phrase referring to the judge. See there.
Even if one litigant brought one witness, etc. Through the later statement, "if the judge sees merit for one side," all these are Rambam's words and the Tur cites them. The Tur then says this does not appear consistent with the Yerushalmi, which states that when Rav Huna saw a meritorious argument for someone who did not know how to present it, he would open the argument for him, based on "Open your mouth for the mute." In Derishah I demonstrated that the Tur also disputes Rambam's rule that the judge may not tell a litigant, "We do not accept a single witness." I explained the underlying disagreement and that the Tur follows the Rosh's approach. It is surprising that Rama did not mention their view at all. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who writes that everyone agrees it is forbidden to say, "We do not accept one witness."
