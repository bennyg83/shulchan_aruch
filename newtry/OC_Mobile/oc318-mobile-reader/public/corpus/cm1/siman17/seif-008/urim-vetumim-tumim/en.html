He should not say to him, "One witness does not obligate payment," etc. Sema, in Derishah, understands Rosh to disagree with this rule. Rosh holds that beit din advances a litigant's meritorious argument when he does not know to raise it himself. According to Sema, this also resolves the difficulty from siman 42, where Tur rules that when a defect appears in a document, beit din raises the issue even if the defendant did not notice it. Maharshal asks why that differs from the case of one witness. According to Sema, Rosh and Tur simply disagree with the one-witness rule. This is strained. Tur does not present the one-witness matter as a dispute, and it is difficult to say that two teachings of the Yerushalmi contradict one another, especially when both are stated in the name of Rav Huna. The correct explanation, as Bach and Shach also indicate, is as follows. If the plaintiff brings one witness, the judge may not immediately tell the defendant, "One witness is not believed." By saying that, he is putting an answer into the defendant's mouth and effectively teaching him to deny the witness. The defendant may learn from the judge's words how to lie. Rather, the judge should say: "What do you say? Here is a witness who testifies to this matter." If the defendant admits it, well and good. If he says, "What you say is false," then even if he does not know the law that one witness alone cannot establish the monetary claim, the judge may afterward rule according to law and reject the single witness as insufficient. What difference does it make that the defendant did not know the legal rule? He nevertheless denied the substance of the witness's testimony. What is prohibited is opening the defense for him and placing the denial directly into his mouth. The rule in siman 42 is the same. Suppose a promissory note contains a defect. It originally said three, there is visible scraping, and it now appears as thirty. If the defendant says, "I do not owe him that much," then even if he himself did not identify the alteration, beit din can raise the defect in the document for him. What can he do if he is not expert in forgery and deceptive alterations? But if the defendant does not make any claim related to the amount at all and instead says, for example, "This was an accommodation note," then beit din does not independently invent the claim that he owes only three because of the alteration. He never raised anything resembling that defense. Thus the two rules are entirely consistent. This is the correct practical distinction.
