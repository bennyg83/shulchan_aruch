He ruled for one of the litigants. Responsa Rama, siman 112, explains why he answered one of his students concerning the student's right to his wife's inheritance. Although the Torah says, 'Hear between your brothers,' this case was different because it did not depend on disputed factual claims between two parties, but on the laws of inheritance, where the law does not change with the presentation of claims. Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 64, supports this from the daughters of Tzelafchad, who asked for an inheritance share without any recorded argument from their paternal relatives, yet Moshe, Aharon, and the elders heard them and ruled the law. He notes that many later authorities permit answering one side alone even more broadly, but he himself forbids it and writes that this is the correct rule, especially in generations when beit din cannot compel parties to litigate under Torah law. If one tells a questioner even orally that he is likely to lose, he may evade a Torah adjudication. A God-fearing decisor should therefore refrain from answering one side alone. In volume 3, siman 99, however, he answered one side concerning someone who had insulted a Torah scholar, because Chazal warned that one may not hear a Torah scholar being demeaned and remain silent, and one should certainly advocate the merit of a Torah scholar. Maharshal, responsum 24, likewise permitted advocating for a widow when the question came from afar and the respondent would not serve as judge. Rashbam, Yoreh Deah 133, writes that when the purpose is to separate a person from prohibition there is not even a pious reason to refrain, and no greater separation from prohibition exists than preventing contempt of a Torah scholar. Similar support appears in Gittin 7 concerning Mar Ukva's question to Rabbi Elazar. Avodat HaGershuni, siman 47, answered one side concerning the proper forum because, although early authorities avoided answering one side in monetary disputes, withholding an answer there would prevent selection of judges, since that very issue was the dispute. Shev Yaakov, simanim 2 and 3, and Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, acted similarly. Avodat HaGershuni, siman 124, cites Maharam Padua, siman 80, that where a mitzvah concern is involved one need not fear this prohibition, and on that basis he answered one side to prevent the dissolution of an orphan girl's engagement. Meil Tzedakah, siman 53, initially questions later authorities who devised distinctions permitting an answer where the questioner is known to seek the truth and not instruction in deception, or where a mitzvah is involved, since the prohibition stems from the ancient warning, 'Do not make yourself like advocates for litigants,' as Rashi explains in the name of Rav Yehudai Gaon. Nevertheless, he concludes that the practice of venerable rabbis is indeed to answer one who is personally known to them as trustworthy; see there.
