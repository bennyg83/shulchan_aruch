Seif 9. Like those who arrange the arguments before the judges. The prohibition against coaching litigants does not prevent giving a person lawful advice to save his property where no false claim is being invented. For example, if a tenant threatens to assert a right of priority and thereby deters other tenants, or a buyer occupies a house under a sale later discovered to be invalid and his continued occupancy discourages purchasers, one may advise the owner how to terminate the tenancy or possession properly and thereafter market the property. This falls under "open your mouth for the mute," teaching a person the Torah's lawful procedures so that he not lose money needlessly. The case of Runya is different because there the question was whether to inform someone of a right he had apparently waived through ignorance. Silence may sometimes be preferable where informing him would cause another person a loss and the supposed waiver can reasonably be treated as intentional. Mere causation of loss may be exempt in human court but can still be wrong before Heaven. Here, by contrast, there is an element of returning lost property by informing the owner of a lawful method to preserve his assets. The fact that another person might have benefited from the owner's ignorance does not require us to leave the owner uninformed. "Arranging the arguments" means devising a tactical action or false structure for litigation. Explaining a truthful legal claim is generally the opposite, fulfilling "open your mouth for the mute." Even according to authorities who would not proactively disclose a right in a case like Runya's, one who informs a party of a genuine truthful right has not committed wrongdoing so long as he does not teach falsehood. The Sages permitted a person to assist his relative in presenting a case, and the Gemara's great Amora did so. This reflects both ordinary family responsibility and honor for Torah scholars who may be unfamiliar with a particular practical rule. The later qualification concerning an important person applies where public imitation would lead others to go too far. Where there is no such risk of imitation, the prohibition does not automatically expand. In compromise, the concern of "arranging arguments" is still weaker. Chosen arbitrators commonly advocate strongly for the side that selected them, and even the neutral third arbitrator may explain a consideration favoring one side, especially where he knows a relevant point that the party would otherwise have no way to raise. The prohibition is against manufacturing false litigation, not against clarifying legitimate considerations.
