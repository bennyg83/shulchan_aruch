The law is with him, etc. Sema holds that this does not depend at all on the wishes of the plaintiff or defendant; rather, beit din itself must compel the matter. This does not appear to be Mordechai's view. Mordechai writes that otherwise the litigant's arguments may become confused, and then adds that the litigant expressly says that his arguments are being confused. This indicates that the concern applies specifically when he says so explicitly; otherwise, beit din need not intervene. Although Mordechai compares this to the rule that if one litigant is wearing a garment worth one hundred while the other is dressed cheaply, the court tells the wealthy one to dress the other similarly, the two matters are not identical. There, what concern is there if the wealthy litigant dresses the other similarly? No substantive interest of his is impaired. Here, however, so long as the litigant does not reveal that he objects, it may actually be beneficial to him that other people remain present. He may prefer to resolve all related disputes at once rather than litigate separately with each person. It may also be beneficial because one person can be embarrassed to lie in front of another. Chazal say that two people do not readily coordinate in a lie, and for that reason we do not say migo against two people. Likewise, the litigant may prefer that relatives and friends sit near the defendant because he knows them to be upright people who will not tolerate the defendant presenting a dishonest or deceptive claim. Maharam writes that one should not ask from the beginning of Shevuot HaEdut, where the Gemara asks, "Do two people come to litigate and not three?" He says the difficulty is easily resolved. According to Sema, however, it is not so easy. If beit din must always prevent additional people from sitting there, then in truth three people do not normally come to litigate. Tosafot, by contrast, write that litigants commonly come with others. This indicates that so long as the opposing litigant does not expressly object, beit din does not remove those additional people and it is indeed common for them to attend. The same applies to relatives and friends sitting near the defendant: so long as the other litigant does not protest, beit din need not remove them. Bach likewise rules this way. There is some support from the beginning of Perek Kohen Gadol. The Gemara speaks of testimony before a king. Tosafot explain that the case must concern testimony involving the king's son, because a father cannot judge his son. They answer that the king may sit in the beit din but does not participate in the judgment. According to Sema, however, beit din should object to the father's presence as a relative, and all the more so when a king sits beside his son, since that could greatly confuse the opposing litigant's arguments. One can answer that there the matter could not be arranged otherwise, because without the king present the Kohen Gadol would not testify. Nevertheless, the more natural reading supports the approach stated above. This is therefore the correct rule.
