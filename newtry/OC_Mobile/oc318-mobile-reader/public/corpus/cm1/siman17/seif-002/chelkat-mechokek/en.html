There is a presumption that a woman does not brazenly lie in her husband's presence. Rambam, chapter 12 of Hilchot Gerushin, cited below at the beginning of siman 152, rules that if the husband says, 'I divorced my wife,' he is not believed, and even if she agrees that he divorced her he is not believed. Maggid Mishneh explains that although she would have been believed had she alone made the claim and he contradicted her, when he first makes the claim she is not believed by joining him, because when the husband supports her she can be brazen. According to this, our law should have been limited to where she tells her husband, 'You divorced me,' and he denies it; if he agrees, she would not be believed. Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef, also writes that because she stated the matter in her husband's presence and he did not object, it is as though she said she had been divorced from him, implying that if he objected she would not be believed. Beit Yosef rejects this, saying that the presumption that a woman does not brazenly lie before her husband applies even when he protests. His wording implies that all the more is she believed where he does not protest, contrary to Rambam and Maggid Mishneh. Perhaps one can distinguish: if she initiated the brazen claim, she is believed even if he protests, and all the more if he agrees. But if he first says, 'I divorced you,' and she merely follows his statement, there is no brazenness in her agreement. Rabbeinu Yerucham also writes that if she remains with the first husband she requires a get from the second, implying that return to the first husband is permitted. Darkei Moshe and Bach object that one may not return to a former wife once she has been betrothed to another, as above, siman 10. Bach struggles to reconcile the ruling and concludes practically that she may return to the first, bringing proof from the mishnah in Kiddushin 65a where she says, 'You betrothed me,' and he says, 'I did not,' so he is permitted to her relatives while she is prohibited to his. But the cases are not comparable. A wife generally cannot brazenly tell her husband that she is no longer his wife in order to free herself from him. By contrast, many women can brazenly tell another man, 'You betrothed me,' thereby seeking to bind themselves to him. Bach also reasons that since the husband denies her, necessarily one of them is brazen, and since we do not know which, she should remain permitted to the husband and require a get from the second. This is incorrect. It is not considered brazenness for a man to say to a woman, 'You are my wife,' after he in fact divorced her. The special brazenness is for a woman to tell her husband, 'You are not my husband,' when he never divorced her. This resembles the rule that a borrower does not brazenly deny a lender who did him a kindness, while a lender may act boldly toward a borrower. Bach further writes that she renders herself forbidden and therefore requires a get from the second if she remains with the first. But if without a get she is forbidden to the first according to her own words, the second get cannot help, for by her own account she remains forbidden to the first as one returning to a divorced wife after betrothal to another.
