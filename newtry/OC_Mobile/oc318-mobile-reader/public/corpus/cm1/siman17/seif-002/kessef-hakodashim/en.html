Seif 5. To hear the words of a litigant. Perhaps it is permissible for a judge to hear a party's position through an intermediary rather than directly from the litigant. The danger in hearing one side first is that "the first in his dispute appears righteous" and the listener may incline toward him, even while intellectually knowing that a person does not see his own liability and that the opponent may answer differently. That initial inclination resembles the partiality associated with friendship or hostility. Such affection can disqualify a judge, but it does not ordinarily invalidate a witness. Therefore an intermediary who heard the litigant may be somewhat inclined toward his account, yet if he is not the judge his inclination does not necessarily corrupt what he reports. The judge who later hears the report knows that it is only one side's presentation and that the other can respond. Another possible reason for the prohibition is that a litigant speaking outside his opponent's presence may exaggerate or become habituated to a false version he would have been ashamed to present face to face. Yet we do not find that a person who heard one party is barred from later sitting with the judges merely because of this. This suggests that the principal concern is the judge's own premature inclination. Perhaps where the statutory reason, falsehood, is absent, there is room for leniency. The matter requires further study. Panim Meirot writes that even an arbitrator should perhaps not hear one side alone. In the common zabla system, however, each party does present his position privately to his own chosen arbitrator. One possible justification is that where the compromise will be decided with a neutral third arbitrator, that third person hears both sides and the parties knowingly accepted the procedure. Since monetary rights can be waived, their consent may validate a procedure that would otherwise be improper. Bribery is different: even if both parties know and consent, one should not infer permission to accept bribes, and Panim Meirot correctly treats that as prohibited. Apart from actual bribery, the problem of one-sided hearing may have two aspects: real risk of bias and an independent disqualification fixed by Torah law even where the judge intends to rule correctly. Acceptance of such a judge would therefore at least require a formal kinyan. Lodging with one side after appointment may also create subtle benefit and inhibition, unless that arrangement was part of the original accepted procedure. The concern is strongest regarding the neutral third arbitrator, because each party expects his own chosen arbitrator to understand his position. After the fact, however, a judge who heard one side alone is not automatically invalid.
