We seat the Torah scholar, etc. Tosafot in Sanhedrin ask how Shimon ben Shetach could tell King Yannai to stand. The honor of a king should be comparable to the honor of a Torah scholar, for whom the court allows sitting. They answer that capital cases are different. According to this, in testimony concerning matters that can produce capital liability, even a Torah scholar should stand. However, this must be only an additional stringency rather than the strict law. Why should civil and capital cases differ in the basic standing requirement? The verse "the two men shall stand" is written regarding civil litigation. Capital law is learned from civil law through "one law shall be for you." If capital cases created an absolute legal disqualification against a seated Torah scholar, then the Gemara in Zevachim could still challenge its kal vachomer: what is unique about one who sits? He is disqualified from testimony in capital matters, even if he is a Torah scholar. Yet the Gemara resolves the objection simply by citing a seated Torah scholar, implying his testimony remains valid. Therefore, as a matter of strict law, a Torah scholar may sit even in a capital case because the positive obligation of honoring Torah is stronger. Tosafot's distinction must refer only to an added level of stringency. There is, however, another way to remove Tosafot's original question entirely. Rashi in Berakhot identifies the King Yannai of Shimon ben Shetach's era with the Yannai mentioned in Kiddushin, who was a Sadducee. If so, there was no obligation to honor him as king. The law "you shall not curse a leader among your people" applies only to one who acts as a member of your people. A Sadducee does not receive that honor. There was therefore no conflict between royal honor and the duty to stand before beit din, and no distinction between civil and capital law is needed.
And we say to the unlearned person, "Sit." Tosafot in the fourth chapter of Shevuot ask regarding the wife of Rav Huna. The Gemara asks how the court could seat her, since at final judgment everyone agrees the judges sit while the litigants stand. Tosafot ask: perhaps she was sitting at final judgment because a Torah scholar may sit. They answer that there is concern that the opposing litigant's claims will become confused. Maharsha asks why the court could not simply seat both Rav Huna's wife and the opposing litigant. He answers that seating both is permitted only at the beginning of the case, but at final judgment both litigants must stand. That does not appear to fit Shulchan Aruch's wording, which implies that if one litigant is a Torah scholar the court may seat both even at final judgment. We must therefore rely primarily on Tosafot's second answer, also adopted by Ran and Rosh, that the wife of a Torah scholar does not receive the scholar's own right to sit. According to that answer, when the litigant himself is a Torah scholar, both can be seated even at final judgment in order to avoid confusing the other party's claims. Still, I am inclined to say that Shulchan Aruch's instruction to seat both applies only before final judgment. At final judgment, the ordinary Torah rule is that the litigants should stand. The Torah scholar may remain seated because the positive obligation to honor Torah overrides that requirement. But from where do we derive permission to seat the unlearned litigant as well? This is especially so at final judgment, where concern that his claims will become confused is substantially reduced because the parties have already presented their arguments. Therefore the better practical understanding is that both are seated to avoid embarrassment and confusion during the pleading stage, but at final judgment the Torah scholar alone may remain seated.
