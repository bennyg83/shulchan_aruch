In the words I raised in women’s testimony, it is permissible for the IDF to be seated and VAT to be improved first:
If he does not sit, they are not particular about it. If a Torah scholar already sat and the court messenger then made him stand, the judges need not go back and tell him to sit again. Sema. Mechaber's wording also implies that even at final judgment the court seats a Torah scholar and likewise tells the ordinary litigant to sit. See Tumim.
It is permitted to stand before him. As long as the Torah scholar remains standing, the judge himself should also stand in his honor. At final judgment, however, the judges sit, and the Torah scholar too is seated. Torah study and judicial activity may be conducted while seated, and the positive commandment of honoring Torah allows this. Tosafot and Ran in Shevuot.
We are not concerned that his arguments will be silenced. This applies to the Torah scholar himself. Concerning the wife of a Torah scholar, however, although there is also a mitzvah to stand before her, the Gemara feared that such honor might intimidate the opposing party. Rav Nachman therefore instructed his attendant to release a goose so that he would stand in a manner not obviously directed toward her. Possibly this was merely an extra measure of piety. By strict law, because of the concern that the opponent's arguments may be silenced, one need not stand for the wife of a Torah scholar. This may explain why the decisors omitted the specific law of a scholar's wife. Rashi, Tosafot, and Ran imply that this concern applies specifically to the widow of a Torah scholar. While her learned husband is alive, she receives his full status: one stands before her, there is no concern that the other party will be silenced, and she is immediately seated even at final judgment just like the scholar himself. Likewise, one should stand before an elderly unlearned person where the mitzvah of honoring age applies. There is no special intimidation concern, because it is publicly known that one must honor the elderly just as one honors a sage. Why, then, when a Torah scholar is seated and the ordinary litigant is also seated do we not fear the ordinary litigant will be intimidated? Because everyone knows that the scholar is entitled to the honor of Torah. Possibly that particular source is less widely known, but in any event whatever inequity can readily be corrected should be corrected. Seating both parties is feasible. The judge cannot, however, stand for an ordinary litigant merely to avoid revealing his honor for the scholar. The unusual device of releasing a goose is not ordinarily available.
