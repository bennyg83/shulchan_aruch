He hears through interpreters. Bach holds that where there is no alternative, such as when there is no beit din in the city whose judges understand the foreign language spoken by the litigants, judgment may be conducted through interpreters. Accordingly, when the Gemara challenges Rava for appointing interpreters for those foreign-speaking litigants on the ground that a Sanhedrin should not hear through an interpreter, the reason is that another beit din was available in his city whose judges understood that language. This also clarifies a responsum of the Geonim, siman 170. They write that capital cases may not be judged through interpreters, but civil cases may be judged through interpreters in order to impose liability upon the liable party and vindicate the innocent. If a written judgment is issued, it should state that the ruling was made through interpreters because the judges did not understand the litigants' language. Their intent is a case where there is no other beit din in the city that understands the language. In that circumstance civil cases may proceed through interpreters. Capital cases, however, remain invalid even then. We are not responsible to force such a proceeding; on the contrary, Torah says, "the congregation shall save." Thus the Gemara's challenge against Rava remains fully consistent. His case was civil, but another court was available whose judges understood the language. In that circumstance even civil adjudication through interpreters is improper. Kenesset HaGedolah questions the Geonim and leaves their words almost unresolved. According to the explanation above, however, there is no difficulty. Nor do Rambam and Tur contradict the Geonim. We therefore should not depart from the Geonim's ruling, for all their words are received tradition.
