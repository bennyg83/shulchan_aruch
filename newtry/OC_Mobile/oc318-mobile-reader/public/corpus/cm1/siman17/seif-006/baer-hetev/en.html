To answer. If the judge does not need an interpreter to understand the litigant and the interpreter is used only because the litigant wants someone to present or clarify his words to the judge after he himself has stated his claims, it is permitted. One can also distinguish between a case where the litigant gave the interpreter full authority, in which case the interpreter is regarded as the litigant himself and it is permitted, and a case where no such authority was granted, in which case it is forbidden. The present custom to adjudicate the claims of foreign-language speakers through their authorized representatives is based on the principle that anyone who comes before an established court is regarded as having accepted this procedure, and after such acceptance there is no objection.
