One who robs a swarm of bees. Tur rules that a woman or minor is believed to say, "This swarm came from here," provided that the owners were pursuing the bees and the woman or minor spoke innocently, without intending formal testimony. This is the rule of Bava Kamma 114. Rashi explains that the owners must already have been pursuing the swarm before the minor spoke. Maharshal argues that if the pursuit was already occurring before the minor's statement, the minor's words may no longer qualify as innocent conversation in this context. Ritva, however, explains that the Sages believed a minor in this rabbinic acquisition because the minor speaks immediately and innocently while the owners are pursuing the swarm. He expressly adds, based on the Jerusalem Talmud, that the statement must be made immediately. I am surprised that the practical authorities omitted this requirement of immediacy here. In Even HaEzer siman 17, Rama rules regarding innocent speech by minors that it is effective only immediately, such as when they say, "We have just come from the eulogy of so-and-so"; if time has passed, their statement does not suffice. Shitah Mekubetzet likewise cites the Jerusalem Talmud that the rule applies only on the spot. If the children left and later returned, we suspect that they spoke from fear or persuasion. Rashba also indicates that the minor must already be of an age capable of independent movement and observation. Terumat HaDeshen, however, cites Or Zarua that the requirement of immediacy is disputed by Raavan and Raavyah, with Raavyah validating even after time has passed. In a Torah matter we would follow the stricter position. Here, however, the entire matter is an unresolved monetary doubt in a rabbinic acquisition. Therefore, where neither party has stronger proof, the rule can be that whoever is stronger prevails, as Rama states at the end of siman 139.
