An incident involving one who said to his agent. The Tur cites Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia as limiting Isi's rule, "Where witnesses are available, do not rely on an oath," to custodial oaths. He explains the episode of the four hundred barrels of wine as a deposit: the principal had deposited money with the agent and instructed him to use those deposited funds to buy wine. The agent later claimed he bought wine and it soured. Beit Yosef argues that even if the original money was a deposit, the resulting oath seems not to be a classic custodial oath and therefore the episode proves Isi's rule applies more broadly. In my view there is substantial reason to classify it as a custodial oath. If the principal suspects that the agent never bought the wine at all, the claim is that the deposited money may still be in his hands. Alternatively, if he bought vinegar or otherwise deviated from the instruction, the issue can become one of negligence or of an oath that the deposit is no longer in his possession. There is, however, another way to understand Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia according to his own principles. He holds elsewhere that if a person tells his agent, "Go borrow together with my cow," the agent's presence can count as the owner's presence because an agent is like the principal. On that theory, an ordinary agent holding property during the agency might be regarded as guarding while the owner is with him, which removes the ordinary custodial oath. The four hundred barrel episode would therefore need to involve money that had already been deposited before the agency began. Since the deposit was taken before the principal became "with him" through agency, the original custodial obligation remains. A further possibility follows his law concerning a custodian who says, "The garment that was lost was yours." If witnesses establish that a garment exactly like the one claimed was lost, the resulting dispute over identity may not require a custodial oath. But if deposited money remains in question, then the agent must still swear concerning the money itself. This may explain why Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia insists on construing the episode as one involving a prior deposit. The matter nevertheless requires further study.
