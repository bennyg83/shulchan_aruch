He must bring proof that the wine was not vinegar. Beit Yosef asks that this is not a custodian's oath. I am surprised. Even if the principal admitted that the agent bought good wine, once the case assumes the wine was deposited with the agent and was later found to be vinegar, the agent would certainly have to take a custodian's oath that it soured through ones and not through negligence. Likewise, if the principal could not identify the particular wine as his, the agent would have to swear the ordinary custodian's oath that the wine is not still in his possession. Perhaps the good wine purchased for the principal remains in the agent's hands and the vinegar produced now is old vinegar belonging to the agent himself, meaning nothing entrusted to him was ever lost at all. This differs from siman 294, where it is known that a particular cloak was lost through ones and therefore a custodian's oath may not be needed. There at least it is known that some entrusted object suffered the loss. Here it is not known that anything belonging to the principal was lost while in the agent's custody. The agent must therefore swear that the principal's wine is not in his possession and that he was not negligent. By gilgul he must also swear that the wine was not vinegar from the outset. Moreover, four hundred barrels of wine souring creates a public report. That widespread report functions like witnesses that the agent did not suffer an otherwise unknown custody loss. If such an enormous accident had occurred in his own property too, it would have become known. He therefore cannot rely on a custodian's oath and must pay. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that the oath is d'oraisa because the principal suspects that the agent never purchased wine with the deposited money and perhaps the deposit itself is still in his possession, creating the oath that it is not in his possession. This does not appear correct. If so, in the case of a shopkeeper on his ledger, where the employer advanced money and the shopkeeper says that he delivered goods to the workers, siman 91 says the shopkeeper takes only a heset oath. Yet there too the employer could suspect that he never delivered the goods and still holds the money. Evidently, a claim that one delivered funds or goods to the person designated by the owner is legally like a claim of return. Ketzot further writes that if the agent is suspected of buying vinegar, this itself is like an oath that he was not negligent. That too is incorrect. Wine and vinegar are two distinct types of item. If he bought vinegar when instructed to buy wine, he did not merely guard negligently. He failed to fulfill the agency at all and is treated as one who caused damage.
