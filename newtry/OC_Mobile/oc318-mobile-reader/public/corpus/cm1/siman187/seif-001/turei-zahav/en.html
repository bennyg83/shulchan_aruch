Seif 3, in the gloss: Specifically regarding a guardian's claim. These are the Rema's words as cited by Rabbeinu, but their explanation requires study. The Gemara's case concerns one who told his fellow to buy four hundred barrels of wine. The agent later said that the wine had soured. Rava ruled that such widespread souring would be publicly known, so the agent must bring proof that the wine was sound when purchased. The difficulty is that the oath he would otherwise take is not a standard guardian's oath, but an oath that the wine was sound at the time of purchase. Calling every oath arising from custody a guardian's oath would also make an oath of return in a deposit comparable to repayment, which it is not. The correct distinction appears to be this. When exemption depends on an unintended event, such as unavoidable loss, it is unreasonable to claim that the event occurred without witnesses, and proof may be required. When exemption depends on a human act, such as repayment or return, people often act privately, so an oath may suffice. In the wine case, it initially appears that the agent was appointed only to buy the wine, after which the principal would collect it. The agent would not be a guardian at all, and even negligent souring would not be his responsibility. His defense would simply be that he fulfilled the agency by buying good wine. Why then did Rava demand proof rather than allow an oath? The answer is that the wine remained a deposit in his hands. He therefore had a duty to attend to it, and if four hundred barrels soured, the event should have become known. The absence of public knowledge undermines his claim and requires proof that the wine was sound at purchase. The Gemara need not expressly state that it was a deposit. The wording implies that the agent already held the principal's money, and the purchased wine automatically replaced the deposited money as a deposit. The same rule would apply if the principal gave him money now and expressly left the wine in his custody after purchase.
