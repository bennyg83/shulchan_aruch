The same law applies to the claims of custodians, etc. This is the Rambam's language. It is somewhat difficult, because regarding custodians this rule is obvious, since the verse "there is no witness" itself was written about custodians. See the Derishah.
Specifically regarding custodians' claims, etc. The Tur, after citing the Rambam's rule, brings the Ramah, who says that this principle applies only to the oaths of custodians. In ordinary claims such as payment or return, it does not apply. The Ramah therefore explains the incident of the four hundred barrels of wine as a case that was a deposit from beginning to end: the owner gave the custodian money to purchase wine for him, the custodian later said that he had purchased it, and the wine remained deposited with him until it soured. Since the Ramah was compelled to describe the case as a deposit from beginning to end, it follows that in his view if the owner merely gave the other person money to purchase wine and the latter swore that he bought good wine and it later soured, he could be discharged by oath even in a place where witnesses were normally available. The reason is that specifically with custodians and the classic claims of theft, loss, or unavoidable accident, events that occur outside the owner's presence, the custodian is expected to create publicity and report what happened. By contrast, most ordinary claims involve repayment or return, which usually occur privately between the parties, and a person does not suspect that his fellow will deny the event and therefore has no reason to publicize it. The Sages therefore did not distinguish even where an unusual noncustodian claim arose in which publicity might have been expected, such as wine souring. The Mechaber rules like the Rambam and extends the rule also to agents and partners. It is difficult that Rama places the Ramah's words after the Rambam as though there were no dispute. Some emend Rama's text to read, "And some say, specifically regarding custodians." That is strained. Rather, Rama may understand that the Rambam too agrees regarding ordinary claims of payment and return, since such acts are normally private. He inferred this from the Rambam's phrase, "and all similar cases," following the incident of the barrels of wine, implying cases comparable to that one rather than every payment or return claim. The Ramah adds that even within custodial contexts the special rule applies specifically to true custodians. Since most ordinary disputes involve private payment and return, the Sages did not extend the rule generally. Thus he limits the incident of the barrels to a case in which the money and wine were actually held as a deposit. This is where he disagrees with the Rambam. See the Perishah and Derishah.
