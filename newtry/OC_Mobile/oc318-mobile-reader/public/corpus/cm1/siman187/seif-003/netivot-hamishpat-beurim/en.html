The same applies to a custodian's claim. See Sema, seif katan 4, who asks that this is obvious, since the verse concerning custodians itself says, "There is no witness, then the oath of Hashem shall be between them." The answer appears as follows. At first Mechaber states the rule concerning the custodian's oath, meaning where the custodian is actually obligated to swear. In that situation, if there were witnesses available, he must pay unless he brings them, because Scripture expressly limited the oath to where there is no witness. Then, in seif 3, Mechaber adds that the same rule applies to any factual claim falling within the category of custodian defenses even in a case where no oath would otherwise be required. For example, if the depositor makes a definite claim, Shach in simanim 72 and 92 holds that the custodian may sometimes be exempt from the special custodian's oath. Even according to Tumim, who disagrees and generally requires a custodian's oath against a definite claim, where the custodian has a migo he can be exempt. The reason a migo ordinarily does not help a custodian avoid the oath is that we do not use a migo involving brazenness to exempt from an oath. But where the depositor already makes a definite allegation, the custodian is already being brazen in his present claim, so the migo is not a greater act of brazenness and can operate. One might therefore have thought that where the custodian is legally believed without an oath, he should remain exempt even if witnesses were available. Mechaber teaches otherwise. Whenever the defense is substantively a custodian defense, if the event supposedly occurred in a place where witnesses were available, the custodian must produce them. This is true even where no actual custodian's oath would otherwise be imposed. Beit Yosef goes even further in the case of four hundred barrels of wine. Even where there is no custodian's oath as such, because the obligation arose through the custody relationship, the defendant must produce available proof.
But regarding a claim of payment and return. The distinction appears to be this. A claim of ones normally concerns an event that occurs outside the owner's presence. Therefore, if the custodian says the event occurred in a place where witnesses were available, we treat it as though witnesses should exist. Had the object truly been lost through ones in his hands, he would have immediately brought those witnesses to beit din, both to avoid suspicion and to avoid the oath. Payment is different. If we adopted the same reasoning, then every debtor could always be told, "You should have paid before witnesses." That would effectively destroy the entire rule that a debtor may pay privately. A debtor may say: "I paid without witnesses because I did not suspect that the creditor would deny receiving payment in my face." Therefore, even if he paid in a place where people were present, we do not say that he was obligated to gather those witnesses and bring them immediately to beit din. He had no reason at the time to anticipate denial. The same applies to return of entrusted property. The custodian may reasonably return the object privately and trust that the owner will not later deny receiving it. The special rule requiring available witnesses therefore concerns an alleged external custody accident, not ordinary voluntary payment or return.
