And the owners despaired. Raavad appears to hold that despair can acquire a slave. He proves that slaves can be stolen where they are no longer before us, from the rule that a captive slave who was redeemed for freedom after the master's despair is not re-enslaved to either the first or second master. If the slave can acquire himself through despair, he is not entirely like land. Therefore, if the slave later dies or is burned, the robber pays his value. The Shach accepts Raavad's logic and connects it with Tosafot's rule that just as land cannot be stolen, despair does not acquire land. Rashba disagrees and says that despair does not acquire a slave, just as it does not acquire land. The verse concerning recaptured captives is explained instead through the special acquisition of conquest in war, which can operate even on land. This raises a parallel question concerning debt instruments. They too are not subject to ordinary robbery. One would therefore expect despair not to acquire them. Yet Tosafot in Bava Batra 76 explicitly says that a debt instrument can be acquired through despair, discussing one who found a document after the owner's despair and sold it back to the owner at an inflated price. The distinction is that the connection between robbery and despair applies specifically to despair arising from robbery. A slave cannot be acquired through the robber's despair mechanism if he is not legally subject to robbery. But despair of lost property can operate differently. Thus a runaway slave can acquire himself through despair in the lost-property sense, and the same applies to debt instruments. Tosafot's case concerns a lost document, not a stolen one. Kapot Temarim argues that land can never be acquired through despair, even outside robbery, such as where an owner leaves the city and despairs of his land. That is because land stands permanently in its place. Slaves are different. Their inability to be acquired through despair is tied specifically to the law of robbery, not to a universal rule that all despair is ineffective for them. The passage in Niddah concerning a runaway slave supports this distinction. Kapot Temarim also cites Maharashdam's difficulty from the law of *sikarikon*. We rule that land is not stolen and ordinarily cannot be acquired through despair, yet in places with an effective governmental court system despair can matter. My brother, Rabbi Yehudah Kohen, explained from Rivash that there the oppressor did not simply steal the land. He pressured the owner through another threat until the owner himself said, in effect, "Take my land and leave me alone." Ordinarily, a coerced gift is ineffective. But where an accessible authority exists before which the owner could readily complain and he nevertheless remains silent, that silence shows genuine consent under the coercive circumstances. Rashba likewise explains that in such a case the land returns without payment where it was a coerced gift, but in Babylonia, where an available public authority existed to remedy seizures, continued silence can demonstrate waiver. Thus the *sikarikon* case is not proof that ordinary despair acquires stolen land. The acquisition rests on the owner's own coerced but ultimately accepted transfer. This also explains Tur's formulation in siman 236 that the oppressor "came upon him indirectly": because of that pressure the owner voluntarily transferred the land. If the land were simply stolen, no amount of despair would transfer it.
