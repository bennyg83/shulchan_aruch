And the owners despaired. See Shach, seif katan 2, who cites Tosafot and Rosh that ye'ush can apply even to land, based on the Yerushalmi. Tosafot in Bava Metzia and Sukkah discuss this at length. At first glance there are several major difficulties. The Bavli appears to say that land is not acquired through ye'ush, while the Yerushalmi seems to say it can be. Why should ye'ush apply to land at all merely because land is excluded from ordinary robbery law? Ye'ush derives from lost property, and there is no explicit exclusion of land, slaves, or documents from ye'ush itself. Shach also alludes in siman 236 to the question from a place with government courts: the sources say the owner clearly despaired, yet ordinary land supposedly does not pass through ye'ush. Terumat HaDeshen likewise explains the case of one who lost a path through his field, where a single person comes through rights of four different predecessors, as based on ye'ush. Again, why should ye'ush work on land? Moreover, even if the Yerushalmi recognizes ye'ush in land, why should ye'ush alone acquire without a change of possession? With movables, ye'ush alone ordinarily does not acquire stolen property. In my view the key is Ramban's explanation in Milchamot in the chapter Eilu Metziot. Ramban writes that ye'ush cannot operate on property still legally in the owner's possession. Where a lost object was taken before ye'ush by someone intending to return it, that finder becomes the owner's custodian. The object is treated as legally in the owner's domain, and later ye'ush does not help. This explains land. With ordinary stolen movables, the object immediately leaves the victim's legal possession. Before ye'ush, neither victim nor thief can ordinarily consecrate it: one because it is not his, the other because it is not in his possession. Therefore ye'ush can later operate. Land, however, is not stolen in that legal sense. The victim can still consecrate the land even while another occupies it. The land remains legally in his possession. As long as that is true, ye'ush does not operate. But where the owner cannot recover the land through beit din, for example because he lacks witnesses, the land can become legally outside his control. Bava Metzia 7 says that property one cannot recover judicially cannot be consecrated. Once it is no longer in his effective legal possession, ye'ush can operate. This explains Terumat HaDeshen's case of a path whose exact location is lost among claims deriving from four different owners. The claimant cannot recover any defined path through beit din. The land is therefore outside his legal control, and ye'ush can work. It also explains the case of government courts. If those courts exist and the owner nevertheless does not recover the land, that can demonstrate he has despaired precisely because he believes he cannot obtain it even there. The property is then judicially unrecoverable and legally outside his control. The Yerushalmi can be speaking of such a case, where the owner's name has disappeared and he cannot establish a claim. Why does ye'ush alone suffice there without an additional change of possession? Because the reason ye'ush alone ordinarily fails with stolen movables is that the robber received them through prohibition and already became obligated in restitution. The object remains needed for that obligation. Land is different. Land is not acquired by robbery and the occupier does not incur the same restitution duty. Thus once the land is outside the true owner's legal control and he despairs, the obstacle that prevents ye'ush alone from acquiring stolen movables is absent. All the more so, if another person later takes possession after ye'ush, that person can acquire. Tosafot in Bava Kamma say that even where ye'ush alone does not acquire for the robber, another person who subsequently takes can acquire in principle, except where the object remains needed for the robber's restitution duty. With land there is no comparable need. The same reasoning extends to slaves. A slave can genuinely become a lost object, unlike land, which always remains in place. If a slave runs away and the owner despairs, the verse concerning every lost item can include him. There is therefore no reason to say ye'ush cannot operate. Likewise, if a slave is robbed and thereby becomes effectively lost to the owner, ye'ush can operate more like movables than like land because the owner's practical control has ended. Documents can similarly be subject to ye'ush as to the physical document itself when lost, as explained above in siman 66.
