If the shop was established first, the Gemara challenges Rava, who holds that one may not place a potential nuisance beside the boundary even before the injured use exists, and answers, "A dwelling is different." Rashi explains that we do not prohibit a person's ordinary residential use while there is still no object being harmed. Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash explains differently: a person does not ordinarily convert his dwelling into a storeroom, and even under the first version, which considers that one may later change one's plans, such a change is not ordinarily contemplated. Ramban objects to that explanation from the Jerusalem Talmud, which says a dwelling is all the more protected than a storeroom. Yet Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash's approach can still be understood in the Gemara's unresolved questions concerning one who swept and sprinkled the area in preparation for a storeroom, enlarged the windows, or made a portico beneath the house. Those acts plainly reveal an intention to make a storeroom. However, Abaye is the one who raised these questions, and according to Abaye one may ordinarily place a potential nuisance before the injured object exists. His question was whether such preparations already count as the injured object being present, or whether mere preparation is not legally significant. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua later asks about dates and pomegranates. Since his question is appended to Abaye's series, it too can be understood as being asked according to Abaye. This explains why Rif omitted these unresolved questions. We rule like Rava, who generally forbids preliminary placement even before the injured object exists. The exception of a cattle stall preceding a storeroom can then rest on Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash's reason that an ordinary dwelling is not expected to be converted into a storeroom. There is, however, another possible way to understand Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash. Regarding an orchard and vines, he holds that if a field is not normally planted with vines, the owner cannot later say, "I now intend to plant vines," and thereby force his neighbor to distance a tree. If so, even according to our law, the questions concerning sweeping and sprinkling could matter. If such preparation still does not make the place legally a storeroom, the owner may not be able to force the neighbor to distance merely because he says he will convert an ordinary residence into a storeroom. Only if the storeroom itself is considered to have preceded would the neighbor have to distance. This may indeed be why Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash formulated his position regarding vines as he did. Rif, however, who omitted these questions, may reject that rule concerning an atypical future use, as Ramban does regarding vines. He can still accept Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash's explanation of the cattle stall case. On that reading, the unresolved questions were simply Abaye's and were omitted because the halachah follows Rava.
