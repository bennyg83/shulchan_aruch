Ibid., Shach, seif katan 9, concerning the Mordechai. Note: this requires study. It appears that Rabbi Meir's reason there is that one who was ten years old when the mound was formed would know what occurred, and most people live until seventy. Since no one is found who remembers a stillborn child having been buried in those mounds, we rely on the absence of such memory. Rabbi Yehudah is even more lenient: even if there is one exceptionally old person who remembers the mound's formation but recalls no impurity, the mound is deemed pure. Although the fact that he did not see impurity is ordinarily not proof that none occurred, Rabbi Yehudah is lenient because of the purity of Eretz Yisrael. See the Gemara there. Rabbi Meir, however, is stringent regarding "we did not see" as nonproof unless there are many people. See this principle in the Shach above, end of siman 37. If so, this has no connection to our case. Nevertheless, the Mordechai's citation from Maharam may be explained somewhat by saying that the cases are not alike in reasoning but share only the terminology of an old city and a new city. Yet it is unreasonable to rule here like Rabbi Yehudah, such that in two cities, if one happens to contain an extraordinarily old man aged one hundred twenty we would say it still requires the protection created by regular traffic, whereas in the other, perhaps because its inhabitants descend from Eli or because it suffered plague, Heaven forbid, no one remembers its construction and we would therefore say it does not require such traffic. I would reverse the conclusion. The proof of the alternative opinion cited by the Sema from Tzoar is therefore plausible.
