Rif explains the rule that one who maintained the required distance for a fire and nevertheless caused damage can be exempt as a case of an extraordinary heavenly event. He contrasts this with an oven, where the Sages give a required distance yet still make the owner pay if damage occurs. Rif says a person lights an ordinary temporary fire only as needed for the moment. If it was placed at the prescribed distance and nevertheless spread far beyond what was reasonably foreseeable, the event is unavoidable. With an oven, however, the owner uses it regularly and must continually inspect whether it is causing danger. If he fails to do so he is negligent and liable. Were it not for Rif, I would distinguish the cases more simply. The prescribed distance for an oven is only a minimum below which harmful contact is considered sufficiently certain that one may not operate it at all. Even at the proper four amot distance, however, it remains physically possible that the fire will spread and cause damage. Therefore the owner remains liable if it does. By contrast, in the case in Perek HaKones where one is said to have "distanced properly," the required distance is measured according to the size and nature of the fire so that, under ordinary conditions, it should be impossible for the fire to reach the neighbor's stack. If it nevertheless does, the event is a complete accident, a heavenly blow, and the person is exempt. Thus the difference lies in what the prescribed distance represents: for the oven it is only the threshold below which the risk is impermissibly high, while in the other fire case it is a distance that should ordinarily eliminate the danger entirely.
