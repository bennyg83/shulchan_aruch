Rama explains the rule as involving parties who acquired the land from the king, following Maggid Mishneh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Nimukei Yosef. Maggid Mishneh says some ask why Reuven's land should become subjected to Shimon merely because Shimon built a wall. They therefore explain that the Mishnah speaks specifically of buyers from the king. The king's ordinary grant is understood to include four cubits outside the wall for foot traffic that strengthens the wall, and the later purchaser from the king must leave those four cubits because they were already subjected to the first purchaser. Hagahot Maimoniyot cites Ritzba similarly. However, our Tosafot appears to assume the rule even where the parties did not purchase from the king and the land was not previously subjected. Tosafot asks, according to Rava, how the first person could have placed his wall close to the boundary at all, and answers that the wall itself does not directly harm the neighbor but merely reduces beneficial foot traffic, so initial placement is permitted. Ramban says likewise that so long as the neighboring wall does not exist, the first owner merely prevents a possible future benefit rather than causing present harm. Had they assumed the rule only where a prior servitude had already been acquired from the king or from ownerless land, their question would not arise. Still, the conceptual problem remains. Why should one person's wall automatically gain a right to four cubits of his neighbor's ground for foot traffic? A person who opens a high window does not thereby force his neighbor to leave four cubits of courtyard empty merely to provide him light. Rashba explains the difference between a storeroom and a window. A storeroom exists wholly within its owner's property. The neighbor must refrain from causing harm, not because his land has become subject to the storeroom but because he may not damage another. A window, however, uses the airspace of the neighbor's courtyard, and one cannot acquire such use of another's property without consent. The foot traffic required to strengthen a wall appears more like the window. The wall owner is effectively using the neighbor's land. Therefore, in my view, Rama is correct that the rule requires some prior legal right, such as purchase from the king, acquisition from ownerless property, or an established presumption. Even where the owner acquires from ownerless land, however, he does not acquire the body of the neighboring four cubits. He acquires only a servitude for the needed foot traffic. This is proven by the rule that a wall may be built from the side where it does not reduce the foot traffic. If the first owner had acquired full ownership of the four cubits, the second could not build there at all. Thus the acquisition is only a right of use for strengthening the wall, not ownership of the soil itself.
