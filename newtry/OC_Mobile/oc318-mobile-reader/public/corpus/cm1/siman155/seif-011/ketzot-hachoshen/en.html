The sugya of mustard and bees generated several distinct approaches among the early authorities. The Gemara first asks, regarding the Sages who require mustard to be distanced from bees, whether the mustard owner had already placed his mustard. If he had not, where does Rabbi Yosei's permission arise? Rav Pappa answers with a case of purchase. Ravina then explains the dispute in terms of who must distance a nuisance. Rashi and Tosafot understand the initial stage as follows. According to Abaye, who allows the first party to place a potential nuisance before the injured use exists, the case is where the bees were there first. Even if at that stage the Sages also thought bees and mustard damage each other, they could still require the second comer, the mustard owner, to distance fully. Rabbi Yosei could argue that because the bees themselves also harm the mustard, the first owner acted at least somewhat improperly and the mustard owner may therefore place his mustard without distancing. It is not like a cistern, where every shovel immediately loosens the adjacent soil. According to other commentators, even the view that permits initial placement gives the first party only the benefit of remaining in place. The second party need distance only half the required measure. Under that understanding, the Sages' rule would mean half distance, while Rabbi Yosei's permission would allow the mustard owner to place fully at the boundary. Ramban explains that because bees also damage mustard, each side would ordinarily bear half the distance, but if half distance is useless because damage still occurs, Rabbi Yosei allows complete placement rather than imposing an ineffective half measure. Under Rashi and Tosafot, where a nuisance was placed lawfully and the injured use later appeared, lawful priority does not generally preserve the nuisance. The later nuisance rules can require removal, except for a tree because of its great loss, or, according to Ramban, because of settlement of Eretz Yisrael. This raises a difficulty. Abaye says the first party may place the nuisance and, when the injured use later appears, the first party does not need to move. Likewise, according to Rava, if a cattle stall precedes a storeroom it may remain. Why does lawful priority help there but not in the case of a purchaser? The answer is that Abaye's rule is not based upon the general doctrine that an act done permissibly can never later be removed. Rather, Abaye defines the law of neighboring nuisances itself: there is no duty of distancing until an injured use presently exists. If the nuisance was placed before there was any injured party, the later owner must protect himself. Rava rejects that general priority rule and normally requires the first party to anticipate the later injured use. Therefore, for Rava, prior lawful placement by itself does not usually help. The cattle stall beneath a future storeroom is a special exception because even Rava agrees that ordinary residential use cannot be prohibited before a storeroom exists. In that particular category, the legal structure resembles Abaye's general approach. When the owner later chooses to create a storeroom, he must protect himself from the existing lawful residential use. A further difficulty arises from a field not ordinarily used for cisterns, where even Rava permits the first owner to place a nuisance. If the second owner later digs a cistern, why should the first not then have to distance, since prior lawful placement generally does not help under Rava? According to Tosafot's view that where both uses harm one another and the first was established lawfully, the second must provide the entire distance, the answer is straightforward. In a field not ordinarily used for cisterns, the first owner acted fully lawfully. If the second later digs a cistern, he must take the entire distance, and once he does so no damage remains. According to authorities who say that even when initial placement was permitted the second owner need distance only half, the question remains, because some damage would continue. One can answer that where the first placement was wholly lawful, as with a purchaser, and both uses damage one another, the second owner must indeed take the whole distance. The rule that the second owner gains only half applies where the first act was technically allowed but still somewhat improper. Still, a field not ordinarily used for cisterns may not be exactly like a purchaser. Perhaps its initial permission is weaker. Therefore the stronger answer is the rule of Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash regarding atypical future uses: where a field is not ordinarily used for cisterns, the second owner cannot later create the atypical use and force the first owner to move. The same principle explains his position regarding vines and the cattle stall beneath an ordinary residence. According to Ramban, who rejects Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash's rule regarding vines, the case can instead be treated as one of fully lawful prior placement that shifts the entire distancing burden to the later injured use.
