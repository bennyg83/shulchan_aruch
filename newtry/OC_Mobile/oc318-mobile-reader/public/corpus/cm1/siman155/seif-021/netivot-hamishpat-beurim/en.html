And some disagree with this. Tur indeed cites this view in the name of Rashi and Rif, but Beit Yosef questions it and says that Tosafot write that this applies only according to the Sages. Pnei Yehoshua, in a responsum in Choshen Mishpat, question 8, objects that R. Yose also agrees in a case of direct arrows of damage. He overlooked Tosafot in Bava Batra 17, who expressly write that the question concerning olive refuse applies only according to the Sages and not according to R. Yose. Although Tosafot later, on Bava Batra 22, write that all the cases are treated as direct arrows of damage, that does not prove that according to R. Yose one must distance even when the vulnerable object does not yet exist. Tosafot's point is that when the damage exists at the time the damaging act is performed, it may be considered direct arrows. There is likewise no proof from Tosafot on Bava Batra 18, who cite Rashi's explanation of the Gemara's question according to Rava, "If he did not adjoin it, how can the case occur?" The Gemara establishes the case as one of purchasers. One might argue that if Rashi held that according to R. Yose one may adjoin the damaging use before the vulnerable object exists, the Gemara could have answered without resorting to purchasers. The Sages hold that bees do not damage mustard, so the mustard owner must distance himself. R. Yose could hold that one may adjoin the mustard where there were no bees yet and that bees themselves also cause damage, so the mustard owner would not later have to distance because the bee owner acted improperly by coming second, as Tosafot explain according to Abaye. It might then seem that Rashi must disagree with Tosafot and hold that according to Rava even R. Yose forbids adjoining the damaging use before the vulnerable object exists. This is not a proof. In the Gemara's initial assumption it had not yet considered the later question from a pit and a tree. At that stage it could have assumed that R. Yose agreed with the Sages and did not require direct arrows of damage. If so, according to that initial understanding R. Yose too would require distancing even when the vulnerable object was absent, just like the Sages. From the conclusion there is likewise no proof. The Gemara ultimately says that R. Yose spoke according to the Sages' own reasoning, because of the difficulty from steeped flax and vegetables, which are not direct arrows even when the vulnerable object is already present. Once R. Yose is answering according to the Sages' framework, the Gemara must establish the mustard case as involving purchasers. Without purchasers, the original problem returns, because each party should distance half the required distance. The same must be said according to Rabbeinu Chananel's interpretation cited by Tosafot that, according to Ravina, Rava ultimately retracted and held that even according to the Sages one need not distance when the vulnerable object is not yet present. At first glance this is surprising, because according to the Sages, who hold that bees do not damage mustard, there was no need to retract anything. The only difficulty concerned R. Yose. One might have expected Tosafot to say merely that according to R. Yose one may adjoin before the vulnerable object exists. Necessarily Tosafot needed to formulate the conclusion in terms of the Sages because the final sugya proceeds on the basis that R. Yose was addressing the Sages according to their own reasoning. The same answer is therefore available for Rashi. There is no proof from his words that he disagrees with Tosafot on page 17. Likewise, Tur's statement that according to Rashi and Rif even one who originally adjoined lawfully, such as a purchaser, must later distance, does not prove that this is the rule according to R. Yose. In the sugya itself that rule was said according to the Sages, and according to the initial understanding of R. Yose before the Gemara concluded that R. Yose requires direct arrows. Once we reach the conclusion that the halachah follows R. Yose and that only direct arrows are actionable, it is entirely possible that one who originally adjoined lawfully need not later distance, because his conduct is no longer considered direct arrows. This is no worse than one who adjoined before the vulnerable object existed, which Tosafot on page 17 do not treat as direct arrows. Rif actually proves the opposite. Ramban, explaining the Gemara's principal question according to Rif, says that according to R. Yose, who permits mustard, Rava's view should require the bee owner to distance himself. This difficulty forced the Gemara to establish the case as one of purchasers. Rif then writes that even in the mustard Mishnah the halachah follows R. Yose: since the case is established as one of purchasers and neither came first, whether one reasons like the Sages that bees do not damage mustard or otherwise, the halachah still follows R. Yose because it is not the mustard owner's direct arrows. The wording implies that according to Rif, even if mustard were otherwise considered a direct damaging cause, once neither party preceded the other through wrongdoing, the situation is no longer treated as direct arrows. The person who adjoined lawfully is therefore not treated as directly damaging. There is a parenthetical gloss in the Alfasi edition in the name of Maharash Shapira deleting the words "because it is not his direct arrows." With respect, that emendation is mistaken. Rif is trying to prove that the halachah follows R. Yose. If those words are deleted, the proof would be merely that neither came first, but the Sages themselves also address a case where neither came first and still require distancing. The text as printed is therefore correct. Even Ramban, who disagrees with Rif and holds that the bee owner must distance himself, can be understood as agreeing that in other types of damage one who originally adjoined lawfully need not distance. Bees may be exceptional because they are comparable to smoke, a continuously renewed damaging force. Likewise, when Ramban writes regarding two purchasers that the one causing damage must distance, he may have said so only according to the Sages and according to the initial understanding of R. Yose. According to the final halachah that R. Yose requires direct arrows, prior lawful proximity is not treated as direct arrows and therefore is permitted, except for damages actively renewed each day, such as pounding grain, or damages that never acquire chazakah, such as smoke. Those must be distanced even when originally established lawfully. Concerning advancing claims for a purchaser, Tosafot cite Rabbeinu Chananel, who explains the purchaser case as one where a person bought a tree from another, and beit din advances the seller's claim for the purchaser. Tosafot object that if so, even where the pit preceded the tree the purchaser should also be protected. They further ask that because beit din advances a claim for a purchaser only after three years of chazakah, the Gemara could instead have established the case without a purchaser but with a possessor who had three years and a supporting claim. Similar difficulties appear in Tosafot later regarding their interpretation of a purchaser. In my view Rabbeinu Chananel can be understood consistently. The rule that beit din advances a seller's claim for a purchaser only after three years applies to something that ordinarily requires a kinyan and a written deed, where the purchaser should have preserved evidence of title. But where the underlying neighbor right can be established immediately without three years, beit din certainly advances the seller's claim for the purchaser even without three years. When the one causing damage adjoined only after the vulnerable object already existed, then even if the injured owner expressly consented verbally, that consent is insufficient without a kinyan, as Tur writes at the end of this siman and Bach explains. The reason is that it resembles one who says, "Tear my garment and be exempt," who can retract even during the tearing. A kinyan is therefore required to strengthen the waiver and make it irrevocable. Since a formal kinyan is needed, beit din cannot advance for a purchaser without the usual three year chazakah that substitutes for documentary proof. By contrast, with a tree and a pit, even according to the view that the one causing damage must distance himself, if the tree was originally planted lawfully before the pit existed and the future pit owner expressly consented to the planting, there is no stronger case of lawful prior proximity. Once the future pit owner later digs his pit, the tree need not be cut merely because of the later vulnerability, especially where cutting causes great loss. Mere consent is enough because at the time of planting there was no existing vulnerable use being injured. Since simple consent suffices and no formal kinyan is needed, beit din may advance for a purchaser even without three years the claim that the seller consented to the original planting. This resolves the case where the pit preceded the tree. There the vulnerable object already existed, so consent without a kinyan would be insufficient and beit din does not advance such a claim for the purchaser without three years. But where the tree came first, even if it was planted merely with the other owner's consent and without kinyan, the consent is enough to make the planting lawful and protect it later. Therefore beit din advances that claim for a purchaser even without three years. The same explains the case of mustard and bees. Suppose the mustard owner was the purchaser and the bees were placed afterward. The Sages hold that the one causing damage must distance himself even when he originally adjoined lawfully. Therefore, even if we advance for the mustard purchaser that the bee owner originally consented, and the mustard was thus established lawfully, the damaging mustard must still later be distanced. R. Yose, according to the initial understanding, holds that the injured party must distance himself where the damaging use was lawfully established first. Therefore the purchaser is protected by the advanced claim that the original placement was with consent. According to the final conclusion, where both uses cause damage and unlawful priority would matter, the same conceptual distinction remains. One further rule should be noted. Even according to the opinion that one who originally adjoined lawfully must later distance, that applies specifically where the lawful status arose through a purchaser transaction in which the seller and purchaser can be viewed as continuing one another's ownership. One can say that when the seller later creates the vulnerable use, he must distance from the damaging use he had previously established, or that a purchaser of the damaging use receives it without a right to cause future damage, as explained in siman 154, seif 28. But where a person lawfully adjoined a damaging use to ownerless property and another later acquired that ownerless property and created the vulnerable use, the original user certainly need not distance. Rivash in siman 322 says this, and siman 154, seif 21, proves it. Conversely, even according to the view that prior lawful proximity generally protects the one causing damage, there are cases such as siman 154, seif 16, concerning a wall between neighbors that later fell, where according to one opinion he must still distance. All the more so according to Ramban, who holds that where two purchasers are involved and the injured use was itself made lawfully, the damaging party must distance. These details depend on whether the later vulnerable use was itself created with full right and whether the damaging activity constitutes a continuously renewed direct harm.
