There is a scribal error in the cross reference, and it should refer to the rule later in this seif that he is exempt from payment. See also Shach in siman 418, seif katan 4, who cites Maggid Mishneh's question. Shach answers that here, in siman 155, the dust is carried contrary to the person's intention. He intends only that it fall to the ground nearby, not that it travel far. In the case of fire, however, the wind assists the fire in traveling in the same direction the person intended. This explanation is difficult. Why should it matter that the result was contrary to his intention? If one places his stone or knife on a rooftop intending only that it remain there, he is nevertheless liable when an ordinary wind blows it down and it causes damage. In my view, the broader structure of the laws of neighbor damage resolves the issue. Many of the distancing requirements listed in Lo Yachpor and codified by Rambam and Mechaber appear at first glance to involve the ordinary four primary categories of damages. Bees eat mustard, pigeons eat produce in fields, moisture travels and harms another's wall, and roots from one's tree enter another's property. Why are these not treated as ordinary ox, pit, or fire damages? The implication of the chapter is that even where distancing is required, one who failed to distance is often exempt from payment after the fact. Why should ordinary damage liability not apply? The answer must be that the Torah imposes liability for the four primary damages only where the damaging object can practically be retained within one's own property through ordinary guarding. In such a case the Torah obligated the owner to guard it, and failure to guard results in payment. The neighbor damage cases in Lo Yachpor are different. They concern uses that cannot realistically be maintained at all if the user must guarantee that no harm ever reaches the neighbor. The only way to avoid harm would be to abolish the use entirely from his property. The Torah did not impose a duty of guarding that amounts to surrendering the use of one's property altogether. This is proved from Bava Batra 23 concerning artisans, where the Gemara asks, "But did they not establish chazakah?" If ordinary chazakah could excuse a person from the duty to guard one of the four primary damages, that would be astonishing. Rashi's language there also indicates that in these cases the only way to prevent the damage is to remove the underlying use entirely. That is why R. Yose holds that the injured party must distance himself in many neighbor damage cases. Why should the one causing the damage forfeit the use of his property altogether when the injured party can protect himself by distancing? In ordinary tort cases, by contrast, the injured party often cannot predict when the damaging force will arrive, while the owner can guard his animal or property. The Torah therefore places the duty of guarding upon the owner. This explains why Tosafot in Bava Batra 26 ask specifically about sparks from a hammer as a form of fire, rather than asking about every damaging use in the chapter. Ordinary fire can be guarded without abolishing the underlying use. But sparks from hammering resemble the airborne chaff there: neither can be fully controlled except by abandoning the use. The two are therefore comparable. The same resolves the tree adjoining a pit. One might ask that roots emerging from one's tree are his property and should be like his ox or pit causing damage. But if liability would require him to guarantee that roots never travel, the practical result would be to forbid planting trees altogether near neighboring land. Since the neighbor can protect himself by proper distancing, the Torah did not impose ordinary tort liability in the same way. Accordingly, Shach's question from siman 418 falls away. Here, in siman 155, the relevant uses cannot be rendered entirely harmless by ordinary guarding without abolishing them from the owner's property, while the injured party can protect himself through distancing. The Torah therefore did not impose ordinary liability merely because wind helped carry the dust. In siman 418, by contrast, the case is ordinary fire damage, one of the four primary categories, where the owner can guard the fire without losing the right to use his property. There the Torah places the duty to guard on the person creating the fire, not on the injured party. Sparks from under a hammer are still liable even though avoiding every spark may also require major restriction, because the Gemara explains that the craftsman actively intends the force to travel outward and strikes with power so that it leaves his immediate domain. That active directional force makes it direct enough to impose liability. This distinction appears correct.
