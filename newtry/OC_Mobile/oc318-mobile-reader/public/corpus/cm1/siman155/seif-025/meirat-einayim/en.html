He must distance four cubits. The reason is that people ordinarily plowed around trees, and the length of the plow in Eretz Yisrael, which is mountainous, was four cubits, while outside Eretz Yisrael it was two cubits. Therefore one who comes to plant trees must distance them four cubits, even if he himself does not intend to plow around them or enter his neighbor's field with the plow. His neighbor, who already planted a tree within his own field, can object and say: I already established the right to plow around my tree, which stands at the edge of my field, and to bring my plow into your field as needed. See the Perishah. There I wrote that this appears to apply specifically where the neighbor's tree stands directly on the boundary. If it stands one or two cubits from the boundary, the person who now comes to plant in his own field need distance only the balance needed to complete four cubits. We assume that from the outset the parties effectively accepted that one would distance his tree from the boundary by that amount and the adjacent owner would supply the remainder, with the intervening area serving the plowing needs of both trees. This resembles what the Maggid Mishneh writes and Rama cites here, that when both come to plant simultaneously, each distances half the required measure.
And between vines and other trees. This applies specifically where one's neighbor has vines or a grain field and he comes to plant other trees beside them in his own field. In the reverse case he need distance only the ordinary spacing between tree and tree, because he is harming himself. This is evident from the Rosh and Tur. It appears, however, that this limitation follows specifically their explanation that the greater distance required by the Gemara is enough room for birds to fly, as in the alternative opinion cited by Rama. That rationale applies when one plants tall trees beside low vines or grain, but not in the reverse direction. According to the Rif and Rambam, who require only four cubits, it appears that they had a different rationale for the distance. Otherwise, Rabbi Yonah's words cited by the Tur in seif 36 imply that any meaningful flight path is at least eight cubits, and Rama's formulation of the Rambam followed by "some say" likewise indicates that the Rambam's four cubits are not based on bird flight. According to that different rationale, perhaps the same requirement applies in the reverse case as well. This requires study.
But if he plants a seed, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that according to the Rosh, who explains the direct force of the damage to be that while the birds stand on the tree they fly from there and settle upon the vine, there should be no distinction. Even one who plants from seed must distance it. This is straightforward. See Hagahot Darkei Moshe on this point.
