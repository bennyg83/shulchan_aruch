This applies to other forms of damage, except for four: smoke, the odor of a privy, dust and similar matter, and vibrations of the ground. None of these can acquire an established right. Even if the injured party remained silent for many years, he may later object and compel the other party to distance them. Rama: This is so even if the source of damage was established before the object or person subject to damage arrived. However, if both parties acquired ownerless property, the one who acted first acquires the right, and the one who comes afterward cannot object. The same applies to visual intrusion where a partition is required, such as a jointly owned courtyard that is subject to division. Either partner may compel the other to build a wall in the middle so that the other cannot see him while he uses his portion. Since they harm one another automatically, without any affirmative act, no established right arises. Even if they remained for many years without a partition, either may compel the other to build one whenever he wishes. These harms differ from other harms because a person's mind cannot tolerate them, and it is presumed that he does not waive a permanent injury of this kind. If, however, he made a formal act of acquisition confirming his waiver of these harms, or sold or granted the right to cause them, he may not retract.
