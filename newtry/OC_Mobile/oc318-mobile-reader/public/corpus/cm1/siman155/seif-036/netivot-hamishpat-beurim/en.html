Even if he placed it there before the vulnerable object came. See Sema, seif katan 49, who writes that Tur says this according to his own approach. According to that explanation Rama's words are difficult, because above in siman 154, seif 16, Mechaber rules in accordance with R. Yose and Rivash that one may object, and Rama does not gloss there to disagree. It is possible that Rama relied on Ramban's position cited there, that even where one can object, the use does not create chazakah. More likely, however, Rama is not discussing revocation of a previously acquired right at all. His point is that although many poskim in seif 19 permit one to adjoin a damaging use where no vulnerable object is present and then require the later vulnerable use to distance, smoke and a privy are different. Everyone agrees that even if they were originally placed lawfully, once the vulnerable use arrives the smoke or privy must be distanced. Beit Yosef writes this explicitly here. The reason is that smoke and a privy renew their damage continuously, similar to pounding grain, and therefore remain actionable despite their original lawful placement.
And if a kinyan was made from him. Meaning, if a kinyan was made confirming his waiver. Without a kinyan the waiver is ineffective, as explained by Tur. In my view the same is true of other neighbor damages according to the opinion that three year chazakah and a supporting claim are required. Rama in siman 153, seif 2, says that the possessor must come with a claim just as in chazakah of land, and as I wrote above in siman 146. This shows that the matter is treated like a property right and requires a kinyan. Rashbam in Bava Batra 58, concerning a water spout, writes that the owner "certainly agreed with him for money," and the poskim generally use language of a claim that he purchased the right. This implies that mere waiver is insufficient. The rationale given by Bach also supports this. Consent to ongoing damage resembles one who says, "Tear my garment and be exempt," who may retract. A kinyan strengthens the matter and prevents retraction. That rationale applies to every type of neighbor damage. Nor do we find in the Gemara any distinction between smoke and a privy and other damages except regarding chazakah. Smoke and a privy never acquire the evidentiary force of three years, while other damages may. There is no basis to invent a separate distinction that mere waiver without kinyan is effective for other damages but not for smoke and a privy. Therefore, whenever the vulnerable object already exists at the time the damaging use is established, mere waiver without kinyan does not suffice for any of these damages. All resemble "Tear my garment." Before the vulnerable object exists, however, if the future injured party expressly gives permission to establish the damaging use, the result is different. Even for smoke and a privy, such permission can be effective without kinyan. The permission then does not resemble "Tear my garment," because he is not authorizing present damage to something that exists. Rather, he is effectively saying that he will not later place a vulnerable use there in a manner that would require removal. Concerning that future choice, explicit permission can be effective. This is the distinction reflected in Rabbeinu Yonah, cited by Tur, who says that if the possessor claims that the owner sold him the right or gave him permission, the claim may be effective. If the permission was granted only after the vulnerable use already existed, then smoke and a privy certainly require a kinyan. Even if permission made the initial proximity lawful, smoke and a privy must be distanced once the owner retracts, because even prior lawful proximity does not protect them. Once the owner retracts, the situation again resembles one who said, "Tear my garment," and then withdrew consent. The same is true of other neighbor damages where the vulnerable use already existed. Although prior lawful proximity can sometimes protect other types of damage, Ramban in Milchamot explains that this is where the injured party later comes to establish his vulnerable use after the damaging use had already been lawfully established. The later injured use then comes without full right against the preexisting condition. But where both uses were already lawfully present and the injured owner merely tolerated the damaging activity, once he retracts the one causing damage must distance. His earlier permission without kinyan did not permanently transfer the neighbor right. This appears to me the correct framework for these laws.
