And they do not descend immediately. This is difficult because seif 20 states that when water penetrates only after accumulating, Reuven must remove the damage, whereas here the ruling permits a case in which the water pauses and only afterward falls. I later found that the Rosh addresses this question in responsa, rule 108, responsum 10. He answers that the case here is different because the upper resident cannot eliminate his use of water, since a person cannot reasonably live without water, while the lower resident can easily prevent the damage by making a small repair to the ceiling plaster. With a modest layer of plaster, the water will no longer descend but will be absorbed. The wording also indicates a very minor washing use. But where the lower resident cannot prevent the damage through a small repair and would have to incur substantial expense, the upper resident must remove the source of the damage even where the water first pauses and only later penetrates. The questions raised by the Sema and Shach in seif katan 3 will, with Hashem's help, be explained in siman 164.
And moist sand. See Shach, seif katan 4, who asks that since the damage of moist sand is caused by dampness, it should resemble an irrigation channel, which requires both distance and plastering with lime. His question is surprising. An irrigation channel also involves the reason that each stroke of the digging tool weakens the neighboring earth, as Tosafot write at the beginning of Perek Lo Yachpor. Moist sand, by contrast, plainly resembles olive refuse. Indeed, on page 19 the Gemara asks why moist sand is not listed together with olive refuse, and Rav Oshaya likewise counts moist sand with olive refuse, showing that their law is comparable. The same is true of urine, whose damage also comes from dampness, yet when there is no pit it does not require plastering with lime.
