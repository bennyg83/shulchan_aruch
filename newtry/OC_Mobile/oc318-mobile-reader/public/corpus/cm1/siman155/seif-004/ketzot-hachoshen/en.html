Seeds must be distanced. Taz asks why this differs from a tree beside a cistern, where the tree need not be distanced merely because its future roots will later cause harm. Here too the seeds do not damage until the roots develop and loosen the soil. Taz's proposed distinction is difficult to understand.
Ramban already addressed this difficulty in his novellae to Perek Lo Yachpor. He cites Rabbeinu Chananel, who explains briefly that all the nuisances listed in the Mishnah are direct forces because the damage is already present from the moment of placement. Only a tree is different, because when it is planted the roots that later cause harm do not yet exist. Ramban notes that this explanation is difficult for hand sown seeds, because the damaging roots also appear only later, which would seem analogous to a tree. He suggests that the seeds themselves, even before rooting, draw moisture from the soil and thereby loosen and dry it. Since this absorption begins naturally at the place where they are planted, the damaging force begins from the point of placement and can be considered his direct force. A cistern damaged by roots is different because the harm is not from absorption at the planting point, but from roots that later leap outward from place to place. Ramban is not entirely satisfied with this answer because even absorption develops only over time and is not literally produced by the person's immediate force. He therefore offers a broader principle. Lime and rocks are each like a coal placed beside a wall, even if the burning effect emerges later. Likewise, when one places something in a location whose very presence damages the neighboring soil, the nuisance can be prohibited even though the harm results gradually and through combined causes. It is not comparable to water that first collects and only afterward travels elsewhere. A smoldering coal that burns later and a seed that steadily draws moisture and dries the surrounding earth can both be treated as damage issuing from the place of placement.
