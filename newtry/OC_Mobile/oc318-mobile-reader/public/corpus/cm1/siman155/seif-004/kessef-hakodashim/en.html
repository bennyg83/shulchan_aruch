Seif 36. (And the odor of a privy.) A privy does not acquire a prescriptive right to continue causing harmful odor. The same principle applies generally to a genuinely foul odor. Still, the rule may apply primarily where the odor creates immediate bodily distress. Certain commercial or industrial smells may be disliked by some people but are not necessarily classified as the kind of harmful foul odor for which Torah law requires removal, and local practice may tolerate them. Where the smell is of the type that must be removed, a prescriptive right may not help even if there is no proven danger of weakness or bodily illness. On the other hand, a smell below that threshold may resemble a covered privy, where a prescriptive right can at least have some effect. The odor from making tallow candles should be evaluated by knowledgeable experts to determine whether it has the legal status of smoke or similarly severe nuisance. If neighbors regularly use a fixed privy over a period of time, there may be room to say that a prescriptive right can arise depending on the nature and degree of the odor.
