The injured party. The Sema writes that this requires study, because in siman 164, seif 1, the Rama writes that all repairs needed for the roof are the responsibility of the owner of the upper story. The Shach answers that one who studies Responsa Rivash, siman 507, will see that the Rama's words are correct. Here, even though the roof owner repairs the roof as required, rainwater will still descend upon that ceiling, because the case is one where there is no drain from the roof and the water necessarily comes onto the ceiling. He is therefore no longer negligent, and the injured party must make the repair. The Bach answers that the Rivash dealt with rainwater arriving without negligence by the upper-story owner, such as a driving rain, whereas in siman 164 the owner was negligent in failing to repair the roof. The moist material. Its moisture damages the wall even though it gives off no heat. Although the Gemara and poskim state that sand increases heat, that is only when something hot is insulated in it. When sand lies next to a wall, however, it is cold. So writes the Sema. The Shach writes: this is difficult in my eyes. According to the Mechaber, who follows the Rambam and rules stringently in the unresolved question concerning moisture damage, requiring both distance and plastering with lime, as stated in seif 10, wet sand is likewise moisture damage and resembles a water channel. Why, then, is it included among the cases that do not require both measures? The Tur too requires some study. The Rambam does not mention wet sand at all, but relies on the law of a water channel and the teaching of the Mishnah. The Taz writes that Tosafot imply that where the sun strikes the area near the wall, even dry sand must be kept at a distance, because once heated it increases the heat.
