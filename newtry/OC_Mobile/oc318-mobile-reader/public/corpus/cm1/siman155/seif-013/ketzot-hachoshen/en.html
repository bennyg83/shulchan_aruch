The third approach is Ramban's own. He understands Rav Pappa's purchaser case as one owner who divided the field between two purchasers, one taking the soaking pit and the other the vegetables. If the first buyer had acquired only the soaking pit before the other owner planted vegetables later, the first could say that his use was established lawfully and the later planter must bear the burden. But where each buyer acquired his use from the same seller, both uses came into existence lawfully and neither has a priority claim. The soaking pit now damages the vegetables and therefore must distance. The Gemara then asks whether Rabbi Yosei therefore holds that the injured party must distance himself, so that the lawful acquisition of the mustard protects it even though the bees too were lawfully established. If so, why would the soaking pit and vegetables differ? The answer is that Rabbi Yosei too agrees in principle that the damager distances. In the mustard case, however, both parties damage each other. Rabbi Yosei's statement is not initially that the mustard owner has an absolute substantive right to remain, but rather an argument against the Sages: if the bee owner is not distancing his harmful bees, why must the mustard owner alone distance? The Gemara later concludes more broadly that Rabbi Yosei really does place the burden on the injured party outside direct force cases, and that his argument to the Sages was framed on their own assumptions. Under Ramban's approach, lawful initial placement helps in ordinary nuisance cases, except where the injured use was itself simultaneously established lawfully. Ramban therefore says that if the seller sold the soaking pit but retained the vegetables, or sold the vegetables and retained the soaking pit, the result can depend on the principle that a seller sells generously. Where the harmful use was sold and the injured use retained, the buyer may receive the full benefit of the use and not have to distance. Ramban leaves some of these variations uncertain. Rabbeinu Yerucham states more decisively that where a person sells a house but retains the courtyard, the buyer's windows cannot later be blocked because a seller sells generously. The same should apply where he sells the soaking pit and retains the vegetables: the buyer receives the soaking pit with its ordinary use and need not later distance merely because the seller retained the injured use. This also explains the Gemara's statement that the tree case is one of a purchaser. Tosafot understands the purchased tree as being in the buyer's hands, while Rashi explains it as remaining in the seller's hands. Under Ramban's theory, if both the tree and cistern already existed in the seller's property and then one was sold, both uses have lawful standing and the damaging tree should ordinarily have to distance. The case in which "the tree preceded" must therefore mean that the tree had already been established before the other party's lawful objection arose. This resolves Tosafot's question why the Mishnah specifies that the tree preceded. One cannot simply say the cistern preceded but the tree had been planted lawfully, because if both uses already had equal lawful standing the tree owner would have to distance. Rashi's interpretation, where the tree remains with the seller, is especially natural because if the cistern had preceded, the seller who later created or retained the tree would have no buyer's generous grant to rely upon.
There is another reason to prefer Rashi's interpretation that the tree remains with the seller. Tosafot explains that "the tree preceded" can mean it preceded the cistern owner's objection. If the tree is in the purchaser's hands, the uncertainty whether the tree preceded can become a question whether it already existed while the property was still in the seller's domain or arose only after the buyer acquired. We have a general rule that where a doubt first arises in one person's domain after property has passed from another, the present holder bears the burden of proving that the relevant condition existed earlier. Tosafot in Chullin applies this to a borrowed garment found stained: because the garment itself remains the owner's property, the doubt is not treated as passing from one domain to another. But if the tree itself is now in the buyer's property, one would naturally presume that the questionable growth or planting occurred in the buyer's domain rather than in the seller's. Accordingly, under Ribam's interpretation that the tree belongs to the purchaser, the rule that in doubt the tree is not cut becomes difficult. Rashi avoids the problem by explaining that the tree remains with the seller. The relevant doubt then arises within one continuous domain, and the present possession supports leaving the tree in place.
