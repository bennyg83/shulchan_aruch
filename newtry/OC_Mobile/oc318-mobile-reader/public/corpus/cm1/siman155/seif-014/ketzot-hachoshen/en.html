The fourth approach is Rabbeinu Chananel's, as cited by Tosafot. He explains Rav Pappa's "purchaser" as a purchaser who has held the use for three years, so that beit din advances the predecessor's claim for him. Tosafot objected that the sugya does not appear to fit this, and Rabbeinu Tam asks why the Gemara would need a purchaser at all. It could simply discuss a person who himself held the use for three years with a direct purchase claim. The sugya can nevertheless be reconstructed according to Rabbeinu Chananel. Under the initial understanding, Abaye's view that preliminary placement is permitted would make the case simple: the bees were there first. The Sages could require the later mustard owner to distance, while Rabbi Yosei could say that because the bees also damage the mustard and the first placement was not entirely proper, the mustard owner need not distance. According to Rava, however, if preliminary placement is generally forbidden, where could such a case arise? Rav Pappa answers: through a purchaser who has held the use for three years. Because the Gemara does not initially specify whether the purchaser is the bee owner or mustard owner, it asks which side was purchased and why the respective authorities rule as they do. Ravina then clarifies that the purchaser is the bee owner. The key distinction is that, according to the Sages, the damager must distance even where the damage is not a direct force. Therefore a bee owner who had openly maintained the bees for three years acquired a presumption, because the neighbor could have protested during those years. Beit din can advance for the purchaser the claim that the right was bought. Once that right was lawfully acquired, the later mustard owner must distance. Rabbi Yosei, however, holds that outside direct force cases the injured party bears the burden. If the original bee owner was legally permitted to keep the bees from the beginning, the neighbor had no right to protest. Where there is no right to protest, there is no presumption. Thus the purchaser's three years prove nothing according to Rabbi Yosei. The bee owner cannot establish that the nuisance right was lawfully purchased. The mustard owner, claiming with certainty that no such right was sold, can therefore place his mustard. The Mishnah's rule about soaking pits and vegetables is then read as the Sages' ordinary rule requiring the damaging soaking pit to distance. Rabbi Yosei should, according to his own theory, allow the soaking pit as well because the later damage is not his direct force. The Gemara ultimately says that Rabbi Yosei's statement to the Sages about mustard was an argument within their framework. This also explains why Rabbeinu Chananel specifically needs a purchaser. If the bee owner himself claimed with certainty that he bought the nuisance right and had a full three year presumption, that definite claim could be used even to extract legal consequences from the mustard owner. A purchaser whose claim is only advanced for him by beit din can be protected in what he presently possesses, but we do not ordinarily advance a doubtful predecessor claim in order to extract from another possessor. Thus the purchaser can retain the bee use yet cannot force the mustard owner away. The fourth approach therefore fits the sugya if one carefully distinguishes between a purchaser's defensive presumption and an original possessor's certain claim.
This reconstruction also answers Rif's challenge to the Geon's view that soaking water and vegetables count as direct force according to Rabbi Yosei. Rif thought the sugya implies Rabbi Yosei permits the soaking pit beside vegetables, which would prove it is not direct force. Under Rabbeinu Chananel's reading, the Gemara's statement that Rabbi Yosei would permit the soaking pit refers to preliminary placement of the soaking pit before the vegetables exist. At that moment the damaging moisture has not yet reached a present injured object and therefore it is not his direct force, except in the special cistern case where each shovel immediately loosens the soil. Ravina's phrase, "The Sages hold the damager must distance," can be understood accordingly. The Sages require distance even from indirect neighboring damage. Rabbi Yosei requires direct force. There is also an important procedural point. Rosh says in a responsum that beit din does not advance a purchaser's predecessor claim in order to extract property. We advance claims for a purchaser when he is holding the property, not when his uncertain claim would remove another's possession. Maharash Alshikh similarly rules that even where the buyer or his seller had three years of possession, if the buyer is no longer in possession we do not advance a doubtful purchase claim to extract. Thus, even though the bee purchaser has three years, he cannot use that uncertain predecessor claim to compel the mustard owner to distance. He may use it only to maintain his own bees. Since the mustard owner says with certainty that no nuisance right was sold, and the mustard is on his own boundary, we cannot remove him based on the buyer's doubtful claim. Ordinarily, when two neighboring uses damage each other, each would have to take half the distance. But here one side cannot be compelled because his possession is protected by the purchaser's presumption, while the other side cannot be compelled because he makes a definite claim that the first use was never lawfully acquired. In the law of neighboring nuisances, where one harmful use is not being removed, the other side is likewise not always forced to distance alone. This is the force of Rabbi Yosei's language, "Just as you acted within your own, so I act within mine." This also explains why Rabbeinu Chananel cannot simply establish the case with the original possessor making his own direct claim. A certain claimant with a valid three year presumption can use it even to extract legal consequences. The special balance arises specifically with a purchaser whose claim is advanced only defensively. The same approach answers Tosafot's further question from the tree and cistern case. According to Rabbeinu Chananel, the purchaser language there likewise refers to the special presumption rules. If the original owner himself had a direct and certain claim of purchase, the result would differ. The purchaser formulation is therefore meaningful rather than unnecessary.
