He may cut and dig. The reason is that since the tree owner was permitted to adjoin the tree and his use does not create chazakah against the neighbor, there is no independent right to draw nourishment from the neighbor's soil. Even the concern that the roots draw nourishment from another's land is covered by the enactment of Yehoshua only so long as owners are not particular about it, as explained at the end of Lo Yachpor. Therefore, when the neighbor needs to dig a pit, he may remove his own soil so that the other person's tree no longer draws nourishment from it. This also explains why he may cut the roots even if the tree will dry out. It is not comparable to a pit that was lawfully placed first, where later digging near it may directly weaken the pit walls and each stroke causes damage. Here the tree does not dry because of an affirmative injury to the tree itself, but because the neighbor refuses to continue supplying nourishment from his own land. Yehoshua's enactment required him to tolerate the nourishment only so long as he had no need for the place. Once he needs his land, he need not continue giving nourishment. He may therefore cut the roots to prevent them from drawing from his property. Regarding a purchaser, it appears that if the landowner sold the field but retained the tree for himself, then because a seller is presumed to sell generously, he did not retain a nourishment right in the buyer's land except whatever temporary right derives from Yehoshua's enactment. The buyer may therefore cut the roots when he needs the land. But if the landowner sold the tree, one can say that because a seller conveys generously, he also transferred the nourishment rights necessary for the tree, and the landowner may not cut the roots. According to this, Tosafot in Bava Batra 18, who establish the root case as involving a purchaser, speak of a seller who sold the land while retaining the tree for himself.
