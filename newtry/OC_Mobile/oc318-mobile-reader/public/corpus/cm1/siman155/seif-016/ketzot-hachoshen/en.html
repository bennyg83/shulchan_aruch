The rule requiring three years for a nuisance presumption raises a conceptual question. My distinguished brother, Rabbi Yehudah Kohen, asked why the injured owner cannot later retract his tolerance. If one tells another, "Tear my garment and be exempt," the permission exempts what was already torn, but before further tearing the owner can revoke permission. Even after the tearing begins, once the owner protests, additional damage becomes actionable. Neighboring nuisances seem similar. The injured owner did not give away his property. He merely tolerated damage. Why should silence or even a kinyan prevent him from later saying, "From now on do not damage me"? The answer is that a nuisance presumption does not work because the injured owner permanently waived compensation for damage. It works because of the substantive law of neighboring nuisances. Where the harmful use was lawfully established and the harm is not a direct act of damage, the burden can shift to the injured owner to protect himself, as with a tree that preceded the cistern. When the injured party waived or sold the right to object, the harmful use was thereby established with his authorization. Once it stood lawfully for even a moment under the acquired permission, it receives the status of a lawful prior use. Even Tosafot, who generally gives limited effect to a use that merely arose permissibly, can agree where it arose specifically through the injured owner's own authorization, whether by explicit kinyan or the legally recognized presumption of silence. That is stronger than a use that happened to be lawful because no injured object yet existed. Therefore, after the right is acquired, the injured party cannot revoke at will. If he wishes to avoid the harm, he must distance or alter his own use. This also answers Tosafot's question against Rabbeinu Chananel's purchaser interpretation in the tree case. Tosafot asks that if the tree owner has three years of nuisance possession, then even if the cistern actually preceded the tree the acquired nuisance right should protect the tree. That is correct according to the principle stated here. A nuisance presumption means the use stood with full authorization for a time and therefore is equivalent to a lawful priority. There is, however, a difficulty in the Mishnah's ruling, "If it is uncertain whether the tree or cistern came first, he does not cut the tree." If Rabbeinu Chananel reads "tree preceded" as meaning the nuisance right was held for three years, then the doubtful case would mean doubt whether the three years were completed. Ordinarily the possessor claiming a three year presumption must prove the full period. According to Rav Nachman we tell him, "Clarify your possession." How then can an unresolved three year period leave the tree standing? The sugya concerning "I was in the inner rooms" records a dispute. Rav Nachman requires the possessor to prove the quality of his possession, while Rava says the burden lies on the challenger. Since the tree sugya is itself being used in discussion of Rava's view about preliminary placement, Rabbeinu Chananel can read the uncertain tree case according to Rava's procedural position, under which the challenger bears the burden. But our final law follows Rava regarding preliminary nuisance placement while following Rav Nachman regarding proof of a land presumption. That makes Rabbeinu Chananel's reading difficult for practical law and the issue remains unresolved. A further difficulty arises from Tosafot's own nuisance theory. They say prior lawful placement generally does not preserve a nuisance, except for a tree because of extraordinary loss. Yet in the mustard case they establish the nuisance with a purchaser and still require the mustard owner to distance. If a purchased nuisance right is no better than merely lawful placement, why does the buyer's purchase help? The answer is our distinction: acquisition of the nuisance right from the injured owner is not merely "lawful placement." It is an affirmative authorization of the use and gives the harmful use full legal standing. That is stronger than a use that happened to begin without wrongdoing.
