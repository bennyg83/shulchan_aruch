The smoke referred to, etc. See the responsa of Mahari ibn Lev, siman 46, and the responsa of Maharit, simanim 88 and 114, page 146.
Some say that regarding smoke, etc. The Rama writes that this should be the practical ruling, but in my view the Mechaber is correct. The Rama writes in Darkei Moshe that Mahari Trani inferred this from the Mordechai, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Hagahot Ashri, and that we should rule accordingly because Mahari Trani is the later authority and is fit to be relied upon, contrary to the Beit Yosef, who preferred the Ramah because he was a renowned authority. Had the Rama examined Mahari Trani's own ruling, he would not have written this, for Mahari Trani himself was not certain of this ruling or of the inference he drew. At the end of his responsum he states that he does not call those judges mistaken, since they found explicit support in the rulings of the Geonim, and that he does not generally engage in fine inference when the law is stated expressly in commonly available Geonic rulings. It appears that this was the Beit Yosef's intention in ruling like the Ramah. In truth, Mahari Trani's inferences from those sources are not compelling. The Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot cite Rav Mattityah concerning one who wished to establish an oven, whose neighbors could prevent him because its damage was great and frequent. Mahari Trani inferred that otherwise they could not prevent him. Yet the sources merely state the facts as they occurred. They also emphasize that the oven's damage was great and frequent because otherwise possession could be acquired. Mahari Trani further inferred from Hagahot Ashri, in the name of Mahariach, that smoke which is not constant may not be prevented even initially. Hagahot Ashri writes regarding a shop in a courtyard that one may prevent the noise of those entering and leaving, but may make utensils and sell them in the marketplace. It then states that even one who now seeks to begin such a trade may not be prevented, because Rav's statement that there is no prescriptive right for nuisances is limited by Rav Zevid to smoke and a privy, while other nuisances can acquire a right. Mahari Trani understood this as proof regarding conduct ab initio. In my humble opinion, he did not interpret it correctly. The actual wording of Hagahot Ashri is that in all the cases in which the Mishna says one may prevent, this applies when one now seeks to begin the trade, because Rav Zevid limits Rav's rule concerning nuisances without a right to smoke and a privy, while other nuisances do acquire a right. The passage plainly refers to the first clause of the Mishna, concerning a shop in a jointly owned courtyard, and means that one may prevent the business when it is newly begun, but if the nuisance has already been maintained, a right may be acquired. According to Mahari Trani's own reading, Hagahot Ashri would prove the opposite, namely that initially one may prevent every nuisance. One might distinguish a jointly owned courtyard, but this does not establish his conclusion. Mahari Trani also writes that in a dispute among authorities the injured party bears the burden of proof. This is unpersuasive. That is true only after the alleged right has already been established. When one now seeks to begin a harmful use, the person causing the harm must prove his right, since absent proof the prior status remains. It is also reasonable that one cannot impose even a minor harm upon another against his will. According to what I have explained, no authority disputes the Ramah. Therefore, since the Ramah, the Tur, and the Nimukei Yosef in Lo Yachpor all hold that even smoke which is not constant may be prevented initially, and no one truly disagrees, this is certainly the practical ruling.
