Seif 10: “So that the water will not be absorbed and damage his fellow’s wall.” Perhaps there is a distinction in countries such as ours, where sloped roofs have been customary for many generations. Rainwater from his roof flowing toward his fellow’s wall may more readily be considered his direct force. Without the sloped roof that he constructed, even heavy rain would flow in its ordinary manner and would not damage the wall; by constructing the sloped roof he created the direct cause of damage to his fellow’s wall. Even if the rain does not fall from his roof onto his fellow’s roof, but instead falls to the ground next to his fellow’s wall and may thereby damage the foundation, one can likewise say that this is considered his direct force. Without the concentration of a large amount of water together by the slope of the roof, there would not be as much damage. However, regarding this point, it may be that the repair of paving the ground with stones on a slope in order to protect the foundation from damage is incumbent equally upon both of them. For if only his fellow’s sloped roof existed, there would ordinarily also be some concern of damage to the opposite foundation. This is unless it is known that, because of the particular form of the construction, there is no concern of damage except to one wall; in that case the obligation falls only upon one party.
