Ibid., Shach, seif katan 3, in the name of the Bach. Note: see Baer Heitev. In my opinion, there the case is where the upper resident must repair the roof, which is not the issue here. Here they are not presently litigating over the roof. Rather, rain fell in the meantime, and the lower resident claims that because the upper resident failed to repair his plastered floor, rain descended from the roof through his ceiling onto the lower resident. The ruling here is that this is not a valid claim unless he proceeds against him on the basis of the duty to repair his roof. The rules of roof repair have changed in our times because buildings may contain second and third floors. The lowest resident bears no responsibility for repairing any ceiling. Each intermediate resident repairs the floor beneath him, not the ceiling above him. The uppermost resident must repair both his floor and his ceiling. However, the highest attic, situated under a sloped roof and not used as a residence but only for storing straw and wood, drying hides, and similar uses, is equally available for the use of all residents of the building. Therefore all residents share equally in the cost of repairing that sloped roof, each with respect to the length corresponding to his dwelling below. If one resident wishes to withdraw entirely from use of that attic and renounces his rights to it, the others can no longer compel him to participate in repairing the sloped roof.
