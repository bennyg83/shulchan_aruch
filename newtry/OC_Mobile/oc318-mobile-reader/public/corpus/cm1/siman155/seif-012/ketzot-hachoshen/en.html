The second approach is Rabbeinu Tam's. He reads Ravina as retracting from the earlier assumption. The Sages themselves hold that the person presently causing damage must distance. Where no injured object yet exists, the first use is not yet a nuisance, and even Rava permits placement except for a cistern, where each shovel immediately loosens the neighboring soil. Thus the Sages and Rava both allow preliminary placement of ordinary nuisances when the injured object does not yet exist. This makes the mustard and bees dispute understandable without a purchaser case. The first party may have placed the bees initially. According to the Sages the later mustard owner must distance because, once both are present, the mustard is the relevant damaging use. Rabbi Yosei allows the mustard because the bees also damage it, and the first party therefore cannot demand full removal. Ramban attacks this reading. He argues that the language, "The damager must distance himself," ordinarily comes to increase the distancing duty rather than narrow it, and that Ravina says, "The Sages hold," even though the Sages' view had not changed. Yet Rabbeinu Tam's approach can be defended because Rava himself explicitly gave the special reason of each shovel loosening the earth only in the cistern case. Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef explain that this reason is needed specifically according to Rabbi Yosei's direct force standard. The Gemara's broader challenges to Rava concerning other nuisances were therefore only according to the Sages, who do not require a direct force. Ravina's conclusion can thus mean that the earlier challenge incorrectly assumed that, according to the Sages, Rava forbids all preliminary placement even when there is no presently injured object. Ravina clarifies that the Sages require the damager to distance only where he is actually damaging something now. When no injured use yet exists, he is not yet a damager. Only the cistern is different because the act itself immediately loosens the adjacent soil. Ramban also asks why the Gemara now infers, "It follows that Rabbi Yosei holds the injured party must distance," since the initial arrangement of the case could have been maintained. Tosafot explains that the inference is a later question following the new reading, and under Rabbeinu Tam's structure the sequence is coherent.
