The dovecote must be distanced. Nimukei Yosef in chapter Lo Yachpor writes that all the Mishnah's cases constitute direct causal force. Certainly this applies to a pit, ditch, and cave, where each stroke of the spade loosens the neighboring soil. Distancing a dovecote also has another rationale, namely the pigeons' eating, which resembles the tooth damage of an ox. For purposes of liability for their damage we treat the pigeons as though they were fully his, even though he does not have full ownership sufficient to create theft liability and his right in them exists only because of considerations of peaceful relations. Ramban in Milchamot, in the mustard sugya, appears to disagree. He writes that if ownership of bees were Torah law, their eating would indeed constitute tooth damage for which the Torah imposes payment through 'and it shall consume.' But since ownership of them exists only because of peaceful relations, nevertheless where their damage is common one must remove them, and this is the same as the dovecote case. Accordingly, if one failed to distance his dovecote from the city and the pigeons went and caused damage, the monetary liability depends on the dispute discussed in seif 3. According to Raavad, in all these distancing cases, if one failed to distance and damage occurred he is exempt from payment. Therefore, according to Ramban, dovecote pigeons are likewise exempt, because liability cannot be imposed as tooth damage when his ownership is only rabbinic for peaceful-relations purposes. According to Rosh, however, in all these distancing cases he must pay because the damage is treated as his direct force. Dovecote pigeons are likewise his direct force. Even though he does not fully own them, this resembles one who places another person's animal upon another person's standing grain, who is liable for tooth damage even though the animal is not his, as stated in Bava Kamma 56. See Tosafos there, s.v. 'one who places another's animal.'
