Assistance given in error. Responsa Rishmei Sheelah, siman 56, discusses Reuven and Shimon, partners in a courtyard. Shimon had the right to build only four amot into the width of the courtyard, but built about half an amah more. When beit din asked how he had encroached upon the partners' property, Shimon answered that he was away during the construction and his wife told him that Reuven himself had been present when the four amot were measured and had marked the place where the building presently ends. Reuven denied this. Asked why he had remained silent for more than three years, Reuven replied that he had not noticed the excess until the partnership deed was read aloud in beit din and he heard that Shimon was entitled to only four amot. The responsum writes that, according to the wife's account, if Reuven himself marked the place, possibly after measurement, this would not ordinarily be treated as a mistaken chazakah but as evidence that he knowingly waived the excess. The difficulty is that Shimon himself does not make a certain claim, but relies on his wife's report. Although a claim transmitted through a relative can be treated as a definite claim for purposes of imposing a heset oath, it is not necessarily sufficient to extract property. The case would then resemble one who says, 'I do not know whether you waived it for me.' Tumim explains that a claim of waiver is believed only where supported by a migo such as payment or complete denial. Thus here the underlying obligation is certain and the waiver is doubtful, resembling 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' which ordinarily requires payment, subject only to a general ban. Although Tumim notes a dispute where the defendant could not have been expected to know, and the possessor may say kim li, here there is an additional difficulty because waiver itself requires affirmative proof. The responsum leaves the matter requiring study. Concerning whether the entire structure must be demolished, see what I write below in siman 377.
