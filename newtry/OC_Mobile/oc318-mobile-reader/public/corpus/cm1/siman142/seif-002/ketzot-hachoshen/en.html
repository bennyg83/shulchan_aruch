Since the assistance was given in error. In chapter Chazakat HaBatim, Bava Batra 41a, Rav Anan diverted a watercourse onto another person's land and the boundary returned onto the other's land. When he came before Rav Nachman, Rav Nachman told him to restore it. Rav Anan argued that the owner had waived his right, because he came and helped erect the boundary with him. Rav Nachman answered that it was a mistaken waiver: just as you yourself would not have done so had you known, he too did not know. This implies that if the assisting owner had known, his act would have been effective even though Rav Anan, the beneficiary, did not know. So writes the Tur in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, and so rules Shulchan Aruch here.
Nimukei Yosef writes that we learn from here that had the transferor known, even though Rav Anan, who was acquiring, did not know, Rav Anan would acquire. That is why Rav Nachman did not suffice with saying, 'You certainly did not know.' Although Yevamot 52 teaches that one who hoes the property of a convert while thinking it is his own does not acquire because the acquirer himself did not know, Raavad explains that this case is different because there is another person consciously transferring ownership. See what I wrote in siman 275, seif katan 4. This also resolves Tosafos' difficulty here, s.v. 'he said to him, it is a mistaken waiver.' Tosafos asks that in chapter Eizehu Neshech Rav Nachman appears to hold that a mistaken waiver is effective, whereas here he holds it ineffective. Hagahot Ashri there explains Rav Nachman's view that a mistaken waiver is effective even though a mistaken kinyan is reversible. The distinction is that when the property remains in the transferor's possession, he can retract because he transferred it in error, and hekdesh likewise does not take effect by mistake because it requires a complete mental resolution. But if a person has money and sells it to another and waives his right to it, even mistakenly, the other acquires it like ordinary hefker, because the waiver creates the equivalent of owner's despair. The point is that ye'ush can operate even when based on error, for ye'ush of a lost item itself is ordinarily mistaken in the sense that, had the owner known where the item was, he would not have despaired. Thus, if Rav Nachman's reason for upholding mistaken waiver is that it functions as ye'ush, here he correctly said, 'just as you did not know, he too did not know.' Rav Anan did not acquire through ye'ush or hefker because the acquirer himself did not know, and one who thinks ownerless property is already his does not acquire it. Nor can he acquire through the conscious intent of another transferor, because that would be a mistaken waiver and therefore a mistaken kinyan, which is ineffective.
