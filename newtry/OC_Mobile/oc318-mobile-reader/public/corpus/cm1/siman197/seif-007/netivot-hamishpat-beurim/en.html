And he pulled, he did not acquire. Mechaber's words imply that even if the animal is standing in the buyer's courtyard at the end of thirty days, he does not acquire, following Rambam. Beit Yosef likewise implies this. At first glance Bava Metzia at the beginning of HaMafkid is difficult. The Gemara distinguishes between language meaning, "When it is stolen and you pay me, my cow is acquired to you from now," and language meaning it is acquired near the theft, with a difference where the cow is standing in a meadow. This seems to imply that if it stands in the buyer's courtyard, even without "from now" he acquires. There is no difficulty. Here the seller specifically said, "Pull and acquire." He thereby revealed that he wishes to transfer only through meshichah. Therefore the buyer cannot later acquire through courtyard. In HaMafkid no specific kinyan was stated. The law rests on an assessment that the owner wishes to transfer by whatever valid kinyan is available, and therefore courtyard can work. Ran in Ketubot takes another approach and says that even if the animal stands in a meadow at the later time the buyer can acquire, treating the original meshichah as continuing. The HaMafkid sugya seems difficult for him as well, because it implies that without "from now" a meadow does not suffice. Ran can be defended through the distinction between the transferor's intent and the recipient's intent. The Gemara says that we view the depositor as though he had said, "If it is stolen and you pay, my cow shall be acquired to you." But ordinary acquisition requires intent of both transferor and acquirer. The presumed assessment can supply the owner's intent: had he known the cow would be stolen and paid for, he certainly would have wished to transfer it. As Shach in siman 358 says, a clear presumption can make a gift effective despite the donor's lack of actual awareness. But such a presumption does not replace the acquirer's intent to perform a kinyan. One who hoes a convert's property thinking it is his does not acquire, even though it is obvious he would have intended to acquire had he known it was ownerless. Acquisition without kinyan-intent is no acquisition. Therefore, in HaMafkid the recipient cannot acquire through the earlier meshichah, because he did not then intend that meshichah as an acquisition of future ownership. He acquires only through courtyard, which can acquire without his awareness, once the owner's presumed intent to transfer exists. This is why a meadow is insufficient but his own courtyard works. By contrast, where he originally performs meshichah specifically as an act of acquisition whose legal effect is deferred, Ran can treat it as an extended meshichah and allow acquisition even if the item later rests in a meadow. There is another way to defend Ran. Ran in responsum 90 distinguishes a condition from a fixed future date. A vow subject to a condition is considered not yet effective, while a vow scheduled for a future date is considered already initiated because time comes automatically. So too, "pull now and acquire after thirty days" is an acquisition whose effectiveness has already begun and will ripen automatically with time, allowing the original meshichah to be viewed as extended. In HaMafkid, however, "when it is stolen and you pay" is conditional. The meshichah occurred before the acquisition began at all and cannot support it. On this approach Ran may even agree with Rambam in a truly conditional case. Rambam still must be reconciled with the law of a get, where a woman can be divorced after thirty days even without the words "from now." Rambam and Shulchan Aruch appear to understand the formulation, "This is your get, but you shall not be divorced until after thirty days," as transferring ownership of the get to her immediately while deferring only the legal divorce. A woman can own the document before the divorce takes effect. This is evident from cases where a get already affects her status for certain purposes, and where taking it back and redelivering can suffice without a new transfer from her to him. Thus, when she receives the get now and later places it in a side alley, the place occupied by her utensil is treated as her domain, because Tosafot in Kiddushin say the place of a person's utensil is acquired to its owner. At the later moment of divorce the get is therefore still in her legal domain. By contrast, when one says, "Pull the cow and do not acquire until after thirty days," ownership of the cow remains with the seller during the thirty days. When it is then left in the alley, the area occupied by the seller's animal is treated as the seller's, so the buyer has no kinyan at the later time.

I am astonished that these authorities did not consider siman 257, seif 1, in the gloss concerning one who consecrates his property. There it is clear that in monetary law the words "from now and after thirty days" are not treated as simple retraction, condition, or reservation; rather, we interpret them as transfer of the body from now and fruits after the period. Bava Batra states this explicitly. Tosafot in Kiddushin 63, who require "on condition from now," follow their own view in Yevamot that "from now and after thirty days" is not interpreted as body now and fruits later. But Shulchan Aruch's rulings show that he does interpret it that way, following Ran in Ketubot. Therefore Mechaber correctly rules here that "from now" alone can suffice: the body is transferred now while use remains deferred. Shach, seif katan 4, appears to agree by referring to Ran. There remains a difficulty from siman 257, which rules that the consecration does not take effect. One might instead say that where body-now/fruits-later is impossible, the language should be treated as a condition, as with a get. Rashba's responsum, the source of that law, contains difficult wording, saying even a condition does not help, which requires emendation. The Yerushalmi expressly treats such language as a condition in cases of ownerless property and recognizes consecration in certain forms. The better rule is that where the phrase is genuinely doubtful between retraction and condition, we leave money in the prior owner's chazakah and treat the transfer as ineffective upon retraction. The Yerushalmi says that with a get the result is doubtful divorce, while with emancipation the slave is not freed. This is because in monetary matters we preserve the prior ownership. The same applies to ownerless declaration where prior ownership remains until the uncertainty is resolved. This is relevant specifically if the transferor retracts within the thirty days. If he does not retract, then even a simple statement "after thirty days" works, and saying "from now" should not make it worse. Where body-now/fruits-later is a sensible interpretation, however, that interpretation is certainly preferred, as shown here and in siman 257. This applies specifically to the formulation "from now and after thirty days." By contrast, "from now if I do not come," or another express condition, is genuinely conditional. We do not interpret it as body now and fruits after fulfillment. Such a condition, once fulfilled, operates retroactively. Thus, in kiddushin under a true "from now" condition, she can be divorced before the condition is later fulfilled, because fulfillment merely reveals retroactively that the kiddushin had taken effect. With "from now and after thirty days," by contrast, Rashbam says the acquisition begins now and is completed only later, so it is not merely retroactive clarification. This is also why Rama in siman 211, seif 7, can allow a sale to another before fulfillment of a true "from now" condition. The general rule is: if "from now" is stated in conditional language, it is a condition and fulfillment reveals the earlier effect retroactively; if the language is "from now and after thirty days," ownership of the body begins now while fruits or completion are deferred.
