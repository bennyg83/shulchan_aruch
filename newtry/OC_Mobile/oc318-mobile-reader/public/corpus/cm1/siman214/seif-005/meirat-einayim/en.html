Some say that with a courtyard he acquires, etc. The Tur writes in the Rosh's name that the same applies to a ruin. Perhaps the Mechaber omitted it because it follows all the more readily: a ruin stands for rebuilding, while a courtyard does not necessarily do so. The fact that Rashba disputes the law of a courtyard but not a ruin also points in this direction. Still, this is not a complete proof, because in the previous seif the authorities say that in a ruin he may build only up to the measure of an ordinary house, while here the Rosh says that with a courtyard he acquires all the airspace above it, however high. The Rosh likewise writes this regarding a ruin. See the beginning of the previous seif.
Even though he wrote to Shimon, “from the depth of the earth,” etc., until “he retained for himself only the airspace,” etc. The responsum explains more fully that although he expressly wrote to Shimon the depth and height of the house that he had previously sold to Reuven, and wrote that the house was sold upward, Shimon nevertheless did not acquire the airspace. This is unlike selling a palm tree for its fruit. In a palm tree the fruit is not yet in existence, so it cannot be reserved except by reserving the body of the tree for the fruit. The airspace over a house, however, remains reserved automatically because it never entered Reuven's purchase when the seller failed to transfer the height. The seller therefore has no ownership in the body of the house itself. How then can he transfer that airspace to a third person through a physical body that belongs to someone else, when the seller himself has no share in that body? This is the substance of Rashba's reasoning, stated here in abbreviated and clarified form. We learn that even where the airspace remained with the seller and he may build there upon pillars, he still cannot later sell that right to someone else, because what he seeks to transfer is only airspace, and airspace by itself is not susceptible to acquisition. Even according to what I wrote in the Perishah, that Ramban, who disputes the Tur, agrees that where the seller originally reserved something for himself he can later sell that right, that applies specifically to an upper story, which has tangible substance and can be transferred, not to bare airspace.
The airspace is sold with it and Shimon acquires it, etc. The correct reading is “is sold,” because airspace alone cannot be acquired. This nevertheless requires study from Rashba's responsum, which does not appear to say this, and Darkei Moshe likewise does not cite it in Rama's present formulation. Rather, it says: if he sold him his entire courtyard, marked the boundaries, and wrote, “Everything within these boundaries I sold to you and retained nothing,” then he sold Shimon the depth beneath the house previously sold to Reuven, including the pit and cistern, and also the roof over Reuven's house if such a roof exists. Since the roof is included in Shimon's purchase, the airspace above that roof to the sky is also acquired by Shimon, because it is acquired together with the body of the roof. Thus Rashba does not say that the airspace is automatically acquired merely through boundary descriptions. He says this only where a roof above Reuven's house is itself included in what was sold. Rama must therefore mean the same: where there is a roof and it is sold to Shimon under the general phrase “everything in the courtyard,” even though the roof was not separately specified, Shimon acquires the roof and with it the airspace above.
