He may give him an upper story. See Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 5. Reuven and his wife had given their son-in-law Shimon the right to reside in half of their house for fifteen years. After Reuven died, the widow and her son needed to redeem and sell half of the house, since part of it had been mortgaged and would otherwise have been forfeited. They sold half of the house to Shimon. Later the widow became engaged again, and Shimon initially agreed to lend her money secured by the other half, which had been assigned to her for her ketubah, but after a quarrel he withdrew. When she sought to mortgage that half to someone else, Shimon objected, claiming that one half was already his for residence and the other half belonged to him outright. She answered that his prior residential right attached to one portion, while the remaining half was hers to dispose of as she wished. The rabbi who initially ruled in the case held for the widow. He reasoned that the half sold to Shimon outright could be identified with the half in which Shimon already possessed residential rights. Although the deed said that the beneficiary's hand should be uppermost, that principle applies to genuinely ambiguous language, not where an expression has a definite minimal legal meaning. The initial decisor compared the case to one who sells "a house" without specification, where the seller can provide the lesser house included within the term's meaning. Avodat HaGershuni disagrees and rules for Shimon. The analogy to siman 214 is not persuasive. There, a small house is itself fully called a house. Likewise, if there were two separately identifiable half-houses and the seller said, "I sell you half a house," he might choose the lesser one. But where the seller says, "I sell you half of my house," the natural meaning is one half of the entire house, not merely the inferior half already burdened by the prior residential right. He proves this from the rule in siman 218:20 concerning one who sells half a field: the parties measure the whole and the buyer receives a portion whose value equals half the entire field, even if that portion is taken from the poorer section. At first, this might suggest that during the fifteen-year residential term Shimon should have use of three quarters of the house, namely one half by virtue of his original residential right and another quarter by virtue of owning half of the remaining property, and afterward own one half outright. On further analysis, however, Avodat HaGershuni concludes that the later sale, stated without qualification, was an ordinary sale of substance and use. The language of the deed also indicates that the parties sold him the other half, for it states that "from now and immediately Shimon may reside in it, rent it, and place tenants in it." If the deed referred only to the half in which he already had a residential right, the new acquisition would not be the source of his present right to reside there. The clause that the widow "reserved the second half for herself in order to collect her ketubah from it" does not prove otherwise. Earlier the deed had said that the widow and her son were selling half the house, which might have implied that the other half remained jointly theirs. It was therefore necessary to clarify that the remaining half was reserved to the widow alone for her ketubah. Avodat HaGershuni thus concludes that Shimon has the right to reside in the entire house for fifteen years, and after that term he remains owner of one half of the house. He adds that he does not accuse the first decisor of an obvious legal error, because the case is novel and opinions can differ, but if the case came before him he would rule for Shimon for the reasons stated.
