One who acknowledged that he sold, etc. Rashba writes in a responsum, cited by the Beit Yosef, that all the restrictive rules apply specifically at the time of the sale or gift itself, when we do not include in the acquisition more than the grantor actually transferred. Thus, if he should have written, “I sold you such and such and retained nothing for myself in this sale,” and did not do so, we infer that he retained something. But if he merely appears before us and acknowledges that he previously sold his house to another, he need acknowledge only the general transaction. We then say that everything was included, just as one who says, “I divorced my wife,” is believed regarding the future and we do not inquire whether he divorced her completely or retained rights to her earnings. Rashba writes similarly in another responsum: although one who sells a house does not ordinarily sell the pit and cistern, if he now merely records and acknowledges what previously occurred, together with some additional matter he presently adds, and says, “I sold my house to so and so, bounded by such and such boundaries,” then we treat his acknowledgment as including everything within those boundaries. This is not the time of conveyance, when we would infer a reservation from omitted language. Darkei Moshe cites both responsa, and they concern the same principle. From all this it appears that only an acknowledgment by way of narrative is treated broadly. But if the seller or donor intends this acknowledgment itself to create the transfer, then he must specify all the particulars, because this is the beginning of the acquisition before us, even if his wording says that he already transferred it. Rama appears to hold the same, but he abbreviated and thereby left room for readers to stumble.
