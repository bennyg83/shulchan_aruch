A non-Jew, etc., and not through money, etc. Meaning, even where land is ordinarily acquired by money alone, a non-Jew neither acquires nor transfers it except through a deed. This differs from Rashbam and Tosafot, Bava Batra 54b, s.v. veyisrael, who maintain that a non-Jew's law regarding land is the same as a Jew's. They explain that the passage concerns a place where deeds are customarily written, where even a Jew does not acquire through money alone; according to them, a non-Jew may also acquire through possession. Rambam, however, follows the Geonim. Rav Hai Gaon writes that although a Jew ordinarily acquires from another Jew through payment alone, a non-Jew who purchases from a Jew, and a Jew who purchases from a non-Jew, do not acquire until a deed is written. He cites the rule that one claiming through a non-Jew has the same status as the non-Jew, who establishes no possessory title without a deed. Thus, when a Jew buys a house from a non-Jew in Eretz Yisrael, a deed may be written even on Shabbos. If a Jew sold to a non-Jew and received the money, the property nevertheless remains in the Jew's possession. If a non-Jew sold to a Jew and received payment, it leaves the non-Jew's possession, and whoever first performs possession acquires it, while reimbursing the first Jew. This follows the rule that a non-Jew's property becomes like ownerless desert land. Responsibility nevertheless remains upon the non-Jew, not upon the first Jew. In summary, acquisition between a Jew and a non-Jew is the same in either direction: neither money nor possession completes acquisition, and of the three modes of acquiring land only a deed is effective.
Nevertheless, rental, etc. Beit Yosef in siman 192 writes that according to Rashbam, who explains the rule that a non-Jew's property is like desert land because even between Jews money does not acquire without a deed where deeds are customarily written, it follows that where deeds are not customary, money alone acquires. Rabbenu Yerucham writes similarly. Consequently, since rental agreements are not ordinarily written, money alone acquires a lease. See above, siman 195, seif 9, in Rama. This follows the authorities who equate the law of a non-Jew with that of a Jew. The ruling here that an express stipulation is effective likewise follows those authorities, as Beit Yosef explains from Rabbenu Yerucham and Rashbam. According to Rambam and the Geonim, however, the rule is otherwise. Darkei Moshe first notes this distinction, but then suggests that an express condition may be effective even according to them, and Rama here extends the same rule to rental. His ruling requires study, even regarding an express condition, because the Geonim appear to hold that acquisition between Jew and non-Jew is legally possible only through a deed. The Gemara's formulation, that a Jew claiming through a non-Jew has the non-Jew's status, indicates that the reason is not merely fear of coercion, but the legal mode of acquisition itself. This should apply all the more to rental, for the Sages equate rental of land with land itself. Darkei Moshe apparently inferred otherwise from Tur's statement at the end of the siman that all this concerns land, concerning which the parties do not rely upon payment, whereas Rambam and the Geonim hold that movable property is acquired by money on Torah law. Bach and Taz likewise challenge Rama. See what I write in seif 2.
