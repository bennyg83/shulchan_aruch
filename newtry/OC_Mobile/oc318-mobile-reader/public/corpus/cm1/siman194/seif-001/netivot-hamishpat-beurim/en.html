By chazakah. The Taz writes against the Sema that there is no practical difference between their views. This is surprising, because according to the Sema a Jew purchasing from a gentile acquires by chazakah, whereas according to Beit Yosef he does not. The principal view is nevertheless that of the Taz. The Sema's further statement, that if the gentile obeys the law it is difficult why he would not have a chazakah, is also very surprising. A gentile does not obtain the evidentiary status of chazakah in this setting merely because he generally obeys judicial rulings.
And he acquires only by a deed. The Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 1 of Hilchot Zechiyah, writes that the gentile too may retract from the sale. He compares it to a transaction between two Jews where land is purchased with money but no deed is written. Since the buyer does not fully rely upon the transaction, both parties may retract. He says the same should apply when a Jew buys from a gentile. In my humble opinion this is very difficult. The Rosh in Bava Batra 54 writes that the Jew cannot litigate with the gentile to recover his money, saying: "How can he take the money back from the gentile, since under their law the gentile has acquired the money, and even under our law the sale has been completed through payment? The gentile is no longer obligated to preserve the purchase for him if under Jewish law another person has acquired it by taking possession." If the gentile could retract by law, then surely both parties could retract. The Jew could simply retract and claim his money in our beit din, for in an equivalent transaction between Jews, if the buyer retracts, the seller must return the money. Moreover, if the gentile can still retract, why should the land be regarded as ownerless? If the gentile cannot retract, the rule is understandable. It resembles Keritot 24, where one gives a gift and the recipient says, "I do not want it," and anyone who takes possession acquires, because the donor, being unable to retract, has effectively relinquished the item. So too here. But if the seller still has a right of retraction, there is no basis for treating the land as ownerless. It has not left his ownership at all, since he can retract and keep it. Rather, as against the gentile, the sale is fully effective and the gentile cannot retract. The Jewish buyer likewise cannot demand the money back, because with respect to the gentile the transfer of the land out of his ownership was complete upon payment. The Jew nevertheless has not yet acquired the land into his own possession because his reliance upon the acquisition is uncertain. He fears that the gentile may overpower him and repudiate the sale in practice. This is similar to Bava Batra 142 concerning property of a convert whom people heard had died, where they do not acquire because their confidence in the facts is uncertain. This is entirely unlike a sale between Jews with money and no deed. There, because the seller knows that the buyer does not rely upon the payment and has not acquired, the seller himself also does not fully intend to convey, and the field therefore remains in his ownership. In a sale from a gentile, however, the gentile does not know that the Jew distrusts his willingness to honor the purchase. He fully intends to convey, because he knows that under both legal systems payment effects the relevant transfer from him. Once the gentile has fully relinquished ownership while the Jew has not yet taken title, the land is ownerless, comparable to a donor whose intended recipient rejects the gift.
However, rental. See Sema, seif katan 5, and the Taz, who disagrees. Ketzot HaChoshen brings a proof for the Rama from Bava Metzia 45. Where consecrated money is given to a bathhouse attendant, misuse is committed immediately. Tosafot explain the case according to Rabbi Yochanan as involving a gentile bathhouse attendant, and misuse occurs because rental of land is acquired with money. This seems to show that rental from a gentile is acquired by money alone without a deed. According to what I wrote above, however, this proof fails. As against the gentile, the acquisition is certainly complete, as the Rosh states, and Jewish law cannot recover the money from him. Once the gentile acquires the money, misuse of consecrated property has occurred. As for the Taz's principal question against the Rama, the answer is that even in a sale the transaction is fully effective against the gentile and neither party can retract from the other. The deficiency exists only from the Jewish buyer's perspective, allowing another Jew to acquire the land as ownerless. In a rental, however, the concept of ownerlessness does not apply. In a sale, once the gentile accepts the money he has entirely withdrawn from that house, and the gap before the Jew acquires can create ownerless property, similar to a rejected gift. In a rental, by contrast, siman 316 explains that if a tenant wants to leave the house empty, the landlord may rent it to others. The Sema there, seif katan 3, explains that the new rent belongs to the landlord even if he already received rent from the first tenant. Thus, until the tenant or one acting through him actually occupies or uses the house, the landlord retains an operative right to rent it to someone else. The landlord has not fully withdrawn from the house, so the property cannot be treated as ownerless. A seller and a landlord also differ conceptually. A tenant has a type of personal claim against the landlord to provide the rented premises, as I wrote below in siman 312:2. If the landlord wrongfully terminates the first rental, he must provide another house and cannot discharge himself merely by paying its value. A seller who steals back the object he sold and sells it to a gentile, by contrast, owes only its value and need not supply another identical object. Likewise, if one sold an unspecified house or donkey and the only house or donkey is destroyed, he may be exempt from supplying another, while one who rented an unspecified house or donkey becomes personally obligated through his assets to provide one. Even with a specifically identified donkey there is a lien upon the property, as Sema, siman 310, seif katan 6, explains. Tosafot in Bava Metzia, chapter HaOmanin, distinguish similarly between a specified house and a specified donkey. I discussed the reason at length in simanim 310 and 312. Accordingly, the law of ownerless property has no application to rental, and the Rama correctly writes that rental is acquired by money alone. His intent is that the rule of seif 2, under which another person who first takes possession acquires, does not apply to rental. Since no one else can acquire through ownerlessness, the first tenant's monetary acquisition remains effective as explained above. However, regarding the Rama's further statement that if the parties expressly stipulated that money alone should acquire, the stipulation is effective, the Bach, Taz, and Shach are correct in disagreeing. According to the Rashba and Raavad, such a stipulation does not cure the deficiency.
