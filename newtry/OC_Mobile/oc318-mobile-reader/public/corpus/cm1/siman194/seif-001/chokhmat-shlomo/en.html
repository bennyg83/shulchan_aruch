Seif 1. Note: Ketzot HaChoshen discusses whether acquisition through admission can operate in matters of prohibition. In my responsa on Pesach I proved that such an acquisition can operate even in ritual matters. Elsewhere I explained that admission cannot, according to any view, transfer something not presently in one's possession or where it would prejudice third parties. Regarding siman 196, seif 1, I was asked about a statement in Netivot HaMishpat that a Canaanite slave purchased from a non-Jew “is not acquired by deed,” whereas Yoreh De'ah siman 267 states that such a slave is acquired by money, deed, or possession, whether bought from an Israelite or a non-Jew. There is clearly a textual error in Netivot. Since the note refers the reader to the explanations, where the matter is not found, some reference has already been corrupted. I suggest that the intended reading is not that the slave is “not acquired by deed,” but that he “is acquired only by deed.” A Canaanite slave is legally like land. Just as land purchased from a non-Jew ordinarily requires a deed because the buyer lacks full reliance without it, so too a slave purchased from a non-Jew may require a deed in the ordinary case. This does not contradict Yoreh De'ah. That source states the intrinsic modes of acquisition: money, deed, or possession. The additional requirement of a deed in a purchase from a non-Jew stems only from lack of reliance, not from the basic law of acquisition. Therefore, where the buyer expressly states and demonstrates that he fully relies on the transaction, there is greater room to allow acquisition by another mode. Even those who hold that an express condition cannot cure lack of reliance in land may agree regarding a slave, because a slave can physically be brought into the buyer's home and guarded, unlike land. Thus one cannot say with certainty that the buyer never relies on the transaction. I also wrote elsewhere that even according to those who permit an Israelite to acquire from a non-Jew by money, this may require payment of the entire price. If part of the price remains unpaid, the law may differ from an ordinary sale between Israelites.
