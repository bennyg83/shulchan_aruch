And some say that even if the first person took possession. Responsa Knesset Yechezkel 93 writes regarding his case that although Rambam disputes Raavad and Rashba on the rule in siman 194, the Rama decides in favor of Raavad and Rashba, namely that even if the first person took possession, the later purchaser acquires. We have a received principle that when the Rama expressly decides between views, his decision governs even to remove money from the present possessor. In the case before him, however, this ruling was unnecessary. Even Rambam, who says that if the first person took possession before the second one the property remains with the first, refers only to a direct chazakah in the land itself, of the type used to acquire ownerless property, such as locking or fencing. In the case at hand, the father certainly did not personally lock, fence, or perform a similar act. Such acts would have been done through workers, and there is then no acquisition because they did not intend to acquire for him, as explained in siman 275. Even according to the view that spreading produce or arranging cushions can also constitute chazakah, it is well known that in our generations prominent people do not perform such tasks personally, but through servants. This also removes concern that the heirs might argue that their father acquired the property as ownerless property under the enactment cited by the Rosh. Apart from the fact that one may argue that the Rosh speaks only where witnesses actually saw him acquire it as ownerless property, an unusual act is not presumed without witnesses. Here it is certainly unusual for a person of his standing to perform the necessary act on the land personally rather than through workers or servants. See there further.
