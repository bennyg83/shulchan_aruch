But with movable property he acquires. See Shach, seif katan 4, who says that before the first buyer performs meshichah another person may take possession of the movable property as ownerless. He is forced to interpret Rama narrowly. In my view Rama's ruling is straightforward and correct. Rashbam and Tosafot make the ownerless status depend on the fact that, from the non Jew's side, the money itself completed his transfer. Wherever even the non Jew has not completed a kinyan, there is no ownerless property. With movable property, it is at least uncertain whether money acquires from a non Jew. Therefore, if another Jew has taken the goods, the first buyer may act to recover them from the non Jew under either possibility. If money acquires from the non Jew, the goods are already the first buyer's and he may protect his property by lawful means. If money does not acquire, the goods remain the non Jew's and the second Jew is holding stolen non-Jewish property, so the first Jew may truthfully inform the non Jew. Even in our beit din, because it is a doubt of law whether money acquired, the second holder cannot retain the object against the first buyer: perhaps the goods are the first Jew's and the second Jew is certainly unable to establish a superior title. Ramban in responsum 228 writes that according to the custom of non Jews, money alone can acquire because they customarily transfer by payment, except that Rabbeinu Tam may still require meshichah in a technical movable-property sale. According to that, Rama's ruling is even more readily understood and Shach's question disappears.
