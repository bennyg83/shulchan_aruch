But with movables. The earliest authorities already dispute whether a gentile acquires movables by money or by meshichah. Therefore, regarding sale of a firstborn animal, the practical authorities required both money and meshichah in order to avoid the Torah doubt. Tosafot in Bava Metzia writes that even according to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that by Torah law money acquires movables for a Jew, it is possible that money also acquires for a gentile. The familiar inference from the law of a Jew does not necessarily prove that the gentile's mode must be meshichah. Rabbi Chaim Kohen therefore treated money as a possible acquisition for the gentile as well. Accordingly, Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah require both methods when transferring an interest in a firstborn animal to a gentile.
In the sale of chametz, however, physically drawing every barrel is often impractical, and money alone does not remove the uncertainty. I therefore considered how to construct an acquisition that avoids the doubts. Nachalat Shivah cites Rabbi Shmuel of Koidanov. He notes that the common method for a firstborn animal is for the gentile to pay a perutah for an interest, for the Jew to transfer to him the place where the animal stands, and for the place to acquire the animal. The same method was used for chametz by selling the room containing the chametz with money and document so that the room would acquire the chametz. Rabbi Shmuel questioned relying on courtyard acquisition. A courtyard acquires by the law of agency when its owner is not standing beside it, and there is no ordinary agency for a gentile. He therefore preferred Rabbeinu Yerucham's formulation: transfer the place to the gentile and transfer the movable item incidentally with the land. On this understanding the movable is acquired by incidental acquisition with land, not by courtyard. He therefore recommended that for chametz one sell the room to the gentile and transfer the chametz incidentally with that land, rather than relying on the room as the gentile's acquiring courtyard.
Rabbi Shmuel's objection does not actually apply to the formulations of Tosafot, Rosh, and Tur concerning a firstborn animal. They do not say the gentile acquires the animal automatically by the room as a courtyard. They say that if the gentile has no premises, the Jew transfers a room to him, the gentile takes possession of that room, and then physically draws the animal into it. Meshichah into one's own domain certainly works and is no weaker than meshichah into an alley. For chametz, however, physical drawing of every barrel is impractical, so one must rely either on courtyard or on incidental acquisition with land. Incidental acquisition itself is not free of difficulty. Tosafot and Rosh say that such acquisition may be only rabbinic. If so, there is concern whether a rabbinic acquisition can remove Torah sanctity of a firstborn animal, and some authorities hold that a gentile does not acquire through a rabbinic enactment at all. I therefore prefer, in principle, to establish the acquisition through the gentile's courtyard. The objection that a gentile has no courtyard because courtyard acquisition is a branch of agency requires analysis. If a gentile had no courtyard acquisition at all, even when standing beside it, the problem would be broad. A male minor lacks courtyard acquisition even when nearby because his courtyard derives from agency, while a female minor's courtyard in divorce can function like her hand. Gentiles do not stand within the divorce framework, so their courtyard cannot simply be treated as a hand. Yet the Gemara in Avodah Zarah assumes that a gentile's vessel acquires wine when the wine enters its airspace, and a person's vessel acquires through the law of courtyard. This is strong evidence that a gentile does possess courtyard acquisition. One might answer that the sugya there follows Rav Ashi, who held that a Jew can be an agent for a gentile even though a gentile cannot be an agent for a Jew. On that theory a gentile can certainly be a principal for courtyard agency. But the practical halachah rejects Rav Ashi and says that the requirement, "as you are members of the covenant, so too your agents," applies to the principal as well. Nevertheless, later halachic rulings concerning a gentile's vessel continue to assume that the vessel acquires for him. This shows that the gentile's courtyard acquisition survives even according to our law. The conceptual reason is that the Torah specially strengthened courtyard acquisition beyond ordinary agency. A courtyard itself is not a covenant member or a person of legal capacity, yet Scripture empowered it to function for its owner. Once the law of courtyard has been revealed as a special agency mechanism that does not require the agent to resemble the principal, the limitation that ordinary agents and principals be covenant members need not bar a gentile's courtyard. This differs from a minor. A minor's inability to appoint an agent does not derive merely from the covenant comparison. The primary agency verses themselves concern legally competent adults, and the male minor is excluded from the very institution of appointment. Thus special courtyard agency does not necessarily supply him a courtyard right. Tosafot likewise indicates that the Torah's special inclusion of courtyard can overcome ordinary agency limitations. If a gentile's courtyard were excluded merely because a gentile lacks ordinary agency, the same Talmudic proofs from theft would be difficult, since a gentile is subject to the prohibition of theft and can physically acquire stolen property by hand. The broader structure therefore supports courtyard acquisition for a gentile. Accordingly, sale of chametz can in principle be effected by transferring the premises and allowing those premises to acquire the chametz as the gentile's courtyard. Where the chametz is not in the seller's city and neither meshichah nor local courtyard acquisition is convenient, the later authorities struggled to find a complete method. Incidental acquisition with land can be used where land is available, but, as noted, that method has its own rabbinic concerns. I therefore suggest the acquisition known as odaita, formal admission. The seller can write or declare before valid witnesses that he has sold all the chametz to the named gentile through a legally effective mode of acquisition. A formal admission itself operates as an acquisition even if both parties know that the stated historical fact is not literally true. In my view odaita is a complete acquisition and can affect not only monetary rights but also prohibitory law. Tosafot asks why money could not be transferred through admission as in the case of Isur the convert, even though everyone knew the admitted ownership had not previously existed. Their question is in a context involving second tithe, showing that the acquisition can have consequences in ritual law as well. Another Tosafot passage appears to distinguish Torah ritual law and says that when the Torah requires the money truly to belong to a person, a false admission cannot suffice. Even there, however, the language suggests that the limitation is because a Torah level ownership status is required. Chametz after valid nullification requires sale only by rabbinic law. Therefore even on that restrictive view, odaita should suffice for the chametz sale. I discuss the nature of odaita at length below. For present purposes, the practical point is that a gentile can acquire chametz through a formal admission that it was sold to him in an effective manner, especially where the Torah prohibition has already been removed through nullification.
Taz cites a responsum as proof that admission does not work to transfer a gentile's debt, but careful reading proves the opposite principle. Baal HaIttur says that an oral debt itself cannot be transferred by odaita. If it could, the Gemara discussing the special power of a deathly ill person to transfer a loan could simply have said that a healthy person can accomplish the same thing by admission. This shows that the problem is not that odaita is ineffective in ritual matters. It is that an oral debt is property outside the creditor's possession and cannot be transferred through ordinary acquisition at all, except by the special law of a three party arrangement. Where the subject is property actually within the transferor's control, odaita is a genuine acquisition and can have consequences even for prohibitory law. Tosafot's question concerning a deathly ill transfer of a loan can be understood according to the opinion that a loan is treated as sufficiently within the creditor's control to be transferred incidentally with land. Baal HaMaor holds that only property wrongfully withheld against the owner's will is outside his possession, while an ordinary debt expected to be repaid can be consecrated. Under that view the inability to transfer a debt by sudar is merely because coins do not pass through barter, so if incidental acquisition works, odaita should work as well. Our practical law follows Ramban that a loan was given to be spent and is therefore outside the creditor's possession even where the debtor intends to repay. No ordinary acquisition can attach to it. Odaita therefore cannot transfer the debt either. Baal HaIttur's ruling is correct. This also explains Tosafot's discussion of the three party arrangement. If that arrangement worked only with the debtor's consent, then an ordinary deposit willingly held could already be transferred by standard mechanisms, including odaita. The special enactment would be needed chiefly where the holder denies the deposit and thereby makes it property outside the owner's possession. If the enactment works even against the holder's will, its necessity is clear because it reaches property otherwise incapable of ordinary transfer. Rashbatz argues that neither a deposit nor a loan can be transferred by admission in the case of a healthy person, and attempts to prove from Rambam's omission of odaita as a general method. Much of his proof disappears once we distinguish between property susceptible to ordinary acquisition and a loan outside the owner's possession. Rambam's statement that a debt transfers only through a three party arrangement concerns precisely the latter category. There is indeed an early dispute whether odaita works for a healthy person at all, but the Shach has already ruled that the Isur the convert episode is not limited to deathly illness and that the principal authorities accept odaita for a healthy person. Nimukei Yosef similarly reports in the name of the sages of Provence and Ritva's teacher that a person can create a new monetary obligation through the language of admission even where everyone knows he was not previously obligated. This accords with Rambam's law of a person who writes, "I owe you one hundred," and becomes obligated without any prior debt. The same principle appears in the law of emancipation. Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia says that if a healthy master states by way of formal admission that he made his slave free, the slave acquires the monetary freedom immediately, although a separate bill of emancipation is still needed to permit marriage to a free Jewish woman. Odaita transfers the monetary ownership but does not itself replace the ritual divorce style document required to remove the personal prohibition. Rashba likewise indicates that language phrased as past transfer can function through admission without needing a new transfer document. Therefore, once odaita is recognized as a real acquisition, it can affect ritual consequences that depend upon ownership. In the sale of chametz, especially where biblical ownership has already been neutralized through nullification and the sale is needed by rabbinic law, a formal written admission, or an oral admission before two valid witnesses, can effect the sale. Because the acquisition is the admission itself, valid witnesses are integral to establishing the formal admission, not merely a safeguard against later denial.
Odaita itself is an acquisition. Therefore, when a person admits that he owns land and that he transferred movables incidentally with that land, the admission can transfer the movables even if we know in fact that he owns no land. The admission operates as a valid acquisition statement and can vest movables because movables are independently capable of acquisition through sudar, meshichah, and other mechanisms. A lien is different. Movables alone are not independently subject to a continuing lien against third parties. Even if the debtor formally subjects them through sudar, the lien is ineffective unless they are subjected together with land in the recognized manner. Therefore, where we know that he has no land, a false admission of land cannot create the land based lien on the movables. This explains the Gemara in Bava Batra. Where the issue is whether movables were subjected together with land, witnesses who know that the debtor never owned land defeat the lien, despite any admission. The admission cannot supply a legal category of lien that does not exist without actual land. An authorization document is different. There the issue is acquisition, not continuing lien. Since movables themselves are capable of acquisition, odaita can operate upon them even if the stated land does not truly exist. Thus the same admission can be effective for acquisition while ineffective for creating a land dependent lien. This distinction resolves the Tosafot difficulty.
