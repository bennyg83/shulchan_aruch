Shmuel says that the property of a non-Jew is like ownerless desert land once the money reaches the non-Jew, because the non-Jew withdraws from ownership while the Jew does not acquire until the deed reaches him. The Rashbam explains this according to the ordinary Jewish law that money alone does not complete a land sale where a deed is customary. The Rambam, Rashba, and Raavad, however, understand that a Jew buying from a non-Jew requires a deed in every case because the non-Jew is coercive and the buyer does not rely upon payment alone. On that approach, the matter does not depend on whether money would suffice in an analogous transaction between Jews. The Beit Yosef derived several rulings from the Rashbam, including that rental for a fixed term is acquired without a deed and that an express condition to acquire with money alone is effective. Those rulings are difficult according to the Rambam's approach, since the concern about the non-Jew's coercion applies equally to sale and rental, and an individual's confidence cannot nullify the general presumption. The Rosh's advice that a prudent buyer should take possession as ownerless property after paying also proves that mere subjective reliance is insufficient. The Rama later cites the Rashba that possession does not help, which accords with the Rambam and not the Rashbam. He should therefore not have first cited rulings that follow only the Rashbam. Since the Rambam, Rashba, and Raavad are the majority, the matter requires study.
