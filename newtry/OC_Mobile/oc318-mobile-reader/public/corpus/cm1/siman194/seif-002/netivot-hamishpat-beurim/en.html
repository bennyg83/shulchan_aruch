Even if the first person took possession. See Sema, seif katan 8, through the words, "What harm is there if he relies on this act to acquire?" At first glance this is unclear, because the person who already paid can likewise intend to acquire by chazakah. Sema must mean that the payer's later chazakah ordinarily is not performed with a distinct intent to acquire from ownerless property. If he had such intent, he would certainly acquire, as Sema himself concludes. Rather, he intends to complete the original sale. Since he already paid, his mind says: if the non Jew is not violent, I already acquired through the money; if he is violent, perhaps this chazakah will not protect me either. His act therefore lacks a fresh, unambiguous intent to acquire from ownerless property and resembles one who hoes a convert's property thinking it is already his. A different person, however, who has no earlier sale, enters specifically to acquire through this chazakah. Therefore, if the first buyer performed chazakah before paying, the chazakah works: at that point it is his first and obvious act of acquisition, and even if the non Jew may later prove violent there is no harm in intending now to acquire through a valid kinyan. Tosafot in Bava Batra 34 say similarly.
And he gives the first buyer the money. See Sema, seif katan 10, who explains that the land is in the later possessor's hands as though pledged for the first buyer's money. Since the first Jew did not acquire, his money became a debt owed by the non Jew if the land failed to reach him. He paid on the understanding that if the field did not become his, the money would remain a debt and the field would secure it. Thus, although the non Jew's property became ownerless, a creditor can collect from property acquired from ownerlessness, as explained in siman 245. According to this rationale the later possessor must repay all the money actually advanced, not merely the field's market value, because the creditor can say, "To me it is worth the full amount," as in simanim 109 and 115. Other authorities give a different rationale: the later possessor must pay because he was enriched through the first buyer's payment. Under that reason he pays only the value of the enrichment. This creates a difference for movable property. Shach, seif katan 4, writes that if money was given to a non Jew for movable property before meshichah, another person can likewise acquire it as ownerless. According to Sema's pledge rationale, the later holder need not reimburse the first buyer because an oral creditor cannot collect movable property that passed to another, and ownerless acquisition has the law of encumbered property. Even if the seller intended the movable item as security, until the pledge reaches the creditor's hand no pledge right arises. This is like designating an ox as an apoteiki and later selling it. Tumim questions even court-imposed pledges that were temporarily returned to the debtor. Here, where the pledge was never acquired, collection is certainly unavailable. But under the enrichment rationale, the later possessor must at least pay the object's value even with movable property. The point requires study.
And responsibility remains on the non-Jewish seller. See Sema, seif katan 11. Although Mechaber's word "responsibility" might seem to include the case where the property is later taken by a true owner or an earlier creditor, that cannot be correct. If an earlier creditor initially collected the field from the second possessor and the second possessor paid him money to redeem it, and afterward a true owner or still earlier creditor removed the field, it is inconceivable that the first creditor may keep the money he received while the later possessor loses both field and payment. The payment was made on the assumption that the creditor truly had a lien on this field. Once it is shown that the field was stolen or subject to an earlier lien, the payment is mistaken and must be returned. The buyer cannot necessarily recover that second payment from the original seller, because ordinary sale warranty covers one loss, not unlimited additional redemptions. Even if the seller would owe it, where he is insolvent there is no reason the earlier creditor may keep money received by mistake. The same follows under the enrichment rationale: if a still earlier owner takes the field, the first creditor was not enriched by the payer's money in a legally sustainable way. Fruits consumed before later dispossession may be deducted under the enrichment rationale because there was actual benefit; under the pledge rationale, perhaps not, since the field itself was never subject to the first creditor at all. The issue requires study. Accordingly, Mechaber's statement that responsibility is on the non-Jewish seller means only that if the non Jew later forcibly refuses to give either the land or the money to the first payer, the later Jewish possessor is not required to refund the redemption money already paid to the first payer. The later possessor paid to remove the first buyer's claim and that lien was thereby extinguished. If the non Jew afterward wrongs the first buyer, the first buyer must pursue the non Jew. Darkei Moshe contains a scribal error and should read accordingly. Nimukei Yosef's statement that the return of money is conditional if the non Jew later takes the property means that the non-Jewish seller is the one who must return the money.
A non Jew who had a pledge. See Sema, seif katan 9. I am very surprised by his statement that even if the non Jew did not die the same law applies. Rashbam in Bava Batra 54 explains that a non Jew's acquisition is by money and once he receives the money the sale is complete for him and he cannot retract. Tosafot, although disputing details of Rashbam's explanation, likewise rely on the fact that non Jews have a recognized money acquisition, as shown by Efron. This implies that if the non Jew himself did not complete any kinyan, the property would not become ownerless. Here the second Jew received the item only by a document, and a document is not a kinyan for a non Jew even in land, all the more so in movable property. Why should the pledge become ownerless? Nevertheless, the practical result can still be correct for another reason. The second Jew cannot sue the first Jew, because he never acquired title from the non Jew and the first Jew can say, "You are not my litigant." The first Jew, who received the pledge permissibly as a deposit, need not return it to the non Jew merely to help the second Jew. This resembles permitted withholding of a non-Jewish debt where there is no chillul Hashem. Even entrusted property can enter that category, as Chelkat Mechokek notes in Even HaEzer siman 28. Sema's further statement that Shimon may tell the non Jew that Reuven did not give him the item is more difficult. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 114 indicate that even where one may technically withhold a non-Jewish debt, witnesses who volunteer to testify and thereby cause loss act improperly. Here, however, the non Jew gave Shimon the item as a gift. A gift is comparable to sale, because presumably he received some benefit from the recipient. Reuven's refusal to return the pledge therefore directly injures Shimon. By informing the non Jew, Shimon is protecting himself from loss rather than gratuitously causing Reuven harm. It may even be permissible for Shimon to petition the non Jew to recover the item and give it to him. If the non Jew later performs a valid kinyan transferring the object to Shimon, it further requires study whether Shimon may take it directly from Reuven, since until then it remained the non Jew's property and was capable of valid transfer. This differs from movable property already denied by a debtor, which can no longer be transferred in the same manner.
