Because a non-Jew neither acquires land from a Jew nor transfers land to a Jew except by deed, if a Jew bought a field from a non-Jew and paid the price, and before he took possession another Jew entered and took possession in the manner used to acquire ownerless property, the latter acquires it. Rama: Some say that even if the first purchaser had already taken possession, the second acquires, because the first purchaser does not rely upon the acquisition without a deed. This appears correct. If the first took possession before paying, however, he acquires according to all opinions. The second must reimburse the first for the purchase price. Once the non-Jew received the money, he relinquished his ownership, while the Jew did not acquire until the deed reached him. The land therefore became like ownerless wilderness property, acquired by the first person to take possession. The first purchaser's recourse is against the non-Jewish seller. Rama: The second purchaser is nevertheless called wicked. Even so, if the first attempts to use the non-Jewish authorities to remove the land from the second, he is considered an informer, since the second reimburses him. This applies where there is no established royal law. If the law of that kingdom provides that land belongs only to the person named in the deed, or to the one who paid the price, or has another defined rule, the royal law is followed. If a non-Jew had a pledge or deposit in Reuven's possession and transferred it to Shimon by deed, Reuven acquires what is in his possession. The non-Jew relinquished it when he transferred it, while Shimon does not acquire movable property until meshichah; it is therefore ownerless and Reuven acquires it as one acquires ownerless property.
