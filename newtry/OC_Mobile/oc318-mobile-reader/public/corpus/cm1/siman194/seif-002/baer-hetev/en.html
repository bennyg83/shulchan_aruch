The money. This is Rambam's view and that of those who follow him. The reason is that although the buyer did not acquire the property, it remains in his hand in the nature of a pledge until the seller returns his money. The Rosh and Tur, however, hold that the possessor need not give him the money, since the property did not initially come into his hand as a pledge. Ibid.
The seller. Meaning, even if the second party has already paid the first party, the first party bears no responsibility. Accordingly, if the non-Jew later comes and removes the property from the current possessor, the first party need not return the money. This is how the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe explain the Maggid Mishneh and Nimukei Yosef there. See there. Ibid.
Wicked. The Beit Yosef writes that the two matters depend upon one another. Since the current possessor must pay the buyer, the case is not comparable to finding ownerless property, but rather to the rule of a poor person pursuing a cake, where another may not preempt him. If, however, he would not have to reimburse the buyer, it would be like a find, to which the rule of one who is already pursuing the opportunity does not apply, as stated in siman 237. Ibid.

His money. The Rosh writes that the current possessor need not return the buyer's money. Nevertheless, the buyer may not sue the non-Jew, and if he does so and thereby causes the non-Jew to remove the field from the possessor, the buyer is deemed a moser. The Tur likewise cites this in the Rosh's name. See there. Ibid.
The king. See Maaseh Chiya, siman 12, which writes that there are several distinctions in this matter. Rashach, part 2, siman 117, likewise writes that this rule applies specifically in such a case, where the land itself is acquired by money and the only reason a deed is ordinarily required is that the buyer's mind is not settled without it.
