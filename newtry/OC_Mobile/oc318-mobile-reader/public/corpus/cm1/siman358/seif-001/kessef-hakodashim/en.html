Whenever they say to conceal it, it is prohibited. Regarding the rule that whenever sellers say to conceal the purchase it is prohibited, it appears that an excuse does not ordinarily help. For example, a craftsman may claim that he fears people will wrongly suspect him of taking surplus materials from his work and therefore does not want the transaction publicized. Since Chazal stated the rule without qualification, such explanations should not ordinarily permit the purchase. Where, however, the seller is a non-Jew and it is certain that the concealment concerns property taken only from another non-Jew, the matter requires analysis. A non-Jew is bound by the Noahide laws against theft and injustice, so lifnei iver could in principle apply. But if he can readily sell the goods to another Jew or non-Jew, the classic two-sides-of-the-river condition may be absent. There are also cases of indirect facilitation, which are more lenient. For example, non-Jewish weavers' wives may bring wheat or other goods to Jewish homes and ask that the sale be concealed. Where the husband's own conduct regarding leftover thread is the only established irregularity, we should not automatically presume broader theft by the wife in unrelated goods. We do not establish people as thieves beyond the specific conduct actually shown, especially where ordinary occupational practice can explain the issue. There may therefore be cases that amount only to indirect facilitation, and further leniencies may arise where the goods came into the seller's hands permissibly or where there is conscious despair of recovery. Even so, I warned that although there are situations in which competent authorities may find grounds to justify existing practice, avoiding such purchases is the proper course. Forest guards who privately sell wood present another possible distinction. They often lawfully possess a large quantity of wood for their own use, like other villagers, and may ask for secrecy only because they also take more than their official allowance. It is possible that the wood being sold came from the quantity lawfully taken, while the excess remained at home. Since the great majority of what they possess may have been taken lawfully, ordinary majority principles and compounded doubts may apply. If the wood has undergone a substantial physical change, the purchaser's right becomes stronger. In addition, where it is known that if this buyer refuses there are many others who will buy, that further reduces the concern. I have written elsewhere additional grounds used to justify some existing communal practices.
