We do not concern ourselves with the unqualified wording. See Beit Yosef, who gives two interpretations of the Rosh's phrase "unqualified." One is that the deed itself was written without the usual publicity clause. The second is that the donor simply told the witnesses, "Write," without expressly telling them to write it publicly, yet they may still do so. Beit Yosef prefers the second interpretation. See there. This is also implied by Shulchan Aruch here and above, siman 61, seif 5. Sema, seif katan 10, likewise explains the rule as meaning that the donor did not expressly tell the witnesses to write it publicly. It follows that where the deed itself is written without the required form, the defect remains and the document is invalid even nowadays. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 139, who discusses this at length, including why the Mechaber states the rule as "there is one who says." Regarding his case, he concludes that as a practical matter it is difficult to remove land from the possession of heirs. Even according to Rosh, Beit Yosef's settled interpretation may be that an unqualified written deed does not acquire. Responsa Maharit, part 3, siman 54, and Responsa Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 25, likewise reach that conclusion, and Mishpetei Shmuel writes that even though the matter remains doubtful, the land should remain where it presently stands. Rabbi Akiva Eiger therefore concludes that compromise is certainly proper, with the compromise weighted more heavily toward the heirs. See there:
