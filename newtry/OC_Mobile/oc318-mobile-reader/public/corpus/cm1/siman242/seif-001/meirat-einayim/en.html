If he was coerced to give, it is not a gift. Only in a coerced sale do we say that because of the coercion together with receipt of money, the seller resolves and transfers, unless he delivered a modaah, as explained in siman 205. With a gift no money is received. Therefore even a simple disclosure showing that the donor is not pleased with the gift is sufficient to void it, as the Tur and Mechaber conclude here and in siman 205. When they begin by saying, "One who gives a gift because of coercion," they do not mean only severe coercion such as beating or physical suffering. Whenever it is clear that he does not wish to give, there is necessarily some external pressure causing him to do so without full consent. This is why they conclude that a modaah can invalidate the gift even though the witnesses do not know the precise coercion. At first glance this seems difficult, because earlier they appeared to require knowledge of the coercion. The Ir Shushan gives an extended explanation, but much of it is unnecessary.
Even if he accepted property warranty in the deed. The Tur writes: Even if he accepted warranty, it is not a gift because he was coerced to do so. The Mechaber's omission of those final words could lead to error. One might think that if the donor was not separately coerced to accept warranty, the warranty proves that the gift was voluntary, as Rama writes in siman 205 that a gift containing warranty has the law of a sale. But the Tur's phrase "because he was coerced to do so" sounds as though coercion to give ordinarily includes coercion to write warranty. More likely, the Rivash and Rama's rule that a gift containing warranty is treated as a sale concerns a dispute about the nature of the transaction. The donor says he gave under coercion, while the recipient says the property came to him through a sale and that the document used gift language only for advantages such as bar metzra or the broader rights of a gift. In that setting, the presence of warranty supports the claim that the transaction was actually a sale, because gift documents ordinarily do not contain warranty. The Tur and Mechaber state this explicitly in siman 175. Here, by contrast, even the recipient admits that the property came as a gift rather than a sale. Warranty therefore does not transform the nature of the transaction. See also siman 205.
