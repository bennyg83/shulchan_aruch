Seif 3. Every gift, whether to a healthy person, etc. Note: At first glance it would appear that if the donor did not tell the witnesses, "Write it publicly," the witnesses are prohibited from writing a gift document at all. This would parallel siman 205, where witnesses may not write a modaah unless they recognize the coercion, lest an erroneous beit din rely upon the document. Here too one might fear that an erroneous beit din will not know that the document must state that it was written publicly. But if this were true, why is a gift document invalid when the required public wording is absent? We should rely on the presumption that witnesses acted properly. Since they would have been prohibited from writing without an instruction to publicize the gift, we should presume that the donor did instruct them, even if the document omitted that fact. According to what I wrote in siman 39, namely that the Mechaber's ruling in siman 205 that a modaah is ineffective unless it records "we recognized his coercion" is itself only a doubt in law, the same doubt should exist here: perhaps omission of the explicit public wording does not invalidate the gift. A practical difference would be that writing the document might suffice because of the legal doubt. However, this cannot be the whole explanation, because in seif 5 the Rama cites Rabbeinu Yonah that even today it is preferable ab initio to write the public wording explicitly. Certainly under the original Talmudic law this would have been required. Yet according to Rabbeinu Yonah's approach to modaah discussed above, the operative facts need not always be stated in the document. One would therefore have expected the Rama to distinguish the cases. The correct answer is that this case differs from the fear of an erroneous beit din. It is similar to siman 255, where witnesses may write even without investigating all background facts because we do not fear judicial error in the same manner. More fundamentally, there is no reason here to protect the donor from his own chosen risk. The concern about an erroneous beit din applies when the document could improperly harm another person. Here the donor himself is asking the witnesses to write the gift, while apparently seeking secrecy. If a later beit din errs because the public wording is omitted, the resulting loss falls upon the donor who requested the document. If he does not protect himself from that risk, we need not be more concerned for him than he is for himself. This appears simple and correct.
