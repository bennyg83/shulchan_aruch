When he claimed. Only where both witnesses testify to loans do we require that the claimant sue for both. But if they testify to admissions, or even if one testifies to a loan and the other to an admission at different times, their testimony combines. We can say that the defendant admitted before the second witness to the very money that had already been lent to him. Even if the date of the admission precedes the date of the loan, we say that the lender did not happen to have the money available to lend on that earlier day, and the defendant admitted on the basis of his promise to lend it afterward. It appears that the same rule applies where the claimant simply sues for two hundreds and says that he has forgotten when the loans were made, whether together or one after the other; this is likewise not a contradiction. Beit Yosef writes that although one witness is ordinarily ineffective where another contradicts him, if the matter is capable of clarification the first is believed. The Sema explains that this means the second witness says, "I will clarify my words"; until he does so, the first remains believed. See siman 70 and the beginning of siman 75 regarding whether a claimant must clarify his claim. The Shach disagrees: why should the first be believed more than the second? Rather, the case must be where the first says that he will clarify his words and the second refuses. The Shach also questions the underlying rule. It was said in matters of prohibition because one witness is ordinarily believed, and only a contradicting witness prevents reliance upon him; where the matter can be clarified, we may rely on him stringently until clarification. In monetary law, however, no such principle was ever established. The primary practical rule is therefore that in monetary matters there is no distinction between something capable of clarification and something incapable of clarification. The words of Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe require study.
