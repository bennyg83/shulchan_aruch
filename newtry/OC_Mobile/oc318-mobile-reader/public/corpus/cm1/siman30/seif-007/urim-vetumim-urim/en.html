He claimed two maneh. In admission followed by admission, and likewise admission followed by a loan, the plaintiff need not claim two maneh. It may be one maneh that the borrower admitted several times or admitted after borrowing. But where a loan follows an admission, Sema also holds that one need not claim two maneh, because we may say the earlier admission referred to the maneh that would later be lent. In Tumim I wrote that, as practical law, money should not be extracted from the possessor on that basis. If the lender says, “I lent him two maneh and I do not know whether at one time or at separate times,” the testimony is also valid. The same is true if he makes no definite claim at all but sues only on the basis of the witnesses, because there is no contradiction from the claimant’s own mouth. Sema, citing Maharik, further discusses a case where one witness is contradicted by another but says the matter can be verified. Meirat Einayim gives a strained explanation. Shach correctly connects it to Kiddushin 66, where one witness says a person has a blemish, another contradicts him, and the first says, “Remove his clothes and the blemish will be seen.” In Tumim I explain that such a witness has the force of one witness in monetary law to impose an oath, not the force of two witnesses. Shach wants to distinguish ritual law from monetary law, but I see no reason to distinguish regarding obligation of an oath.
And they erred concerning the intercalation of the month, etc. One witness thought the preceding month had twenty-nine days and therefore counted the present date as the third; the other thought it had thirty days and therefore counted it as the second. This applies only where they did not also contradict each other concerning the day of the week. If they did, that contradiction invalidates the testimony. Sema. In Tumim I conclude that even where there are two witnesses we attribute the difference to an error in fixing the month. Thus if they said the event was on the second of the month and were discredited by witnesses saying, “You were with us then,” the substantive testimony can still remain valid because perhaps they erred in the calendrical date. This is unlike Maharsha.
And if the matter became clear, etc. For example, they differ by two days, one saying the third and the other the fifth, or they testify after most of the month has passed, when everyone generally knows the calendar and such mistakes are uncommon. In Tumim I conclude that even regarding repayment we say that after most of the month people do not usually err. This limitation concerns the time when the witnesses saw the event. If they saw it before most of the month had passed but testified in beit din only later, the earlier calendrical mistake may have become fixed in their memory, and we can still attribute the present discrepancy to that original mistake. Shev Yaakov, though a great scholar, erred in this point. I further explain that if after most of the month both witnesses agree on the day of the week but differ on the date of the month, their monetary testimony remains valid because formal interrogation is not required. But if they first stated the day of the week and afterward gave dates of the month that contradict the earlier statement, their testimony is void because they retracted from their first testimony, assuming the change was made within the period in which retraction is effective. Sema adds that contradiction concerning hours also invalidates because time is among the seven interrogations. I explain in Tumim that even though Rambam holds a person ordinarily errs by only one hour, the practical result can still allow a broader margin. If one witness says the beginning of the second hour and the other says the end of the third, the testimony can stand by placing the event at the beginning of the third, one witness erring forward and the other backward. Likewise, for hazamah the later witnesses must establish that the first witnesses were with them through the relevant wider time range, because the discrediting witnesses themselves may err by an hour. I further discuss the first hour around sunrise and the differing views of Tosafot and Rambam. All this concerns extracting money. For repayment witnesses, broader discrepancies can still leave the debtor in possession because of doubt. Thus even if one says repayment occurred at two and the other at five, the burden of proof can remain on the claimant. Likewise, if one says five and one seven, Rambam’s view that people can err by two hours may allow the event to have been at six, producing doubt and leaving the money with the possessor.

He took possession of land, etc. Sema says that if one witness testifies that in Nisan the owner gave Shimon the land as a gift in his presence and in the presence of another witness who has since died, and another witness testifies that the owner gave Shimon the same land in Tishrei in his presence and in the presence of another deceased witness, the two witnesses do not join. According to the second witness, the second gift would imply that the first gift had become ineffective, like two deeds concerning the same field, so there is no single joined transaction. Rabbeinu Yerucham. In Tumim I limit this: it applies where, at the time of the Tishrei gift, the other witness to the Nisan gift was still alive. If that witness had already died, one can say the owner repeated the gift only because the first gift no longer had surviving witnesses, not because he meant to cancel it.
