Meisharim writes that if one witness says, "In my presence, and in the presence of another witness who has since died, the giver gave this property in Nisan," and another witness says the same regarding a gift in Tishrei, the case resembles two gift documents concerning one property, where the later gift cancels the earlier one. Consequently, only one witness supports the later gift. Beit Yosef calls this astonishing. Darkei Moshe says he does not understand the objection. The recipient himself admits that the two single witnesses cannot both be describing one effective gift, and once the later disposition cancels the earlier, only one witness remains for the operative disposition, exactly as in siman 240 and Ketubot. Bach attempts to reconcile by saying Beit Yosef understood both witnesses to testify that the same gift was made to Reuven, whereas Darkei Moshe dealt with the first gift to Reuven and the later gift to Shimon. That cannot be correct. Darkei Moshe plainly understood both witnesses to speak about a gift to the same person, but reasoned that if the second gift cancels the first there remains only one witness to the operative disposition, exactly as in siman 240 and Ketubot. Beit Yosef's objection appears instead to be that the rule, "the second deed cancels the first," was said specifically about two documents. There one can say the recipient admits the first document was forged or invalid, as Rashi explains. Even if the signature is independently recognizable, perhaps it was skillfully forged. But where living witnesses testify orally to two events, we do not readily say that the recipient thereby admits the first witness is false or disqualified. Tosafot indeed rejects Rashi's implication that the first set of witnesses themselves become disqualified merely because the recipient relies on the later document. According to Tosafot's second explanation, only the signature on the first document is undermined as possibly forged. Thus Beit Yosef can maintain that the cancellation rule belongs to documentary evidence, not necessarily to two oral testimonies. Rama may follow Tosafot's first explanation, under which reliance upon the later act can indeed undermine the first witnesses even orally.
