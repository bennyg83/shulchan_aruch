One says on the second of the month and one says on the third, etc. Sema writes that before most of the month has passed, we can reconcile the witnesses by saying one knew the preceding month had been full and the other did not. After most of the month, however, everyone already knows the fixed date and we do not ordinarily attribute the difference to this mistake. Rosh was asked about a get in which the date was written as the twentieth of the month and also as Wednesday, when in fact Wednesday was the twenty-first. He ruled that the get was valid. He writes that if even in capital law we follow the majority and say witnesses can err concerning the intercalation of the month, all the more so here, where the document itself shows the mistake because Wednesday could not have been the twentieth. He adds that although this occurred after most of the month, there is no difficulty because the contradiction itself proves that an error occurred, and people are not ordinarily mistaken regarding the weekday. At first glance Rosh's words are difficult. In capital cases the witnesses are also asked the weekday, because it is one of the seven formal interrogations. If one says the second of the month and another the third, and they nevertheless agree on the weekday, the same internal contradiction is present. Yet after most of the month the ordinary rule is that we do not attribute the discrepancy to calendrical error. Why is the get different? The answer is that the legal effect of their later statement differs. Tosafot explain that where witnesses first state the numbered day of the month and then state the weekday, the latter statement can be viewed as a retraction from the earlier date. Tosafot themselves hold that once the witness has testified, he cannot retract, even within the process of interrogation in the manner they discuss. But as I explained above, before the formal interrogations are completed a witness can still clarify or retract according to Rambam, Tur, and the Yerushalmi. Even so, in capital cases the problem remains. The Torah requires all seven interrogations. If the numbered date is abandoned through retraction, one of the required interrogations is missing, and the testimony is invalid even if the witnesses personally remain fit. A get is different. The numerical day of the month is not one of seven Torah interrogations necessary for the validity of the get. If the witnesses' specification of Wednesday demonstrates that the numerical date was mistaken, the get can remain valid through the weekday. The exact day of the month can simply be treated as corrected. This explains Rosh's kal vachomer. In capital law we go so far as to rely on the ordinary calendrical mistake even though a missing interrogation would invalidate the testimony. In a get, where there is no such requirement and where the weekday itself demonstrates the correction, there is even stronger reason to preserve the document. This also explains why Rosh can validate the get after most of the month. Even if we no longer say there is a normal majority of people who confuse the month's intercalation, the document itself cannot be read literally in both respects. Either the numerical day or the weekday is mistaken. Since error concerning the weekday is less common and the weekday identifies the correct calendar day, the numerical date can be treated as the mistaken component. No capital-law interrogation is thereby lost. In civil testimony, our practical law generally does not require the seven interrogations. Therefore a similar internal correction can sometimes work, especially while the witnesses are still within the permissible period of clarification. However, one must distinguish the order of the statements. If they first say the day of the month and afterward, within a legally effective retraction period, say the matching weekday, the latter can clarify the former. If they first give the weekday and then give a conflicting numerical date after their testimony is already fixed, the latter cannot automatically repair the earlier testimony. The rule of "once he has testified, he cannot testify differently" remains relevant. There is another question: when the Gemara says that after most of the month people do not err concerning the intercalation, does it mean no one ordinarily errs at all, or only that there is no longer a majority who err? If it is merely the absence of a majority, then several practical differences follow. In capital law we cannot execute from doubt, and in monetary law we cannot extract money from an established possessor from doubt. Thus, if witnesses offered by the defendant differ by one day after most of the month, perhaps the mere possibility that they erred could still prevent the claimant from extracting. Likewise, in kiddushin, where the consequence is prohibitory and we often act stringently in uncertainty, one might have to consider the possibility of calendrical error even after most of the month. Maharsha in Sanhedrin appears to understand the rule more strongly. He explains the need for certain interrogations on the assumption that after most of the month witnesses are not treated as capable of the intercalation error, so hazamah can operate. If the matter remained a balanced doubt, we could not impose the penalty of hazamah either, because perhaps they had merely erred. This suggests that the legal presumption after most of the month is sufficiently firm that we do not preserve the testimony through that excuse. Tosafot in Pesachim, however, show that ordinary error must still be carefully distinguished from a later attempt to change the content of testimony. Once witnesses have given a fixed statement, they cannot add another detail solely so that the first statement becomes subject to hazamah if the added detail would amount to retraction. The matter therefore depends not only on whether a mistake is conceivable, but on whether the original testimony, at the time it was given, is legally interpreted through that recognized mistake. A further dispute concerns two witnesses who agree with one another in the same mistaken date. Mahari Beirav, as discussed by Maharlbach, was inclined to say that the principle of ordinary mistake should not be applied to two witnesses who independently agree. Falsehood does not ordinarily create agreement, and if both say the same date perhaps their agreement itself makes a common accidental error less likely. Maharlbach disagrees. Where there is an external cause for mistake, such as uncertainty whether the preceding month was full or deficient, the same cause can lead two people to the same error. There is no reason to say a common calendrical misunderstanding can affect only one witness. The latter position appears more persuasive. Rosh's responsum about the get necessarily concerns two signatories, yet he readily attributes the mistaken date to calendar confusion. The poskim in siman 43 likewise discuss mistakes in dates of documents without saying that the fact two witnesses signed the same date prevents the possibility of error. Maharsha nevertheless introduces another distinction. Where one witness says the second and another the third, the alternative date is actually present in the testimony: one of them has identified the third. It is therefore reasonable to say the one who said the second was mistaken about the month's intercalation. But if both witnesses say the second and later a set of zomemim say they were elsewhere on the second, perhaps the original witnesses cannot answer that they really meant the third, because no one originally testified that the event occurred on the third. Maharsha uses this to explain how hazamah can still exist even in a calendar period where ordinary date confusion is possible. I find this distinction difficult. Tosafot in Pesachim discuss the range of temporal error that refuting witnesses must exclude. If a witness can ordinarily be mistaken by a certain amount, the zomemim must say the witness was with them throughout the period the testimony could reasonably mean. The very law of "give him all his possible error" shows that the law does not always bind the witness to the exact conventional time-label he pronounced. If a recognized human error can shift the legal meaning of his words, it should matter even when no other original witness happened to identify the alternate time. Perhaps Maharsha means only that for purposes of saving the defendant from conviction we can consider a possible alternate time even if the witnesses did not state it, but for purposes of imposing the special punishment of hazamah upon the original witnesses we require greater certainty. The Torah's interrogation system was given to make the testimony capable of refutation, but not necessarily to maximize the punishment of the witnesses. That distinction is plausible, although it does not resolve every passage. In practical civil law, the simpler rule is this: before most of the month, a one-day discrepancy of the type produced by a full versus deficient prior month is treated as ordinary error and the testimony can be reconciled. After most of the month, we do not ordinarily use that explanation unless the testimony or document itself contains an independent feature that demonstrates which component is mistaken, as in Rosh's get case. If the witnesses are being used merely to retain money and the factual circumstances leave a genuine unresolved doubt, the burden-of-proof rule remains relevant. But one should not use a remote possibility of calendrical mistake after most of the month to extract money or validate otherwise contradictory testimony. This is the correct practical distinction.
They erred regarding the intercalation of the month. Sema writes that after most of the month has passed we no longer attribute a discrepancy between witnesses to confusion over whether the preceding month was full or deficient, because by then the fixed date of the month is generally known. Rosh, however, writes in responsum klal 45, siman 25, concerning a get in which the date was written as the twentieth of the month and also as Wednesday, when in fact Wednesday was the twenty-first, that the get is valid. Rosh argues: if in capital law we follow the majority and say witnesses can err concerning the intercalation of the month, all the more so here, where the date written in the get itself demonstrates that one component is mistaken, because Wednesday cannot have been the twentieth. Rosh further says that although the mistake occurred after most of the month, this does not invalidate the get, since the witnesses' own wording proves that they erred, and people do not ordinarily err concerning the weekday. His words require explanation. In capital cases the witnesses are also asked the weekday, for that is one of the seven formal interrogations. When one says the second of the month and another says the third, if they later agree on the weekday, that too creates an internal indication of which statement was mistaken. Yet after most of the month we do not ordinarily say they erred concerning the intercalation. Why, then, is Rosh more lenient in a get? It appears that Rosh follows the point Tosafot make, which I have cited several times above: when the witnesses subsequently state the weekday, that later statement can function as a retraction or clarification of the earlier numbered date. Tosafot hold that once testimony has been completed, one cannot retract. I explained above that according to Rambam and Tur, before the process of interrogation is completed the witnesses can still retract or clarify. Even so, in capital law the testimony can remain invalid, because the seven formal interrogations are Torah requirements. If the numbered day of the month is discarded, one of the required interrogations is missing, and the testimony is deficient even if the witnesses themselves are not personally disqualified. A get is different. A get does not require the seven interrogations for its validity. If the witnesses' statement that it was Wednesday shows that the numerical day written was wrong, the numbered date can simply be treated as corrected. The weekday remains, and there is no Torah requirement that both calendrical descriptions appear as independent interrogations. This is Rosh's kal vachomer. In capital law, where the consequence of a missing interrogation is severe, the Gemara nevertheless relies on the common error of calendar calculation where a majority supports it. In a get, where there is no such seven-interrogation requirement and the document itself discloses the mistake, there is even more reason to preserve the document. This also explains why Rosh validates the get after most of the month. Even if after most of the month we no longer have the ordinary majority that people err concerning the prior month's intercalation, the two statements in the get cannot both be true. Since weekday error is less likely and the weekday objectively identifies the calendar date, the numerical date can be treated as the mistaken element. Alternatively, the later or more reliable statement can be viewed as a correction of the earlier one before the document became legally fixed through signature. In civil testimony, because we generally do not require formal interrogation and examination, a similar clarification can work where the witnesses are still within the period in which their statements may be corrected. But the order matters. If they first state the numbered date and afterward, within the period of permissible clarification, give a weekday that fixes the correct date, the later statement can clarify the earlier one. If the order is reversed and the numbered date comes after the weekday in a way that would constitute a retraction from completed testimony, the law of "once he has testified, he cannot testify differently" can block the attempted correction. In Rosh's get case, the ordinary drafting formula may have listed the weekday before the numbered date. Nevertheless, the get remains valid from another direction as well. If the witnesses' final written date is treated as a retraction from the weekday, then the get is not necessarily antedated. The question is only which part of the formula accurately identifies the day. Since the instrument does not depend on all seven interrogations, the internal contradiction does not destroy it. Either way, the get can be preserved. There remains an important doubt regarding the meaning of the Gemara's rule after most of the month. When Chazal say that after most of the month people no longer err concerning the calendar, does this mean that as a legal matter we treat the date as certain, or only that there is no longer a majority of people who make the mistake? If the latter, the practical consequences differ. Suppose the defendant brings repayment witnesses who disagree by one day after most of the month. If there remains a genuine minority possibility that the discrepancy reflects calendar error, perhaps we should not extract money from the defendant, because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Likewise, in kiddushin, where we may have to act stringently in doubt, perhaps the woman remains subject to the possibility that the witnesses merely erred even though the majority would not. Maharsha in Sanhedrin, Perek Hayu Bodkin, appears to understand the rule more strongly. He explains why a particular date-interrogation creates testimony capable of hazamah. If after most of the month the date remained genuinely doubtful because perhaps the witnesses erred, the original witnesses could not be punished as zomemim with certainty. The fact that Maharsha allows the fixed date to support hazamah indicates that after most of the month we treat the calendar error as legally unavailable, not merely less common. Tosafot in Pesachim, however, emphasize that hazamah must account for the ordinary range of error recognized for testimony. If the law itself says a person can be mistaken within a certain period, the refuting witnesses must place him with them throughout that entire period. Therefore the precise status of calendar error is central. Another major dispute is whether the same error can be attributed to two witnesses who agree with one another. Mahari Beirav, as discussed in the responsa of Maharlbach, was inclined to say that we do not ordinarily attribute the same mistake to two witnesses acting together. Falsehood does not naturally produce identical agreement, and where both witnesses say the same thing, their mutual agreement itself reduces the likelihood that both accidentally erred. Maharlbach disagrees. Where the mistake results from a common external cause, such as uncertainty whether the preceding month had thirty days, there is no reason two people cannot share the same mistaken assumption. The same cause can mislead both. I believe Maharlbach's position is the stronger one. Rosh's responsum concerning the get necessarily concerns two signatories. Yet he readily says they erred concerning the date. He notes the timing after most of the month but does not say, "In any event two witnesses cannot both make this mistake." This indicates that calendar error can be attributed to two. The same appears below in siman 43, where the poskim discuss incorrectly dated documents and the possibility that the witnesses erred concerning a full or deficient month. The fact that a document necessarily contains two witnesses does not remove the error explanation. Maharsha introduces a different distinction. Where one witness says the second of the month and another says the third, the alternate date is already present within the original evidence: one witness actually said the third. We can therefore say the witness who said the second was mistaken about the prior month's intercalation. But where both original witnesses say the second and later a set of zomemim says, "You were with us on the second," Maharsha suggests we do not answer that perhaps the event truly happened on the third and the original witnesses merely misnamed the date. No original witness said the third. Maharsha uses this to explain how the date interrogation can make the testimony capable of hazamah even before most of the month. This distinction is difficult. Tosafot in Pesachim discuss the rule that refuting witnesses must give the original witnesses their full allowance for ordinary time-error. If a person who says "the second hour" could legally mean a range extending earlier or later, the zomemim must place him with them throughout the entire range. That principle does not depend on another original witness having named the alternate hour. Why, then, should calendar error work differently? One possibility is that Maharsha distinguishes between the standards for saving the defendant and those for punishing the witnesses. The Torah required many interrogations primarily to enable the court to test and, where necessary, save the accused. If there is a plausible alternate date, perhaps the accused cannot be executed even though the witnesses themselves likewise cannot be executed as zomemim unless the alternate range is excluded. The system need not guarantee that every possible ambiguity leads to punishment of one side. This may explain why Rashi says certain interrogations are asked so that the witnesses place the matter carefully in mind and the court can test their precision, rather than solely to maximize the possibility of hazamah. Still, Maharsha's distinction between a date stated by one witness and a date not stated by any witness remains difficult to apply consistently. Maharshal offers another answer: the court asks the weekday so the witnesses cannot later excuse themselves through intercalation error and thereby escape hazamah. But this too raises the question that ordinary recognized error must still be given its proper range. The matter becomes clearer when we examine the Gemara's attempt to identify an eighth formal interrogation. The Gemara asks why the court does not ask the witnesses, "At what point within the hour did the event occur?" It answers that because people err concerning time within an hour, such a question cannot function as an independent formal interrogation. Maharsha asks: even if people can err, why not ask the question so that the range of error given to the witnesses becomes smaller? If the witness says the event was in the first half of the second hour, perhaps the zomemim would need to exclude only a narrower range. This question suggests that the Torah interrogations are not simply any questions that make hazamah somewhat easier. A Torah interrogation must identify a real legal category whose answer can, at least in some circumstances, fix the event without perpetual dependence on a conventional error allowance. This principle helps resolve the broader sugya. The seven interrogations are derived from Scripture. Therefore they must be stable categories applicable across cases. We cannot add a new Torah interrogation merely by dividing conventional time into smaller and smaller units. The division of the day into twelve numbered hours is itself, to a considerable extent, conventional. One could divide the day into twenty-four half-hours, forty-eight quarter-hours, or any other number. Scripture did not command twelve humanly named units as such. The natural division of the day is more basic: before midday and after midday. The sun itself distinguishes them, being in the east before midday and in the west afterward. This is an observable natural boundary, comparable in some measure to sunrise and sunset separating days. When the Gemara speaks of the interrogation "which hour," the Torah category can therefore be understood as identifying the natural portion of the day. Later human convention subdivides that portion into smaller numbered hours so that beit din can question the witnesses more precisely. Once we understand this, the Gemara's refusal to count "how far into the hour" as an eighth interrogation is clear. That question does not identify a new natural category. It merely subdivides an existing temporal interval more finely. One can continue subdividing indefinitely; therefore it is not a separate Torah interrogation. This also explains why the law gives witnesses an allowance for ordinary estimation error within the conventional hours. The Torah's natural temporal category remains intact even though human beings estimate the smaller conventional subdivisions imperfectly. The verse says, "You shall inquire and investigate carefully, and behold, the matter is true and established." The formal interrogation must therefore be capable, at least in some ordinary circumstance, of identifying the event in a definite way. If a proposed "interrogation" always remains subject to another conventional margin of error and can be made ever finer, it is not a distinct Torah interrogation. This also sheds light on Rashi's explanation concerning the weekday and the numbered day of the month. A weekday is bounded by the natural rising and setting of the sun. The numbered day of the month is likewise a stable calendar unit once the month is fixed. The Torah can therefore require both as distinct interrogations, even though before most of the month ordinary people can sometimes miscalculate the numerical date because of uncertainty whether the prior month was full. The question then is not whether the category itself is conventional, but whether the witness's answer in a particular case is subject to an ordinary recognized error. Now we can revisit Rambam's apparent contradiction concerning how many hours a person can err. Rambam writes in Hilchot Edut, chapter 2, that if one witness says the event occurred in the second hour and another in the third, their testimony is valid because a person can err by one hour. Yet in Hilchot Chametz he gives the reason for extending the prohibition earlier in the day in terms that appear to assume a possible error of roughly two hours. In his responsum, cited by Kesef Mishneh, Rambam's concern includes confusing the fifth and seventh hours. How can the same Rambam say in testimony that a person errs only one hour? One attempted resolution follows Mahari Beirav's idea that we do not attribute the same error to two witnesses. On that approach, in testimony, if one says the second and one the third, we minimize the discrepancy by saying the event occurred around the transition into the third hour and each witness erred by one hour in opposite directions. In the laws of chametz, however, the concern applies to the public generally, and around midday error is more common. Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah in Pesachim states that the sixth hour is rabbinically prohibited, and the fifth according to R. Yehudah because on a cloudy day a person can err by an hour. Rambam does not describe the sixth hour itself as requiring the same error calculation. The period around midday is uniquely confusing because the sun is near the middle of the sky. This can explain why broader precautions exist for chametz than the ordinary courtroom assumption concerning witnesses. But the explanation based on two witnesses is not fully satisfactory, because I do not accept Mahari Beirav's proposition that two people cannot make the same ordinary mistake. Maharlbach and Radbaz are stronger on that point. Indeed, if one witness estimates too early because the day is unusually dark and another estimates too late because it is unusually bright, those opposite errors depend on different atmospheric causes. It may be less natural for both causes to operate simultaneously than for two witnesses to share one common mistake. The better approach is to distinguish the standard of proof. In capital testimony we do not execute from a balanced doubt. Rava's question is precisely, "How can we be exacting with these witnesses and kill a person from doubt?" The court may reconcile testimony only where the ordinary human error is sufficiently established, not merely possible. In the laws of chametz, a Torah prohibition can require stringency even where the possibility of error is not a majority in the same way. Thus Chazal can create a preventive buffer for possible error even when a court would not rely on that same possibility to execute a person. This also explains the Gemara's dispute about the sixth hour, when the sun is near the middle of the sky. Some people can err there and some do not. In the prohibition of chametz, doubt itself can justify greater caution. In capital law, a mere possibility does not permit conviction. The Gemara's discussion under Abaye and Rava can be understood accordingly. Abaye is willing to interpret one witness who says the third as meaning the end of the third and one who says the fourth as meaning the beginning of the fourth, thereby preserving the testimony so long as such an interpretation is possible. Rava objects that we cannot execute based on a speculative favorable interpretation of the witnesses. We need a stronger basis, such as an ordinary majority of human error. Thus, when the Gemara asks how the chametz hours fit R. Meir's testimony rule, the assumptions about majority error and mere possible error must be tracked carefully. Rava initially explains R. Yehudah's additional hour as time given to gather wood for burning. On that preliminary answer, one might derive a larger practical buffer. But Rava later abandons that explanation and attributes the rule to a cloudy day, when time-estimation is less reliable. According to the final explanation, Rambam can hold that in ordinary testimony a person is presumed to err by one hour, while in the special cloudy-day concern of chametz Chazal feared a wider practical range. This is strengthened by the reality of timekeeping in the period of Chazal. People did not generally carry precise mechanical instruments marking equal conventional hours. They estimated parts of the day from the sun's position. On a cloudy day, when the sun is obscured, precise statements such as "the fifth hour" and "the seventh hour" are themselves much less meaningful. Therefore the chametz safeguard is not proof that an ordinary witness under normal conditions is presumed to err by two full hours. The practical outcome between Tosafot and Rambam is often the same. If one witness says the event occurred at the beginning of the second hour and another at the end of the third, the testimony can be reconciled by placing the event in the intervening period. According to Tosafot, one speaks of a broader individual error allowance. According to Rambam, one can attribute a smaller error to each witness from opposite sides. The difference becomes more important when testimony is used to retain money rather than extract it. Suppose the borrower brings repayment witnesses and one says the repayment occurred in the third hour while another says the fifth. The lender seeks to collect despite them. The court is not now using the witnesses to extract from a possessor; the borrower uses them to retain. Even if the time statements cannot be reconciled with enough certainty to extract money or impose capital punishment, a reasonable possibility of ordinary estimation error can be sufficient to prevent extraction. The burden of proof remains on the lender. Accordingly, if the one who says "three" could mean the end of the third and the one who says "five" could mean the beginning of the fifth, and the difference lies within the ordinary range of time-estimation, the repayment testimony can be sufficient for the defendant. Even where one says the second and one the fifth, there may be circumstances, especially around the accepted human margin of estimation, in which the possibility is enough to leave the money with the possessor. Similarly, one says the fifth and one the seventh. Around midday, where the sun's position makes conventional hour-estimation more uncertain, the possibility that the event occurred in the sixth can matter for retaining money, even though the same evidence would not suffice to execute or extract. This follows the basic rule: we do not kill from doubt and we do not extract money from doubt. I have not found the later authorities discussing all these precise repayment cases, likely because full interrogation and examination is uncommon in contemporary civil law. But the principles compel the result. Returning to the dispute over common error by two witnesses, the practical rule should be that where an external cause naturally produces the error, we may attribute it to two witnesses as well. There is no sound basis for saying a mistake can exist only in one witness. This is especially clear from documents. Two signatories can both miscalculate a calendar date. The same is true of time estimation on a cloudy day. But where the witnesses' agreement itself concerns a matter not ordinarily subject to shared error, their mutual consistency remains evidence against inventing a mistake merely to preserve testimony. Thus, the law must distinguish a recognized common human error from a speculative one. This principle also answers Maharsha's proof from Tosafot concerning why the weekday interrogation is necessary. Tosafot's question assumes that once witnesses have already testified to the numerical day, a later weekday answer cannot simply be used to alter the testimony if that would amount to retracting completed evidence. The added interrogation therefore has evidentiary significance only within the formal process itself. We do not say that witnesses may first give a testimony not fully capable of hazamah and then, after it is completed, add a new statement that suddenly exposes the first testimony to hazamah. Once testimony is complete, their later addition cannot change its legal character. Consequently, the requirement of the weekday must operate as part of the original interrogation process, not as a later repair. This is why Tosafot's question does not prove that ordinary calendrical error is unavailable in hazamah. It proves only that the court cannot allow a later statement, after the original testimony is fixed, to reshape the legal meaning of what they first said. From all this the practical halachah is as follows. Before most of the month, a one-day discrepancy that is naturally explained by the preceding month's intercalation is treated as ordinary error and the testimony can be reconciled, even where more than one witness is involved. After most of the month, we ordinarily no longer rely on that explanation unless the testimony or document itself supplies a strong internal reason showing which component is mistaken, as in Rosh's get. For time discrepancies, we distinguish the ordinary recognized range of human estimation from mere speculative possibilities. Capital punishment and extraction of money require the stronger standard. Retaining money can follow a genuine doubt because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. The formal Torah interrogations identify stable categories of the event. Humanly conventional subdivisions within those categories do not create endless additional Torah interrogations. Rambam's statement that a witness can err by one hour in testimony and his broader precaution in the laws of chametz are therefore not contradictory. The former concerns ordinary evidentiary estimation under the standards of adjudication; the latter concerns a preventive prohibition, especially under cloudy conditions where time estimation is unusually unreliable. Accordingly, where one witness says the beginning of the second hour and another the end of the third, their testimony can stand through the ordinary range of error. Where the defendant brings repayment witnesses with a somewhat wider discrepancy, the possibility of error can still suffice to leave the money in his possession when it would not suffice to extract money or impose capital liability. This is the practical result of the entire discussion. It remains to clarify more precisely the relation between the seven interrogations and the witness's ordinary margin of error, because several passages appear inconsistent unless this is understood. Tosafot in Sanhedrin explain that the period from dawn until sunrise occupies approximately the first hour and part of the second, yet ordinary people are not exact in this calculation. A person who says "the second hour" may mean a time after sunrise, because the masses can treat the dark interval after dawn as still belonging to night. Thus the conventional hour named by the witness does not always correspond exactly to the astronomical division. If so, why does the Gemara say that when witnesses are refuted regarding a statement in the second hour, the zomemim must sometimes establish their presence elsewhere from the beginning of the first hour through the end of the third? Why include the first hour if the witness's statement appears to concern a period after sunrise? The answer is that we cannot execute the witnesses from doubt. Perhaps this particular witness was knowledgeable about the actual divisions of time and used "second hour" precisely. Since the law of hazamah itself imposes punishment, the refuting testimony must exclude the full range in which the original witness could reasonably have meant his words. Accordingly, if a witness says the event occurred in the first hour, and according to the relevant calculation the first hour is certainly before sunrise, the refuting witnesses need only place him elsewhere through the actual range of that hour. People do not ordinarily confuse night and day beyond the recognized limits. This helps explain why the Torah does not create a separate interrogation of "which half of the hour." For some witnesses such a distinction is not objectively stable. The legal category has to be one that can function in every ordinary case, not merely for a person with exceptional astronomical precision. Tosafot in Pesachim, however, explain the weekday interrogation in part through the case where one witness says the second day of the month and another the third. This implies that some interrogations are useful only in certain factual configurations. It is not necessary that every one of the seven add a new practical distinction in every individual case. It is enough that each is an independent category of factual identification established by Torah. This raises difficulty for Rambam, who counts the hours differently and does not place the dawn-to-sunrise period within the twelve daylight hours in the same manner. According to him, the first daylight hour begins after sunrise, so Tosafot's particular explanation does not apply. The resolution is the more general principle already stated. The Torah's temporal interrogation is not the human convention of twelve numbered clock units as such. The Torah requires a sufficiently definite identification of the temporal portion of the event. Chazal then translated that requirement into the ordinary system of hours used by people. The conventional hour can be subdivided, but those subdivisions remain part of the same interrogation rather than new Torah interrogations. This is why the Gemara cannot count "how far into the hour" as an eighth. The natural division at midday is especially significant because it is visible in the sun's position. Before midday the sun is in the east; afterward it is in the west. That distinction is not merely conventional. Therefore a witness who says the fifth hour and one who says the seventh cannot ordinarily be reconciled as though both merely chose different conventional labels for the same natural period, because the sun's position distinguishes the sides of midday. Nevertheless, around the precise point of midday the sun's position can be difficult to estimate, and on a cloudy day even more so. Chazal therefore imposed wider preventive boundaries in chametz than the courtroom would use for capital conviction. This explains the Gemara's discussion under R. Meir and R. Yehudah. R. Meir's testimony rule can allow one witness's hour-statement and another's adjacent hour-statement to coexist because people err in estimating the conventional division. R. Yehudah allows a broader range. But when the difference crosses a natural boundary that people ordinarily recognize, such as the clear east-west position of the sun, the testimony is contradictory. The Gemara's chametz enactments are not an exact mirror of these evidentiary rules. The prohibition of chametz involves preventive legislation for the public, including cloudy weather and imprecise estimation, and therefore Chazal can prohibit earlier than the strict evidentiary margin would require. This is why Rambam can simultaneously say in Hilchot Edut that a person errs by one hour and in Hilchot Chametz adopt a wider safeguard. One law defines how far testimony can be reconciled under a judicial standard; the other defines how far Chazal feared error in order to prevent a Torah prohibition. One should also distinguish two different questions that are sometimes conflated. First: how far can a witness ordinarily be mistaken? Second: when two witnesses differ, can we attribute an error to both, one early and one late, so that their testimony meets in the middle? Even if the ordinary individual error is one hour, two witnesses can create a two-hour span if each is off in a different direction. Conversely, even if an individual can sometimes be off more than one hour, we do not automatically assume the maximum possible error in order to convict or extract money. The legal use of the error depends upon the burden of proof. This is precisely why Rava objects to killing on a speculative interpretation. It is not enough that one can invent a sequence in which both witnesses are within some conceivable range. The reconciliation must rest on an ordinary legal presumption. For a defendant retaining money, however, the standard is different. If his repayment witnesses can reasonably be reconciled and the claimant cannot prove they are false, the money remains with the possessor. The same doubt that is insufficient for extraction can be sufficient to prevent extraction. Hence the practical cases stated above. One witness says repayment was in the third hour and another in the fifth. If their words can reasonably describe one repayment through ordinary estimation, the borrower can rely on them to resist collection. One says the second and another the fifth. This is a larger difference and generally would not establish a claim affirmatively. But where the circumstances create a genuine time-estimation doubt and the borrower merely seeks to retain, the claimant cannot extract unless he defeats that doubt. One says the fifth and another the seventh. Ordinarily the difference across midday is serious because the sun is on opposite sides. Yet where the relevant period was cloudy or the witnesses' language itself shows imprecise estimation, the possessor can still benefit from the doubt even though such testimony would not support capital punishment or affirmative extraction. This does not mean every imagined mistake saves testimony. The error must be one recognized by ordinary human experience or by the particular circumstances. The discussion of whether two witnesses can share a mistake should likewise be understood through cause. If the supposed error depends on an individual lapse with no common source, exact agreement by two witnesses is evidence against the claim of mistake. But where the error comes from a shared calendar assumption, weather condition, or conventional way of counting hours, both can naturally make it. This is why the principle "falsehood does not produce agreement" cannot be transformed into an absolute rule that two witnesses never err together. The same distinction appears in documents. If two witnesses date a document according to the wrong assumption about a full month, their common mistake is intelligible. If they both make an unusual factual mistake with no shared cause, the court is not required to assume coincidence merely to preserve the document. There is still an unresolved aspect of Maharsha's distinction regarding hazamah. He says that where no original witness identified the alternate day, the court does not relocate the event there merely to defeat hazamah. Yet the rule of giving witnesses their full recognized range of error appears broader. The best reconciliation is that an ordinary error allowance changes the legal meaning of the witness's words only where the halachic system itself recognizes that particular uncertainty. We cannot create an entirely new event outside the recognized range. Thus if the calendar error legally means "second or third depending on intercalation," the zomemim must address that range. But if the proposed alternate date requires a second, unrelated factual assumption, the original witness cannot use it. Similarly, an hour statement carries the ordinary legal margin assigned by Chazal, but not every conceivable atmospheric or personal error. This also explains the phrase "give him all his error." We give the witness the error that the law recognizes for that kind of statement, not unlimited possibilities. Accordingly, the laws of date and hour should be read in one system: fixed Torah interrogations identify the event; Chazal define ordinary human margins within those interrogations; hazamah must exclude the recognized margin; conviction and extraction require a stronger reconciliation than retention of possession; and shared error is possible where a common cause naturally produces it. With this, Rambam's rulings, the sugyot in Pesachim and Sanhedrin, and the practical monetary consequences can be read consistently.
