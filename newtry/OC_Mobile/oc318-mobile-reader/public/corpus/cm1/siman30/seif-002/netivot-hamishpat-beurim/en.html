We judge the matter as contradictory testimony. This seif concerns witnesses who joined together in seeing and testifying about what they understood to be one transaction, and afterward contradicted one another in the formal inquiries. If they truly joined as one testimony, contradiction concerning the time can invalidate the entire testimony. This differs from two independent groups testifying about two distinct loans, where at least one group may remain valid and the borrower can be liable on a certainty basis. Likewise, if the witnesses did not join in seeing or testifying as one group, their contradictions can be treated like two sets of witnesses contradicting one another rather than as one testimony whose members invalidated the whole. In monetary law, where separate testimony can combine, the phrase 'not so' is therefore important to show that each witness is contradicting the other's account of the very transaction on which they joined. In capital law, where separate testimony cannot combine, this is less necessary because the witnesses must be speaking of one act. This also explains why certain disputes about contradiction in inquiries differ between monetary and capital cases. As to kiddushin witnesses who agree on the weekday but mistakenly also say the event occurred on a market day that was known to be a different weekday, that latter detail is not one of the formal inquiries. It can be treated as an incidental mistaken detail rather than as contradiction invalidating the testimony.
And this one claims both. The reason the claimant must claim both amounts is that contradictory testimony concerning incidental details is preserved only by attributing the discrepancy to imprecision in the detail. Thus, if one witness says one hundred and another says two hundred, we can preserve the common one hundred by saying the witness who said one hundred was certain only about the minimum and did not carefully attend to the excess. But if the claimant himself insists that only one hundred was ever involved, then the witness who said two hundred is known to have erred in the very amount. Once we know that he erred upward in the amount, we can no longer confidently rely even on the minimum, because perhaps the real sum was less still. The same applies to wine versus oil when those commodities differ in value. But where the discrepancy concerns a detail that does not affect the amount of money, such as from which purse the money was taken, we can preserve the testimony without requiring the claimant to assert both versions. The same explains the rule concerning wheat and barley in possession claims where the crop type itself does not change the legal character of possession. Where two full groups contradict each other, the claimant's own contradiction is less destructive because each group is independently complete. This framework also explains the rule where the borrower claims half the debt was paid while witnesses say all was paid: the document is impaired because some payment certainly occurred, but the witnesses cannot establish the precise amount against the borrower's own position.
He takes a Torah oath on one hundred. Taz explains that the oath cannot be derived from Rabbi Chiya's rule through testimony that itself could not create the analogous penalty liability, because the relevant derivation comes from witnesses whose evidentiary force is complete. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that special testimony can sometimes operate in penalty matters. The distinction is between two witnesses who separately observe parts of the same legally significant act, which can combine, and witnesses who each observe a wholly distinct event. Separate observations of two distinct events cannot be combined merely to produce a certainty that one liability exists. The same principle applies to admissions. Two witnesses who hear separate admissions concerning the same monetary obligation can sometimes combine because the admissions refer to one debt. This is not comparable to two witnesses each seeing a distinct penal event. Further, although formal inquiries are biblically required, the Sages relaxed them in monetary law and treated testimony lacking them as though properly investigated. That relaxation can therefore support the associated oath as well.
