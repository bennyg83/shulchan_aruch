If they contradict one another in the auxiliary examinations, etc. The Gemara distinguishes seven formal inquiries capable of producing hazamah: in which sabbatical cycle, which year, which month, which day of the month, which weekday, which hour, and which place. Other questions are called auxiliary examinations. Rambam also treats a contradiction concerning the essence of the event as a formal inquiry in capital law, such as whether the victim was killed with a sword or another instrument, and in monetary law what very obligation was incurred, such as barrels of wine or oil.
How so, one says in Nisan, etc. The same applies if one says the act occurred in Yerushalayim and the other names another location. Time and place are formal inquiries because hazamah depends on them. Therefore the testimony is void even if the claimant asserts both events.
Likewise, if one says barrels of wine, etc., until 'for they contradicted in a formal inquiry.' This is Rambam's language. He holds that any contradiction concerning the substance of the transaction is treated as a contradiction in derishah. I cited his wording in the Derishah.
And the second says it was oil. In the Derishah and Perishah I proved that even if the second does not expressly say 'No, rather,' the statements can contradict where both testify without specification, because we assume they refer to the same event. It is as though the second says, 'The very loan you say involved wine involved oil.' If one testifies to wine in Nisan and the other to oil in Iyar, and the claimant demands both, they are not contradictory unless one expressly negates the other's event.
Their testimony is void. Meaning the claimant is not even entitled to an oath against either witness. Even if he claims both wine and oil, because the contradiction is in a formal inquiry the testimony is wholly invalid.
And the second says it was a white maneh, etc. The examples from here onward are auxiliary examinations. Hazamah does not depend on them, and they do not necessarily disagree about the essential obligation. Both testify to the same monetary amount, and similarly a dispute whether it occurred on an upper or lower floor may not concern the essential place where each could have observed the event.
And he claims both from him. If the claimant does not claim both versions, one witness is contradicted by the claimant's own mouth and there is no basis to combine him with the other. If the claimant asserts both, even though the witnesses contradict one another only in an auxiliary detail, money may be extracted according to the smaller common denominator, because one hundred is included within two hundred. Nimukei Yosef holds that for black versus white coins the claimant need not necessarily assert both, and according to that approach the same may be true where one says one hundred and the other two hundred unless they expressly describe different times. Ir Shushan's attempt to distinguish these cases is unsupported.
On the upper gallery. This refers to an upper structure projecting from a house toward the public domain, beneath which the public passes.
