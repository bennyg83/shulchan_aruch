If the witnesses contradict each other in the formal interrogations, their testimony is void. This is unlike Ramban's responsum to the sages of Regensburg, who holds that even a contradiction in the formal interrogations can be accepted in civil cases. Most authorities disagree. Shev Yaakov, responsum 14, attempts to prove Ramban correct from the Gemara's discussion of a promissory note dated in Nisan of the Sabbatical year. Witnesses come and say to the signatories, "You were with us elsewhere on that date," yet the document can remain valid because perhaps it was written later with an earlier transaction date. The Gemara asks that if civil cases require interrogation and examination, why should we preserve the document rather than assume the witnesses are false? It answers that Torah law requires interrogation, but Chazal relaxed the requirement in civil law to avoid closing the door before borrowers. Shev Yaakov argues that if a contradiction in the formal interrogations would still invalidate the testimony, the problem remains. The witnesses were contradicted regarding the date written in the document. This is not correct. According to Rashi, the question is conceptual. If Torah ordinarily requires interrogation in civil cases because witnesses are not presumed truthful until they survive detailed examination, then where a serious flaw appears, such as proof that they were elsewhere on the date written, why should beit din search for an uncertain explanation such as "perhaps they wrote it late"? The answer is that after the rabbinic enactment, civil witnesses are again treated with a presumption that they are speaking truthfully unless clear contradiction makes that impossible. We therefore interpret the document in a manner that preserves their credibility and say it was written later. But where two oral witnesses directly contradict each other in a formal interrogation, one of them is certainly speaking falsely. There is no way to preserve both under a presumption of truth. That is an entirely different case. Indeed, even Ramban agrees that formal hazamah invalidates. Yet in the document case the signatories are being contradicted by witnesses who say they were elsewhere. Why is the document not automatically disqualified? Because once the rabbinic framework presumes valid civil documents, we interpret the written date itself as not necessarily the day of signing. The witnesses are therefore not truly refuted. This does not show that direct contradiction between witnesses is tolerated. Nor can one compare ordinary oral testimony where one says Nisan and the other says Iyar by suggesting perhaps one made a mistake or the event was delayed. If the kind of mistake is common and reasonable, we already say the testimony is not contradictory. But where the alleged mistake is not one people ordinarily make, we cannot invent an explanation. The authorities from Lunel, cited by Baal HaTerumot, correctly distinguish the matter and their approach remains the stronger halachic position. Accordingly, Shev Yaakov's proof does not establish Ramban's view, and a contradiction in the formal interrogations voids the testimony in civil law as well.
Their testimony is void. Rambam rules in Hilchot Edut that where there are many witnesses, if two agree in the formal interrogations and a third says, "I do not know," the testimony stands through the two and, in a capital case, the defendant can be executed. But if the third contradicts the two, even in the subsidiary examinations, all the testimony is void. In capital law this is understandable. Contradiction even in subsidiary examinations can invalidate. In civil law, where contradiction in subsidiary examinations ordinarily does not invalidate, there remains a serious question whether a third witness who contradicts two in the formal interrogations invalidates the whole testimony. A great scholar in a responsum of Beit Yosef argued that Rambam's rule applies only when all the witnesses testify together, meaning within the short interval of one another. If the contradicting witness comes later, the first two have already been accepted and the later single witness cannot undo them. Lechem Mishneh appears at first to say the opposite. He asks how Rambam can rule that if the third witness says, "I do not know," the remaining two are valid, when the Gemara says this is not according to R. Akiva, who treats a group of three as one testimony for the laws of hazamah and disqualification. Lechem Mishneh answers that the Gemara concerns witnesses who testify together, whereas Rambam speaks where the third comes later. If so, when Rambam then says that a contradicting third witness does invalidate, even though he comes later, this would appear to reject the distinction of the scholar cited by Beit Yosef. But Lechem Mishneh's answer is strained. If the Gemara explicitly gives the rule for witnesses who testify together, why would Rambam omit that law entirely and record only a different case not stated in the Gemara? Why would he not explain the distinction? A better reading of Rambam is based on the dispute in Makkot concerning the rule that when one of three witnesses is found related or disqualified, all testimony is void. R. Yehudah, in the name of R. Akiva, treats the group more fully as one unit. Rebbi distinguishes between witnesses who came to testify and people who merely came to observe. According to Rebbi, one who merely observed does not disqualify the others. The underlying dispute is how completely we equate three witnesses to two. With two witnesses, if one is disqualified, nothing remains. Therefore no distinction is needed between one who came to testify and one who merely saw the event. If the comparison of three to two is absolute, then likewise any disqualified third destroys the testimony. Rebbi holds that the comparison is not absolute. Where two valid witnesses remain, an outsider does not destroy them unless he joined as an actual witness. The same reasoning applies when the third witness says, "I do not know." If we compare three completely to two, then just as with two witnesses one who says "I do not know" leaves no complete testimony capable of hazamah, so too with three the entire testimony should fail. But according to Rebbi, where two valid witnesses remain and the third is effectively absent rather than disqualified, the testimony can stand. Rambam rules like Rebbi in Hilchot Edut concerning people who merely came to observe. He therefore also rules that if a third says "I do not know," the testimony survives through the remaining two. Thus the baraita is fully consistent with the halachah and Rambam need not be speaking of witnesses who arrived at different times. Accordingly, the scholar's distinction regarding contradiction remains plausible: a third witness can invalidate the two only when his testimony joins theirs as one evidentiary unit, meaning he testifies together with them. If he appears only later, after the first testimony has already been accepted, his contradiction is no stronger than one witness against two. Tosafot in Makkot distinguish the laws of hazamah from the laws of a related or disqualified witness. In hazamah, since the witnesses did not actually see the event, their joinder begins only when they testify in beit din, so testimony within the short interval is necessary. Where they all actually witnessed the event, their joinder can begin at the time of seeing it. One might therefore argue that a third witness who contradicts the others in the formal interrogations joined them from the original event and can invalidate even if he testifies later. But that reasoning does not fit Rambam's own approach. Rambam holds that where a related or disqualified person intended to testify at the time of seeing the event, he can disqualify the whole group even if he never later comes to court at all. In the present case of contradiction, however, nothing happens merely because the person saw the event. The problem arises only when he actually comes to beit din and contradicts the others. Thus the decisive joinder is the courtroom testimony, more like hazamah. The witnesses should therefore have to testify as one unit. Still, if they do testify together and the third contradicts the two in the formal interrogations, there remains a question whether the same rule applies in civil cases. Beit Yosef, in the responsum mentioned above, decides that the special invalidation applies only in capital-type matters and not in ordinary monetary law. I was initially hesitant. Rambam's broad wording suggests that contradiction in the formal interrogations may function similarly in civil and capital matters, and no explicit Talmudic source clearly distinguishes them. But the practical law can be clarified from the rule of the sages of Lunel. They explain why two witnesses who contradict each other about the date in a civil case do not combine. If one had merely said, "It was not in Nisan," and the other said, "It was in Tishrei," one witness would be contradicting the other and there would not be two witnesses for the claim. The added positive statement about another date does not strengthen the first witness. Accordingly, where two witnesses say Nisan and a third says, "No, it was not Nisan but Iyar," the third witness's contradiction is still only one against two. His additional positive statement about Iyar does not elevate his contradiction. One witness has no standing against two. This reasoning applies especially in civil law, where our practical ruling follows the compromise of the sages of Lunel rather than Rambam's broader conceptual framework. There is further support from the laws of Sotah and eglah arufah. Where two witnesses say one thing and one witness directly contradicts them, the single witness is disregarded even if they appear together. The same should apply where the third witness agrees with the basic event but contradicts only a detail of the formal interrogations. Therefore Beit Yosef's conclusion is correct for practical civil law. If two witnesses agree and a third contradicts them in the formal interrogations, the third does not invalidate the two merely by that contradiction. One witness cannot stand against two. Likewise, if two witnesses first contradict each other in the formal interrogations and a third later comes from the marketplace and agrees with one of them, the agreeing witness can join that one. The result becomes two against one, and the single contradictory witness is disregarded. This is the correct practical ruling.
If one said it was a barrel of oil, their testimony is void. Sema writes that the defendant need not even swear, even if the plaintiff claims both versions, because the contradiction concerns a matter included among the formal interrogations and the testimony is entirely void. That follows Beit Yosef's reading of Rambam. But for practical halachah I am not convinced. The sages of Lunel, cited by Baal HaTerumot, agree with Rambam's result that witnesses contradicted in the formal interrogations do not combine, but for a different reason. They explicitly write that where one witness says the loan was in Nisan and the other says, "No, rather in Tishrei," the first witness is not treated as a valid witness for monetary payment, but the testimony can still function as a single witness and obligate an oath. This is a significant practical difference. According to Rambam's own conceptual approach, the contradiction destroys the testimony entirely and not even an oath remains. According to the sages of Lunel, the two do not combine for payment, but at least one of them must be truthful, and one valid witness remains for an oath. Their wording is explicit and this is precisely why Baal HaTerumot says they agree with Rambam in result but not in reason. One should not force their case into a situation where only one witness denies the other's account while the other does not deny his. The ordinary language implies mutual contradiction, and in any event the whole point of their explanation is that contradiction prevents combination but does not erase the one-witness status. Therefore the practical halachah should follow these mediating authorities, especially since Baal HaTerumot appears to agree. In the present case of a barrel of wine versus a barrel of oil there is an additional reason to require an oath. Rashi holds that this difference may not even be part of the formal interrogations. Many authorities disagree with Rambam's classification. Accordingly, there is no basis for a claim of kim li to avoid the oath. The defendant must therefore take the oath required by one witness. There is no objection that because one of the witnesses is certainly lying the situation resembles a group where one witness is found related or disqualified. Tosafot in Sanhedrin explicitly distinguish contradiction between witnesses from the rule of a related or disqualified witness. Therefore the oath remains required.
And he claims both. Mechaber omits this condition, following Nimukei Yosef, because witnesses can easily confuse a black maneh with a white maneh, so there is no need for the plaintiff expressly to claim both. Sema follows that understanding. Rama, however, follows Tur, who explicitly requires that the plaintiff claim both amounts. Later authorities struggled to explain why. The reason appears clear. Even though witnesses can confuse a black maneh with a white maneh, the two remain distinct types, at least like wheat and barley. Indeed R. Yehudah holds that witnesses can confuse wheat and barley, yet he is more stringent concerning a black and white maneh. This shows that black and white are certainly not less distinct than wheat and barley. There is also a difference between witnesses and litigants. Witnesses can make mistakes in identifying categories that a litigant himself would ordinarily distinguish. Rosh makes this point elsewhere regarding the difference between a borrowed and rented animal. Now Tur follows Rosh's reason why one who claims wheat and receives an admission of barley is exempt: the claimant's failure to claim the barley is treated as waiver. Apply the same logic here. If the plaintiff claims only a black maneh, one witness supports him and the other says it was a white maneh, then even though witnesses can confuse those types, the plaintiff himself ordinarily does not. By claiming only the black maneh, he has effectively waived any separate claim to the white maneh. If the truth is according to the witness who says white, the plaintiff waived that debt. The witness who says black is then false. Therefore the plaintiff must claim both. Once he does, no waiver can be inferred and the witnesses can be reconciled through the ordinary possibility of confusion. Nimukei Yosef, who does not require both claims, follows the authorities who explain the wheat-barley rule through the defendant's ability to say the admission was merely joking or similar, not through waiver. According to that approach, the present case is different because the existence of some loan is independently established. Thus Tur and Nimukei Yosef follow their respective broader theories consistently. Rama therefore correctly preserves Tur's requirement that the plaintiff claim both.
If the places are close enough that one can see from one to the other. This is Tur's view. He explains the Gemara's rule that where one witness says the event occurred on the upper gallery and the other on the lower gallery the testimony can stand because the locations can be seen from one another. There is therefore no meaningful contradiction and the testimony remains capable of hazamah. Rivash gives a different reason: people do not ordinarily remember carefully whether an event occurred on the upper or lower gallery. According to him, even where the two places cannot actually be seen from one another, the contradiction may still be disregarded because it concerns a detail people commonly confuse. This appears difficult from Makkot 5. Two witnesses say an event occurred on the east side of a large hall, while refuting witnesses say, "You were with us on the west side." If the two locations can be seen from one another there is no hazamah; if they cannot, there is hazamah. This seems to support Tur. According to Rivash, why not say the witnesses may simply have confused east and west? One answer follows Maharsha. Although witnesses can sometimes be assumed to confuse nearby details when their own testimonies differ, hazamah is different. If they positively said the event was on the east and the refuting witnesses place them on the west, we do not invent a different location not contained in their testimony merely to save them from hazamah. I have reservations about Maharsha's broader formulation, but it resolves this passage. R. Betzalel Ashkenazi, responsum 5, cites Ran together with Rivash and infers that the tolerance for such confusion applies in civil law but not in capital law. That is another strong answer. The Makkot passage concerns capital law, where witnesses are expected to be more exact. This distinction is supported by authorities who say that even if a woman alone contradicts witnesses in a kiddushin matter, the case can become one that appears fraudulent and requires formal interrogation. Still, Tur's approach raises a separate question. The interrogation "where" appears to contain several possible subquestions. The witnesses can be asked in which city the event occurred, such as Sura or Nehardea, and also within that city, in which house, room, upper floor, lower floor, east side, or west side. Each detail can theoretically generate its own hazamah. Why then does Sanhedrin struggle to identify an eighth formal interrogation, eventually suggesting the difficult question of which Jubilee cycle? Why not count two separate place interrogations, city and precise location? The answer may depend on the view of R. Yaakov Berav, cited by Maharlbach, that where two witnesses make the same mistake together we do not generally attribute it to ordinary error. The possibility of confusion applies more naturally to one witness, while the Torah's formal interrogations must be questions that independently matter even to a single witness. If so, the precise internal location cannot always serve as a separate Torah-level interrogation because a single witness may be assumed to err about it. This resolves the count. But according to Maharlbach's view that even two witnesses together can be treated as mistaken in appropriate circumstances, the question remains and requires further study. The better practical distinction may be that "which place" is one formal interrogation, with city and internal location functioning as details within it rather than separate Torah interrogations. The matter still requires study.
Even if one said one hundred, etc. This follows Rashba's view that Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel disagree even within a single set of witnesses, and Beit Hillel hold that two hundred includes one hundred. Rambam appears to rule the same way in Hilchot Edut. Yet in Hilchot Nezirut he writes that where one set of witnesses says a person undertook two periods of nazirut and another set says five, he observes two because five includes two. Why does Rambam formulate the nazirut case specifically with two separate sets of witnesses rather than one witness saying two and another five? This suggests that in nazirut he follows the opinion that Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel disagree only where there are two sets, whereas in one set the testimony is divided. The answer is that "two" versus "five" contains a significant difference and can be treated as contradiction in the subsidiary examinations. The Yerushalmi in Sanhedrin and Nazir suggests a comparison to one witness saying the killing was by sword and another by a different weapon. In monetary law, contradiction in subsidiary examinations does not invalidate because the rabbinic relaxation for civil law applies. Therefore we can say that two hundred includes one hundred and preserve the shared core of the testimony. Nazirut is different. There is no concern of closing the door before borrowers, so the ordinary rules of interrogation remain stronger. A material contradiction in subsidiary details invalidates a single set, like capital law. Rambam therefore requires two separate sets of witnesses in the nazirut case. At least one complete set remains valid, and the smaller common obligation can be imposed. Nazirut testimony also requires interrogation because violations of nazirut can lead to lashes, and false witnesses can themselves become liable to lashes through hazamah. The two rulings of Rambam are therefore consistent.
