The formal interrogations are seven: in which sabbatical cycle, which year, which month, which day of the month, which day of the week, which hour, and which place. Other questions are called examinations. The Rambam writes that a fact upon which the substance of the event depends, such as whether the barrel contained wine or oil, is also treated as part of the formal inquiry. See Netivot, seif katan 1. Even if witnesses testified in Nisan, were interrogated in Tishrei, said the event was in Shevat, and were then rendered zomemim, they are disqualified retroactively from Nisan. If it is uncertain whether they originally testified in Nisan or Iyar, they are disqualified from Iyar. The same applies to an undated document where they later say the event occurred in Nisan: their disqualification begins from the time it is known that they signed the document. Likewise, if witnesses contradict one another in formal interrogations, they become disqualified from the time of their testimony for purposes of joining other testimony. See Netivot, seif katan 2. Testimony is invalidated by contradictory interrogations only where the witnesses joined together concerning one obligation through both seeing and testifying together. If they saw together and testified within the required brief interval, then whether they merely contradict one another about the time, or one group says concerning the other's time, "You were with us elsewhere," while maintaining that the jointly witnessed loan happened at another time, all the testimony is void and the attempted hazamah is also void. Neither side is punished, and even an oath against a single witness is not required. If they did not join in that way, for example two first testified concerning Nisan and another pair said, "In Nisan you were with us elsewhere, but in Iyar we independently saw the defendant borrow," or the latter pair's Iyar testimony concerns a different loan they themselves saw, then the first witnesses are treated as zomemim and the second testimony remains valid. If the latter say the lender himself was with them elsewhere, the two groups are simply contradictory groups, and the defendant must pay one maneh whichever way the truth lies. Similarly, one witness against one witness creates an oath. The Raavad, cited by the Shach, holds that witnesses who contradict one another in interrogations can remain valid in monetary law, specifically where they agree on the substance of the loan and only disagree as to time. The Shach, however, concludes that contradiction in formal interrogations invalidates monetary testimony. If one says the event was on the second of the month, and that day was a public market day or other widely known event that clearly did not occur on that date, this is not treated as a formal interrogation contradiction that invalidates the testimony.

He claims both. If he does not claim both, one witness is contradicted by the claimant himself. Likewise, in a dispute of one hundred versus two hundred he must claim two hundred, and in a dispute of wine versus oil he must claim both. If two witnesses say two hundred and he claimed only one hundred, while the defendant denies everything, the defendant must nevertheless take a Torah oath analogous to Rabbi Chiya's rule based on witness testimony: it is certain there was a loan, only the amount is unknown. This need to claim both applies specifically where the witnesses contradict one another on details affecting the amount, such as wine versus oil where oil is more valuable, or old versus new coin where people are particular about the currency and one may be more readily accepted or more valuable. But where they differ only on a detail irrelevant to the litigants, such as a black purse versus a white purse, or one says he ate wheat and another barley in a chazakah case where any eating suffices, the claimant need not assert both. See Netivot, seif katan 3.
