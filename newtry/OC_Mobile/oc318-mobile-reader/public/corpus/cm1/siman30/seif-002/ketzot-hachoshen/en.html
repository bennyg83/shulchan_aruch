We excuse discrepancies in secondary details when the witnesses contradict one another, because they may simply have erred. But where the contradiction is between a witness and the litigant, we do not normally attribute the error to the litigant. The Sema writes that according to Nimukei Yosef, where one witness says the loan was in black coins and another says white coins, the claimant need not demand both versions. If so, the same should apply where one says one hundred and another two hundred. Ir Shushan's distinction, requiring both claims for one hundred versus two hundred but not for black versus white coins, follows no consistent view. Nevertheless, there is compelling proof that in one hundred versus two hundred we cannot merely attribute the conflict between the witness and litigant to mistake. Bava Batra 128 rules that where the lender says nothing was repaid, the borrower says half was repaid, and witnesses say all was repaid, the lender swears and collects half from free assets. If the difference between one hundred and two hundred could be treated as a harmless detail even against a litigant's own claim, then the witnesses should establish repayment of at least half without contradiction. Rather, where the contradiction concerns the amount of the money itself, the litigant can contradict the testimony. I later found this very proof in Ran. Ran explains that harmless factual details, such as black versus white coins, need not create contradiction even against the litigant. But one hundred versus two hundred concerns the substance of the monetary obligation. The witnesses can combine among themselves because two hundred includes one hundred, yet a litigant may disqualify evidence offered for his benefit by contradicting it. Therefore a claimant must maintain a claim consistent with the combined monetary testimony.
