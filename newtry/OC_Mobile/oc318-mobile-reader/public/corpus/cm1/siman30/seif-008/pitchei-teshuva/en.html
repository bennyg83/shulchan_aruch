We regard one hundred as repaid. See Sema, seif katan 27, who explains that the borrower claims to have repaid two hundred in accordance with the combined testimony of the two witnesses. But if the borrower himself says that he repaid only one hundred, the witnesses do not combine, and the lender may take a Torah oath against the single witness and collect the full two hundred under the note. Tumim writes that this must refer to a borrower who admitted, before learning the witnesses' testimony, that he repaid only one hundred. If he made that admission only after their testimony, he would have a migo that he could have claimed repayment of the full two hundred supported by witnesses, and as the possessor he should be believed regarding one hundred. Netivot HaMishpat questions this. Shaar Mishpat adds that where the borrower claims repayment of two hundred, the lender would seemingly have to swear regarding the balance as one who partially impairs his note, since the oath applies even when the note is impaired through witnesses. One could argue, however, that because the lender was established as a denier by having claimed the entire note, he becomes suspect regarding an oath. Under the Rambam's view, cited in siman 82, that if the lender is suspect the borrower swears a rabbinic oath and is exempt, the entire note should then fail. Shaar Mishpat therefore questions why the Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch state the rule without qualification. Yet the Baal HaTerumot and Tur themselves rule in siman 82, seif 8, like the Ramah rather than the Rambam, that the lender collects without an oath, since the Sages did not enact that he should lose his money because he cannot swear. Shach there states that this applies whether the lender's oath arose because the borrower demanded, 'Swear to me,' or because the note was impaired. Accordingly, their unqualified ruling here is understandable: one hundred is treated as repaid, and as to the second hundred, since the lender has become suspect, he collects without an oath. Still, the Taz and Tumim distinguish between an oath arising merely from the demand to swear and an oath expressly required by the Mishna, such as one on an impaired note, in which case a suspect lender loses the note. Beit Meir further argues that the Shulchan Aruch did not decisively rule like the Ramah in siman 82 for purposes of extracting money. Therefore the practical ruling here remains difficult and requires study. See Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer, siman 115, seif katan 3.
