He pays seven hundred, etc. Since in monetary law separate loans may be combined, as explained in seif 6, the witness who testifies to four hundred combines with the witness who testifies to five hundred, obligating four hundred. The witness to two hundred combines with the witness to three hundred, obligating another two hundred. The remaining one hundred from the three hundred testimony combines with the remaining one hundred from the five hundred testimony, producing a further one hundred. Thus the defendant must pay seven hundred. The witness who testified to one hundred has no partner with whom to combine, so the defendant must take a Torah oath contradicting that witness. The Tur writes this, and the poskim infer that if the witness to the one hundred could itself be included in a valid combination, it would not independently create an oath. For example, if one witness says one hundred and another says two hundred, so that together they compel payment of one hundred, we do not say that the extra one hundred contained in the second witness's testimony should still require a Torah oath. We do not impose such an oath. See Tumim, where I wrote that the reason requires study.
He may impose by gilgul upon him. Meaning, the remaining eight hundred. One might ask why he does not independently owe an oath. The claimant demands one thousand five hundred, the defendant denies everything, and witnesses establish seven hundred. This appears to be the rule of Rabbi Chiyya's first case, where partial contradiction by witnesses requires an oath on the remainder. The Sema and Bach answer that testimony established only through combination does not trigger Rabbi Chiyya's first rule. See Tumim, where I concluded with the Shach that this distinction is strained and unsupported. Rather, the case clearly is one where the claimant himself does not assert the debt with certainty but relies on the witnesses. Rabbi Chiyya's first rule therefore does not apply. The remaining amount is sworn only through gilgul, which may be imposed even on a doubtful claim. See there.
And if they testified without specification, etc. I discussed this at length in Tumim and concluded that the earlier rule that we reconcile witnesses whenever possible applies only to a matter concerning which people commonly err and where the majority supports the assumption of error, just as we rely on the majority to say that witnesses erred regarding fixing the new month. Where people do not commonly make such an error, we do not force their statements into agreement. Rather, we treat them as contradicting each other and apply the rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. This is contrary to the Taz, who seeks to distinguish according to whether treating them as contradictory would cause one of them to become disqualified as a witness. If two witnesses have already testified in beit din and their testimony has acquired a presumption of validity, and afterward contradicting or impeaching witnesses arrive, we make every reasonable effort to reconcile the first testimony so that it will not be treated as contradicted or impeached. Since the first testimony was already established as valid, we do not remove it from that presumption where reconciliation is possible.
Because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. From this it follows that if witnesses testify concerning repayment, with one group saying, "On the first of Nisan he repaid four hundred," and another two witnesses saying without specifying a date, "In our presence he repaid five hundred," we do not assume both groups refer to the same day and therefore contradict each other. Rather, we say the unspecified testimony concerns a different day. Accordingly, he is credited with repayment of nine hundred. I wrote so in Tumim. See there.
