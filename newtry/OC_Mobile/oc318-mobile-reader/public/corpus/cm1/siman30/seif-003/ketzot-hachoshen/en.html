A Torah oath. The Sema writes that Rabbi Chiya's rule, that partial testimony by witnesses creates an oath through an a fortiori argument, does not apply where the two witnesses combine only through separate testimony. The Shach disagrees. Taz offers a sharp explanation: the oath from witnesses is learned by an a fortiori argument from one's own admission and from one witness. A person's own admission cannot obligate a fine, while two witnesses can, and therefore witnesses certainly can obligate an oath. But testimony formed only through later combination, or "special testimony," cannot impose a fine, just as one witness says seclusion occurred in the morning and another in the evening cannot combine for matters touching capital status. Therefore the a fortiori argument does not apply. In my view fines are not comparable to marital prohibitions. The stringency in the latter is because the matter has the character of a capital case and therefore requires seeing together and testifying together. Fines, however, follow monetary procedure in this respect. Bava Kamma 74 says that when one witness testifies today and another tomorrow, they can combine under Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korcha, and Rashi expressly applies this to liability for a fine. Capital law certainly requires both witnesses to testify together. Therefore fines do not share the capital rule of joint viewing and joint testimony. The a fortiori argument can thus apply even to combined testimony, and the Shach's conclusion is correct. Tosafot in Bava Metzia also implies that the a fortiori argument from two witnesses to an oath is stronger than the argument from fines specifically, and can include testimony that becomes a valid pair through combination.
