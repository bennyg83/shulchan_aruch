And he must take a Torah oath concerning the one hundred. See Urim. From this Tur appears to derive that a witness whose testimony has already been joined with another witness to create a monetary payment cannot additionally impose a Torah oath on the same defendant based on the unused portion of his statement. The source of this rule is difficult. One can suggest a dilemma. The witness testified concerning two hundred. Together with another witness who testified only concerning one hundred, his testimony has already been used to extract that one hundred. If we then require the defendant to swear against the same witness concerning the remaining one hundred, one of two things follows. If the defendant swears truthfully that the witness lied, how could we simultaneously rely upon that witness's testimony to extract the first hundred? If the witness is telling the truth, then we are compelling the defendant to swear falsely. To avoid this contradiction, it is preferable to use the witness for the monetary joinder and not impose an additional oath from his testimony. There is some support from Bava Metzia, where the Gemara distinguishes one witness from two witnesses on the ground that two are subject to hazamah while one witness is not. One might ask that a single witness can sometimes become subject to monetary hazamah in part. If one witness says three hundred and another says two hundred, and together they cause collection of two hundred, a later hazamah could create payment corresponding to the amount extracted through their combined testimony. Why then say one witness is categorically outside the law of hazamah? The answer may be that once a witness has been used through joinder for monetary payment, he no longer functions as a separate oath-producing witness. Therefore the classic case of one witness who imposes an oath never itself produces a monetary hazamah consequence. This is not a conclusive proof, but it fits Tur's rule. Still, there are objections. According to authorities who hold that if a defendant swears against a single witness and later the litigant joins with that witness, the two can combine to disqualify the defendant from testimony and future oaths, we do not say the witness is unusable merely because the defendant's oath contradicted him. Likewise the witness may still testify regarding a separate remaining maneh. Ramban, who is the principal source for Tur's rule, indeed holds more restrictively in the disqualification case, which may explain his consistency. Kenesset HaGedolah claims Tosafot in Ketubot 88 disagree with Tur because they discuss joining an earlier and later witness in a manner that still leaves an oath. I do not see such a proof. The Gemara says that where one witness says a ketubah was paid and the husband later pays again before another witness, the two witnesses can join. Tosafot ask from Sanhedrin that loan after loan testimony can combine. Their question concerns only whether the two testimonies can be joined for the underlying payment fact. Once they are joined for monetary consequence, there may indeed be no remaining Torah oath, exactly as Tur holds. Tosafot's question is therefore not against Tur. One can even explain Rashi there as saying that if the testimonies cannot combine, the woman can separately swear against each witness and collect again. Tosafot object that they can combine and prevent the additional collection. Thus there is no evidence that Tosafot disagree. For practical halachah I have found no early authority explicitly rejecting Tur's rule. Although its reasoning requires study, one should not rule against Tur.
And we roll onto him an oath concerning the remaining six hundred. Why does Tur require the oath regarding the remaining amount only through gilgul shevuah? At first glance the witnesses themselves should create a Torah oath through the rule of R. Chiya the First. The defendant denied everything, while the witnesses establish part of the claim. The classic kal vachomer of R. Chiya says that where witnesses establish part, the defendant must swear concerning the rest. Sema and Bach answer that R. Chiya's rule applies only where the witnesses saw one single event together. It does not apply where the monetary liability is created only through combining separate loans or separate testimonies. Shach correctly asks for the source of such a distinction. In my youth I suggested, and later found Taz saying similarly, that R. Chiya's first kal vachomer rests partly on the fact that witnesses can impose payment of fines, whereas a litigant's own admission cannot. Separate testimony assembled only through joinder would not suffice for fines or capital-type matters, so perhaps the kal vachomer does not apply. But I retracted this. Even an admission followed by another admission would not qualify in capital law, yet monetary joinder can still be effective. And if the limitation depended on the ability of the precise witnesses before us to impose a fine, then witnesses who cannot answer all formal interrogations in a civil case should likewise fail to generate R. Chiya's oath. No authority says that. The kal vachomer concerns the general legal category of witnesses, who are in principle capable of imposing fines when their testimony satisfies the required standards. It does not depend on whether this particular configuration could impose a fine. Moreover, the final form of R. Chiya's derivation is from one witness, with the litigant's own admission used only as the answering proof. That comparison can exist even with separately joined testimony. The better explanation is that R. Chiya's rule does apply in principle even where the witnesses are combined from separate events. The present case, however, is one where the plaintiff himself does not make a definite claim for the full amount from personal knowledge. He sues based upon the testimony of the witnesses concerning separate loans. The wording of the case suggests that he is relying on their accounts rather than saying with certainty, "You borrowed this amount from me on this day and that amount on another day." Shach writes explicitly that R. Chiya's oath does not apply where the claimant himself lacks a definite claim. The witnesses have already been joined to establish the amount they can prove. As to the remaining six hundred, there is no definite claim by the plaintiff and no separate witness testimony supporting that specific balance. There is therefore no independent Torah oath through R. Chiya. But once another Torah oath is already required concerning the amount established by the witnesses, the claimant can roll the remaining doubtful claim onto it through gilgul shevuah, because gilgul applies even to a doubtful claim. This explains Tur without inventing a new rule that separately joined witnesses can never generate R. Chiya's first oath. That is the preferable interpretation.
Likewise, if they testified without specifying details, we treat the matter as contradictory for leniency, because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. This rule requires some explanation, and I must discuss it at some length. Ramban states it, as cited by Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 66, part 2, and derives it from the Gemara in Zeh Borer. The Gemara challenges Rav, who holds that testimony concerning one loan does not combine with testimony concerning another loan because the maneh to which this witness testifies is not the maneh to which the other testifies. If so, the Gemara asks, why should one admission combine with another admission? We should likewise say that the maneh concerning which this witness testifies is not the same maneh concerning which the other testifies. The force of the question is that with successive admissions one could have said that both witnesses refer to one and the same maneh: he admitted it once and later admitted it again. Nevertheless, the Gemara assumes that for purposes of extracting money we do not force that interpretation. This shows that where the testimony is left unspecified and can be understood in a manner that prevents extraction, we follow the rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. From this it appears clear that if witnesses testify concerning repayment, for example two say that on the first of Nisan the borrower repaid four hundred and two others say without specifying a day that he repaid five hundred, we need not say the second set refers to the same payment and contradicts the first. Since their testimony is unspecified, it can refer to a separate payment, and the lender can thereby be treated as having received both amounts. The whole basis is that the borrower is the possessor and the burden of proof rests on the one who seeks to extract. From this law Sema derives that where one witness says the barrel was of wine and another says it was of oil, even if neither says expressly, "Not as you say," we treat the ordinary wording as contradiction where the subject is one barrel. The statements are not automatically repaired by assuming two separate barrels or some other circumstance, because the claimant bears the burden of proof. Taz objects that Mechaber's words appear to contradict what he ruled above in siman 29, seif 1, from Ran: whenever we can interpret a witness's words so that his later statement does not contradict his earlier statement, we should do so, just as we interpret the statements of two witnesses in a manner that preserves their agreement. Rama likewise rules here in seif 2 that wherever the words of the witnesses can be reconciled so that they do not contradict each other, we reconcile them. How, then, can Mechaber rule here that unspecified testimony is treated as contradiction? Taz answers that the two cases differ. In the case of successive admissions, or Ramban's example of two saying four hundred and two saying five hundred, even if the testimony is treated as contradictory, the witnesses themselves do not thereby become disqualified. The contradiction concerns subsidiary examinations or matters of a sort that do not establish one of them as a false witness. There is therefore no reason to strain to preserve their personal presumption of fitness. We apply the monetary rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. But where the contradiction is in the formal interrogations, such as one saying the second of the month and another the third, then if we do not reconcile them one of the witnesses is false and the testimony is disqualified. There, Taz says, we labor to reconcile their statements in order not to remove them from their presumption of fitness. According to this, Sema's ruling concerning one saying wine and one oil would depend on whether that distinction is a matter of formal interrogation. If it is, we should reconcile rather than treat them as contradicting. At first sight Taz speaks well. Yet I have difficulty with his distinction. Ran himself, whose responsum is the source for the rule that we reconcile testimony, proves his point from the Gemara's rule where one witness says the second of the month and another the third, and we say that one knew the preceding month had been intercalated and one did not. But according to what I explained above in siman 29, the witnesses need not become personally disqualified even if their original statements cannot stand. If, during the process of interrogation, they later agree on the weekday, they may still be able to clarify or retract while the examination is incomplete. The defect may be that one of the seven required interrogations is missing or not properly completed, not necessarily that the witnesses themselves acquire the status of wicked false witnesses. Thus Taz's distinction between preserving the witnesses' personal fitness and merely losing the testimony is not enough to explain Ran. There is another difficulty from Pesachim 12. Rava challenges Abaye's reconciliation of one witness saying the third hour and one the fourth. Abaye explains one as meaning the end of the third and the other the beginning of the fourth. Rava asks: how can we be exacting with witnesses so as to execute a person on that assumption? Perhaps the one who said the third meant the beginning of the third and the one who said the fourth meant the end of the fourth, in which case the testimonies are contradictory. The Gemara does not say that because otherwise one witness would be false we should preserve them through a favorable interpretation. It treats the possibility of contradiction as enough to prevent capital punishment. Likewise Tosafot ask regarding one saying the second hour and another the third, and the Gemara in Sanhedrin concerning ben sorer umoreh derives that we follow a majority in capital cases from the rule that witnesses can be reconciled through ordinary error concerning the intercalation of the month. Scripture says, "the congregation shall judge and the congregation shall save," and yet we rely on the majority of people who err in that matter. The proof itself shows that absent a majority basis, we would not merely say, "Preserve the witnesses in their presumption of fitness." We would treat the uncertainty against conviction. One might answer that capital law is different because of the command to seek grounds of acquittal, whereas in civil law we preserve testimony more readily. But Ran's own proof for the civil rule is precisely from the capital-law case of the second and third of the month. Moreover, if Taz were correct, then in civil law one witness saying the third hour and another the fifth could perhaps be reconciled by saying one meant the end of the third and one the beginning of the fifth, since that falls within the range of ordinary human error according to some views. Yet no authority distinguishes civil from capital law in that manner, and Rambam states the rules of time-error without such a distinction. The proper explanation is therefore more limited. When Ran says we reconcile witnesses, he is speaking about a matter in which witnesses are ordinarily prone to error, and there is a recognized halachic basis, generally a majority or established common human mistake, for interpreting the statements that way. His responsum, siman 34, says expressly at the outset that in matters in which witnesses are commonly mistaken, the court may interpret their words so that their testimony is not contradictory. He then cites the intercalated-month case because there too we follow a majority concerning a common mistake. Accordingly, there is no contradiction to Ramban's rule. Where no such ordinary majority or established error exists, we do not create an interpretation merely to preserve testimony. For purposes of extracting money we treat the testimony according to its ordinary sense and apply the burden-of-proof rule. Where a strong majority of people err in the relevant way, that is not a speculative reinterpretation but the normal legal understanding of human testimony. One might ask that in monetary law we generally say we do not follow a majority to extract money. I already explained in the Kuntres HaTefisot that the primary view follows Tosafot in Sanhedrin: where the majority is complete and strong, even monetary law can follow it. The common error concerning the intercalation of the month is such a majority. It defines the content of the testimony itself. Accordingly, Ramban's proof from successive admissions is sound. There, absent a majority showing that two unspecified admissions ordinarily refer to the same debt, we do not force them into one maneh in order to extract money. The same is true of Sema's barrel of wine versus oil. Since there is no majority basis establishing that witnesses ordinarily confuse those exact objects in the manner required to preserve the claim, the statements can be treated as contradictory and the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Still, another matter troubled me. At the beginning of Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot, the Gemara discusses a document dated the first of Nisan in the Sabbatical year. Witnesses come and say that the signatories were with them elsewhere on that date. The document can nevertheless be preserved by saying that it was written later and antedated to the time of the transaction. Similarly a later-dated document can be upheld through an explanation of delay. Why do we strain to preserve that document? Writing a document later is not ordinarily preferred, and the poskim say one should not intentionally delay the date. Why not treat the signatories as contradicted and leave the money with the possessor? At first I thought this supported Taz: wherever treating the testimony according to its apparent sense would disqualify witnesses, we reconcile them if possible. But this is still difficult, because in the hourly contradictions of Pesachim the witnesses would likewise appear false, yet we do not always reconcile them. I then considered distinguishing between two witnesses and one witness. Perhaps when two witnesses together have established a fact, we are more reluctant to treat both as false and therefore seek a reconciliation. This would explain a signed document, where two witnesses support the loan. It could also explain Tosafot's discussion where two witnesses agree on an hour. But Ramban's own example concerns two sets of witnesses, two saying four hundred and two saying five hundred, and he nevertheless applies the burden-of-proof principle rather than force a reconciliation. His proof from successive admissions also does not allow such a distinction. Our reasoning cannot stand against Ramban. The better distinction appears procedural. When two witnesses have already testified in beit din and their testimony was accepted without any flaw, the testimony has acquired a presumption of validity in court. A signed document is even treated as though the testimony had already been examined in beit din. If only afterward another set appears with a challenge, we do not remove the first testimony from its established judicial presumption unless the contradiction is clear. Where there is a reasonable explanation, such as saying the document was written later, we preserve it. But when the uncertainty arises immediately within the first presentation of the testimony itself, before the court has ever accepted it as coherent evidence, there is no established testimony to preserve. The court must first receive clear evidence before extracting money or imposing capital liability. If the statements are naturally contradictory and there is no recognized majority basis for reconciling them, the testimony never acquires the judicial presumption in the first place. This also explains why later hakchashah does not necessarily destroy an already accepted document where an interpretation remains open, while an immediate contradiction between the witnesses themselves can prevent acceptance. The same principle accords with the difference between testimony given sequentially after the first has already been completed and testimony that arrives together as one unit. Accordingly, Mechaber and Sema are not contradicted by the rule of siman 29. Ran's rule applies where there is an ordinary, established way in which witnesses make the relevant mistake or where an already accepted testimony can reasonably be interpreted consistently. Ramban's rule applies where the testimony is unspecified or contradictory from the outset and no strong basis compels one interpretation. There the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Therefore Sema's ruling concerning one witness saying wine and one oil remains correct if, in the circumstances, no recognized common mistake reconciles them. We do not create a speculative second transaction or barrel merely to extract money. The testimony is treated as contradictory for the monetary result. There is another aspect of Ramban's ruling that should be stated explicitly. If two witnesses say that four hundred was paid and two others say five hundred, the court does not automatically say all four speak about one payment and thereby create contradiction. If the claimant wishes to extract on the assumption that all refer to one event, he must establish that. Otherwise each set can refer to a different event. This is precisely the force of "the burden of proof rests on the claimant." The court does not create a contradiction merely to help one party. The reverse is also true. Where the testimony itself expressly identifies one and the same event, the court cannot separate it into two events merely to preserve the witnesses. For example, if both sets identify the same date, place, borrower, lender, and transaction and then disagree on an essential amount or object, the contradiction is real unless the discrepancy belongs to an established category of ordinary human mistake. This explains why ordinary interpretation remains important. We do not always choose the reading that invalidates or the reading that validates. We first ask what the witnesses' words naturally mean and whether Chazal recognized the particular discrepancy as one in which people commonly err. Only then do we apply the burden-of-proof rule. The rule of siman 29, that a witness's words should be reconciled where possible, does not authorize a court to insert an event never mentioned or to invent a remote factual possibility. It means that where the language itself can naturally bear a consistent interpretation, especially in a matter commonly confused, the court should not seize upon a strained contradiction. Likewise, when two witnesses appear contradictory, the court may reconcile them through an ordinary linguistic or factual explanation. But if the reconciliation requires assuming an additional transaction, an additional barrel, or a different date without any basis in their words, that is no longer interpretation of testimony; it is construction of a new factual case. For extracting money, the court may not do that against the possessor. This is also why the rule concerning a signed document is not comparable. A document already presents itself as one legally operative instrument. The court's task is to interpret the date and legal effect of that instrument. Saying that it was written later can be an interpretation of the document's own legal form, recognized by the laws of documents. By contrast, separating two oral testimonies into two transactions where neither said so can be an invention rather than an interpretation. Accordingly, the cases fit one framework. An already valid legal instrument is preserved through recognized document-law explanations. Testimony involving a common human mistake is reconciled through the ordinary majority of human behavior. But where testimony is initially ambiguous or contradictory and no established rule resolves it, we do not extract money by supplying facts the witnesses never gave. This is the meaning of Mechaber's ruling that unspecified testimony is judged as contradictory for leniency: the court leaves the money with the possessor unless the claimant establishes the version needed for collection.
