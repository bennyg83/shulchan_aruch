We treat the matter as contradiction, leniently. Taz distinguishes between contradiction in the formal inquiries and contradiction in ordinary examination. Where a discrepancy can be reconciled without disqualifying witnesses, we ordinarily try to reconcile it, especially in monetary law, because witnesses have a presumption of fitness. The Talmudic rule in capital cases that one should not force a reconciliation that preserves incriminating testimony rests on 'the congregation shall judge and the congregation shall save.' That special leniency does not govern ordinary monetary cases. Therefore in monetary law, if two accounts can reasonably be harmonized, even somewhat narrowly, we should do so rather than declare the witnesses invalid. This is consistent with the rule in siman 29 that we reconcile a witness's words where people commonly use the relevant expression imprecisely. The distinction is not that inquiries are always treated more strictly in money, but that in capital law the Torah itself directs the court toward rescue, whereas in money preserving qualified testimony is itself a legitimate goal.
He took possession of the land. Beit Yosef questions Rabbeinu Yerucham's comparison between two gift deeds and two witnesses. If a recipient presents two gift deeds, one dated Nisan and another Tishrei, production of the second can amount to an admission weakening the first. If the first deed were truly valid and still in his possession, why would he produce a later deed that worsens his own position? That can justify saying the later deed supersedes the first. But where he has one witness to a gift in Nisan and another witness to a gift in Tishrei, there is no such admission. The first witnesses may have died, gone away, or refused to write a deed, and the donor may therefore have repeated the kinyan before different witnesses. Presenting both witnesses is likewise necessary because neither alone suffices. Therefore the witnesses can combine to establish the recipient's title. The same can apply even in a sale: one may repurchase or renew the act to secure proof when earlier witnesses are unavailable. A written deed differs because the claimant already holds the first proof and yet voluntarily presents the second instrument that undermines it.
