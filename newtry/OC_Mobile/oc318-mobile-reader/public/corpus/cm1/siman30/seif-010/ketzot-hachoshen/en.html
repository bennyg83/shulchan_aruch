I repaid. The same principle means that one witness signed in a document can combine with one oral witness who testifies to a kinyan in order to collect from encumbered property. At first this is difficult. With two separate witnesses to distinct events, the Torah trusts the combined pair not to lie. Here, however, perhaps the witness in the document tells the truth and the oral witness lies. One document witness alone does not collect from purchasers. According to the Sema in siman 51, who says the kinyan occurred also before the signed witness, the matter is easier. But Kesef Mishneh and the Shach imply that combination works even without that. There is a further difficulty even with an ordinary two witness document, because the witnesses may sign one after another. Perhaps the first signed alone truthfully, while the second later signed falsely without the borrower's knowledge. The answer follows the responsa attributed to Ramban, siman 94: even one witness is not suspected of fabrication. His testimony can be trusted for an oath and for certain documentary matters, such as validating an erasure. The Torah's exclusion of one witness from ordinary monetary extraction is not because he is presumed dishonest. It is a formal evidentiary rule, like the exclusion of relatives even if they are as truthful as Moshe and Aharon. Therefore, once two witnesses stand before us in legally combinable forms, we rely upon them, even if each alone could not have produced the same monetary consequence. This is strong support for Ramban's rule that one witness can be trusted in validating an erasure.
