Seif 11, in the gloss. “But others who heard the matter cannot join, for this would be testimony from the mouth of another witness.” Note: See the Sema, who writes that merely seeing witnesses whose testimony was examined in beit din does not help. The Taz disagrees and holds that it does. With due respect, the Sema seems to have overlooked Tosafot in Bava Kamma 98a and Ketubot 18, where it is apparent that witnesses may testify, “We saw testimony that was examined in beit din.” Ketzot HaChoshen later noticed this as well. In youthful novellae to the first chapter of Gittin, in the sugya concerning before how many a get is given, I brought support for the Taz against the Sema. I now see further proof from Bava Kamma 70b. Abaye says: does Rabbi Akiva not concede that where two witnesses say that a woman was betrothed and another two say that she cohabited, even the witnesses to cohabitation need the witnesses to kiddushin? Since the kiddushin witnesses do not need the cohabitation witnesses, this is called a complete matter. At first glance the logic is difficult. What does it help that the first pair does not need the second? The second pair still needs the first. The answer is that once the witnesses to kiddushin testify in beit din, their testimony is complete. The witnesses to cohabitation who hear that testimony can then themselves testify that they saw witnesses whose testimony concerning kiddushin was examined in court. Hearing completed testimony before beit din is legally comparable to witnessing the established fact. Thus the second pair can be treated as though they themselves know the kiddushin, and their testimony is not merely half of a matter dependent on another pair. Where two sets truly depend upon one another, by contrast, the testimony of each set is not complete on its own. Hearing incomplete testimony does not establish the underlying fact. But where the first testimony is independently complete, anyone who hears it in beit din can later testify to the judicially established testimony. This is even stronger where the woman thereby becomes publicly established as married. Tosafot themselves imply that witnesses to cohabitation require independent kiddushin evidence only where she had not otherwise been established as a married woman. Accordingly, even if the cohabitation witnesses were not present when the kiddushin witnesses originally testified, the fact that such testimony could be judicially transmitted to others means the kiddushin has become a legally established fact independent of the original witnesses. This is strong proof that testimony of the form, “We saw witnesses whose testimony was examined in beit din,” is valid.
