Where the case appears fraudulent. Rivash writes that although monetary cases suspected of fraud require examination and investigation, they are still not identical to capital cases so that testimony is automatically void whenever a witness says, "I do not know," in response to investigative questions. Rama rules this way below in seif 15. Responsa Maharshal, however, holds that a fraudulent monetary case returns completely to the Torah law of capital procedure, and testimony not properly examined is invalid. This helps explain Rashi in Yevamot 31. The Gemara asks why no date was instituted in kiddushin documents, and answers that if the document were left with the woman she might erase the date. Rashi writes that if she did so she could be executed because she would remain under her original presumption as a married woman. Tosafot challenges this from Gittin 17, where the Gemara asks what the enactment of dating a get accomplished, since she could cut off the date, and answers that we do not suspect deliberate fraud. This implies that without a date she would not be executed. According to the view that a fraudulent monetary or status case reverts fully to Torah examination, the matter is resolved. The Nimukei Yosef writes that the rabbinic relaxation of examination in monetary law was instituted to avoid locking the door before borrowers, and similarly in gittin the Sages validated undated documents through their authority over kiddushin. Where clear fraud exists, however, the original Torah requirement returns. Thus if she deliberately erases the date, that very fraud restores the requirement of full investigation. The undated document then fails, and her original married status remains. This is what Rashi means when the Gemara says we do not suspect such obvious fraud.
A matter involving prohibition. Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer 17 and 42, rules that testimony concerning gittin and kiddushin ordinarily does not require formal examination and investigation. Maharshal, citing Agudah, nevertheless requires such examination, and several authorities agree. Their view seems difficult from Gittin 17, which asks why the Sages instituted a date in a get, for without a date there would be no answer to the investigative question, "On which day?" Likewise, a get remains valid if it contains only the week, without the year, month, or day, and kiddushin contain no dating requirement at all. How then can these authorities require full investigation? The answer follows Ran in Sanhedrin. If witnesses come before beit din and say, "Today so and so lent so and so a maneh," there is no need to ask the year, week, month, and day, because "today" itself fixes the time. Witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din. At the moment they sign a get or kiddushin instrument, their testimony is effectively, "At this very time he divorced," or, "At this very time he betrothed." Thus all temporal investigation is conceptually satisfied at the time of signing, even though when the document later reaches us we no longer know the calendar date. Nimukei Yosef's proof from an undated get must follow the other view cited by Ran that even testimony saying "today" still requires the full series of questions. According to Ran's primary view, oral testimony about an earlier event must be investigated, while contemporaneous documentary testimony is already fixed to the moment of signature. This also explains the Gemara's question concerning later dated monetary documents. Rashi says the loan occurred in Nisan and they waited until Iyar to write the document. At the moment of signing they are testifying about a prior loan, not about an act occurring before them now, and therefore temporal investigation is lacking. But in a get or kiddushin signed at the time of the act, the witnesses' statement is effectively about that present moment and is sufficiently investigated.
