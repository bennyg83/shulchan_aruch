Where the case appears fraudulent, interrogation and examination are required in order to reach the truth. See above, siman 15, where Rama cites Rivash that one need not necessarily conduct the complete seven formal interrogations as in capital cases, but should clarify the matter in whatever manner the circumstances require. Kenesset HaGedolah cites many authorities who disagree and require full interrogation and examination. The plain language of the Gemara supports them. The Gemara explains the Mishnah's statement that civil and capital cases both require interrogation and examination by establishing the civil case as one that appears fraudulent. This strongly implies that the same formal process is required. I find further support from Tosafot in Bava Batra 32. Tosafot ask why, where two witnesses say a woman was divorced and two deny it, if she married before the contradicting witnesses arrived she does not have to leave the marriage. Their answer is that she relies upon the possibility that the first witnesses will be refuted through hazamah or disqualified for robbery. The question arises: if witnesses to a divorce ordinarily do not require formal interrogation and examination, how could hazamah ever be possible? Without a fixed time and place there is no practical way to refute them through "you were with us elsewhere." The answer is that once contradictory witnesses arrive, the case itself becomes suspicious and fraudulent. One of the sets is certainly lying. In a fraudulent case, full interrogation and examination are required, including time and place, and hazamah becomes possible. This explains why Tosafot frame their question specifically where the woman married first and the contradicting witnesses came later. At the time the first witnesses testified there was as yet no suspicious circumstance, and formal interrogation was not required. Their testimony may therefore lack the information necessary for hazamah. If both sets had appeared together from the outset, the contradiction itself would make the case fraudulent and require full interrogation and examination. Tosafot can then say that she is careful because the witnesses might be refuted. Their alternative answer, that she also fears the witnesses may later be disqualified for robbery, covers the case where hazamah is not technically possible. This reading is further supported by Rashba's responsa concerning kiddushin, where he indicates that a suspicious case requires full interrogation and examination. Therefore Rivash's limitation requires study, and there is substantial basis for the stricter view that a genuinely fraudulent civil case requires the full formal interrogations.
