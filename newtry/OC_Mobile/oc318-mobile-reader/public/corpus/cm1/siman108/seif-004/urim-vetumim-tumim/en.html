We do not plead for them that it was lost through unavoidable accident. The Shach argues at length in favor of the dissenting authorities, but his words are not sufficient to decide against Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur, whose view is supported by the straightforward reading of the Gemara. The opposing camp was forced into many strained explanations, as is well known from the works of the authorities. The Shach's principal argument is: how can we plead for the heirs that the deposit was returned by virtue of a migo that it was lost through unavoidable accident, when we do not plead the claim of unavoidable accident itself for them? This is no argument. According to every view one must explain that when the Gemara says the creditor collects the whole amount, the heirs admitted that no unavoidable accident occurred, or as the Baal HaMaor explains, that if an unavoidable accident had occurred the decedent would certainly have instructed his household concerning it. Yet all agree that if the decedent himself were alive he could claim that he returned it by virtue of the migo that it was lost through unavoidable accident. The same structure can therefore apply according to Tosafot and their school. This is also logically correct. Every case where we charge heirs on a documented loan rests upon the inference: if the debt had been paid, why is the document still in the creditor's possession? Here that inference is absent. We can say that the depositary returned the object and did not insist on retrieving his document because he continually thought: whenever the depositor sues me, I will be believed by a migo that the object was lost through unavoidable accident. The fact that this alternative claim requires a bailee's oath is not enough to make him insist on retrieval of the document. Perhaps he was willing to risk having to take an unnecessary oath and did not regard that concern as sufficient to prevent him from leaving the document outstanding. This is precisely what the Gemara asks in Bava Batra 70 concerning Rav Chisda's rule that a depositary is believed to say "I returned it" by a migo that he could say "it was lost through unavoidable accident." The Gemara asks: does not the latter claim require an oath? In other words, how can a person return the deposit without taking back the document merely because he expects to have the migo of unavoidable accident, when that migo would still force him to swear? Why should he return without his document and expose himself to an oath? The answer is that he is indeed believed only with an oath, but fear of an oath is not enough to make it implausible that he left the document. This is simple. The authorities have discussed it at unnecessary length, and there is no difficulty in the Shach's argument. There is, however, a genuine difficulty for this position. The Gemara says that Rav Chisda's rule, that one who deposited property under a written document is believed to say "I returned it" by a migo of unavoidable accident, is dependent on a tannaitic dispute. A baraita teaches concerning a sh'tar kis issued against orphans that the judges of exile say the holder collects the entire amount, while the judges of Eretz Yisrael say he collects half. The Gemara labors to explain the dispute. Why did it not explain simply as follows? At the initial stage, the dispute appears very broad. According to the view that he collects half, the claim "I returned it" must be sufficiently common that we plead it for heirs. According to the view that he collects all, that claim is not even within the range of ordinary concern. Why not say that the latter holds a claim of return is uncommon and therefore not pleaded for heirs, while the former holds it sufficiently common and therefore pleads it, allowing collection only of half? If the depositary himself were alive and expressly insisted, "I returned it," what difference would it make that the claim is uncommon? According to all opinions he would be believed by his migo. Rav Chisda would then be correct without depending upon the tannaitic dispute. One might answer even according to those who disagree with Tosafot and hold that we do plead uncommon claims for heirs. The dispute could instead be whether an uncommon migo is accepted. The view that the creditor collects the whole amount might hold that the migo from "it was lost through unavoidable accident" is uncommon and an uncommon migo is ineffective, like the view of Rabbi Shimshon of Kinon cited above in the rules of migo. The opposing view could hold either that an uncommon migo is accepted, like those who disagree with Rabbi Shimshon, or that the claim of return itself is also uncommon and therefore can be supported by an equally uncommon migo. But this is not satisfactory if the claim of return is regarded as common because the holder may have retained the document for the scribe's fee. Then there is no migo to an uncommon accident claim according to the first view. The other view could disagree either about uncommon migo or about how common the claim of return is. Yet this entire discussion concerns a sh'tar kis, where the document might indeed be retained for the scribe's fee. With an ordinary deposit, by contrast, all should agree that a return without retrieving the document is uncommon. Why would he leave the document? Precisely because the return claim itself is uncommon, it can be supported by the equally uncommon migo of unavoidable accident. Rav Chisda, who discusses an ordinary deposit, would therefore remain correct according to everyone. The Gemara's presentation as a tannaitic dispute would still be difficult. According to what I wrote above in the rules of migo, however, all agree that "I returned it" is intrinsically an uncommon claim when a document remains outstanding. The reason we nevertheless plead it for heirs is the combination of two possibilities: perhaps the item was lost through unavoidable accident, and if not, perhaps it was returned. The accumulation of two distinct possibilities elevates what would otherwise be an uncommon concern into a sufficiently plausible one for heirs. On this basis the Gemara's structure is explained. It never considered saying that one opinion regards "I returned it" as independently common. Rather, that opinion holds that the combination of possibilities makes the overall defense sufficiently plausible. The view that the creditor collects the entire amount must therefore hold that "I returned it" is not a legally cognizable defense at all in this setting. It is strained to say that the tannaim dispute the bare statistical proposition whether combining two concerns converts an uncommon claim into a common one, since the underlying reasoning is sound. The Gemara can therefore correctly treat Rav Chisda's rule as dependent on their dispute. This also answers the first difficulty according to Tosafot. We cannot say that the view collecting the entire amount merely regards "I returned it" as uncommon and therefore does not plead it for heirs. Everyone agrees it is uncommon standing alone. The only reason to plead it is its combination with the possibility of unavoidable accident. It is not plausible that the tannaim dispute whether two combined concerns have cumulative force. The view that rejects the defense must therefore regard return as legally unavailable in this documented setting, and the dispute genuinely bears upon Rav Chisda's rule. According to this, however, Tosafot's view that we plead "I returned it" for heirs must refer to a case where the decedent had not instructed his household and the heirs do not know that no unavoidable accident occurred. If it is known that no unavoidable accident occurred, the return claim remains alone, and is an uncommon claim not pleaded for heirs. There is then no combination. This requires study, because neither the Tur nor the Shulchan Aruch hints at such a qualification. There is yet another difficulty. What compelled Tosafot to decide that we do not plead unavoidable accident for heirs because it is uncommon? They ask that here Rava rules that the holder of the sh'tar kis collects half upon oath, whereas elsewhere in Shevuot he appears to rule against Rav Chisda that one who deposited under a document is not believed to say "I returned it" unless he has witnesses. The Rashbam, Tosafot, and Riva offer several answers. But initially, before the Gemara had found its later explanation for the opinion that the holder collects the entire amount, it was forced to say that "I returned it" is not pleaded for heirs. Tosafot therefore had to press themselves to say that even unavoidable accident is not pleaded because it is uncommon. Once the Gemara concludes by explaining that the decedent would have instructed his household, however, perhaps everyone agrees that even uncommon claims may be pleaded for heirs. The reason the holder collects the whole amount could simply be that if an unavoidable accident had occurred the decedent would certainly have left instructions, as the Baal HaMaor explicitly writes. He would be even more likely to give instructions concerning an unavoidable accident than concerning an ordinary return, because the depositor would otherwise have no knowledge of what happened. On that basis, Rava could disagree with Rav Chisda entirely. A claim of return in the face of a written document might be legally unavailable according to everyone. The opinion collecting the entire amount would say that even the unavoidable accident claim is not pleaded because the decedent would have instructed his household. The opinion collecting half could hold that death itself prevented him from doing so, and therefore we plead unavoidable accident and allow collection only of half. Rava could then rule like the latter, while still maintaining that if the depositary himself is alive, admits no accident, and says only "I returned it," he is not believed and must prove return by witnesses. That would explain the authorities who rule against Rav Chisda. Accordingly, to defend Tosafot we must explain the earlier stage more carefully. So long as the Gemara assumed according to everyone that death may prevent instruction, it treated as an established principle that if a claim of return is not pleaded for heirs because it is uncommon, then the depositary himself also cannot be believed by such a claim supported by the accident migo. Why? If he truly returned the item, why should its uncommonness matter when he personally asserts it? The answer is that if the depositary could safely rely on a migo of unavoidable accident, that reliance itself explains why he returned the object without retrieving the document. But if he knows that, should he suddenly die, the heirs will not be given the benefit of that defense because it is too uncommon, then it is implausible that he would leave the document in the depositor's hands. He might die unexpectedly without instructing his household, the creditor would collect from the heirs, and strangers would consume his wealth. Why would he create such a danger by returning the property without taking his document? Therefore, unless the law also protects his heirs by pleading the combined possibilities for them, his own claim of return is weakened to the point that the migo cannot explain his conduct. Thus, at the initial stage, the cases are linked. One cannot say that the heirs are denied the uncommon defense but the living depositary is believed by it, because the very fact that his heirs would be exposed makes his conduct of returning without the document unreasonable. Once the Gemara introduces the possibility that a person normally gives instructions to his household, however, the matter changes. A depositary could return the item and rely on the fact that if he dies he will have instructed his household. That can make his own return claim credible even if, in a case where no instruction is found, the heirs receive no such plea. This is the basis of the later distinction. This also explains why Tosafot can infer from the final structure that an uncommon claim is not ordinarily pleaded for heirs. If the Gemara held that even an uncommon claim is freely pleaded, it would not need the special explanation that the decedent would have instructed his household. It could simply distinguish a sh'tar kis from an ordinary deposit by saying that the return of a documented deposit is uncommon, yet is still pleaded for heirs, and Rav Chisda's case would create no difficulty. The fact that the Gemara needs the instruction rationale shows that mere uncommon defenses are not pleaded for heirs. This is precise, and the entire sugya is thereby coherent. The same logic also illuminates the Shach's earlier point concerning repayment within the fixed time. If repayment within the fixed time is so uncommon that we would not plead it for heirs, then even while alive the debtor's claim is weakened. How could he have repaid while leaving the document, relying upon an argument that would fail if he died unexpectedly and the creditor collected from his heirs? This supports the approach explained above.
