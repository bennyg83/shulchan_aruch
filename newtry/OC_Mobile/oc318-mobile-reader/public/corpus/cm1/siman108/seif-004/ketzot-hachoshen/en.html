The reason beit din does not advance for heirs the plea that a deposited object may have been lost through unavoidable accident is that this is not a sufficiently common event to be advanced automatically. Rosh explains that an administrator may sometimes raise a less common defense where there is some basis for it, even though beit din itself would not introduce it. A wholly implausible or apparently false plea may not be raised even by an administrator, because of the verse, "Distance yourself from falsehood." Accordingly, unavoidable accident is not a thing that never occurs. An administrator who has some factual basis for concern can raise it. The court does not do so on its own because the possibility does not naturally trouble the judges. This resembles the phrase "I am certain" in other areas, meaning that one's heart is not troubled by the doubt. If the administrator's heart is genuinely troubled, his plea can be heard. This suggests that adult heirs who themselves say, "Perhaps it was lost through unavoidable accident," should be no worse than an administrator. A difficulty arises from the sugya of one who robbed and fed the stolen property to his children. When adult heirs say, "We do not know our father's accounts with you," the Gemara does not simply exempt them on the possibility that their father repurchased the property, because repurchase was considered uncommon. Yet if an administrator can raise an uncommon possibility, certainly the heirs themselves should be able to. One cannot answer simply that repurchase is wholly impossible, since the heirs themselves are the litigants and should in principle be able to plead even a very uncommon possibility. This point requires clarification.
The words "or its substitutes" mean that if witnesses establish that the property presently before us is the identifiable substitute of the original business capital, there is no remaining concern for an oath. Tumim suggested that where the original merchandise was exchanged, perhaps the recipient could still claim that he had returned the first property and that the present goods were acquired from his own funds, relying on a migo of unavoidable accident. This is not so where the witnesses actually saw the exchange and can identify the substitute. There is therefore no dispute between the sources. Responsa Maimoniyot speaks where witnesses knew the original business property but did not witness the later exchange. Once the original merchandise changed form, the recipient could say he had returned it and that the new merchandise was his, with a migo of unavoidable accident. Rif, Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch speak where witnesses establish that the present object derives from that very business property or its identifiable substitute. In that case a plea of unavoidable accident is impossible, because the property is presently before us. The distinction is straightforward.
