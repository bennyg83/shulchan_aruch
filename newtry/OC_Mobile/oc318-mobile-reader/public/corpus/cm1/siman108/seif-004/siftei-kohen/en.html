Or if he died within the term, etc. This ruling requires study. The Beit Yosef's references to Tosafot in HaMokher Et HaBayit and to the Rosh's responsum, rule 86, support only the rule that we do not plead unavoidable accident for orphans. They do not establish the Tur's implication that, within the term, even the custodian himself could not claim that he returned the item. This is difficult, because at the beginning of Bava Batra the Gemara asks whether a migo is accepted against the presumption that a person does not pay before the due date, and we rule leniently for the defendant, as in siman 78:4. According to the Tur, one must distinguish: there the defendant could have remained silent about early payment, so his statement is treated as 'the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted.' Here, however, a document exists, so that doctrine does not apply. Therefore, although he has a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident, he is not believed against the presumption. The Ramban discusses a similar distinction in HaMokher Et HaBayit concerning the argument, 'Why is your document in my possession?' An unvalidated document is not yet a document at all, and the defendant's denial creates the very basis of the claim, but where a valid document exists, an ordinary migo may not overcome that evidentiary force. Accordingly, even if a migo suffices after the term, perhaps it does not suffice before the term. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 72 similarly distinguish between migo and 'the mouth that prohibited.' Nevertheless, Tosafot in HaMokher Et HaBayit, 70a, appear not to accept this distinction here, since they offer other answers. The Ramban himself also concludes that the issue may remain only an unresolved question. Indeed, Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Batra imply expressly that no such distinction exists, because they compare the presumption against early payment with the rule of Rami bar Chama that one who deposited under a document cannot claim return through a migo of unavoidable accident. Their question makes sense only if an actual document would not alter the treatment of the presumption. This is contrary to the Tur and Mechaber. Their ruling therefore requires study. Perhaps they mean only that we do not plead return for orphans in such a case, because return within the term is unusual. Yet their plain wording does not indicate this, and according to what I explain below, that we plead all possible defenses for heirs, the difficulty remains.
Some say that we plead for them that it was lost through unavoidable accident. This appears to me the primary view. Otherwise, how could we plead after the term that the item was returned through a migo of unavoidable accident, if unavoidable accident itself is not pleaded for heirs? Tosafot and the Rosh say that such an accident is uncommon, but the sugya at the beginning of HaMafkid causes me doubt, as I wrote in siman 82 concerning migo. The Ramban, in Milchamot at the end of HaMokher Et HaBayit, argues at length that the opposite is true, and that the sugya proves we do plead unavoidable accident for heirs. The Rif at the end of HaMekabel rules that on a partnership note against orphans the creditor swears and collects the loan half, while as to the deposit half we suspect that it may have been lost by accident or repaid. Nimukei Yosef explains that we plead for heirs every defense their father could have raised, including unavoidable accident. The Ritva likewise rejects the notion that an uncommon defense is not pleaded for heirs, citing the verse that one may appear poor while possessing great wealth, and follows the Ramban. The Baal HaTerumot states the same. The Gidulei Terumah's assertion that one who rejects Rav Chisda would not even believe the depositor himself regarding unavoidable accident is plainly mistaken. Rav Amram's very question assumes that unavoidable accident is believed; he asks only whether that migo also supports a claim of return, despite the outstanding document. Rav Chisda answers that it does. Thus the Ramban's resolution is correct. Nor is it true that the later authorities unanimously follow Tosafot: Nimukei Yosef, the Ritva, the Baal HaTerumot, and apparently the Ran follow the Ramban. Their position is primary.
