An iska note. An iska is structured so that half the capital is a deposit in the recipient's possession. The profit from that half therefore belongs to the investor. The other half is a loan, and its profit belongs to the recipient rather than the investor, to avoid interest. The recipient receives compensation for his work with the deposit half and therefore has the status of a paid custodian for that half, liable for theft and loss but exempt from unavoidable accidents. With respect to the loan half, he is liable as with any loan. See Yoreh Deah, laws of interest, for the full rules of iska. Accordingly, concerning the loan half the investor swears and collects like any creditor collecting from heirs. Concerning the deposit half, if the recipient were alive he could claim, 'I returned it,' through a migo that he could have claimed that it was lost through an unavoidable accident, and would be believed with an oath. Therefore, after his death we make the corresponding claim for the heirs. We do not say that he was one who was obligated to swear and, being unable to swear, must pay. That principle is not applied to heirs in such a case, where the deceased is no longer alive to know and swear.
Specifically if they have no guardians, etc. Ran and Rosh write this in Kesubos 109b. There the guardian first raised the claim, and Abaye said that he had argued well because Rabbi Yochanan said so. They ask why Abaye waited until the guardian raised it, and answer that a judge does not plead for the heirs until a guardian has been appointed to argue for them. Tosafos there instead write that Abaye had initially forgotten Rabbi Yochanan's ruling, implying that had he remembered it he would have raised the claim himself. Tosafos therefore do not accept this rule. Nevertheless, Rama evidently relied on Ran and Rosh.
And they have no oath. To be exempt, heirs need not take the heirs' oath that their father did not instruct them. They merely accept a cherem that their father did not tell them that the debt had been paid.
Or he died within the term, etc. There is a presumption that a person does not pay before the due date. Shach objects that the recipient has a migo that the deposit was lost through unavoidable accident, and the question whether such a migo is effective against this presumption is unresolved. Shach therefore explains that the reason is instead that a claim of return in such circumstances is unusual, and we do not plead an unusual claim for heirs. According to him, if the recipient himself were alive he would be believed even within the term through the migo, and according to the opinion that we plead unavoidable accident for heirs even though it is unusual, we would likewise plead return within the term. See Tumim. I clarified that Tur and the Mechaber are correct: such a migo is not accepted, and all agree that we do not plead it for heirs. Even if the recipient is alive and makes that claim, he is not believed. See there.
Or he gave the investor credibility. Then the recipient himself could no longer claim that he returned the funds, and therefore we do not make that claim for his heirs. Nevertheless, the investor must still swear, because the credibility clause may have been intended only against the borrower himself, not against his heirs. See Tumim. Above in siman 71 there is a dispute whether an ordinary credibility clause is sufficient against a migo, or whether the creditor must be believed like two witnesses. According to the latter view, the present case must involve credibility like two witnesses. See Tumim above in siman 71.
We do not plead for them that it was lost through accident. That is an uncommon event, and we do not plead uncommon claims for heirs. See Tumim. I wrote that if it is known that no accident occurred, perhaps we likewise do not plead 'I returned it.' See there at length for the resolution of the difficulty on this opinion, and see the general rules of migo concerning an uncommon migo.
That it was lost through accident. If the recipient was an unpaid custodian, whether we plead theft or loss for the heirs, I concluded that these too are uncommon and are not pleaded. See Tumim. But we do plead 'I returned it,' even concerning a deposit, through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident, even though the concept that one preserves a receipt for incidental writing expenses does not apply in the same way. See Tumim and below in siman 296.
Or its substitutes, etc. See Tumim. Where business capital was used to purchase merchandise, and that merchandise was exchanged for other merchandise, it requires study whether the creditor takes the substituted merchandise without allowing the heirs to claim that the original capital was returned. See there.
And if there is profit, etc. This means that the iska was for a fixed term and the recipient died during that term, terminating the arrangement. One might have thought that the heirs receive no profit because profit was assigned only through the end of the iska term. The ruling teaches that since the arrangement terminates early, the heirs receive their proportionate share of the profit. See Sema.
