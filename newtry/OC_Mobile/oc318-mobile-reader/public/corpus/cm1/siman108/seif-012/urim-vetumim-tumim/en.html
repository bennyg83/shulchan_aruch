If the debtor left property, etc. See Urim. This law is the same with respect to a guarantor, and likewise one who seeks to collect from purchasers. If they have no one from whom to recover, as where the debtor left nothing, the heir collects. Accordingly, the same is true where there is an earlier creditor and a later creditor, the earlier creditor died, then the debtor died during the creditor's lifetime, and afterward the creditor died, and the debtor's estate is insufficient to pay both. The earlier creditor collects. Had the later creditor already collected, the earlier creditor could remove the property from him, because with respect to a purchaser, and similarly a later creditor who collected, who is no stronger than a purchaser, we do not say that a person cannot transmit an oath to his children when the later creditor has no one from whom to recover. All the more so before the later creditor has collected, since he has no one from whom to recover, the earlier creditor collects. Certainly, if the heirs themselves are the earlier creditor, such as where they claim under their mother's ketubah, which is earlier, they collect, because they are already in possession and have two advantages. This is straightforward. There is no need for what the Beit Shmuel writes in Even HaEzer siman 96 in the name of Responsa Shevut Yaakov, that the heirs can say, "We waive our mother's oath." There is room to challenge that argument. The other side claims that perhaps she was already paid or was given valuables in satisfaction. How can a waiver by the heirs operate to extract money and weaken the creditor's right? Rather, the law is straightforward for the reason stated: where the estate is insufficient to pay both, an earlier creditor vis a vis a later creditor has the same status as against purchasers. This is also evident from Teshuvot Maimoniyot, Sefer Kinyan siman 18, and Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, Prague edition siman 984, concerning a woman who took possession of her husband's property, and the son waived for her the oath on her ketubah as well as the excess beyond the ketubah. The son later died, and the son's wife challenged the waiver of the excess. See there at length. It is evident that the principle that a person cannot transmit an oath to his children created no problem there. However, in the cited responsum siman 859 it is stated that a man died, his widow seized movables toward partial payment of her ketubah, and she died without taking an oath. A later creditor then came and claimed against the heirs. The ruling was that the heirs are exempt because only half the amount is at issue, and we rule like Rav Nachman that the parties divide; regarding that half we do not apply the rule that a person cannot transmit an oath to his children. This implies that regarding the other half the rule would apply, even though she was an earlier creditor relative to the later creditor and the later creditor had no one from whom to recover. This seems difficult according to what was stated above. It must be that the author of that responsum follows the view of the Rivam cited in the Tur and Baal HaTerumot, who holds that even if the father left no property at all but the heirs have property of their own, there is nevertheless a mitzvah upon them to pay their father's debt from their own property. Accordingly, a guarantor would not be collected from, because he can still return against the heirs. The cited incident likewise involved a case where the father left no property even for that half, but the heirs possessed property of their own, and the creditor could still proceed against them because of the mitzvah upon heirs to pay their father's debt. Therefore the earlier creditor could not collect, and the principle that a person cannot transmit an oath applied. But according to the law we follow, there is no mitzvah upon heirs to pay from their own property. Thus the creditor has no one from whom to recover, and the rule that a person cannot transmit an oath does not apply. I nevertheless have a doubt where there is an earlier creditor and a later creditor, the earlier creditor died without taking the required oath, and the debtor died during the creditor's lifetime. One could ask why the earlier creditor should collect from purchasers. The purchasers might say: "If you remain silent, well and good; if not, we will return the deed to the heirs." Then the property would be in the heirs' presumptive possession and you could not collect from them because of the rule that a person cannot transmit an oath to his children. See below siman 119, where we judge matters as though the deed were returned. This is not a difficulty, because the Rosh writes at the beginning of Bava Kamma that a purchaser cannot retroactively annul the sale merely by returning the deed. Even if he returns the deed, the original purchase is not undone; rather, it is as though he now sells the field back to the heirs. Accordingly, even if they resell the field to the heirs, the heirs derive their right from the purchaser and do not receive it as inheritance from their father. The principle that a person cannot transmit an oath therefore does not apply, because the earlier creditor does not proceed against them by reason of inheritance. With an ordinary later creditor, however, he can perhaps argue, "If you remain silent, well and good; if not, I will conceal my deed." A creditor has only a lien, while the body of the property belongs to the heirs, and a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward. Thus the earlier creditor could not collect from the heirs because of the rule that a person cannot transmit an oath. Where the heirs themselves are the earlier creditor, as stated above, claiming their mother's earlier ketubah, this argument does not apply, because either way they collect. If the later creditor remains silent, the property is theirs in any event. With an ordinary earlier and later creditor, however, perhaps the later creditor can make this argument. Yet there is room to doubt whether the argument "if you remain silent" is available only to a purchaser who is actually in possession, in order to prevent extraction from him. Here the later creditor is not in possession. Why should we formulate that argument for him in order to diminish the earlier creditor's right, when the heirs themselves have no practical interest in the matter? This requires study.
