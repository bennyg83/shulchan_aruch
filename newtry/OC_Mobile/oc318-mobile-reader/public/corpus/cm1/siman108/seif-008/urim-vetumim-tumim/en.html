For if he wished, he could have burned it. Above in siman 93, seif 14, it appears that even where one partner is believed to say a debt document was paid, he must nevertheless reimburse his partner for the partner's share. According to the Sema in seif katan 29 there, the position of the person in whose name the document is written is even stronger than the position discussed here, to the point that even the Rosh, who disagrees here, would agree there because the document stands in that partner's name. Why then must he compensate his partner there, while here, according to the first opinion cited by the Mechaber, the brothers owe one another nothing? The same difficulty follows what I wrote in siman 93, that the partners originally trusted the named partner and even the Rosh would therefore agree that he can bind them. Why then does the Mechaber nevertheless require him to reimburse his partner, whereas here the first view exempts the brothers from mutual reimbursement? Were it not for the Mechaber's comments in Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef, where he establishes the Rambam's case as one in which the document is physically in the admitting partner's hands, I would have explained the Rambam more simply. The document need not be in his hands at all, so he has no migo that he could have destroyed it, and there need not have been any initial special trust. Even so, his admission prevents collection from the debtor because the document is written in his name. Although it is established that the funds belong to the partnership, the debtor can demand, "Swear to me that I did not pay you." Since the debt instrument is in this partner's name and the debtor borrowed from him, the debtor is plainly entitled to such an oath. The partner cannot swear because he admits payment. He therefore cannot collect. This parallels the rule above in siman 47 where a creditor admits that his document was paid while he himself owes others. Although he cannot harm those other creditors merely by his admission, the debtor may still demand an oath unless the document contains an effective waiver. Thus, the named partner's admission is not substantively believed against the other partner. His refusal or inability to swear merely prevents enforcement against the debtor, and for the loss he thereby causes he must reimburse his partner. This is clear and correct. The Raavad evidently does not accept the Baal HaTerumot's reasoning in siman 47, as the Shach notes there that several authorities disagree. This also explains the ruling at the end of siman 71 that where the document is not in the admitting party's hands he cannot harm his partner's share. That case contains a waiver clause running to the bearer and payment was to be made only to the bearer, as the Shach explains, so the debtor has no independent oath defense. Here, by contrast, each heir says as to his own share, "I am prepared to swear that our father did not instruct us that the debt was paid. What concern of mine is my brother's inability to swear? Each heir should collect his own share according to his own oath." On this reading the rules fit together. The Beit Yosef, however, understands the document to be in the admitting heir's hands, giving him the migo that he could destroy it. On that understanding the difficulty returns, because if his admission is fully believed by virtue of the migo, why should the analogous partner above have to reimburse? The Mishneh LaMelekh also notes something of this difficulty in Hilkhot Sheluchin. The Mechaber's formulation therefore requires study.
