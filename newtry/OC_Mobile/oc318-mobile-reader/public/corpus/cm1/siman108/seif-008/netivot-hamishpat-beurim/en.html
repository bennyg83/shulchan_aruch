Alternatively, only as to his own share. See Sema, seif katan 29, through his statement that here the promissory note was written in their father's name and publicly established as belonging to their father. The Sema cannot mean that the note had already been established in beit din before the heir said it was paid, because then the first opinion would not disagree. A migo arising only retroactively is ineffective according to all views. Rather, the case is that he declared it paid immediately when producing the note. The Sema's intent appears to follow what I wrote above in siman 96, Biurim, seif katan 18, that the Rosh's reason is that migo is said only regarding a litigant, or a third party custodian who is comparable to a litigant. One who merely holds another's property is not believed, even with a migo, to damage that property. The bailee's possession is considered the depositor's possession, and this is like using a migo to extract property. This is also the Sema's intent. With partners, where the note was written in one partner's name, the other partner trusted him, for the named partner can collect the entire note himself. See Sema, siman 56, seif katan 7, that the concept of trust also applies to a deposit. He is therefore treated as a litigant and is believed through a migo. Here, however, the note was written in the father's name and is known to belong to him. One brother cannot collect the entire note, as explained in siman 77, seif 9, in the gloss, nor did the note come into his hands as a deposit from his brothers. There is therefore no basis for saying they trusted him, and he is not believed even with a migo. Another distinction also appears to me. The principal reason he is not believed through the migo that he could have burned the note is that this is a migo against a chazakah, as the Shach writes in siman 47, seif katan 6. In Chiddushim to siman 62 and Biurim, seif katan 9, I distinguished that where a person could claim, It is mine, but instead says it belongs to another, and there is no concern for collusion, a migo can work against a chazakah. It is only where there is concern for collusion that a migo is ineffective. Accordingly, the distinction is clear. In siman 93, certainly where the note does not state that it is partnership money, he could claim it is entirely his. Even if it states that it is partnership money, since he can collect the entire note, the borrower is liable toward him for the entire amount and cannot say, You are not my litigant. Such a note can therefore be acquired by transfer without a written assignment, as I explained in siman 66, Biurim, and he is believed to claim it was purchased when he has a migo, or that it is his, or, according to the Sema in siman 60, seif katan 28, that he wrote it merely for appearance or so as not to appear wealthy. Therefore, when he says it was paid, he is believed through the migo. Here, however, the note names his father and he can sue upon it only for his own share, as explained in siman 77, seif 9, in the gloss. It therefore requires both writing and transfer, and he is not believed to say, It is mine. Since there is concern for collusion, his migo is ineffective.
