Responsa Neot Desheh, siman 61, suggests that even according to the opinion that the court pleads on behalf of heirs that deposited property was lost through unavoidable accident, an opinion the Shach favors, the court would not necessarily plead that the creditor forgave the debt or that the debtor had an offsetting claim. One reason is that unavoidable accident may be a relatively common defense, whereas uncommon defenses are not pleaded for heirs. Even if the authorities who allow the plea of accident do so because they hold that the court may plead even uncommon claims for heirs, as Ritva cited by the Shach indicates, these particular claims may be still less common than accident, and one should not enlarge the dispute unnecessarily. See below, siman 130, seif 7, seif katan 2. Shevut Yaakov, volume 3, siman 150, was asked about a guardian of orphans who found a note of their father against Reuven. Reuven produced witnesses that he had given the father money, but the witnesses did not know whether it was payment, a deposit, or a gift. The guardian claimed it was a gift. Shevut Yaakov ruled that because whether an uncommon claim is pleaded for heirs is itself disputed, the burden remains on the party seeking to extract money. Even according to those who say a note is not impaired merely because the debtor proves he gave money to the creditor, that rule applies when the creditor himself makes a certain claim that the money was a gift. A guardian cannot extract from the debtor by advancing an uncommon gift theory for orphans. There is therefore a double doubt in the defendant's favor: perhaps the note was impaired by proof that money passed, and even if it was not inherently impaired because a gift is possible, perhaps such an uncommon claim is not pleaded for heirs. He therefore concluded that the guardian could not collect from Reuven. Panim Meirot, volume 2, siman 17, discusses a communal trustee who died while the community held his signed acknowledgment that a stated sum of communal funds remained in his hands. He ruled that the community could collect from the heirs. The trustee was appointed for a fixed annual term, held all communal income throughout that year, and rendered an account only at the end. His death was therefore analogous to death during the term of a deposit. Local custom further required him to produce written proof for every authorized disbursement; without such proof the community was believed against him and he could not claim that he returned the money. Accordingly, the court would not plead for his heirs that the funds were lost through accident. Even according to the authorities who generally plead accident for heirs, the community could maintain that the law follows those who do not, and in any event this trustee had the status of a paid custodian because he held and used communal monies, unlike a trustee merely holding communal collateral. A paid custodian is liable for theft and loss and can exempt himself only by proving an unavoidable accident. Where witnesses are ordinarily available he cannot merely claim an accident without evidence. In a city, a violent armed robbery would ordinarily become known. The court therefore does not plead for the heirs a defense their father himself could not credibly have made. Panim Meirot adds that under communal enactments the community's claim even takes priority over a widow's ketubah. Panim Meirot, volume 3, siman 10, discusses another case involving an iska note for thirteen hundred gold coins, signed by the debtor and his wife on the twenty-sixth of Tishrei. The debtor intentionally left the detailed terms blank so the lender could later insert the customary safeguards. Because of the lender's business distractions they were never inserted. The debtor died on the fourth of Kislev. The lender, fearing secular suspicion if he later filled in the document, added only a due date of Rosh Chodesh Tevet so that the debt would remain within its term. Some judges wished to treat the instrument as an ordinary iska note without special credibility clauses and therefore to plead accident for the heirs in accordance with the Shach. The lender answered that local custom was to write iska instruments according to the enactment of Maharam, under which the recipient cannot claim that the principal was lost through unavoidable accident without witnesses, and that the debtor had entrusted him with a blank signed instrument precisely so that he could insert all customary terms. The debtor's wife and father also admitted that the money had neither been returned nor lost by accident. Panim Meirot compared the case to a responsum of the Rosh. The debtor had received the thirteen hundred at the end of Tishrei to buy wine, and his actual purchases showed that the funds were used for that purpose. It was implausible that such a large sum had been repaid within six weeks. The note also bore a due date of Rosh Chodesh Tevet, placing it within its term. The fact that the lender himself wrote that due date did not weaken it, because by giving him the signed blank the debtor had trusted him to complete the instrument. Moreover, customary terms of an iska under Maharam are treated as included even if not physically written, just as customary clauses in other instruments may be implied. No rational lender would give an iska whose deposit half could be defeated by an unsupported claim of return or accident. And an accident involving such a large sum would ordinarily become known in a stable kingdom where robbery and plunder are not common. Panim Meirot therefore ruled that the heirs must pay the iska note in full.
