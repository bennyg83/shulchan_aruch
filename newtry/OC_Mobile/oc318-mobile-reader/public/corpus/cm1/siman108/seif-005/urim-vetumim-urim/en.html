Provided that the note was found, etc. If only a receipt is found, one can say that the lender prepared it in advance so that if the borrower came to pay there would be no delay in producing a receipt. If only the note is found among torn notes, one can say that this happened accidentally. But where both defects are present, we are concerned. It is not necessary to know with certainty that the note was actually found among the torn notes. We are concerned that perhaps the heirs found both a receipt and the note among torn notes and are concealing that fact, and therefore we impose an oath requiring them to tell the truth. If we know for certain that the note was not found among torn notes, no oath is imposed merely because a receipt was found, for a receipt alone is insufficient. This is Shach's explanation.
Only that I did not find, etc. He need not swear that his father did not communicate the matter through others. Sema explains that a person does not ordinarily transmit such instructions through others, but would leave a written memorandum for his children. Therefore the heir need swear only that he found no such writing. This is strained. It appears more correct that if the father gave instructions before relatives or one witness, the minor son is not bound to accept their statement to weaken the note, because legal matters are established through living testimony and not merely their report to him. If the instruction was before two valid witnesses, it would become publicly known and they would presumably come to beit din and testify. Therefore there is no independent concern.
That I did not find. If the heir was born only after his father's death, Rambam and Tur together with Maharam disagree. Rambam does not require him to swear, while Tur and Maharam require the same oath as for a child who was alive during the father's lifetime. The Mechaber's general wording follows Rambam. The distinction requires explanation. Why should a child who was one day old when his father died have to swear, while one born immediately afterward does not? One can answer that concerning a child already alive, the Sages did not distinguish between a child capable of understanding and an infant incapable of understanding. A child not yet born at the time of death falls outside that uniform enactment. Additionally, when a man dies not knowing whether he will leave surviving offspring, he presumably arranges his affairs before witnesses or beit din, and the matter would become known.
