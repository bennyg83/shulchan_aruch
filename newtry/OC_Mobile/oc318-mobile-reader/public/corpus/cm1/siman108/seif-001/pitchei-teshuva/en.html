Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 178, seif 15, questions a case where a father admitted, "I became obligated, but I do not know whether I paid," was held liable by beit din, refused to comply, and was placed under a ban to pay, and then died. Are the heirs obligated? The doubt rests upon the reason that heirs may plead payment on a handwritten obligation because, had their father been alive, he could have claimed that he paid. At first glance this is difficult, because the father's credibility would depend upon his certain claim, "I paid," whereas for the heirs one can plead only that perhaps he paid. If they themselves made such an uncertain claim, one might think they should be liable as one who says, "I do not know whether I paid." The answer must be that with heirs the issue is always treated as "I do not know whether we ever became obligated." If their father discharged the debt, the heirs never entered any obligation at all. Therefore, when the heirs say, "Perhaps our father paid," they are exempt. If so, even in the case described, although the father remained personally obligated during his lifetime because he did not know whether he paid, once he dies the heirs may be viewed as saying, "We do not know whether we ever became obligated," and may be exempt. Beit Meir discusses the matter further. Divrei Chaim, Laws of Claims, siman 4, challenges Beit Meir from the Rama above, siman 89, seif 2, who rules that a hired worker who claims within the statutory period may swear and collect from the employer's heirs. Tosafot in Shevuot 45 explain that the employer cannot say "I paid" because, being preoccupied, he is treated as saying "I do not know whether I paid." If Beit Meir's analysis were correct, why may the worker collect from the heirs? With respect to them it should be "we do not know whether we ever became obligated."
