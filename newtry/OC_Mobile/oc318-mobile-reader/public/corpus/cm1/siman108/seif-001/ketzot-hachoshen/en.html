An oral loan is ordinarily collected from heirs through the recognized Talmudic rules, yet we rule that brothers who divide an estate are legally purchasers. The Gemara in Bekhorot seems to imply that if brothers are purchasers and an oral loan does not collect from purchasers, an oral loan should not collect after division. This difficulty is noted by the later authorities. One answer is that where the obligation itself is biblical and liens are biblical, the oral creditor's inability to collect from purchasers is only a rabbinic protection against loss to purchasers. That concern does not apply to heirs who divided, so the Torah lien remains. But this cannot resolve all ordinary loans according to authorities who hold that liens themselves are not biblical. The better answer follows the rule that an heir who sells inherited property in a way that defeats the father's creditor can become liable for damaging the creditor's lien. Before division, the inherited estate was available for payment. If division makes the brothers legally purchasers and thereby removes the lien, their own act of division caused the loss. They therefore remain liable under the law of direct causation. This explains why ordinary creditors can still collect after division even if the brothers are technically purchasers. The rule of redemption of a firstborn son is different. Damaging gifts due to kohanim does not create ordinary liability because the property has no single private claimant. Therefore, if division removes the lien, no direct causation liability replaces it. This also explains why the Gemara chose the five sela redemption case rather than an ordinary debt when analyzing whether divided heirs are purchasers.
