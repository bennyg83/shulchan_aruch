That one who became liable, etc. The Shach in seif katan 4 objects to the Mechaber, and the objection is correct, though not for the reason he gives. Mordechai at the end of Bava Batra quotes Rabbenu Gershom Meor HaGolah: one who was adjudged liable in beit din because of informing to the authorities and then died is collectible from his heirs, even according to the authority who says an oral loan is not collected from heirs, because once he stood in judgment the obligation became fixed. The Shach understands "even according to the authority" as referring to Rav and Shmuel, who in the Gemara hold that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. Since the Gemara itself rules against Rav and Shmuel, the distinction between a claim reduced to judgment and one not yet adjudicated should no longer matter. It is difficult, however, to understand Rabbenu Gershom as stating a practical ruling according to an Amoraic view expressly rejected by the final halakhah. That is not the normal manner of the poskim. Rather, his words refer to the major dispute among later authorities regarding our accepted rule that an oral loan can be collected from heirs. Some explain that this is the strict law because liens are d'oraisa. Others hold that by strict law an oral loan would not bind heirs because liens are not d'oraisa, and that the Sages nevertheless enacted collection to prevent the door from being locked before borrowers. This is a major dispute among early and later authorities, as the Shach discusses above in siman 39 and as I explained there in Tumim. The difference is important for liability arising from informing. If collection from heirs is only an enactment intended to preserve voluntary lending, that rationale does not apply to a tortfeasor or informer. His victim did not voluntarily lend him money, nor should informing be treated as a common transaction for which the Sages needed to preserve access to credit. There is therefore no basis to invent a parallel enactment. If, however, liens are d'oraisa, then once the informer's conduct created a monetary obligation his property is liened like any other debtor's property. Rabbenu Gershom therefore means: even according to the authorities who say that an oral loan does not bind heirs by strict law, but only through the lending enactment, once the informer actually stood in judgment everyone agrees that the adjudicated monetary obligation binds the estate. This follows the Gemara at the end of Bava Batra, which says that according to the view that liens are not d'oraisa, once the debtor stood in judgment the obligation is collectible from heirs. This explains Rabbenu Gershom. Nevertheless, the Shach's practical difficulty remains, because the Shulchan Aruch above in siman 104 gives an earlier oral creditor priority over a later written creditor, which indicates that the Mechaber regards liens as d'oraisa. On that premise, even an informer's liability not yet reduced to judgment should bind land of the estate. There might be ways to reconcile this with the Rama, but the Mechaber himself seems inconsistent, because below in siman 388, seif 2, he rules that heirs are liable for their father's informing without requiring that he first stood in judgment. It is the Rama there who adds the distinction between a case already adjudicated and one not adjudicated. The most likely explanation is as the Shach says, that the Mechaber mentions standing in judgment here because of the concluding clause: with respect to a punitive fine, even an obligation already adjudicated does not necessarily descend to the son. The phrase is therefore stated for the greater novelty of the later clause, not to imply that an ordinary informer is exempt before judgment. There is, however, another useful distinction. If the informer left land, then according to our ruling that liens are d'oraisa the victim plainly collects even without prior judgment. If he left only movables, collection from heirs exists only through the Geonic enactment. Such movables are not liened by strict Talmudic law, so the situation is analogous to the view that ordinary oral debts bind heirs only by enactment. Rabbenu Gershom can therefore hold that the Geonim did not extend their enactment to an unadjudicated informing claim. Once beit din had already fixed the liability, collection is stronger and can be enforced even from the heirs' movables. The Mechaber therefore states the universally valid case, one reduced to judgment, while before judgment the result may differ between land and movables. This is clear.
