It is not collected from heirs. This applies even to adult heirs, because had their father been alive he could have claimed that the debt was paid, and we plead for heirs whatever their father could have pleaded. They need not swear the heirs' oath that their father did not instruct them, because that oath was instituted only when heirs come to collect from another who claims he does not owe them. It was not instituted when they are defendants seeking exemption. See above, siman 69, concerning Rama's ruling whether, where there are grounds for suspicion, the heirs can nevertheless be required to swear even in defense. If the claimant definitely alleges that the heirs themselves know their father did not pay, they are subject to hesset like any other defendants.
And he instructed while ill. Sema explains that in such a case we do not presume that he admitted merely so his children would not be required to swear. The printed wording is mistaken, for certainly a person may claim that he made an admission only to spare his children an oath. Rather, the case must involve circumstances in which such an explanation is impossible, such as a formal admission or similar context. Shach's difficulty therefore does not arise; it is a common printing error.
If the heirs claimed, etc. This means that they say, 'We know with certainty.' They thereby admit that the debt was not paid. If they merely make the claim without saying that they know, there is no admission of nonpayment, for how should they know what their father did? They may simply be assuming. See Sema.
Even though the borrower died during the lender's lifetime. Below, with an ordinary note, we rule that the creditor loses because one cannot transmit an oath obligation to his children. There, the creditor would have had to swear when collecting from the debtor's heirs, and because he died before doing so, that oath cannot be transmitted. Here, however, no oath obligation existed at all because the debt was still within its term. He therefore does not lose the loan.
For we plead on their behalf. Even if the creditor also died and his heirs are seeking collection, we always plead for the party in possession, because the burden of proof rests upon the one seeking to extract.
They collect from the heirs. Although the deceased never instructed his heirs to pay this expenditure and the person who incurred it was close to death, he spent the money with authority and the heirs must reimburse it.
And his wife is not believed. If she says that he spent a certain amount, her admission cannot create liability against the heirs. If the estate contains movable property sufficient only for her ketubah, and her ketubah contains no special movable property clause, we rule that she and the other creditor divide. Accordingly, the creditor may withhold from her ketubah the share corresponding to her own admission that he too is a creditor. If she already collected, however, we rule that what was collected remains collected. Examine carefully.
