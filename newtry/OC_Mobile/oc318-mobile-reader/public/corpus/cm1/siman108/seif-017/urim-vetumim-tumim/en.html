Our father told us, etc. The Shach in seif katan 28 cites the Beit Yosef's doubt whether, if minor heirs themselves clearly say that their father told them the debt was paid, their statement has the force of an admission. There is evidence from the Rosh that a minor's statement has no legal force. The Rosh struggles to explain the rule that where the debtor admitted the debt during his final illness, collection may be made from minor heirs, because testimony is ordinarily not received in the absence of the litigant. He therefore establishes the case where the debtor admitted before witnesses and expressly told them to write the admission, so the written instrument can later be authenticated even in the minors' absence. Why did the Rosh need this strained construction? He could have said simply that the minors themselves appeared in beit din and admitted that their father had made such an acknowledgment in their presence. Evidently the Rosh understood that when the Gemara says collection is made from minors, the minors' own statement that their father admitted the debt does not count as a binding admission. This seems decisive. There may nevertheless be a distinction. If a child while still a minor says, "My father told me that he owed this debt," everyone may agree that the statement has no legal force. The Beit Yosef's doubt may concern a person who is now an adult and says that during his minority his father gave him this instruction. In some areas an adult is permitted to testify about matters observed during childhood, especially family matters. Here the required oath is itself only a rabbinic enactment. The adult heir's report of his father's instruction may therefore be sufficient to waive that rabbinic protection. This may explain why the Mechaber states the rule without qualification when the heir speaks in adulthood. The Baal HaTerumot, shaar 14, also writes that if an heir was a minor when his father died, he does not swear "my father did not instruct me," because we are witnesses that the father would not have entrusted such information to a small child. This implies that if the father did in fact instruct him and the heir reports it after reaching majority, the statement can be effective. Otherwise, even if he claimed to have been instructed we would simply disregard him, and there would be no need to rely on the presumption that a father does not entrust such matters to a minor. His adult admission therefore appears capable of binding his own share.
