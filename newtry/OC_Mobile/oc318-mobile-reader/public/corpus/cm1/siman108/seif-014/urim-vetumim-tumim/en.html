We do not apply this rule, etc. See what I wrote at the end of siman 84 at length. The ruling is correct. The Sema there, however, cites Rabbenu Yerucham in the name of the Ramban that even when a creditor seeks to collect from purchasers, if the creditor had already appeared in beit din and been formally obligated to take an oath and then died, all agree that the principle "a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children" applies even against purchasers. The Sema strongly rejects this, but the Shach notes that the Yerushalmi in the chapter HaKotev supports the Ramban. The Yerushalmi says: if the father impaired his document in beit din, his son cannot collect. Rabbi Chisda asks whether merely because the father walked those two steps to beit din he should lose the claim. If he impaired it outside beit din, you say the son collects, yet if he impaired it in beit din you say he does not collect. These are essentially the Ramban's words. Yet the Babylonian Talmud in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in 48 initially considers distinguishing the heirs' oath based on whether the creditor had already stood in judgment, and Rav Nachman rejects this by saying that beit din does not create the oath. From the moment the debtor died, the creditor became obligated to swear to the debtor's children. The Yerushalmi therefore appears inconsistent with our Talmud, which we follow. Were it not for the Ramban's application to purchasers, I would distinguish as follows. In the case of an impaired document there is no oath until the creditor actually chooses to admit partial payment and presses his claim. Before then he may never impair the document, or the debtor may admit the balance. A denial or admission outside beit din does not necessarily generate the formal oath. Thus, standing in beit din can be decisive. The heirs' oath, however, arises from the mere uncertainty that the deceased debtor may have paid. It comes into existence automatically at the debtor's death, without any judicial act. This would reconcile the Yerushalmi with the Bavli. But the Ramban applies the Yerushalmi's distinction even to collection from purchasers, where the oath likewise arises from an objective concern about payment. To understand his view, one may say that heirs are different because beit din itself serves as the father of orphans. At the debtor's death, beit din immediately stands in place of the minors and asserts the defenses available to them, so it is as though the creditor already stood in judgment. Purchasers and other adults, by contrast, are independent parties. Even though beit din will eventually plead defenses for a purchaser, no formal oath obligation exists until the purchaser actually comes before beit din. On this basis the Ramban can distinguish the heirs' case in the Bavli from a purchaser's case and still rely on the Yerushalmi when a creditor was already adjudged to swear before death. The Ramban's ruling therefore remains open to consideration, because it has support in the Yerushalmi.
