Rather, one who impaired his document, etc. The laws of an impaired document and of a single witness testifying to payment are explained in siman 84; one collecting in the defendant's absence in siman 106; and one collecting from purchasers in simanim 71 and 114. In all these cases the lender himself could not collect without an oath even if the borrower or those standing in his place did not demand one. Nevertheless, he can bequeath the claim to his children, because the Gemara says not to extend Rav and Shmuel's rule. Their rule is confined to a case where the borrower died during the lender's lifetime, so that the lender's initial oath arose because he sought to extract money from the borrower's heirs. Only regarding such money do we say that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children. In the other cases, the initial oath was not created in order to extract from heirs, and therefore even if the borrower or purchasers later die and the lender's heirs ultimately collect from their heirs, the original character of the oath is different and the claim may be inherited. See my discussion in this siman and at the end of siman 84, where I addressed the apparent conflict with Responsa Rosh and rejected Levush's distinction.
For example, where the borrower did not die, etc. The Tur says that if the borrower died during the lender's lifetime, this is precisely the case of heirs collecting from heirs, where one cannot bequeath the oath. Baal HaTerumot says the same. Although this appears obvious, the novelty may be that even if the lender had already taken the required oath to the borrower when he impaired the document, and only afterward the borrower died, the lender still cannot bequeath the claim, for the reason explained above in seif 11.
Or from purchasers. This can apply even where the borrower left land to his heirs, if that land is elsewhere and it is more convenient to collect from purchasers' land that is here, provided the borrower did not die during the lender's lifetime. Responsa Rosh writes that although the borrower had sold property during the lender's lifetime, the debt was still capable of being collected from the borrower himself without an oath so long as he remained alive. Therefore the claim is not necessarily included in the rule that a person cannot bequeath an oath merely because collection from purchasers would have required one. This itself is difficult to reconcile with another responsum of the Rosh and with the Tur's presentation, as I explained above.
Two witnesses, etc. Against one witness the heirs take the heirs' oath and collect. If two witnesses testified that the entire debt was paid, even the lender himself could not overcome them by an oath. But if they testify only to partial payment, the lender would swear and collect the balance, and the heirs likewise swear that their father did not instruct them and collect the remainder.
Likewise, a hesset oath that was reversed, etc. The novelty is that an obligation created by a judgment of beit din is not automatically treated like a written document for inheritance purposes.
The heirs do not swear and collect. This applies even from the renter, robber, or original defendant. Chazal did not institute that heirs may extract money with the non-definite oath that their father did not instruct them except where they hold a promissory note, because the document itself supports the continued existence of the debt.
