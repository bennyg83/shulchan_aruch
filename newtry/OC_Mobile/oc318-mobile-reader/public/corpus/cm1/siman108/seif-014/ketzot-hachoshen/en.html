The stringency of "a person does not bequeath a monetary claim subject to an oath" applies specifically when one comes to collect from heirs. As I explained in siman 82, this follows the framework of Rav and Shmuel, who held that liens were not biblical and that collection from heirs existed by rabbinic enactment. Where the claimant cannot fulfill the rabbinic oath condition, the enactment does not allow collection. Against the borrower himself, the debt is a true personal obligation and collection does not depend on that special inheritance enactment. The same reasoning would seem to apply to purchasers, because collection from purchasers also depends upon lien rules and rabbinic collection structure. Yet Rosh explains that if there remains a possibility of collecting from the borrower himself when he later obtains money, the Sages did not impose the same full stringency in the purchaser case. Ramban, cited by Rabbeinu Yerucham, distinguishes whether the creditor had already stood in judgment and become obligated in the purchaser's oath. Once the oath obligation was fixed through litigation, the heirs cannot inherit it. This distinction is strongest regarding the purchaser's oath, whose entire need arises only because one is collecting from encumbered property. By contrast, where the relevant oath arose against the borrower himself, such as an impaired document or one witness, Ramban can agree that the heirs may still collect from the borrower, because the underlying debt is directly payable by him and the special heir or purchaser stringency does not apply in the same manner.
If a hesset oath had been reversed to the claimant and the claimant then died, Maharshal says the heirs can renew the claim and require the defendant himself to take hesset. In my view that is not correct where the defendant had already validly reversed the oath during the claimant's lifetime. Once the reversal was accepted, the defendant was discharged from his own hesset obligation and the oath rested upon the claimant. If the claimant died without swearing, the defendant does not automatically become subject to a new hesset oath. Had the claimant remained alive and attempted to reverse the oath back again, the defendant would at most be subject to the customary ban, not necessarily a renewed hesset oath. The same should therefore apply when the claimant dies and his heirs sue. They can obtain no more than the sanction that would have existed had their father tried to reverse it back. Bach also cites Maharshal that where an employee dies and his heirs sue the employer, the employer should take hesset. Bach prefers a severe oath, following Ran's view that the employer's oath is the original oath and the Sages shifted it to the employee only for the employee's livelihood. In my view Maharshal is correct regarding the heirs. Even according to Ran, the severe oath imposed on the employer exists for the employee's special livelihood enactment. The Jerusalem Talmud says explicitly that the enactment was made for the employee and not for his heirs. Therefore, once the employee dies, his heirs do not inherit the special severe oath against the employer. At most they have the ordinary hesset structure appropriate to their claim.
