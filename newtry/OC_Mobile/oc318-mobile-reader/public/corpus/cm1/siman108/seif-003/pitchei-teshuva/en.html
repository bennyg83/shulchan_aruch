He must swear and then collects. Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 7, concerns Reuven, who died while Shimon held a debt instrument against him for ten adumim. Reuven's heirs answered that, to the contrary, Shimon owed their father money, for they had heard their father repeatedly complain that he could not recover his money from Shimon. Two blank signatures of Shimon were also found among Reuven's effects. Noda BiYehudah ruled that Shimon could not collect on his note. First, the Sema in siman 48 disagrees with Ir Shushan and holds that one who possesses a commercial note bearing Shimon's signature on otherwise blank paper may assert whatever amount he claims. Had Reuven been alive, he could therefore have asserted a claim against Shimon on those blank signatures for whatever amount he chose. His heirs may likewise say that money was due to their father on those signatures. Although they cannot affirm a particular amount and therefore cannot themselves extract money, their father's repeated complaint at least defeats Shimon's attempt to collect the ten adumim. Second, Noda BiYehudah is inclined to say that even if the heirs had heard nothing from their father, Shimon still could not collect. The reason a debtor cannot plead payment against an authenticated note is the question, "If you paid, why is your note still in the creditor's hand?" Here Reuven might answer that he did pay and had no concern about leaving the note with Shimon because he held Shimon's blank signatures and could, if Shimon sued him again, write an equivalent obligation against Shimon. If the father could have pleaded payment, the court pleads payment for the heirs. At most, a general ban could be imposed. Third, just as under the Sema Reuven could have claimed that Shimon borrowed money on the blank signatures, he could also have claimed that Shimon gave him the signed blanks so that he could write a receipt or indemnity when the exact amount of the debt was not yet known. Although receipts are ordinarily signed on the same side and these signatures appeared in the form of commercial notes, the custom was that when the original note was unavailable the creditor would give the debtor an indemnity written as a full debt instrument and signed in the ordinary commercial-note fashion. It is therefore possible that the blank signatures served that function. Because such a claim would have been available to the father, the court may plead it for the heirs, even if it is uncommon.
