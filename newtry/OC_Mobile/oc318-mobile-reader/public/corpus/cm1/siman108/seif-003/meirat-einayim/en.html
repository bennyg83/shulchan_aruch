One does not collect from minor heirs. Baal HaTerumot writes that there is no distinction whether the minors are sons, brothers, or other heirs, and the same rule applies even if they have a guardian. See Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe to siman 110:2.
If he died within the term, it is necessary, etc. One of the three routes permitting collection is that the debtor died within the fixed repayment period. In that route, collection is permitted only if the evidence was accepted during the father's lifetime. In the other two routes the matter is independently known: if the father expressly instructed payment, the recipients of that instruction know that the debt remains outstanding; and if he died while under excommunication for nonpayment, that too is known. See siman 110:9, where in a fully clarified matter testimony may be accepted even in the minor's absence, although the Tur and Mechaber there require that the instruction have been made before beit din or written in the debtor's recognizable handwriting. In the preceding seif, which concerns adult heirs, testimony may be accepted before them during the term, so the Mechaber could state all three routes together without qualification.
He will be buried in his power. This is already explained in the column and in the words of the author in paragraph 11:
He must take an oath resembling a Torah oath. This is like a lender seeking to collect from the borrower under a document without a credibility clause when the borrower says, "Swear that I have not paid you," in which case the lender must swear while holding a sacred object, as stated at the beginning of siman 82. When he collects from heirs, even though they do not themselves demand an oath, beit din raises the claim for them, as explained above. Darkei Moshe cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that even if the creditor finds, among the debtor's possessions after death, the very movable items he had given the debtor, he still cannot collect without an oath; the Raavad and Ri rule likewise.
His law is as explained in siman 82. The Tur's wording differs. It says that if he refuses to swear, the property is not taken from him; rather, he is placed under excommunication until he swears, like one liable for an oath of hesset. The law of one liable for hesset who refuses to swear is explained in siman 87:9: he is excommunicated for thirty days, and if he still does not swear he receives disciplinary lashes and the excommunication is lifted. But where a claimant holds a document and the defendant says, "Swear to me that I have not paid," siman 82:2 teaches that the claimant must swear in order to collect under the document, yet if he already seized property it is not removed from him. In the Perishah I proved that the Rosh and Tur hold that he is not excommunicated once he has seized, because under strict law a creditor holding a debt instrument collects without an oath by virtue of the presumption that a paid document would not remain in the creditor's possession. The Sages required an oath only when the debtor demands it, to satisfy the debtor's concern, and only where the creditor is still seeking to extract. Once he has already seized, we leave him under the strict law. A Torah scholar differs only in that even before actual seizure we do not remove the document from his possession, so that if he later seizes he may retain it, unlike an ordinary person. Here, however, because the creditor seeks to collect from heirs, the law is more stringent. Examine this carefully.
