And provided that he did not die, etc. The Sema asks that this is obvious, and the question is well taken. Why would one think that merely because the creditor impaired his own document the heirs should acquire a stronger right to extract money? According to what I wrote in siman 84, however, there is a practical novelty. If a creditor impairs his document while the debt is still within its term and the debtor then demands an oath, the creditor must swear. If the debtor dies, we plead his defenses for the heirs, so the same oath obligation arises. The heirs' oath generally rests on the principle that had the debtor been alive he could have demanded an oath. Therefore, if the debtor dies during the creditor's lifetime and the creditor then dies without swearing, the rule that a person cannot bequeath to his heirs money collectible only through an oath applies. The clause therefore teaches that even a debt still within its term can fall under that rule once the creditor has impaired the document, because impairment itself generates an oath obligation. This is what the authorities mean by conditioning the ruling on the debtor not having died first. If the debtor did die first, then regardless of the remaining term, the impaired document brings the oath into play and the creditor cannot simply transmit the claim to his heirs.
