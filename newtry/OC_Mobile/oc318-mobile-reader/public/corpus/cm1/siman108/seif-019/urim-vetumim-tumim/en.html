And even if he stipulated, etc. This follows the Gemara in Gittin. Abaye says, as a proof or explanation, that an ordinary creditor collects intermediate quality land from the debtor, whereas from orphans he collects only inferior land. Tosafot and the Rosh ask what relation that has to a creditor who expressly stipulated for superior land. In the ordinary case, Torah law itself gives a creditor only inferior land, and intermediate land is a rabbinic improvement to facilitate lending. When collection is from orphans the Sages simply leave the original Torah rule in place. But if the debtor expressly subjected superior land, and we hold that liens are d'oraisa, the creditor's claim to superior land appears to arise by Torah law from the stipulation itself. Tosafot answer that the Sages made no distinction and applied the orphan rule categorically. Accordingly, Abaye's phrase "know that" is not a logical proof that the two cases are identical, since one involves the original Torah measure and the other a contractual term. Rather, it gives the policy reason or an analogy for the enactment. The Talmud often uses "know that" in this explanatory sense, as at the beginning of Bava Metzia concerning a creditor's collection of enhancement, "know that, for this is what the seller writes to him," which likewise explains the mechanism rather than proving it independently. Here too the meaning is that all ordinary creditors are moved down to inferior land against heirs, and the Sages made that rule without distinctions. This undermines the Shach's suggestion that from adult heirs a creditor can collect according to an express stipulation. If the reason is truly that the Sages made no distinction, there is no basis to distinguish minor from adult heirs. Indeed, without a stipulation even adult heirs pay only from inferior land. If an express clause suddenly allowed superior land from adults, the very uniformity of Abaye's rationale would disappear. The Shach himself notes that the Tur does not appear to accept such a distinction, and in my view his proposed ruling has no firm basis.
Accordingly, with a ketubah, even if the instrument contains the customary clause subjecting the finest and best property, the widow still collects only inferior land from heirs. The clause is effective principally where she is divorced and the husband remains alive, because even there her default entitlement is inferior land and the stipulation can improve her position against him. The Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 100, seif katan 11, states the matter too broadly, and I have seen judges err on this point. It is possible to argue that because a ketubah commonly says that the obligation binds "me and my later heirs," the husband expressly stipulated against the heirs themselves, which may be stronger than an ordinary designation of quality. Even so, this requires study because the authorities do not explicitly say that such wording defeats the orphan enactment. The Raavad, as cited in the Baal HaTerumot in this shaar, writes more strongly that the usual clause of superior property in a ketubah does not help against heirs. The safer conclusion is therefore that the heirs still pay only from inferior land unless a distinct and effective rule is established.
