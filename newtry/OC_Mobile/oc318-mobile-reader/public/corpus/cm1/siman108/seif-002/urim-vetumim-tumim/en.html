Or if he died within the time fixed for payment. The Shach, seif katan 7, asks that whether we say a migo against the chazakah that a person does not repay within the fixed time is an unresolved question, as stated above in siman 78. Here the depositary has a migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable accident. Why then should it matter that the matter was still within the fixed time? The Shach answers that perhaps the case concerns heirs. A claim of repayment within the fixed time is uncommon, and we do not plead an uncommon claim for heirs. He notes, however, that the wording does not imply this. See his lengthy discussion. In truth, I find no implication in the Tur one way or the other. Yet the Mechaber's words indicate otherwise. Concerning the general rule that we plead for heirs that the deposit was lost through unavoidable accident, he cites two opinions whether an uncommon claim is pleaded for heirs. The ruling here that within the fixed time we do not plead that the item was returned is stated without qualification according to all opinions. This demonstrates that the reason is not merely that such repayment is uncommon. Likewise above in siman 71, seif 22, the Mechaber cites first the opinion that where a creditor granted the debtor general credibility, we plead even for heirs that repayment occurred within the fixed time, despite its uncommon nature. Indeed, it is not clear to me that a matter contrary to the chazakah that a person does not repay within the fixed time should be classified as "uncommon" for purposes of what we plead for heirs. The category of a matter so uncommon that it is not pleaded for heirs requires something genuinely rare. The Rosh writes in Perek Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot that a claim of forgery is uncommon, yet we nevertheless plead forgery for heirs. One must therefore say that only something thoroughly uncommon is excluded. It is known that a majority is stronger than a chazakah. If anything contrary to a chazakah were called uncommon, then all the more so something contrary to a majority would be uncommon. Yet in Ketubot 16 the Gemara asks: since most women marry as virgins, what does it matter if witnesses do not come? Tosafot write that this question is according to Rav, who follows the majority even in monetary matters. Why did the Gemara not instead ask even according to Shmuel from the Mishnah of "she became widowed," since how can we plead for the heirs a claim contrary to the majority that most women marry as virgins? It follows that even a claim contrary to a majority is not automatically treated as an uncommon claim for heirs. All the more so one contrary merely to a chazakah. The Shach's difficulty can instead be answered in several ways. First, the question whether a migo is effective against the chazakah that a person does not repay within the fixed time remains unresolved. According to many authorities, including the Mechaber, whenever a Talmudic monetary question remains unresolved, if the claimant has seized the disputed property, even in the presence of witnesses, it is not removed from him. See above in the laws and rules of kim li and seizure. If so, why would a depositary return property within the fixed time and leave the document in the creditor's possession? One might answer that he relies upon the migo that he could say the property was lost through unavoidable accident, and therefore is not careful to retrieve the document, as the Gemara in Bava Batra explains. But how can he safely rely upon that? The creditor might later seize property from him in the presence of witnesses and claim kim li that no migo is effective against the chazakah. The debtor would then have no means to recover it. Why would he leave the document in the creditor's hands when at any moment the creditor could openly seize his property and no one could rescue it from him? Thus the chazakah expressed by "what is your document doing in my hand?" becomes especially strong, and the claim of return is not believed. A second answer is that the migo of "it was lost through unavoidable accident" is itself a weak migo, because such accidents are uncommon. According to many authorities, a migo based on an uncommon claim is not accepted. See above in the rules of migo. Whether a migo is effective against the chazakah of no repayment within the fixed time is itself unresolved. Here, therefore, there are two weaknesses: the migo runs against a chazakah, and the alternative claim supporting it is uncommon. When both defects are present, no one accepts the migo. Thus within the fixed time, where we would need both a migo against the chazakah and an uncommon alternative claim of unavoidable accident, the migo is ineffective. The Rosh's words in the first chapter of Ketubot point this way. He asks concerning one who says, "I found an open entrance," and is believed because of the chazakah that a person does not toil over a wedding feast and then deliberately destroy it. Why is the woman not believed that she was a virgin by a migo that she could have said, "I was violated after betrothal"? The Rosh answers that she does not wish to disqualify herself from the priesthood. He then asks that the husband should have a migo from claiming that she had been previously violated, and answers that such a claim is uncommon. This implies that the Rosh regards "I was violated after betrothal" as a sufficiently common claim, for otherwise he could have answered the first question simply by saying that the alternative claim is uncommon. Apparently, once according to his account she was in fact not a virgin, a violation during the marriage relationship is not treated as extraordinarily uncommon. Yet the Gemara on 12 says that according to Rabban Gamliel she is believed when she says, "I was violated after betrothal," by a migo that she could have said she was injured by a piece of wood after betrothal, even though that alternative is uncommon. See what I wrote above in the rules of migo, siman 46. The Tur and Rosh can therefore hold that a migo based on an uncommon claim alone may sometimes be effective. But where it is also opposed by an independent chazakah, the two defects combine, and such a migo is not accepted. The same applies here: it is both an uncommon alternative claim and a migo against the chazakah of no repayment within the fixed time. According to this, however, Tosafot's question in Bava Batra 5b, s.v. mi, loses much of its force. Tosafot ask from the rule that one who deposits with a written document must return the deposit in the presence of witnesses, even though he has a migo that he could claim unavoidable accident. This would show that a migo is not effective against a document, and all the more so not against the chazakah that one does not repay within the fixed time, which is stronger than a document because a document still requires an oath when collecting from heirs, whereas a claim within the fixed time does not. According to what was stated, there is no proof. In the case of a documented deposit, there is the chazakah created by the document, "what is your document doing in my hand?," together with the uncommon migo of unavoidable accident. When both are present, no migo is accepted. In the case under discussion in Bava Batra, however, there is only a migo against the chazakah of no repayment within the fixed time, and the Gemara therefore remains uncertain. Tosafot's proof would not follow. There is yet a third answer. The principle "what is your document doing in my hand?" is indeed a chazakah, as Tosafot write, but it is a relatively weak one, and therefore a migo can overcome it, especially because the possessor also has the chazakah of possession of money. When another chazakah is added to it, however, the two chazakot together constitute a strong chazakah. Against a strong chazakah all agree that a migo is ineffective, as Tosafot write in Ketubot 9. See the rules of migo above. Here there is both the chazakah created by the document and the chazakah that a person does not repay within the fixed time. Against two chazakot no migo is accepted. At first glance this too seems to conflict with Tosafot's question in Bava Batra. Tosafot had already written that a document is like witnesses except that the chazakah is weakened by the possibility that the creditor retained it for reimbursement of the scribe's fee. With a written deposit, however, the depositor gives the fee, as Tosafot in Bava Batra and Shevuot explain. The depositary therefore cannot say that the document was retained for the scribe's fee. The document should then be equivalent to actual witnesses, and it would be obvious that no migo is effective against it, because a migo is never effective against witnesses. How could Tosafot infer anything from that case about the weaker chazakah of no repayment within the fixed time? It must be that Tosafot's question follows the Rashbam's explanation on Bava Batra 70. Rami bar Chama asks how the Torah's oath of bailees can ever arise, a powerful question according to the Rashbam, who finds no solution until the Gemara establishes a deposit evidenced by a document that must be returned in the presence of witnesses. Tosafot's later answer, that since the alternative claim itself requires an oath there is no migo to exempt from another oath, is plainly not accepted by the Rashbam. One must say that he holds that the oath on a claim of return is not a Torah oath. Otherwise the question how the Torah's oath of bailees can arise would already be resolved. The Rashbam's words later indeed show that according to Rami bar Chama there is no solution except a documented deposit for which return must be proved by witnesses. If so, one can ask how the oath of a borrower who says the animal died through ordinary use can ever arise. The borrower has a migo that he returned it. The only solution is where the loan was documented and return requires witnesses. But in the case of a borrower, who receives all the benefit, the borrower must surely pay the scribe's fee. Thus the lender may have retained the document for that fee. Even with that weak chazakah, the migo is rejected. Tosafot's question is therefore valid. One might ask why the Gemara could not establish the case as a loan of an object for a fixed period, where the borrower claims within that period that it died through use. Then he cannot claim that he already returned it. At first glance this supports the Shach's question that even within the fixed time the unresolved migo question remains. Yet this is not decisive. Abaye and Rava, who state the relevant discussion, hold that people sometimes repay within the fixed time. Rami bar Chama could follow Reish Lakish, who disagrees, so Tosafot could still ask according to him. Moreover, even Abaye and Rava might agree that their reason, "a person sometimes repays within the time so that the creditor will not trouble him later," applies only to a loan of money, which is spent and may not be readily available at maturity. It does not apply to a borrowed object that must itself be returned. What concern of later pressure is there when the very object remains present? Thus the question from a borrower remains strong. In my novellae I have already written that independently there is little difficulty from a borrower. If the lender retained the document for the scribe's fee, then when he sues for the borrowed object he will surely also claim the scribe's fee owed to him. The borrower who says, "I returned the object," must explain the fee. If he says he repaid the fee too, his claim is a migo against witnesses, because he admits paying everything yet left the document in the lender's hands, which is evidence that his claim is false. If he admits he did not repay the fee and says this is why the lender retained the document, then the lender claims the object plus a small monetary amount and the borrower admits the small amount. This is a partial admission requiring a Torah oath. There is therefore no migo to exempt him from the Torah oath of a bailee, because even the alternative claim of return would itself require a Torah oath. The document itself cannot be treated as immediate payment for the small sum. Even according to those who regard a pledged object as immediate payment, the erased or written parchment is plainly worth less than the scribe's fee, and both the scribe's labor and the value of the document have been consumed. There is no full immediate payment. It therefore seems that Tosafot understood the phrase "he retained it for the scribe's fee" not literally as the only reason. They used one possible explanation. The broader point is simply that retention of a document is not equivalent to witnesses, because there may be some other reason the document was not returned, such as that it was lost and the holder is still searching for it. This point requires some study. In any event, there are three sound approaches to explain the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, and the Shach's difficulty is not decisive.
