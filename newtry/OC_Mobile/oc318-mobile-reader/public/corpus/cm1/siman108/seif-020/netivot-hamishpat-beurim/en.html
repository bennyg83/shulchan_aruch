And the heirs have inferior land. See Shach, seif katan 38, who writes, inferior and intermediate land. In my opinion the correct text is: inferior, intermediate, and superior land. If the heirs have only inferior and intermediate land, seizure during the father's lifetime would not help. The principal reason seizure works is that we treat collection as though it occurred during the father's lifetime. If the heirs have only intermediate and inferior land, while the opposing creditor has superior and intermediate land, as the Shach writes in seif katan 39, then even during the father's lifetime the father would have collected the intermediate land and paid the opposing party from inferior land. Either way, if the father first collected, he would collect intermediate land; and because the father owned no superior land, only intermediate, he would have to pay intermediate. Since even in his lifetime he would collect intermediate and pay inferior, the same is true after death, for the heirs' right cannot be weaker than the father's own right. Rather, the text must mean that the father owned superior, intermediate, and inferior land, while the opposing party owned superior and intermediate land. In that situation, during the father's lifetime he would retain his own intermediate land, as explained in siman 85. Only because the claim is against orphans may the opposing party pay them with inferior land. Since the creditor seized during their father's lifetime, we treat it as though he came to collect then. The Shach's statement in seif katan 39, that the opposing party has intermediate and superior land, is straightforward. If he had no superior land, there would be no need for the reason of seizure, because even without it he could first collect the inferior land from the heirs, which would then be considered intermediate in his possession, since grading is measured relative to the debtor's own fields, and he could then pay the heirs with their inferior land.
His seizure after their father's death. Gidulei Terumah asks that the source of this rule is Ketubot 110, where the Gemara asks why the Mishnah should not be established with orphans who collect intermediate land and pay inferior land, and answers that because he seized, and so forth. According to the Mechaber, why not establish the case with orphans and intermediate land that the creditor purchased after their father's death? In my opinion there is no difficulty. The rule itself otherwise appears puzzling. Certainly, if the creditor has money, he need not give the heirs money and then collect their inferior land. Otherwise, the Gemara there could have established the Mishnah that each collects from the other in a case where the creditor has money and the heirs have inferior land. Evidently the Gemara's answer that one party had seized intermediate land can apply even to money. The meaning is that we treat the intermediate land in his possession as though he had already collected it during their father's lifetime. Likewise, we can treat money in his hand as though he had already paid it to their father and then collected it back. If so, how could one think that yesterday, while the money was in the creditor's hands, he did not have to give it to the heirs even though payment in money is d'oraisa, but today, after he purchased intermediate land with that money, which is subject only to a d'rabbanan preference, he must give that land to the heirs? The money was already regarded as though he had paid it to their father and then recollected it. Why should he now be required to give the land purchased with that money? The explanation is that the reason he need not give money to the heirs, even though money is d'oraisa, is because payment would expose him to a loss. Tosafot in Ketubot 92 say that where heirs collected land, the creditor need not give money because otherwise he may lose all his money if he cannot recover that particular land. Here, however, the heirs possess inferior land worth the amount of his money, so where is the loss? Haflaah asks this. It appears that even though he receives land equal in value to the money, there is still loss because we fear that the field may later be found not to belong to the heirs, similar to the concern in Ketubot 98. If the land he collected is taken from him, he has no further recourse against the heirs, because he already collected the land they inherited from their father. Their personal property is not liable to him, and he cannot recover the money they collected because under Talmudic law the heirs' movable property was not subject to their father's creditor. Thus the land collected from the heirs is without recourse, and if it is taken from him his money is lost. The Gemara calls giving money for land without recourse throwing one's money away, and there is no greater loss. By contrast, when he pays land and then recollects it, there is no concern about recourse. Because of this loss, the money in his possession is treated as though he had already paid it to their father during his lifetime and then collected it back, and he need not give it to the heirs. If afterward he purchases intermediate land with that money, he likewise need not give it to them. The Mechaber, however, speaks after the Geonic ordinance, when creditors collect from heirs' movable property, and according to the Rosh this has the law of land in our time. Consequently, if the creditor has money and the heirs have inferior land, he must give the heirs the money and collect their inferior land. If the field is later taken from him, the heirs will have to return his money because the money and movable property they hold are now subject to him, and where their father left them no other land his own money was in substance already subject to himself through Rabbi Natan's rule. Since he no longer faces the loss described above, apart from the difference in quality between money and inferior land, he must pay the heirs money and collect their inferior land in accordance with the d'oraisa rule. The preference for intermediate over inferior land, as the Rosh and Raavad explain, is merely a d'rabbanan enactment, which is why the Gemara says that seizure is effective there. By contrast, money is the d'oraisa medium of payment and movable property now has the status of land. It is therefore as though the heirs themselves had collected land, and the creditor must give them money. Consequently, the Mechaber correctly rules that even if the creditor purchased intermediate land with his money after their father's death, his holding of that land is only a later seizure and does not help, because on the previous day, while he still held the money, he was already obligated d'oraisa to give it to the heirs. This is clear.
