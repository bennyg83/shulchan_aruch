For example, where he acquired, etc. The Giddulei Terumah asks about the source of the rule that a creditor who already holds intermediate quality land does not have to surrender it in exchange for inferior land of orphans. The source is the end of Ketubot, in the case where two parties owe money to each other. The Mishnah says that each collects from the other, and the Gemara labors, according to Rav Sheshet, to find a case in which there is practical significance to separate collections rather than simply setting the debts off. The Gemara asks why not establish the case where one debtor died, so that the heirs collect intermediate land from the survivor while the survivor collects only inferior land from the heirs. It answers that since the survivor already holds the intermediate land through his counterclaim, the special rule that heirs pay only inferior land does not dislodge him. This is the source of our rule. If so, once the Baal HaTerumot and Tur add that seizure is ineffective when the creditor acquired the relevant property only after the other debtor's death, the Gemara's question seems to return. It could have established the Mishnah where one party died and the survivor acquired or inherited intermediate land only afterward. The heirs would then collect that intermediate land from him, while he would collect only inferior land from them. The Giddulei Terumah leaves this requiring study. Were we relying only on the Baal HaTerumot's unqualified statement that seizure after death is ineffective, and not on the Tur's explicit formulation, I would have explained the rule differently. It need not mean that a creditor who was already owed money during the debtor's lifetime loses the benefit of that existing counterclaim merely because he acquired property after death. If the debt itself was already in his hands during the debtor's life, he was already a holder of value against the other party. Why should acquiring additional property after death weaken that prior possession? Rather, the case could be as follows. Yaakov borrowed from Shimon. Reuven, Yaakov's son, separately lent money to Shimon. Shimon died at a time when Reuven held nothing of Shimon's, neither a debt owed by Reuven to Shimon nor any of Shimon's property. Only afterward did Yaakov die, and Reuven inherited assets together with Yaakov's debt to Shimon. Suppose Yaakov's loan from Shimon expressly stipulated that the creditor could collect intermediate land even from heirs. Reuven cannot now characterize the intermediate land he inherited as property he had already seized during Shimon's lifetime, because at that time he had no right in it at all. He must therefore pay according to the inherited obligation. On that reading the Gemara in Ketubot cannot be established this way, because its Mishnah expressly concerns two people who had already lent to one another during their lifetimes. The Tur, however, states plainly that even where the surviving creditor himself acquires intermediate land only after the other party's death, the later acquisition does not count as effective seizure. The Giddulei Terumah's question therefore remains. It appears that the Baal HaTerumot himself frames the rule as dependent on two distinct explanations for why possession of intermediate land helps. Some say that the Sages' reduction of an orphan's liability to inferior land is a special leniency for orphans. Once the creditor already possesses intermediate land, the Sages did not extend that leniency so far as to remove it from his hands. The Raavad objects that inferior land is not merely a rabbinic leniency for orphans. According to Ulla, Torah law itself gives an ordinary creditor only inferior land, and the Sages upgraded collection from a living debtor to intermediate land in order to facilitate lending. With orphans they simply leave Torah law in place. According to that rationale, the creditor's possession helps only if it can be treated as a collection that was already valid before death. If he acquired the land only after death, at a time when his legal entitlement was only inferior land, there is no prior valid collection to preserve. The first group of authorities, however, evidently follow the alternative Talmudic explanation that creditors receive intermediate land so that people will not deliberately lend against attractive superior land. On that view the baseline may be more favorable to the creditor, and the orphan rule is an additional rabbinic leniency. Possession can therefore protect the creditor more broadly. These underlying theories also connect to the dispute whether land quality is measured relative to this debtor's holdings or relative to the general market. If the source is Ulla's verse that the debtor brings out the least of his utensils, the measure must be relative to the debtor himself. What matters is what is inferior among his own property. If the reason for intermediate land is to prevent a creditor from setting his eyes on unusually attractive property, market standards become relevant, because what is attractive in the market is what creates that concern. We rule that quality is measured relative to the debtor's own holdings, which supports Ulla's approach, as the Raavad notes. Accordingly, we need the explanation that possession works because the creditor is treated as having already collected during the debtor's lifetime. If he acquired the property only after death, that rationale fails. The Gemara's discussion, however, is explicitly according to Rav Sheshet, who holds that quality is measured by the general market. His approach therefore fits the other rationale, that the Sages merely gave heirs an additional leniency and did not extend it to property already held by the creditor. On that approach it makes no difference whether the creditor's possession began during the debtor's life or only after death, so the Gemara could not have answered by using a later acquisition case. The apparent contradiction is therefore resolved. The remaining question is from where the Baal HaTerumot derived the practical distinction for our halakhah, but at least the Talmudic passage itself is consistent with it.
