Or from purchasers. The Sema cites a responsum of the Rosh, klal 86, siman 12. Yaakov lent money to Reuven, Reuven sold his property, and before Yaakov collected from the purchasers Yaakov died. Reuven also died. The question was whether Yaakov's claim falls under the principle that a person cannot bequeath to his children a claim collectible only through an oath, or whether it is governed by the rule that we do not extend that exceptional principle beyond its original case. The Rosh answered that one cannot rely simply on "do not extend," because purchasers who pay can in principle return against the debtor's heirs for reimbursement, making the case analogous to a guarantor who pays and then has recourse against the heirs. Nevertheless, the Rosh gives another reason why the claim is collectible: this is not money that Yaakov could never have collected without an oath. While Reuven was alive, Yaakov could have collected directly from him without the purchaser's oath. The Sema struggles to reconcile this with our ruling that even collection from purchasers generally remains within the limitation of "do not extend." There is actually no difficulty from that alone. Here the purchasers have no recourse against the heirs because the debtor and heirs have no assets. In the Rosh's responsum, by contrast, the heirs evidently did have assets, but Yaakov was pursuing purchasers because the heirs' assets were overseas or otherwise inaccessible. Since the purchasers who pay can return against the heirs, their position is functionally like collection from the heirs themselves. The deeper difficulty is that the responsum appears to say the creditor died before the debtor. If so, collection from the heirs themselves would ordinarily remain possible even with an impaired document, because the special rule applies when the debtor dies first and the creditor's heirs then inherit an oath bound claim. It therefore appears that the Rosh distinguishes among different types of oath obligations. The rule of Rav and Shmuel that one cannot bequeath an oath bound claim applies principally to the automatic heirs' oath, where the creditor must swear without any particular allegation and the Sages especially protected orphans. A creditor whose document is impaired, or against whom one witness testifies to payment, requires an oath only when that issue is affirmatively raised. Such a claim is not automatically characterized as money that can be acquired only by an oath in every setting. Thus, if the creditor impaired the document and then died before the debtor, the heirs' later claim is not automatically barred merely because their father would have had to swear upon a proper demand. Similarly, where the debtor dies first but the creditor seeks payment from a guarantor or purchaser and the estate has no assets, the oath is not imposed for the orphans' direct benefit, so the exceptional rule is not extended. But where Reuven sold his assets, Yaakov was already pursuing purchasers, and after Reuven's death those purchasers retain recourse against the heirs, two adverse features combine. The oath against purchasers is imposed without a particular allegation, and its economic effect ultimately reaches the heirs because the purchasers can recover from them. The case therefore resembles the original rule closely enough that the Rosh needed a separate reason to permit collection. His reason is that while Reuven was alive Yaakov could have chosen to collect directly from him without an oath. The claim was therefore never intrinsically one that could be realized only through an oath. This resolves the responsum. The underlying idea is that the exceptional bar arises when the inherited monetary right itself became inextricably conditioned on an oath, not merely whenever some procedural route to collection would have required one.
