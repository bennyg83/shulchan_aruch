If the borrower left property. See Tumim, who writes that according to the preceding rule, if there is an earlier creditor and a later creditor, the borrower died during the earlier creditor's lifetime, the later creditor is alive, and the estate is insufficient for both, the earlier creditor collects. His reasoning is that even if the later creditor had already collected, the earlier creditor could remove the property from him, and the later creditor could not argue that the earlier creditor is unable to collect because of the rule that a person cannot bequeath a claim requiring an oath, since the later creditor is no better than a purchaser. All the more so, before the later creditor collects, since the earlier creditor has no one else from whom to recover, he should collect. Tumim adds that this is certainly so if the heirs themselves hold the earlier creditor's claim, such as when they come by virtue of their mother's ketubah. The rule that one cannot bequeath such a claim should not apply because they themselves are in possession. From this he objects to Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer 96, who gives a different reason. In my opinion this is incorrect. Certainly before the later creditor has collected, the earlier creditor cannot collect from the heirs. A creditor acquires only from the time of actual collection, so until the later creditor collects, the land belongs to the heirs. Moreover, the later creditor might forgive his debt to the heirs, in which case the earlier creditor certainly could not collect from them. Even if the later creditor already collected, the earlier creditor cannot remove it from him. This is not comparable to purchasers. Only purchasers who bought during the seller's lifetime are subject to the earlier creditor, because they do not derive their rights through the heirs. If the deceased left no assets to the heirs, the heirs themselves are irrelevant, almost as though there were no heirs, and collection from the purchasers is just as it would have been during the debtor's lifetime. For this reason the purchasers cannot say, If you remain silent I will accept your claim, otherwise I will return the deed to the heirs, because the purchasers did not receive their rights through the heirs. The Ran in Yevamot, Perek HaCholetz, explains that the basis of that argument is that the first seller can transfer to the second only the rights he himself possesses. But purchasers who bought from the debtor's heirs cannot be collected from, even without seller's responsibility and even though they cannot recover from the heirs, because the heirs could transfer only the rights they themselves possessed. Since the heirs could have deflected the earlier creditor, so can the purchasers. A later creditor who collected from the heirs is likewise like a purchaser from them, because a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. There is an additional reason. Immediately upon the debtor's death, while the assets were with the heirs, the lien embodied in the earlier promissory note was extinguished by the rule that a person cannot bequeath money that he could collect only through the heirs' oath. At the moment of death, collection and the oath were directed against the heirs. This differs from purchasers who bought during the debtor's lifetime, where no oath obligation against heirs arose during the creditor's lifetime. Such a claim can be bequeathed, because the rule applies only to money that can be collected solely through the heirs' oath. Beit Shmuel therefore correctly needed his separate reason in Even HaEzer, regarding heirs who come under their mother's ketubah against a later creditor, that the heirs can say, We waive the oath requirement. Without that, since their mother herself could not collect her ketubah without the heirs' oath, she could not bequeath such a claim.
