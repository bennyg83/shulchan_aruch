Where the borrower died during the lender's lifetime, there is room to ask whether our bearer notes, written to whoever produces them and not naming the lender, are subject to the rule that one does not bequeath a claim encumbered by an oath. The inability of heirs to collect in the ordinary case is not merely because we fear that their father may have been paid and they cannot swear otherwise. A debt instrument retains its presumption of validity even when the lender's heirs collect from the living borrower. The stricter rule after the borrower died first is a separate enactment, that a person does not bequeath money which he himself could collect only through an oath. A bearer note differs because the present holder can say that the debtor became obligated directly to whoever would later hold the paper. He need not claim merely as heir of the original lender. Rashba nevertheless writes that even where a document says the debtor is liable to any holder, if the borrower died during the lender's lifetime and the lender then sold or gave away the note, the recipient still cannot collect merely by saying he does not come through the lender. This suggests the oath disability remains. Our common bearer notes may be stronger because communal enactment treats them as containing credibility provisions even where not expressly written. If so, the special rule of collection from heirs may not arise at all, because the holder stands as one with an effective credibility clause. The matter therefore depends on the precise enactment governing these notes.
