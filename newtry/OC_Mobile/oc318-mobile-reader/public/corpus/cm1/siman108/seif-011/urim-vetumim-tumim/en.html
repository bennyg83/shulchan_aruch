And afterward he came to beit din, etc. This is the view of the Ramah. The Bach holds that the Rosh disagrees, while the Shach did not accept that conclusion. I do not understand this. According to the Rosh and Tur above in siman 25, if a judge erred in a matter of judicial discretion and ruled in favor of the party who was liable, what he did stands and the judgment is final, even where the judge is not required to pay from his own funds. Even a judge greater than he cannot reverse it. All the more so here, where the first judge did not err in judicial discretion at all, but on the contrary ruled in accordance with Torah law to exempt the defendant from the promissory note. How can another judge have power to reverse and annul that judgment and rule according to the other opinion? We rule that the judgment stands. It is therefore possible that the Ramah follows the Rambam and Rif above in siman 25, who hold that where the judge need not personally pay, the judgment is reversed. Here too, since the first judge does not pay, another judge who follows Rabbi Elazar may, because of the litigant's loss, reverse the ruling. This too is strained, but at least it aligns his view with his general position. According to the Rosh and Tur, however, who hold that the judgment always stands, all the more so here, this law is plainly subject to their dispute. It is difficult to say that when the Tur and Rosh above state that the judgment stands and even a greater judge cannot reverse it they mean only that initially he may not reverse it, but if he did reverse it, it remains reversed. That is not the implication of the authorities or the Gemara. Once the law is that the judgment stands, it stands completely. One might also ask from the earlier ruling that if the heirs preemptively seized the property, etc., their seizure is effective. The wording implies that this is only if they seized before a judgment was issued, but not afterward. Yet the reason seizure helps is that a person may execute judgment for himself, meaning according to Rabbi Elazar. If so, according to the Ramah, even after a judgment it should help, since one who acted according to Rabbi Elazar has acted validly even after the ruling. There is another difficulty: why, if one acted according to Rabbi Elazar and removed the property from the heirs, can another judge no longer reverse that and rule according to Rav and Shmuel, whereas if the first judge ruled according to Rav and Shmuel another judge has power to rule the opposite? See Knesset HaGedolah, who cites Maharit and Maharia as addressing this with fine distinctions. It therefore appears that if the second judge knew of the first judge's ruling according to Rav and Shmuel, he certainly has no authority to rule against it, and his judgment is void, because we rule that the first judgment stands, as stated above. The Ramah speaks only where the second judge did not know of the first judge's decision. He ruled innocently, according to his own understanding, like Rabbi Elazar, and already acted upon his ruling and transferred the money. In that case we do not remove it from the creditor's heirs, because a ruling has already been rendered and the second judge was unaware of the first. Accordingly, there is no difficulty from the law above concerning an error in judicial discretion, where the judgment stands and a greater judge cannot reverse it, because there the greater judge knows of the first judgment. Nor is there a difficulty from the case where the heirs seized first. After one has heard the ruling, one cannot issue a contrary ruling. Likewise, where one acted according to Rabbi Elazar, if the later judge has heard the earlier ruling he cannot rule against it, because the judgment stands. This point still requires study. Alternatively, perhaps the Ramah speaks where the first beit din never issued a formal ruling. The promissory note came before them, they saw that according to their view they would exempt the debtor's heirs, and they simply left the matter without saying anything. The Ramah's language supports this somewhat: "he acted according to Rav and Shmuel and did not collect with it." This implies that they did not issue a judgment, but merely did not collect upon the note. If they had already ruled, that judgment would be equivalent to tearing up the note. This requires study.
