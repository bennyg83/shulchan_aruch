Whether they are sons or brothers, etc. This excludes the initial thought in the Gemara that perhaps the rule applies only to sons. Since brothers too take the oath that the deceased did not instruct them, there is no distinction.
Even if the lender already swore, it does not help. Meaning he swore to the borrower during the borrower's lifetime that he had not been paid, and afterward the borrower died during the lender's lifetime. The lender still could not collect that same debt from the borrower's heirs without a new oath, because beit din would claim for them that perhaps the borrower paid him after the earlier oath. Therefore, when the lender later dies, his heirs cannot swear and collect, because their father could have collected only with an oath and a person cannot bequeath such a claim.
That he cannot collect except with an oath. Meaning an oath that beit din imposes for the defendant's protection even without the defendant's own demand, such as where the borrower died before the lender. The same applies, according to the first "some say" in the preceding seif, where the lender died first and the first generation of heirs then died.
If the judge transgressed, etc. Because the Gemara records a dispute, it concludes that initially we follow the rule that one cannot bequeath the oath, but if the heirs already collected after taking the oath that their father did not instruct them, what they collected remains theirs. The Gemara also says "do not add to it": only where the lender's first oath was one required to collect from the borrower's heirs do we say that he cannot bequeath the claim. In the other cases listed in seif 14, such as an impaired document, the heirs take the heirs' oath and collect.
We do not call him, etc. The reason is that perhaps the heirs can seize property from what their father left, or come to beit din and collect through it.
If the borrower left property. This means land or movable property. Since even under the law of the Gemara heirs have a mitzvah to pay their father's debt from movable property they inherited, the guarantor is treated as following the heirs. Thus, when the borrower died before the lender, the lender's heirs cannot bypass the bar by collecting from the guarantor. But if the borrower left nothing, so that the heirs are not legally liable to reimburse the guarantor, the lender's heirs may collect from the guarantor. The Tur further distinguishes purchasers: if the borrower left no land, the lender's heirs can collect from purchasers even though the borrower died before the lender, because purchasers may have bought property for only a day and cannot necessarily return against the heirs. If the borrower did leave land to his heirs, however, the purchaser has recourse against them, and collection from him is treated as beginning with the heirs. It is surprising that the Mechaber omitted this detail, perhaps because he regarded it as obvious from the guarantor rule.
