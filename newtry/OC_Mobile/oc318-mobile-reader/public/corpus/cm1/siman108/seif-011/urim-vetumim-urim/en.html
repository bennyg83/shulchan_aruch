Who cannot collect it except with an oath. I already explained that the special oath required of one collecting from heirs is stringent. Beit din itself raises the possibility that the deceased debtor may have paid. Until the creditor swears, he has no vested right in the money. If he dies and no heir can swear definitely that payment was never made, they lose the money, just as one who cannot take a required oath loses.
Even if the creditor already swore, etc. This means that while the borrower was alive he demanded, 'Swear that I did not pay you,' and the creditor swore. Some time later the borrower died. The creditor must still swear to the heirs that the borrower did not pay after that earlier oath. If the creditor then dies before taking this new oath, the rule that one cannot transmit an oath obligation applies and his heirs lose.
And if the judge nevertheless ruled, etc. The Gemara says that because the halachah was not definitively stated whether it follows Rav and Shmuel, who say that one cannot transmit an oath obligation to his children, or Rabbi Eliezer, who allows heirs to swear and collect, a judge who acted in accordance with Rabbi Eliezer has acted validly.
And they seized, etc. Baal HaTerumos explains that because a judge who rules like Rabbi Eliezer acts validly, and a person may sometimes execute judgment for himself, seizure is effective, even if made in the presence of witnesses so that no migo exists. See Tumim. In Even HaEzer, Ramah cites an opinion that seizure does not help. I wrote that perhaps this limitation concerns a ketubah, but not other creditors. Even with a ketubah, if the widow herself seized without witnesses she may prevail through a migo that she was not paid on the ketubah, whereas the heirs do not have such a migo. See Tumim. Movable property of heirs seized after death requires study, because perhaps we do not combine two leniencies. This remains difficult. Seizure helps only if it occurred before a final ruling invalidating the note. After such a ruling, seizure does not help. This is why the text says that they 'preceded and seized.' See Tumim, seif katan 11.
We do not tear it. Perhaps another judge will later rule like Rabbi Eliezer and collect for the heirs.
Nevertheless, if they did not tear it, etc. Bach writes that Rosh disagrees, while Shach does not accept this. See Tumim. At first glance Bach appears correct that the issue is disputed. I wrote, however, that even Rama's ruling applies only where the second judge did not know of the first ruling and then adjudicated and transferred the money. If he knew that the first judge had already ruled the defendant exempt, his later ruling and action have no force.
If the borrower left property. This includes movable property, even under Talmudic law when such property was not automatically encumbered to creditors, because the heirs ordinarily have a mitzvah to pay from it. Therefore, if the creditor seeks to collect from purchasers and the deceased left only movable property, under Talmudic law he may nevertheless collect from purchasers because the heirs would not be obligated to reimburse those purchasers. There is no mitzvah to protect someone who knowingly buys land that may be subject to a creditor's lien. After the Geonic enactment making inherited movable property subject to debts, there is no longer a distinction between guarantor and purchaser. If the debtor left even movable property, the creditor does not collect from either, because they can turn to the heirs. If he left nothing, the creditor may collect from a guarantor or from encumbered property in purchasers' hands. This is correct.
And there is no distinction. See Tumim regarding an earlier and later creditor where the estate cannot satisfy both and the earlier creditor is subject to the rule that the borrower died during the creditor's lifetime, so that the oath cannot be transmitted. If the heirs of the earlier creditor are themselves the debtor's heirs, for example they claim through their mother's ketubah and she died without swearing on it, there is no doubt that they collect and the later creditor receives nothing, because no practical loss is created for the heirs. If the earlier creditor's heirs are not the debtor's heirs, it requires study whether the earlier creditor nevertheless collects because the issue does not affect the debtor's heirs, or whether the rule still bars collection. See there.
If the guarantor is the heir, etc. This means that even if the debtor left property, so that an ordinary guarantor who pays could seek reimbursement from that property, here the guarantor himself is also the heir. To whom would he seek reimbursement? Since he is both guarantor and heir, this falls within the limited exception and collection may be made from him. See Knesset HaGedolah, where the scholars of the generation disputed this law. The majority nevertheless agreed with Maharik and the gloss.
