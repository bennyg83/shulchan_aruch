And against those who come in their place, the heirs swear, etc. The novelty is that even according to the view that a credibility clause granted by the borrower to the lender and his heirs does not exempt the lender from swearing against the borrower's heirs if the borrower dies during the lender's lifetime, it still exempts those who stand in the lender's place in the circumstances explained there. The continuation of the source explains how the oath differs where two witnesses testify to full payment versus partial payment: if they testify that the entire debt was paid, the lender cannot overcome them by oath, but if they testify only to partial payment, the lender could swear and collect the remainder, and his heirs likewise take the heirs' oath and collect the balance. Likewise, a hesset oath that was reversed, etc. The ruling teaches that a judgment-created obligation is not automatically treated like a written document. The heirs do not swear and collect. This applies even from the renter, robber, or original defendant himself. Chazal did not institute that heirs may extract money merely by the oath that their father did not instruct them except where they hold a promissory note, for the document itself indicates that the debt remains outstanding.
