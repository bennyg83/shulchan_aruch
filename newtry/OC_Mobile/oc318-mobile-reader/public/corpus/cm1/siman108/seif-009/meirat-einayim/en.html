All the rights that the lender's heirs have against the borrower, etc. To understand the rulings from seif 9 through seif 14, the apparent contradictions among them, and the difficulties in the Rosh, Tur, and the Levush, one must review the sugya. The Mishnah at the beginning of chapter Kol HaNishba'in teaches that just as a woman who admits partial payment of her ketubah, one contradicted by a single witness, one collecting from encumbered property, one collecting from orphans, and one collecting in the defendant's absence collect only with an oath, so too heirs collect only with an oath. In all these cases, even where the defendant does not demand an oath, beit din raises the oath on his behalf before allowing collection, notwithstanding the claimant's document.
The Gemara explains that "heirs" means heirs collecting from heirs. Rav and Shmuel say that the rule allowing them to collect with the heirs' oath applies only where the lender died before the borrower. But if the borrower died first, the lender became obligated to swear to the borrower's heirs, and a person cannot bequeath to his children money that he could collect only through his own oath, because the children cannot swear with certainty that their father was not paid. Rabbi Elazar disagrees and says that the heirs take the heirs' oath, "our father did not instruct us," and collect. Rav Nachman declined to uproot Rav and Shmuel's rule, but said not to extend it: for example, Rav Pappa said that where one impaired his document and died, the heirs take the heirs' oath and collect. The Gemara further establishes that this rule is not limited to sons, and that where a guarantor follows the heirs, the same restriction applies. It concludes that because the halacha was not explicitly fixed, a judge who followed Rav and Shmuel or Rabbi Elazar was not reversed, while Rif and Rosh rule that initially judges should follow Rav and Shmuel.
From this the Tur and Mechaber rule in seif 14 that the rule "a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children" is not extended to every analogous case. Thus where the lender impaired his document, seeks collection in the defendant's absence, seeks collection from purchasers, or is contradicted by one witness, the heirs take the heirs' oath and collect. The conclusion "do not add to it" excludes these cases, even though the lender too would have been required to swear. This creates difficulty with two responsa of the Rosh. In klal 68, siman 19, concerning a widow who produced a document and one witness testified it had been paid, the Rosh wrote that because the lender himself could collect only by an oath, once he died he could not bequeath the claim to his heirs, nor could his creditor collect it. This applies even against the original borrower, not merely heirs. In klal 86, siman 3, Levi, the creditor of Reuven, sought to collect from land sold by Reuven to Shimon. Levi swore the purchaser's oath and the matter remained pending. Shimon then sold the land to Yehudah; Levi died and his son sought to collect. The Rosh ruled that although Levi had already sworn to Shimon, perhaps afterward Reuven or Shimon paid him, and had Levi himself come to collect from Yehudah he would have had to swear again at the moment of collection. Since he died, he could not bequeath that oath to his son. This applies the rule even to collection from purchasers, contrary to the Gemara's limitation. It is surprising that Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite these responsa without noting their conflict with the Gemara, the Rosh's own rulings, and the Tur. The Mechaber even rules at the end of siman 84, in accordance with that responsum, that where one witness testifies that the document was paid, the lender cannot bequeath the claim, and adds that the same applies where the lender impaired his own document, apparently contradicting seif 14 here. Levush attempted to distinguish whether the lender died before or after the due date, saying that where he died within the term the heirs can collect because their father had not yet become obligated to swear unless the defendant demanded it. This is untenable. The very case of heirs collecting from heirs, from which the Gemara derives the limitation, could then be established only within the term, yet within the term the lender can collect from the borrower's heirs without an oath, as stated at the beginning of this siman. Nor is there any greater novelty in the impaired-document case within the term than in an ordinary document after the term where the defendant had not yet demanded an oath. It is likewise difficult to say that the responsa concern some special circumstance not addressed by the Gemara, for the Rosh expressly uses the Gemara's formula. I therefore see no resolution except that when the Rosh wrote those responsa he had not yet interpreted the sugya as he later did in his final rulings. We often find that his responsa conflict with his later rulings for this reason. At first he may have understood Rav Nachman's "do not add to it" narrowly: perhaps only the case of an impaired document was excluded, because its oath itself is a weak rabbinic novelty, while stronger reasons for an oath, such as a single witness or collection from purchasers, might still fall under the rule that one cannot bequeath the claim. Later he concluded, as Baal HaTerumot and Baal HaItur also discuss, that the exclusion extends to all the other oaths listed with it in the Mishnah, whenever the original oath was not one imposed in order to collect from orphans. This is also supported by the form of Rif and Rosh's text, which does not preserve the Gemara's "for example, what?" formulation. Baal HaTerumot himself records the uncertainty whether "do not add to it" excludes only the impaired-document case or all the other cases listed in the Mishnah, and cites Baal HaItur as extending it to the others. Another responsum of the Rosh likewise shows that the issue was debated in his time. Thus the Tur properly followed the Rosh's later rulings and the other major authorities without lengthening the discussion by reproducing the conflicting earlier responsa. One might try to distinguish whether the lender had already stood in judgment and been formally obligated to swear. Rabbeinu Yerucham, in the name of Ramban, writes concerning heirs collecting from purchasers that they may collect if the lender had not already litigated with the purchasers and become obligated to swear before he died. This shows that some authorities distinguished between an oath already imposed by beit din and one not yet imposed. Yet the Rosh's responsa cannot be resolved this way, for they apply even where no prior judgment had imposed the oath, merely because the lender would have needed to swear if he came to collect. Nor does the Gemara itself readily support such a distinction, since Rav Nachman rejected the suggestion that formal litigation is decisive: "Is it beit din that obligates him to swear? From the moment the borrower died, the lender became obligated to swear to the borrower's heirs." Therefore the matter remains difficult. This introduction is necessary both for understanding the Mechaber's rulings and for practical law. The central principle emerging from the Gemara and the Rosh's final rulings is that the rule preventing inheritance of an oath is confined to the case where the lender's first oath arose because he sought to collect from the borrower's heirs. Other rabbinic collection oaths are not included in the extension unless some independent distinction applies. So too they have them against the borrower's heirs. Just as the lender's heirs may swear and collect from the borrower himself, they may swear and collect from his heirs. This means that they are not barred from collecting entirely. As to the oath, the case of the borrower's heirs is stricter, for even if the borrower himself would not have demanded an oath, beit din raises it on behalf of the heirs. That they themselves were not paid by their father. The Tur explains that if their father were alive he could have claimed that he paid them, since they are adults capable of taking the oath. Whatever their father could have claimed, we advance on behalf of his heirs. This differs from the case where the borrower himself remains alive and makes no claim that he paid the lender's heirs.
