He explains that the case is not comparable to a deposit held by a third party, because a third party was deliberately trusted from the outset, whereas documents ordinarily come into the hands of brothers through the normal division of the estate. The brothers were not specially trusted. For the same reason this differs from the case at the end of siman 71, where the relevant person had been entrusted, as I wrote above in siman 93. This approach indeed appears repeatedly in the Rosh's responsa, klal 50 and elsewhere. Yet one must understand what happened to the heir's migo that he could have hidden or destroyed the document. See Knesset HaGedolah, who cites many authorities forced to establish the case after the heir's migo has already passed, so that relying on it would be a retrospective migo. That is strained. It appears that the Rosh regards the presumption, "if the debt was paid, why is the creditor's document still in his hands?" as a powerful presumption. Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Batra 5b even compare it to witnesses. A migo does not overcome such a powerful presumption unless the migo itself existed at the time of the alleged payment, because then the debtor could reasonably have relied on that defense and may have left the document outstanding. Thus, where one admits writing the document but claims payment and the document has not been authenticated, the debtor may have left it because he knew the creditor could not authenticate it and he would have a migo. Likewise, with a deposit the bailee always knew that he could plead unavoidable accident. But if the supposed migo did not exist at the time of repayment, why would the debtor leave the document in the creditor's hands? He could not know that eventually the document would reach a particular heir who would then have the ability to burn it. Nor does the debtor's conduct depend on whether the creditor or later heirs might someday act wrongfully. The outstanding document therefore creates a strong presumption, and a newly arising migo in the heir cannot defeat it. A third party is different because the creditor deliberately entrusted the document to him, and the document's very presence in neutral hands weakens the presumption, as explained above in siman 65. The creditor himself is also different. If he says the document was paid, his position was trusted from beginning to end because the document always stood under his own control. This is why the Rosh and Tur above in siman 47 require that a creditor who admits payment while owing others not possess a migo to destroy the document. His own control is at least as strong as that of a trusted third party. But ordinary heirs have no such initial trust, and the late migo does not overcome the presumption created by the outstanding instrument. A practical consequence follows. Suppose Reuven owed his father Yaakov. Yaakov died, and Reuven and Reuven's coheirs inherited Yaakov. A debt instrument from Reuven to Yaakov came into the hands of some of the heirs, who say it was paid. Reuven's creditors seek to collect on the basis that the debt to Yaakov still exists, while the other heirs argue that the admitting heirs cannot harm their shares and that they stand in their grandfather's place with priority. Here the heirs who physically hold the document and possess a migo to destroy it are believed. The normal presumption, "why is the document still in the creditor's hands?" is absent, because the document has in fact reached heirs of the debtor himself. Even if they admit that they received it through the creditor's estate rather than from Reuven, they still could have claimed that it came from Reuven, and we do not extend the outstanding document presumption so far as to defeat such a present migo in a setting where possession itself is equivocal.
