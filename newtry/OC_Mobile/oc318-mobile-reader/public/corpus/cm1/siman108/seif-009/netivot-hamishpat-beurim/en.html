All the law that applies to my heirs. See Sema, seif katan 30, who writes at length in astonishment over the Mechaber's ruling in light of siman 84 and seeks to reconcile the responsa and rulings of the Rosh. In my opinion the matter can be reconciled as follows. In Shevuot 47, Tosafot, beginning with the words Since he cannot swear, cite Rabbeinu Tam that Rav Nachman agrees with Rabbi Abba, and all the more so where the claimant is obligated to swear, he loses his claim if he cannot do so. The reason the claimant's d'rabbanan oath is different is that one who cannot swear does not lose unless he is subject to a d'oraisa oath. Therefore, with heirs collecting from heirs, where the borrower died during the creditor's lifetime, or where the creditor had impaired his note, both of which involve d'rabbanan oaths, the heirs swear the heirs' oath and collect and do not lose according to Rav Nachman and Rabbi Elazar. Rav and Shmuel, however, hold that even for a d'rabbanan oath the rule that one who cannot swear loses applies. At first glance, what difference does it make if they hold that the rule applies to a d'rabbanan oath? With heirs we do not say that one who cannot swear must pay. Likewise, Tosafot's statement that Rav Nachman accepts Rabbi Abba's rule but does not apply it to a d'rabbanan oath is difficult, because even if it applied to a d'rabbanan oath, where the creditor died the oath falls upon his heirs, and the rule does not apply to heirs. Yet all the authorities make this law depend on that rule. It is also difficult that the Ran writes at the end of this sugya that the view requiring division necessarily accepts Rav and Shmuel. Since we rule like Rav Nachman, one should say only, Do not add to it. It therefore appears that the rule exempting heirs from the consequence of inability to swear applies only where the oath obligation arose after the borrower's death. For example, if the heirs say, We know about fifty and do not know about fifty, the Torah's phrase, The oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them, excludes an oath between heirs. There is no oath obligation on heirs merely because they admit part of a claim. But if the father admitted part of the claim before beit din, became obligated in a d'oraisa oath, and then died, and it is known that he neither swore nor paid before death, such as where he admitted during his final illness, then because he became obligated either to swear or pay, once he died and could no longer swear his property became obligated to pay. Siman 69, seif 6, and siman 87, seif 32, concern a hesset oath, as the Sema's words indicate and as Ketzot HaChoshen also writes. Therefore, in the case where the borrower died during the creditor's lifetime, the creditor became obligated in an oath during his own lifetime. According to the view that the inability rule applies even to a d'rabbanan oath, he became obligated to swear or lose. When he dies and can no longer swear, the claim is lost. Tosafot's words are thus clear. According to this, Amemar's statement regarding one who impaired his note and died means that the son admits his father impaired the note in his lifetime, but there was no litigation during the father's lifetime. Only after the creditor died did the borrower claim full payment, while the creditor's heirs say, We know that fifty was paid and we do not know about the other fifty. Just as, with a d'oraisa oath, when the borrower's heirs say, We know about fifty and do not know about fifty, we do not apply the inability rule, so too when the creditor's heirs say, We know of payment of fifty and do not know about fifty, they do not lose, because the oath obligation first fell upon the heirs. By contrast, in siman 108 and at the end of siman 84, the Mechaber discusses a case in which the litigation arose during the father's lifetime: the borrower claimed full payment, the creditor admitted partial payment and thus became obligated to swear. The wording is precise: the Mechaber writes, and he died before he swore. Since in a d'oraisa oath, where a borrower admitted part and then died, we would apply the inability rule to his estate, likewise where the creditor admitted partial payment and then died we apply it to his heirs, because according to Rav and Shmuel a d'oraisa oath and an instituted oath are equivalent. See the Ran, who writes that according to our ruling like Rav Nachman, the rule of Rav and Shmuel still applies, except that Rav Nachman says, Do not add to it, meaning be careful not to extend their rule. We should not mistakenly say that even where the note was impaired and the creditor then died, but the litigation first arose after death, Rav and Shmuel apply. The ruling teaches that we do not extend it. But where their father actually became obligated in an oath during his lifetime and then died, we certainly apply the inability rule, because Rav and Shmuel's rule that d'oraisa and instituted oaths are equivalent remains valid, and with a d'oraisa oath we would apply it. This is essentially the Sema's distinction based on whether the party had already stood in judgment. The Sema rejected that distinction because of the Gemara's objection, Does beit din obligate him in the oath? According to what I have written there is no difficulty. Where the borrower died during the creditor's lifetime, the creditor became obligated to swear during his lifetime even without having stood in beit din. But where he impaired his note during his lifetime and the borrower did not deny the remainder in beit din until after the creditor's death, there was no oath obligation at all during the creditor's lifetime. It is exactly like the borrower's heirs saying, We know about fifty and not about fifty, where the inability rule does not apply. As to purchasers and collection not in the debtor's presence, the Rosh independently writes, as cited by the Sema, that the rule that a person cannot bequeath such a claim does not apply. See Sema, seif katan 45. This is clear. Nevertheless, the principal practical ruling follows the plain implication of the authorities, that even if their father actually became obligated in an oath in beit din we still say, Do not add to Rav and Shmuel's rule, as stated in siman 69, seif 6.
