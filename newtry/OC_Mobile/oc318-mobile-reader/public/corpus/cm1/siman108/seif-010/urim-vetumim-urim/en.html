For their heirs do not swear, etc. The Sages enacted that a creditor collecting from heirs must swear that the note was not paid. If he dies without taking that oath, he cannot transmit the right to collect together with the oath obligation to his children. The same principle applies to the oath that heirs themselves must take when collecting, that their father did not instruct them that the debt was paid. If those heirs die before swearing, their own heirs do not acquire the money, because this too falls within the rule that one cannot transmit an oath obligation to his children.
And some say that they too, etc. The distinction is that the original creditor would have had to swear definitely that he was not paid. When he dies, his heirs cannot swear that definite oath and can only swear that their father did not instruct them, and therefore the right does not pass. The first generation of heirs, however, also had only an uncertain oath, namely that their father did not instruct them. Their heirs can take the same uncertain oath, so the rule does not necessarily apply. Therefore, if the debtor's heirs themselves make a definite claim that payment was made, causing the first generation of creditor heirs to have to swear definitely that they were not paid, then if those creditor heirs die, all agree that the right cannot pass further. They themselves were obligated in a definite oath like their father, while the next generation could swear only on uncertainty. Shach's distinction is correct.
