And likewise if the debtor's heirs first seized. In Even HaEzer siman 96 the Rama also cites the dissenting view that seizure is ineffective. This is Maharam's view in Mordechai in the chapters Chezkas HaBatim and HaKotev. It appears that the dispute there concerns seizure before witnesses, where the heirs have no migo. The Baal HaTerumot explains the opinion that seizure is effective by saying that since we hold that a judge who acts like Rabbi Elazar has acted validly, a person may also act as his own judge and seize while asserting that he follows Rabbi Elazar. See what I wrote about this in my treatise on seizure. On this theory seizure can be effective even before witnesses, as in other cases of seizure under a doubt in the law. Maharam disagrees with that premise. But where the creditor himself seized without witnesses before he died, there is a distinct reason to uphold the seizure. The creditor was never subject to the Mishnah's oath in the first place because he had a migo that he had not seized. Once he admits seizure and says the amount remains unpaid, his possession and migo support him. This is especially so according to the authorities who allow a migo to exempt from an oath. The rule that a person cannot bequeath to his children money that he could collect only through an oath does not apply, because the creditor himself was never under the special oath. If, by contrast, the heirs seize only after the creditor's death, even a migo does not solve the problem. They do not know whether their father was paid, so their present migo does not supply the missing personal knowledge. Seizure without witnesses is no stronger for them than seizure before witnesses and remains subject to the underlying dispute. The fact that the Rama cites both views in Even HaEzer but not here suggests that he follows Maharam Tiktin's explanation in the glosses to Mordechai that Maharam's restriction concerns a ketubah, not other creditors. A responsum of Maharam concerning a widow who possessed her husband's assets rules explicitly that the property remains presumptively the heirs' and she is merely an administrator. This could support a distinction between a ketubah and an ordinary creditor. Yet the reason stated by Mordechai in HaKotev is broader. He says this case is weaker than Beit Shammai's doctrine that a document standing for collection is considered collected, because there at least the right of collection is certain, whereas here the seizure is clouded by the possibility that the debtor had already set aside payment. That rationale does not distinguish a ketubah from another debt. It may be, however, that the Mordechai passage turns on a different point. The maxim that "one who acts like Rabbi Elazar has acted validly" belongs to the dispute where Rav and Shmuel permit a certain result and Rabbi Elazar differs. Once a later authority such as Rav Nachman conclusively establishes that the halakhah is like Rabbi Yose that they divide, there may be no remaining option to invoke Rabbi Elazar as an alternative rule. A person cannot say "I act as my own judge" according to a view that has been definitively rejected. Thus, in the ketubah context where Maharam understands the halakhah as established like Rav Nachman that division applies, seizure cannot be justified by the alternative opinion. This is conceptually sound. It is true that Rav Hai Gaon in Shaarei Shevuot rules like Rav Nachman and nevertheless recognizes the validity of acting like Rabbi Elazar, but it does not follow that Mordechai accepted that combination. If the alternative ruling is no longer legally available, seizure has no special force. There is another issue. If the creditor himself seized before death, I do not understand how, according to our rule above in simanim 82 and here that a court does not remove property from a creditor who seized but merely places him under a ban until he swears the heirs' oath, his death could undo the seizure. Upon death the personal ban ends. The principle that one cannot bequeath money collectible only through an oath ordinarily applies where the money has not yet been acquired. Here the money has already been seized and belongs to the creditor subject only to a personal sanction to swear. If he dies, the sanction dies with him and the property should remain with his heirs. Perhaps the authorities who reject seizure do not accept the Rosh's rule that only a ban is imposed, but instead compel the creditor to take the heirs' oath as a condition of retaining the property. Under our accepted rule, however, it appears clear that if the widow or creditor seized before death and the court would not have removed the property during life, the seizure remains effective after death. The genuine uncertainty concerns heirs who seize only afterward. One further distinction suggests itself. Where the debt instrument did not contain a clause subjecting movables and land and the heirs seize movables of the debtor after death, the movables were not subject to the debt by Talmudic law at all. Even seizure after death would originally have been reversed. The heirs can reach such movables only because of the Geonic enactment. The Geonim enacted ordinary collection where the debt itself is legally collectible, but there is no reason to assume that they also empowered heirs to combine that enactment with the exceptional assertion, "I follow Rabbi Elazar," contrary to the primary rule. Two leniencies should not be stacked. Thus, where movables are collectible only by Geonic enactment, posthumous seizure may be ineffective even if seizure could work in a case governed by the original law. The Ran's ruling can be understood as addressing the original law, such as seizure during the debtor's life or an instrument expressly subjecting movables and land, while Maharam may be addressing ordinary movables reachable only through the Geonic enactment. This would reduce the apparent dispute. I have not found this distinction stated by the authorities, so the matter still requires study.
