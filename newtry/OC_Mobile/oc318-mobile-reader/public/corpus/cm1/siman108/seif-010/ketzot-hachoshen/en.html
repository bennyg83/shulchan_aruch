Raavad rules that when a lender died during the borrower's lifetime, the lender's heirs had to take the heirs' oath before collecting. If one of those heirs died before taking the oath, the surviving brothers cannot collect the deceased brother's share, because perhaps their father instructed that brother about repayment while saying nothing to them. They can swear only concerning their own knowledge and therefore collect only their own shares. I suggested earlier that where a document is payable to whoever produces it, a holder need not derive his right through inheritance. One might therefore think that the rule, "A person does not bequeath a monetary claim that is subject to an oath," should not apply. The same thought arises among brothers, because one brother can often collect the entire document without an assignment from the others, and the brothers cannot necessarily waive the claim after dividing the documents. Nevertheless Raavad's law shows that the oath disability follows the monetary claim even where the present holder need not formally derive his collection right through the deceased. Thus the rule of noninheritance applies more broadly than the technical chain of title.
