He is obligated in an oath and cannot swear, etc. This principle has already been explained in many places. Just as the Torah gave two witnesses credibility to obligate the person against whom they testify according to their testimony, so too the Torah gave one witness credibility sufficient to require the opposing party to swear that the witness is false. One who cannot or will not swear against the witness must pay as though two witnesses testified. Here the witness testifies that he saw the defendant enter the other's premises without the owner's presence and remove a utensil. The defendant admits entering and taking it. He therefore cannot swear that the witness is lying. We no longer believe him to say, "I took my own property," or, "I collected it on account of a debt." His law is as though two witnesses saw him enter and remove the object, in which case he would have to return it. One cannot say that he should be believed that it was his own through a migo that he could have sworn against the witness and denied taking anything, or through a migo that he later returned it. Such a migo stands against a chazakah: once he admits taking an item from another's premises in the other's absence, the presumption is that what he took belonged to the owner. It is also a brazen migo against the witness. See the Perishah and Darkei Moshe.
He is obligated in an oath and cannot, etc., and therefore pays. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch write above in siman 90, seif 3, that if one witness testifies against him, "he swears that he did not rob and is exempt." That case differs because the witness did not see what he removed. Even if two witnesses saw him enter but did not see what he took, the owner can recover only with an oath, as explained there. In such a case we do not apply the rule that whenever two witnesses would impose money, one witness imposes an oath, followed by payment if the oath cannot be taken, because even two witnesses would not impose money without an oath by the claimant. Here, by contrast, the single witness saw what he removed. In a case of two witnesses who saw the object removed from the premises without the owner's presence, or where he entered to seize collateral, those two witnesses would impose direct monetary liability. Therefore here the rule applies that since he cannot swear against the single witness, he must pay. This is straightforward. See what I write further in seif 7.
