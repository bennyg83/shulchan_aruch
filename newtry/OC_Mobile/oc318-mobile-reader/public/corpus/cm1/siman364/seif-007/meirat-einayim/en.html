Because he is obligated to swear, etc. The reason is that the witness at least saw him remove utensils, even though the witness does not know how many. The defendant himself admits that he removed ten. Although the same mouth that admitted removal now says, "They were mine," that migo stands against the presumption that everything found in a person's house belongs to that householder. The Raavad disagrees, and the Tur cites him. Since the owner was absent when the items were taken and therefore cannot make a definite claim about what was taken, and the witness does not know how much was taken or whether the items belonged to the entrant, the defendant is not considered one obligated to swear who cannot swear. This differs from the Raavad's rule at the end of siman 75 that a person can make a claim based on one witness and thereby impose a Torah oath even though the claimant himself lacks personal knowledge. There the witness at least knows that the defendant owes the money. Here the witness knows only that he entered and removed items, but does not know whether they were his or how many there were. The Rambam may hold that there is no distinction, because the witness at least saw him enter and take property in the owner's absence, and the presumption is that what he takes from another's house without the owner present is not his.
