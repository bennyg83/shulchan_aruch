Into the hand of the lender's wife. See Sema, seif katan 12, who distinguishes a borrower. Shach in siman 72, seif katan 137, writes that the borrower is discharged only where the wife regularly conducts her husband's business. Where she fully manages his affairs, the borrower is certainly discharged even if he returns the object during the borrowing term. She is effectively authorized to acquire and manage property for him. By accepting return of the borrowed item she agrees that it reenters the husband's domain and, in exchange, releases the borrower from liability. One who regularly manages the husband's affairs can likewise receive repayment of a loan, as explained in siman 120. But if she does not manage his business and is merely someone to whom he ordinarily entrusts his own property, returning a borrowed object to her is only like transferring it to a regular depositary. The borrower remains liable for accidents. With an ordinary deposit, however, even such return is sufficient because the custodian has returned it to someone the depositor himself regularly trusts. Tumim says similarly. See there also regarding a paid custodian who returns to the depositor's wife and an unpaid custodian where the wife lacks means to pay.
He sent it through his servant. The wording of this seif implies that return is treated exactly like the original borrowing in every respect. If so, suppose the lender tells the borrower, "Strike it with a stick and let it come back, and you will be discharged." One might think the borrower remains liable, just as in seif 6 Shach says that even where the lender says, "Strike it and I will become liable to you," that statement does not help. Yet siman 120 shows that if a creditor says, "Throw my debt to me and be discharged," the discharge works according to all views. When he says merely, "Throw it to me in the manner of a get," even an express "and be discharged" may not be needed. Likewise, "send it to me through a deaf mute or minor" can discharge without the additional phrase, as siman 121 explains. This demonstrates a real difference between return and initial borrowing. For initial borrowing, sending through a legally incompetent person does not bring the object into the borrower's domain. Ran therefore needed agency or guarantor law to explain liability even through an ordinary messenger. For return, by contrast, the question is whether the owner has consented to receive the object through the indicated channel. Once the owner directs the borrower to return it in a way that cannot reasonably be understood as requiring the borrower to continue accompanying and guarding it, the return can be effective without a new acquisition by the owner. Sema in siman 120, seif katan 1, writes similarly concerning "throw my debt to me": although sending through a deaf mute can discharge, the sea case differs because one might have understood the creditor's intent as requiring the debtor to accompany and guard it. Where that interpretation is impossible, no express phrase of discharge is necessary. Accordingly, if the lender says, "Strike it with a stick and it will come," implying that it should proceed on its own, an express "and be discharged" should not be necessary. This makes Mechaber's source difficult. Why does he rule that "send it to me through your servant" fails upon return? It is possible that the Gemara's question, "the servant's hand is like his master's hand," applies only to the initial borrowing. There we require the item to leave the lender's domain and enter the borrower's domain. The servant's receipt does not accomplish that. On return, however, the object need not leave the borrower's physical domain in the same manner once the lender has accepted a method of return. There is proof from sending repayment through a deaf mute or minor: even if the money is still physically within the debtor's premises when such a person takes it, the debtor can be discharged. Their hands are no better than that of a monkey, yet the law does not distinguish. Likewise, if the creditor says, "Here are your money, bundled," the dispute in siman 120 concerns whether involuntary repayment counts as repayment. Ran implies that if the creditor explicitly accepts the money as repayment and agrees that it remain with the debtor as a deposit, everyone agrees the loan has been repaid, even though the money never physically left the debtor's possession. Siman 293, seif 3, likewise says that even if the owner appoints the depositary himself as his agent, the depositary can be discharged from the original obligation. It therefore appears that Mechaber follows Mordechai's broader principle, cited by Rama in siman 344, that once a borrower has bound himself by a custodial kinyan, he cannot remove that liability by mere words. A new kinyan is needed. Loan repayment differs because an express discharge can work by words alone. Mordechai's position requires considerable analysis and will be discussed in its place, with Hashem's help. Sema's words in siman 293 must likewise be understood as distinguishing an ordinary deposit from a borrowing.
