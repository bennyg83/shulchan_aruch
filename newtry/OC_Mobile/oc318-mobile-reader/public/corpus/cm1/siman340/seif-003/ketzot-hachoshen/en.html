To travel by a specified route. At the beginning of chapter HaShoel the baraita derives the borrower's liability for theft and loss by an a fortiori argument from a paid bailee. One might ask that the argument can be refuted because a paid bailee can become liable for double payment when he falsely claims an armed robber took the deposit. Yet the Gemara says that certain principal payment without an oath is more severe than the possibility of double payment that depends upon an oath. What, however, is the proposed refutation? A borrower too can sometimes become liable for double payment if he falsely claims that an armed robber took the object on the specified road for which it was borrowed, since an armed robber has the status of a thief. Nevertheless, because double payment is far more common with a paid bailee, whereas with a borrower it occurs only where he borrowed the object for travel on a specified road, this can still be regarded as a relevant distinction. See Tosafos at the end of chapter HaMafkid. This also resolves the textual reading at the beginning of Bava Kamma, 4b. The Gemara asks why Rabbi Oshaya does not list thief and robber separately, including double, fourfold, and fivefold payments, and answers that he already listed them through the unpaid bailee and borrower. Rashi explains that an unpaid bailee can encompass both a thief and robber: if he falsely claims theft he becomes like a thief, and if he falsely claims robbery he becomes like a robber. Rashi rejects the reading 'borrower,' because a borrower cannot exempt himself through a claim of theft. Tosafos write similarly. According to what I have written, however, a borrower can indeed encompass thief and robber where he borrowed the object for a specified route. If he claims that an armed robber attacked him on that route, it is a claim of theft; if he claims ordinary robbers attacked him, it is a claim of robbery. In such circumstances those claims exempt him because the loss occurred through the use for which it was borrowed.
He did not notice immediately. This ruling is from the Rosh: since the borrower does not know whether the animal died because of its work or for another reason, he must pay. Beit Yosef asks why he should be obligated because of a doubt and therefore omitted this ruling from Shulchan Aruch. The Shach answers that the borrower is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, because he cannot take a definite oath, and Terumat HaDeshen, siman 333, similarly writes that a bailee who says, 'I do not know whether the loss resulted from negligence,' is obligated to pay because he is obligated to swear but cannot swear. Nimukei Yosef, however, does not appear to hold this way. At the end of Bava Kamma he discusses when a bailee's obligation begins, at meshichah or at negligence, and cites Ramah: the practical difference is a borrower who borrows a donkey for his agent to ride and it dies on the road, and he does not know whether it died because of the work. Since he does not become obligated until the time of the accident, he is exempt, because his claim is 'I do not know whether I ever became obligated.' But if he borrowed a donkey for himself to ride and then gave it to his agent, and there is a doubt whether it died because of the work, he is liable, because a borrower has no right to lend the animal to another. His liability therefore began at the time of that negligence, making his claim analogous to 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' which is liable. This shows that Ramah does not apply the rule that a bailee who says 'I do not know' is automatically one obligated to swear who cannot swear. Otherwise even one who borrowed it for his agent to ride would be liable through that rule. Beit Yosef, siman 291, cites the students of Rashba regarding a paid bailee who entrusted the object to his wife, children, or someone to whom the owner regularly entrusts property. Ramban holds that if the second bailee, who is unpaid, swears that he was not negligent and claims it was stolen or lost, the first bailee remains liable; he cannot say perhaps it died through an unavoidable accident and I am exempt, because it is upon him to establish his exemption. The same applies if the second bailee refuses to swear or traveled overseas. Rabbi Yom Tov ben Rabbi Shmuel agrees. Another opinion, however, holds that if the paid bailee delivered the property to an unpaid bailee and the second refuses to swear, the first is exempt, even if the owner makes a definite claim, because the first says he does not know and his case is like one who is told, 'You owe me a maneh,' and answers, 'I do not know whether I became obligated.' The Shach in siman 291 discusses this at length and asks against this latter view: we expressly rule that even an unpaid bailee who transfers property to a paid bailee is liable because the owner may say, 'You are believed by me with an oath; the other is not believed by me.' Thus even if the second wishes to swear, the first remains liable, all the more so if the second refuses to swear. The Shach concludes that a bailee who says 'I do not know' is like one who says, 'I do not know whether I returned it.' Once he became a bailee, his uncertainty is not whether he ever became obligated. Even according to those who hold that a bailee's property becomes encumbered only from the time of negligence, it is still treated as 'I do not know whether I returned it.' He proves this from Tosafos in chapter HaShoel, 97a, regarding the Mishnah of one who borrowed one cow and another was rented. Tosafos say that 'I do not know whether you lent to me' is exempt but 'I do not know whether I returned it' is liable. Although there the owner gave him two cows, once he returns one, the remaining uncertainty is like 'I do not know whether you ever lent me the other.' The Shach infers that if he did not return either one and simply said he did not know, the case would be 'I do not know whether I returned.' Yam Shel Shlomo in chapter HaGozel cites Nimukei Yosef and questions him similarly. He ultimately rules that even if the lender also says 'perhaps,' the borrower is liable because he cannot swear with certainty. Elsewhere Yam Shel Shlomo treats the case as 'I do not know whether I repaid,' requiring a definite claim by the lender even according to those who hold that a bailee becomes obligated only at negligence.
In my opinion, Ramah's position is correct from every angle. According to the view that a bailee becomes obligated only at negligence, while the object remains intact there is no monetary obligation at all. Yam Shel Shlomo's assertion that the bailee is obligated to return the animal is incorrect. A bailee has no independent mitzvah or monetary obligation of return while the object remains intact. A thief and robber are commanded to return what they took, but a bailee is not. The object remains in the owner's domain wherever it is, and the owner retrieves his own property. As I wrote in siman 86, seif katan 4, and siman 198, seif katan 8, only when the object was lost through negligence does the Torah say 'he shall bring a witness,' meaning that he must bring evidence before beit din concerning what happened. If it was lost through an unavoidable accident, there is no general duty of return, all the more so while the object remains intact. Even according to the version that a bailee becomes obligated from meshichah, Ritva treats the uncertainty as 'I do not know whether I ever became obligated,' cited in Shitah Mekubetzet to chapter HaShoel, 99a. Ritva explains that even according to the opinion that a borrower's liability begins at meshichah, this does not mean a complete immediate monetary liability. So long as the cow remains alive, it remains entirely in the owner's domain and the bailee owes nothing. Rather, if it is later stolen or suffers an accident, his property becomes retroactively encumbered from the time of meshichah, with practical consequences for property sold in the interim. Some Geonim erred on this point, as Ritva states. Thus while the object is intact there is no existing debt and not even an independent obligation of return upon a bailee.
This also answers Yam Shel Shlomo's second contention. Ramah holds that even according to the version that liability begins at the accident, once the borrower gives the animal to another person, that act is at least the beginning of negligence, because a borrower may not lend another's property. Although if it later dies through a true unavoidable accident the borrower may ultimately be exempt, since the transfer to another is not itself necessarily full negligence after the fact, it nevertheless begins a state of fault and creates potential liability. A later unavoidable accident then functions as a discharge from that potential liability, just as according to the version that liability begins with meshichah, an unavoidable accident can discharge the bailee. Thus if he borrowed it for himself and transferred it to his agent, the act of unauthorized transfer creates the initial liability, and when he later says he does not know whether the death was because of the work, he is saying, 'I do not know whether I was discharged.' By contrast, if he borrowed it from the outset for his agent to ride, there was no initial improper transfer and the uncertainty is whether liability ever arose. Ramban's words in Shitah Mekubetzet to Ketubot likewise show that when the father failed before death to instruct his heirs that the cow was borrowed property, this is treated as the negligence from which later liability developed. Nimukei Yosef's language similarly says that the heirs' liability after slaughter arises from the father's negligence, because from the time of negligence that causes the eventual accident, the bailee's liability begins. The same principle applies when a bailee transfers the object to one to whom he was not entitled to transfer it: that act is at least a partial negligence and brings potential liability into existence. A later unavoidable accident can then operate as a return or discharge.
This also explains the opinion among the students of Rashba that a first bailee who transfers the object to someone whom the owner regularly trusts is exempt if the second refuses to swear. The Shach asks that ordinarily a bailee who transfers to another bailee is liable even if the second is willing to swear, because the owner says, 'You are believed by me with an oath; the second is not.' But the ordinary transfer itself is an improper act and therefore creates initial liability. If later there is uncertainty whether the ultimate loss was unavoidable, it is like 'I do not know whether I was discharged.' When, however, the object was transferred to someone whom the owner regularly uses and to whom the first bailee was permitted to transfer it initially, there is no negligence at all in the transfer. The first bailee's uncertainty is therefore whether any liability ever arose, and he is exempt even if the second refuses to swear. This is precisely analogous to Ramah's borrower who borrowed the donkey specifically for his agent to ride.
The same reasoning explains the ruling concerning a shepherd who leaves his flock and we assess whether he could have saved it. If it is not known whether he could have saved it, he is liable. The shepherd was at least somewhat negligent in leaving the flock, so initial potential liability arose. The later uncertainty whether the ultimate event was unavoidable is then an uncertainty whether he was discharged. Moreover, where an event begins with negligence and the eventual loss could have come from that negligence, he is liable unless it is clearly established that the unavoidable accident was unrelated. This is the principle of an event that began with negligence, as in the case of the willow hut. Thus the shepherd remains liable unless it is affirmatively shown that the loss was not caused by the negligence.
Yam Shel Shlomo further argues that if the lender himself also says 'perhaps,' both parties are uncertain and the borrower should be exempt even under the rule of 'I do not know whether I repaid.' Ramah can hold, however, that only in an ordinary loan, where the lender should know whether he was repaid, does the lender's uncertainty defeat recovery. Where the claimant had no reason to know the event, even a claimant who says 'perhaps' can recover from a defendant whose uncertainty is one of repayment, as discussed by Mishneh LaMelech and cited in siman 75. The Shach's proof from Tosafos in chapter HaShoel is also not decisive. Tosafos expressly continue that even with one cow, when he does not know whether it died while borrowed, the case is like 'I do not know whether you lent it to me.' Tosafos therefore explicitly treat even one animal in this way, consistent either with the view that a bailee becomes liable only from negligence or with Ritva's explanation that even under the meshichah view the earlier obligation is only conditional.
One point nevertheless still requires explanation in Ramah. Even if under his analysis the borrower who borrowed for his agent says 'I do not know whether I ever became obligated,' why is he not liable as one obligated to swear who cannot swear, as Terumat HaDeshen rules regarding a bailee who does not know whether the loss was negligent? It appears that Ramah holds that the essential oath of bailees is the oath that the object is no longer in his possession, as Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 4 of Hilchot She'eilah and as ruled in siman 294. If the borrower recognizes the dead animal and the only uncertainty is whether the death resulted from negligence or from the work, that issue is only a matter added through gilgul. He therefore holds that the rule 'one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay' does not apply to a matter added only through gilgul. Certainly, where the principal oath itself is imposed on the basis of uncertainty, one does not create liability through inability to answer the gilgul. Beit Yosef's difficulty against the Rosh can be understood similarly. The Rosh, by contrast, follows his view that the oath 'I was not negligent' is itself one of the primary oaths of bailees, and therefore if the bailee cannot swear it with certainty he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear and must pay. There is also an additional reason not to impose payment merely because the bailee says 'I do not know.' Rashi in chapter HaShoel, 98a, explains that according to Rami bar Chama, the four bailees require not merely an admission but an additional denial, such as 'it never happened' or 'I returned it.' According to Rashi, 'I do not know' is not itself a denial. The rule of partial admission with 'fifty I know and fifty I do not know' creates liability because the admission obligates an oath on the remainder, and the uncertainty then makes him unable to swear. Likewise, with one witness, the witness itself creates the oath. But the special oath of bailees, where there is no independent admission, may not arise at all when the bailee says only 'I do not know,' since uncertainty is not a denial. Tosafos disagree and treat 'I do not know' as a denial, which would make the rule of inability to swear more broadly applicable. Beit Yosef may follow Rashi's approach and therefore refuse to impose payment merely because of the doubt.
See also Rif and Rambam, chapter 3 of Hilchot Sechirut, regarding an armed shepherd confronted by armed robbers, where the unresolved Talmudic question is ruled leniently. At first glance this is difficult according to Terumat HaDeshen, because every bailee must swear definitively that he was exempt, and here the legal status itself is doubtful. The answer is that even Terumat HaDeshen speaks only of factual uncertainty, where the bailee cannot say what actually happened, whether negligence or an unavoidable accident. But where the facts are fully known and only the legal classification is doubtful, he can swear to the facts with certainty, and because of the legal doubt we do not extract money. One might still object based on Maharanach's position that where the uncertainty concerns only a possible discharge from an otherwise existing debt, one cannot plead 'kim li' and the case is like 'I do not know whether I repaid.' However, Kesef Mishneh writes that according to his version of the Gemara the question concerning an armed shepherd against armed robbers was actually resolved in favor of exemption. The difficulty therefore falls away.
