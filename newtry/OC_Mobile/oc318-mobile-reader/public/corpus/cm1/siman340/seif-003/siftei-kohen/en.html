Some disagree, etc. This appears to me the principal view, as the Ramban writes. He asks why an animal that dies because of the work should generate exemption, since a borrower is liable even when it dies naturally, although the Angel of Death caused it. Why should he be exempt where his own use caused the death? The answer is that a borrower is liable for accidents, but not for the lender's negligence. When the lender gave an animal for work it could not endure, the lender was negligent. The Rashba and Rivash agree. Accordingly, where the lender was not negligent, the borrower is liable. This differs from the cat killed by mice, because the cat was lent for confronting mice and the lender assumed the risk inherent in that very task.
And the mice ate it, etc. Terumat HaDeshen writes that if weapons were sent for battle and the townspeople were defeated and the enemy seized the weapons, this is considered loss through the borrowed use and the borrower is exempt. He compares it to the cat killed by mice. I do not accept the comparison. The cat itself performed the borrowed task of killing mice and was killed while attempting it, so the lender was negligent in lending it for a task it could not withstand. Weapons do not themselves wage war; the combatants use them. Their capture results from the combatants' defeat, not from an incapacity in the weapons. The loss is therefore not automatically classified as death through the work. The borrower should be liable unless the parties' agreement or the known circumstances show that the lender accepted the risk of capture.
He cannot swear, etc. He is therefore liable. The Mechaber omitted this because in Bedek HaBayit he questioned the Rosh and Tur: why should uncertainty itself create liability? On the contrary, if the animal died on the road one might presume it died because of the work, and the borrower need swear only that it died on the road. This objection is not compelling. Every custodian must swear to facts he knows with certainty. If he does not know, he remains liable by Torah law, and a Torah oath cannot be taken on a claim of ignorance. Nor is there a reliable presumption that death on the road resulted from the work, especially where the borrower noticed no exhaustion or strain. Rabbeinu Yerucham and Terumat HaDeshen likewise follow the Rosh. The ruling of the Rosh and Tur is therefore correct.
