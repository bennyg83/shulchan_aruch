And the mice ate it. See Shach, seif katan 6, who challenges Terumat HaDeshen regarding one who borrowed weapons in order to fight enemies and the weapons were seized. Shach holds that the borrower is liable. In my view, if the attackers came in a matter of danger to life, the borrower is exempt. This is no worse than one who borrowed a sacred book to perform a mitzvah, where the mitzvah use can exempt even if he used it without the owner's knowledge and an accident occurred. It is also no worse than one who pursues a pursuer in order to save a victim and breaks utensils, whether belonging to the pursuer or to others, who can be exempt because the act includes rescue of others. The victim himself, who acts only to save himself, may remain liable in a comparable case. Here, however, the borrower is also engaged in saving others. The fact that he is simultaneously saving himself does not diminish the exemption. Terumat HaDeshen's case concerns attackers who came for monetary purposes, not danger to life. The same appears true if one takes another's utensils to save property from a fire and an unavoidable loss occurs during the rescue. A fire can be treated as danger to life, since Shabbat may be violated in the relevant circumstances because of that danger.
He cannot swear. See Shach, seif katan 7, who explains the liability on the ground that the defendant cannot swear. At first glance this is difficult. There is hardly a clearer case of something a person could never be expected to know. No human being can know this fact with certainty. In my view the reason is different. Since the fact is inherently unknowable to everyone, the case has the character of derara d'mamona: an objective monetary uncertainty exists before beit din independently of the parties' claims. Tumim, in the rules of seizure, seif katan 43, distinguishes where the doubt concerns payment. For example, if a worker ate from another vine and there is uncertainty whether the value should be deducted from his wages, the employer cannot make the deduction merely from doubt because the uncertainty concerns repayment. That rule implies that although where both parties say "I do not know whether I paid" the defendant can ordinarily be exempt, an objective derara d'mamona can make a payment doubt more stringent. Here too, because the doubt exists for beit din independently of the litigants' assertions, the borrower is not discharged merely because repayment is uncertain. I later found that Maharchash raises this very question and answers that here the matter could have been known had the borrower been sufficiently precise in observing the facts.
