And they ate it. This is the first version in the Gemara, chapter HaShoel. The Tur writes in the name of the Rosh that even if the cat ate many mice, became overheated and died, the borrower is exempt because this is regarded as death resulting from the work. Although the death was not literally caused by the assigned task, since the cat was lent to chase the mice and not to eat them, nevertheless, because it died as a result of eating them, this too is called death resulting from the work. This appears in the alternative version of the Gemara there. The Rama should have written this, and his ruling would then have been known all the more so. Perhaps he rules in accordance with the first version. However, Terumat HaDeshen, siman 328, implies that where the cat ate many mice, this is more clearly death resulting from the work than where the mice gathered against it and killed it. One can therefore say that the Rama selected the latter case as the greater novelty. The same applies if one borrowed weapons to fight enemies and the townspeople fell into the enemies' hands, who then took the weapons. This is considered loss resulting from the work and the borrower is exempt, as Terumat HaDeshen writes there and the Darkei Moshe cites at the beginning of this siman. These are the words of the Sema. The Shach writes that Terumat HaDeshen proves this rule from the case where the mice gathered against the cat and killed it. In my view the proof is not compelling. There the cat's death is properly considered to have resulted from its work, because the mice gathered against it and killed it as a result of its attempt to perform its own task of killing them; thus the lender was negligent in providing it for that work. Weapons, however, do not perform the battle themselves. The combatant does the fighting, and the weapons are lost because he failed to win and the enemy consequently seized them. The lender was not negligent, so as to the weapons this is an ordinary unavoidable accident and the borrower should be liable. It appears to me that Terumat HaDeshen follows the Mechaber's view here, but according to the dissenting opinion the borrower is liable. This is the primary ruling. Shach.
