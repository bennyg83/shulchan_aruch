At first glance this is difficult. Fruit-rights are transferable. A person can sell a field or animal for its fruits. If someone damages the object so that this right can no longer be sold, that is a real monetary loss. Certainly, if a Jew rents a house or animal from a non Jew for two years for one hundred gold coins, and another person burns the house or kills the animal, the wrongdoer cannot escape merely because the Jew had only use-rights and there is no category of unemployment payment for an animal. The reason for exemption, according to the authorities who exempt, appears different. Siman 420 shows that damages in the technical sense are assessed where the victim suffers a lasting bodily diminution, such as loss of a limb or even a scar in the skin. If there is no lasting bodily deficiency and the object will return completely to its former state, the injured person receives only the other categories applicable to human injury. Since an ox pays only the category of direct damage, not the additional human-injury payments, a temporary impairment without lasting bodily deficiency can be exempt. This resembles one who strikes another's coin with a hammer but does not physically diminish it. Even though its sale price drops, he can be exempt. If he actually diminishes the coin, he pays the entire loss, as Tosafot in Bava Kamma 96 explain. Therefore, if one burns a rented house or kills a rented animal, he has caused a bodily diminution and must compensate the tenant for the value of the lost use-right. But if the object will fully recover and no bodily diminution remains, the technical category of direct damage can be absent under these authorities. Accordingly, if through the custodian's negligence an animal suffers an injury that leaves a permanent scar even after recovery, he must pay the full reduction in the animal's market value, including the value of its fruit-rights. That lost value is certainly monetary damage; the issue is only that the technical payment of damages can require a bodily deficiency. Shach, seif katan 4, rules that even this remains a safek din. In my view the dissenting authorities are persuasive. Human injury derives from the verse "hand for hand," where a bodily deficiency is explicit. Damage to an animal derives from "one who strikes an animal shall pay for it," which can imply liability for the striking itself even without permanent deficiency. If the animal is injured so that medical treatment is required for it ever to return to normal, it appears everyone agrees that the custodian or damager must pay the treatment costs. Until treatment occurs the animal will not return to its prior state, and the condition is equivalent to an enduring deterioration. Mordechai in HaGozel Kama writes expressly that one need not pay medical treatment for an animal because damage to another fund is indirect, citing one who throws another's coin into the sea and is exempt from the cost of retrieving it. In my view Mordechai means specifically where the animal would recover by itself and treatment merely hastens the recovery. A person injured in that way may receive medical expenses, but an animal does not. Where the animal will not recover on its own and only a physician can restore it, the damager must pay the physician. Niddah 58 provides a clear proof regarding liability to wash a stain. There too the monetary expenditure is external, yet payment is required because the item cannot be restored without it. Likewise, if a person dismantles another's vessel or building and a craftsman can restore it, surely he cannot say he owes nothing for the craftsman's cost. Every direct damager must restore the injured object to its prior condition where the injury is recognizable, even if the object has not undergone a complete legal change. A robber differs where the stolen object suffers damage not caused by the robber and has not undergone a legal change. He can sometimes say, "Here is yours before you." A direct damager cannot. This explains treatment costs and stain-cleaning. If the object can return to normal without treatment and the owner chooses treatment only to accelerate recovery, Mordechai treats the extra expenditure as damage to another fund and exempts. Likewise, one who throws another's coin into clear water is exempt from retrieval expenses because the coin itself has suffered no bodily damage. Even a robber whose return obligation remains can sometimes avoid paying the owner's large retrieval costs so long as he has not sworn, as Bava Kamma 103 indicates. One may also say that a damager of an animal must pay the reduction in its market value caused by its temporary inability to work. Tosafot in Gittin 43, who invoke the rule that there is no unemployment payment for an animal, mean that one does not calculate each day's lost labor separately as with a human being. Rather, one evaluates the reduction in the animal's overall sale value because of the temporary impairment. This resembles Rashi's explanation in Bava Kamma at the beginning of HaKones, where damage to crops is assessed as part of the entire field rather than as the isolated value of each stalk. A buyer does not discount the whole animal by the full sum of every lost workday. Still, whatever reduction in overall market value occurs because of the temporary inability to work is part of the damage. For a human being, by contrast, loss of work is paid day by day as a separate category. There is support from siman 420, seif 3, where Rama writes that extra food required because of the injury is included in medical treatment. At first glance Gittin 12, concerning one who cuts off his slave's hand, says the slave receives additional subsistence needs through charity. But the Gemara there asks that medical payment belongs to the slave because he needs it for treatment. If extra food is legally part of treatment, why is it assigned differently? The answer is that ordinary increased food is not itself medical treatment. Rama refers to food prescribed as part of the healing process, which is a true treatment expense. The same distinction supports the rule stated here regarding an animal.

Shach disagrees and says there is no such distinction. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that where the damage occurred on its own, a borrower is certainly no worse than a robber, since even a robber can say, "Here is yours before you," and Ritva disagrees with Tosafot in Bava Kamma 56, who say that a custodian can be worse than a robber. In my view Ritva's words are difficult. A custodian can certainly be worse than a robber. According to the view that change does not transfer ownership and even after a change a robber can say, "Here is yours before you," a custodian is nevertheless liable for a damaged entrusted object. As I explained in siman 363, only with damage that is not externally recognizable can a custodian sometimes say, "Here is yours before you," because damage that happened by itself is no stronger than damage done directly where the law does not recognize it. But where the damage is recognizable, even if there was no formal legal change, a custodian is certainly liable. There is proof from Niddah 58 concerning a borrowed garment stained while in the borrower's hands. The borrower must pay to wash the stain. A robber, by contrast, would not necessarily have such a duty where there was no change of name, because a legal change exists only where the object's designation changes as a result, as explained in siman 360, seif 6, in the gloss.
