The flesh became weakened. Tur says that if the animal weakened on its own with a reversible weakness, the borrower is exempt even if the weakness was not caused by the work. Sema distinguishes between direct damage by the borrower's act and weakening that occurred by itself. The Shach objects that a borrower is liable for accidents occurring on their own, so why should reversible weakening be different? The distinction is sound. Reversible weakening does not constitute a legal change. Even a thief or robber can return the animal and say, "Here is yours before you." A custodian should not be worse where the weakness arose by itself. But if a person directly causes the weakening, that can be actionable damage even though the animal will later recover. Shitah Mekubetzet states this expressly: the question of weakening caused by the work must be understood as direct weakening attributable to the borrower's use, because otherwise a robber could simply return the animal. This supports Sema. Tur further says that if the animal later dies and is no longer extant, the borrower pays according to its value at the time of the original injury. The reason is not that the earlier reversible weakness necessarily began the later death. Rather, while the animal was alive the borrower could say, "Here is yours before you." Once it no longer exists, that defense disappears and liability for the earlier actionable injury becomes collectible according to the value at that time. This creates some tension with the Shulchan Aruch's other ruling that reversible weakening is merely analogous to lost labor and is not actionable even if caused directly. If so, later death should not revive liability for the earlier weakness. The matter requires study.
