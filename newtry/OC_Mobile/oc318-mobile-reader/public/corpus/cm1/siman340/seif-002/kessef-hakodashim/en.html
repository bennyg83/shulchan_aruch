If jointly owned grain is stored in sacks belonging to one partner and the sacks deteriorate through prolonged use, perhaps the loss resembles an item that died because of the work for which it was borrowed, even though it might have been possible to shake out or spread the sacks and prevent the damage. The deterioration nevertheless resulted from their use in the partnership. If this is not considered death through work, or if the sacks were stolen, and the grain could not have been saved without using those sacks, the expense or loss may be treated like the cost of hiring shepherds and sticks to save lost property: although one partner was obligated to act to save the property, the cost of doing so is borne by the owner of the property. If, however, the partner could have saved the sacks and negligently failed to do so, perhaps the other partner who did not know of the danger is not liable for that failure, because the duty to rescue the partnership property did not eliminate the duty to protect the sacks themselves. This requires careful study. In another case, a Jewish guest staying at an inn with a non-Jew said to the innkeeper, 'Give the non-Jew a pillow to sleep on,' or perhaps phrased it as a borrowing request, or asked that it be given to him so he could lend it to the non-Jew. The non-Jew stole the pillow. I arranged a compromise because this case is stronger than the case of one who regularly entrusts deposits to another. The Shach in siman 72 writes that there is no distinction there between an unpaid custodian and a borrower, while Urim VeTumim offers a strong reason to distinguish: if the person to whom one usually entrusts property steals it, that is no better than theft occurring in his possession, for which a paid custodian would be liable, though an unpaid custodian would not. Even if that reasoning is uncertain, one cannot extract money where there is a compound doubt. There is also a further issue: when an item was borrowed, the Jewish guest who received it may have had a duty to see that it was returned directly to the owner, and delivery to the non-Jew may be weaker than return to a member of the owner's household. The matter therefore required compromise.
