From the time he performed meshichah. One should consider a borrower who did not perform meshichah and there was no removal of the lender's guarding, but who nevertheless used the object. For example, he borrowed a bench to sit upon in the lender's courtyard while remaining beside the lender, or borrowed a book and studied from it without lifting or drawing it, and an unavoidable accident then occurred while it was in his hand. Does he become liable? In my view he certainly does. Bava Metzia 99 records Rav Huna's ruling that one who borrows an axe from another acquires it for liability when he chops with it. Shitah Mekubetzes explains that although meshichah creates liability for unavoidable accidents even according to the view that meshichah was not formally instituted for custodians, the borrower's receipt of benefit itself is comparable to money, and through that benefit he accepts responsibility. Accordingly, under Rav Huna actual use creates liability even without meshichah, because the benefit of use is equivalent to receipt of money. Those who disagree with Rav Huna and hold that meshichah was instituted for custodians certainly hold that meshichah is effective, but there is no basis to say they deny that actual use is also effective. We find no express dispute on this point and should not multiply disagreements without necessity. Moreover, no authority states that a borrower who has already used the item can nevertheless remain exempt from unavoidable accidents merely because the use took place in the lender's premises. It is therefore clear that actual use creates liability for unavoidable accidents according to all opinions.
And he shall take the custodians' oath that it died because of the work. Ketzos HaChoshen questions this. Since the Mechaber holds that the primary oath is that the item is not presently in the borrower's possession, why require this oath when the object's death is before us and that oath is contradicted by the visible facts? According to what I wrote in siman 294, that the oath that the custodian was not negligent at the time of the loss is a primary oath according to all opinions, there is no difficulty. Even aside from that, the question is not compelling. The authorities who describe the oath against negligence as only an appended oath do so because we do not ordinarily presume people to be negligent. A borrower, however, is liable for unavoidable accidents and the relevant uncertainty is whether the death resulted from the work. In such a case the oath that it died because of the work is certainly the primary oath.
