He struck it with a stick. See Shach, seif katan 10, who writes that according to the view requiring meshichah for custodians the borrower is exempt where the lender merely strikes the animal and sends it. Shach overlooked Tosafot, who establish the case in a side alley where the movement itself can constitute meshichah. Moreover, according to Ran's explanation that liability for "send it to me" rests on guarantor law, the same reasoning can apply when the lender strikes the animal with a stick, according to the view that one who says "throw one hundred into the sea and I will owe you" becomes liable. Nimukei Yosef, however, distinguishes the cases because the lender can still recover the animal after striking it, unlike money thrown into the sea. Shach also asks that according to Rosh, who holds that withdrawal of the owner's guarding can be sufficient, liability should certainly arise here. On that basis he questions Rama. In my view, even Rosh's position applies only where the object reaches a place in which the new custodian can actually guard it. Then the owner's withdrawal from guarding is equivalent to the new person's acceptance of custody. When the lender merely strikes the animal and sends it while the borrower is not yet able to guard it, there is no true acceptance of custody. At most the borrower incurs a bare guarantor-like undertaking. Ran indeed treats "send it to me" and striking with a stick as forms of guarantor liability. Striking with a stick resembles "throw one hundred into the sea." Since our halachah in Even HaEzer siman 30 does not impose guarantor liability for "throw one hundred into the sea," all the more so striking an animal with a stick does not create such liability. Nimukei Yosef himself says that even according to those who impose liability for the sea case, striking the animal can be exempt because it remains retrievable. Rama therefore correctly rules for exemption.
