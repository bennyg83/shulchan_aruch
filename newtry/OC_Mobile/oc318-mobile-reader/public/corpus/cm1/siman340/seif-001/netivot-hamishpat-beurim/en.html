Because it would have burned in any event. At first glance this is difficult. A borrower is liable even when an animal dies naturally, although one could say that wherever it had been, death would have occurred. Likewise, if one borrowed an object to use in the owner's house and an accident occurred there, he is liable. Had Torah not excluded land, a borrower would likewise have been liable for land even though land remains fixed in one place and the same accident would have occurred regardless. One cannot answer that in the pledge case the object serves both borrower and lender, so the borrower lacks all benefit. He still becomes borrower at least of the portion serving him, as where one borrows a bench to sit upon together with the lender. It therefore appears that Mordechai's principal reason is that the borrower did not draw the object and made no kinyan. He added the second reason, that it would have burned in any event, for authorities who hold that custodial liability can begin merely when the owner withdraws his own guarding and no formal kinyan is needed. On that view there was no meaningful withdrawal of guarding, because the pledge was already exposed to the same danger. Alternatively, Mordechai may have been answering a claim based on benefit: one person benefited while the other suffered loss. He therefore says there was no loss to the lender because the object would have burned anyway. This explains Shach's objection to Sema in the case of borrowing a house. Once the borrower made a valid kinyan in the house and entered the borrowing relationship, he is liable for accidents even where the same accident would otherwise have occurred. The two reasons appear to yield major practical differences. Suppose Reuven's object was deposited with a non Jew, and Reuven then lent Shimon the right to borrow money from that non Jew against the object. If the exemption rests on lack of meshichah, Shimon is exempt if the object burns. If the reason is that it would have burned anyway, Shimon can be liable where Reuven could say, "Had you not borrowed against it, I would already have removed it from the non Jew." The same distinction exists where the object was already a pledge in the non Jew's possession and it burned after Reuven had paid his own debt. Under the meshichah rationale Shimon remains exempt. Under the other rationale he can be liable because the lender could otherwise have recovered the pledge. According to authorities who hold that custodial liability requires only the owner's withdrawal from guarding, Shimon is certainly liable in cases where Reuven had the power to remove the pledge but could not do so because of Shimon's loan. Reuven's guarding ended and the pledge came under Shimon's effective custodial responsibility. Yet Mordechai implies no difference between the two rationales. I therefore believe, as I write below, that actual commencement of use creates accident liability according to everyone even without meshichah or an express withdrawal of guarding. Accordingly, when Reuven could have redeemed the pledge and authorized Shimon to borrow against it in a way that prevents Reuven from removing it, Shimon has actually used the pledge. A new legal status has been created in the object: the non Jew now holds it by virtue of Shimon's debt and may retain it until that debt is paid. There is no greater use than this, and Shimon becomes liable for accidents. If, however, the pledge was already in the non Jew's hands in a manner that Reuven could not remove it regardless, Shimon has not changed anything in the object itself. He has merely received permission to obtain credit against an already detained pledge. In that setting the borrowing relationship over the pledge itself may never begin. Even when the pledge was already in the non Jew's hands, Shimon is exempt only if the arrangement required him to repay his loan immediately when Reuven paid his own debt, so that Shimon had no right to prolong the non Jew's possession. Then his use creates no independent continuing restraint upon the pledge, and one can also say it would have burned anyway. But if Shimon fixed a term, for example a six-month loan against the pledge, so that even if Reuven pays his own debt during those six months Reuven still cannot recover the object, Shimon has used the body of the pledge by subjecting it to a new encumbrance. He is then liable for accidents, like one who actually chopped with a borrowed axe. Even then he is not necessarily liable for the full value of the pledge if it burns before Reuven pays his own debt. He pays proportionally according to his loan relative to Reuven's loan. If it burns after Reuven has fully discharged his own debt, Shimon can become liable for the whole remaining value. Where the pledge is held by a Jew rather than a non Jew, the rule is stronger. If the Jewish lender holds a pledge for one hundred and permits another person to borrow an additional one hundred against it, the second borrower is liable in proportion to his loan. A Jew acquires a Jewish pledge to the extent of the debt for certain proprietary purposes, including kiddushin. Thus the original lender effectively allows the second borrower to acquire the corresponding share of the pledge and then transfer it to the lender as security. Although the same lender both conveys the interest to the borrower and immediately reacquires it as pledge-holder, simultaneous or successive acquisitions of this kind are found elsewhere. Tur, Even HaEzer siman 120, treats a get written on the scribe's paper in a similar way: the wife acquires the paper for the husband from the scribe, and then acquires it from the husband for divorce. The same conceptual mechanism applies here.
If it died through the work, he is exempt. Ketzot HaChoshen cites a dispute whether a borrower can obligate himself, without a kinyan, to pay even for death through work. He connects this to the two answers of Tosafot at the end of HaZahav concerning an unpaid custodian who stipulates to have borrower liability without a kinyan, whereas slaves and documents require a kinyan. According to Tosafot's second answer, the unpaid custodian becomes liable because people assume the owner entrusted him in reliance on his accepted higher responsibility. That reasoning would not necessarily let a borrower assume liability beyond the borrower's own law. Likewise, according to Shach in simanim 66 and 331, an unpaid custodian who stipulates to be like a borrower is treated as though he said, "I have received payment," which also would not necessarily allow a borrower to go beyond borrower liability. According to Tosafot's first answer, however, a kinyan is required for slaves and land specifically because they are exempt even from oath, while ordinary custodial objects can accept expanded liability without it. On that approach a borrower could likewise accept liability for death through work. In my view, according to all opinions a borrower can obligate himself for death through work without a kinyan. The special rules concerning a custodian accepting liability beyond ordinary law apply where the custodian undertakes a category of custodial liability that an unrelated person would not bear. Death through work is different. Were the person not a lawful borrower at all, he would ordinarily be liable as a direct damager for damage caused by his use. The borrower's exemption exists only because the owner lent the item precisely for that work and thereby authorized the risk. If the borrower stipulates that he will pay for damage caused through the work, the parties are effectively agreeing that, with respect to such damage, he will not enjoy the borrower's exemption. It is as though the owner did not grant him permission to cause that loss free of liability. Therefore even with slaves the borrower can be liable in this form, because if he causes damage through the agreed work the act is like direct damage once the parties expressly excluded the borrower's exemption. Bava Metzia, in the chapter Eizehu Neshech, concerning a renter who stipulates "rent and depreciation," likewise implies that words alone can suffice. Ketzot cites Ritva concerning orphan money invested on terms close to profit and close to loss, where debts lost without negligence are compared to death through work and the recipient can remain exempt. That case is not comparable. The orphans' share remains a mere deposit with the recipient. He lends it out for the orphans' benefit and with their authorization. It makes no sense to say that if those debts are lost, the act should retroactively be treated as though he had lent on his own behalf without authority. A condition of the type discussed here works when one party transfers something to another as gift or loan for the other's benefit and can define the terms upon which the recipient acquires the benefit. Where the recipient receives no proprietary benefit and merely acts as agent for the owner, such wording is only a bare undertaking of liability. That indeed requires a kinyan.
