I was uncertain whether a borrower who expressly undertakes liability even for death caused by the work can bind himself by words alone, or whether a kinyan is required. Tosafot explains that an unpaid custodian can verbally assume the liability of a borrower because ordinary custodial liability follows what people normally intend to accept, whereas land, slaves, and documents, which the Torah excluded even from the custodial oath, require a kinyan for added liability. According to Tosafot's alternative explanation, the unpaid custodian is believed to have accepted the borrower's liability because people regard him as though he actually borrowed the object and, in return for that benefit, he binds himself. If so, liability greater than that of an ordinary borrower, such as death caused by the work itself, should require a kinyan. This also follows from the Shach's distinction that one can verbally say, in effect, "I accept the status of a borrower," but cannot verbally create liability in categories that remain outside even a borrower's ordinary law. Shitah Mekubetzet, however, appears to say that a borrower can expressly accept liability even for death caused by the work through words alone. Ritva, discussing orphan funds, implies the opposite when he says that if money was entrusted as a deposit with the recipient accepting the liability of a borrower, he would still be exempt for a loss analogous to death caused by the work. This suggests that the verbal assumption reaches the ordinary law of a borrower, but no further. The matter therefore remains doubtful. The borrower should be exempt from liability for death caused by the work even after an express verbal stipulation, unless a kinyan was made. More generally, the reason the Torah's exclusions of land and similar property require a kinyan for added liability is that the Torah already knew that people generally intend to accept custodial responsibility and nevertheless excluded those categories. Mere repetition of the same intention in words adds nothing. By contrast, within property subject to custodial law, the Torah imposed only the level people ordinarily intend, so a custodian can verbally assume a higher recognized custodial level.
