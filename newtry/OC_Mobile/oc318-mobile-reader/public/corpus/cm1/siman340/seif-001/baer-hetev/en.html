The borrower. See the Ir Shushan, which writes: if an unavoidable event occurred not because of the borrower, such as where one borrowed a house and it burned accidentally, he is not liable. This is inaccurate, for there he is exempt in any event because the laws of custodians do not apply to servants or land, as the Ir Shushan itself states in siman 301, seif 1. So writes the Sema, correctly. Moreover, one may object further that when a house is borrowed, an unavoidable loss occurring while it is in the borrower's possession is considered to have occurred through the borrower. Shach.
The lender. The Mordechai also gives another reason: the collateral was not acquired by him because he performed no act upon the body of the collateral. The Sema cites this in siman 72, seif 44. Ibid.
Work. Since he did not change its use and performed the work with the owner's permission, the owners caused their own loss; they should not have lent a cow for work it could not withstand. Sema.
Lighter. See simanim 308 and 311, where the Rosh and Tur conclude that one may change an animal to another task that is no heavier, and certainly to a lighter task. One cannot say borrowers are treated more stringently because all benefit is theirs; there is no reason for that once the animal died from the work and the Torah exempts him. How is this different from siman 342, where if he lent it to others and proves it deteriorated due to the work, he is exempt? Rather, the present ruling means only that if he changes even to a lighter task and there is some circumstance before us from which one can attribute the death to the change, we assume the change caused it. This is like siman 309, seif 2, in the gloss, regarding hiring a donkey for one route and taking it on another where the air differs, and like siman 291, seif 6, regarding initial negligence followed by unavoidable loss: if there is some basis to connect the unavoidable event to the negligence, even remotely, he is liable. Ibid.
