A fruit interest is not like ownership of the body. The Gemara rules like Reish Lakish in several places that a right to fruits is not equivalent to ownership of the body. Tosafot therefore asks how we can also rule that brothers who divided inherited property are treated as purchasers and return their shares in Jubilee, since Rabbi Yochanan argued that if a temporary fruit right were not like body ownership, almost no one could bring first fruits with the required declaration. Tosafot answers that perhaps we rule like Reish Lakish only in the special father and son case because a father waives in favor of his son, while elsewhere a fruit interest can be stronger. The broader decisors, however, appear to rule generally that fruit ownership is not body ownership. This requires a more refined explanation of the Jubilee and first fruits cases.
Ramban helps clarify the distinction. A person who buys a field only for its fruits has no right to alter the body, destroy, build, or dig. By contrast, one who purchases the body of land for a fixed term may build, destroy, and use the body throughout that term exactly like an owner, although the property later returns. A Jubilee sale is of the second kind. The purchaser has the body for the fixed term until Jubilee and can use the land as an owner. The Gemara calls it "fruit ownership" only because it is not perpetual. Ran similarly says that any nonperpetual acquisition can be called fruit ownership in contrast to absolute everlasting ownership. Thus the law can follow Reish Lakish that a true fruit interest, where the holder has no ownership of the body, is not equivalent to body ownership, while still recognizing that a fixed term ownership of the body is sufficiently substantial for many Torah purposes. This is the distinction in the Gemara's phrase: in one case he descended with intent for fruits, while in the Jubilee case he descended with intent for the body. A Jubilee purchaser therefore has temporary body ownership. The same explains the husband's rights in his wife's property where, according to the relevant sources, he can substantially use and alter the property during the marriage. His interest can be stronger than a mere right to fruits. This distinction also resolves Rashbam's explanation concerning first fruits. The Torah says, "which you bring from your land." At the time of bringing, temporary bodily ownership can be enough to call it "your land," just as an etrog gifted with full bodily ownership for one hour is "yours" for the mitzvah. Rabbi Avigdor Kohen Tzedek says precisely that a gift on condition of return can constitute bodily ownership for a limited time. The purchaser nevertheless may not recite, "the land that You have given me," because he does not possess everlasting ownership. For that declaration, temporary body ownership is still called fruit ownership in contrast to the permanent inheritance of the land. Thus he brings but does not recite.
