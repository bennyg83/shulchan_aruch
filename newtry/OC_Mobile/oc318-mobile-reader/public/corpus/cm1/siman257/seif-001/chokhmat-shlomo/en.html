Seif 1. I was asked about an ill person who wrote a will dividing his property among others and stated that he was giving it as a gift of a healthy person "from one hour before my death and onward." At the end he wrote that a formal kinyan was made from him, and the document was signed by the patient himself and bore the date of execution. Some objected that because the gift was to take effect only one hour before death, the kinyan could not help, since by that future time the sudar used for the kinyan had already returned to its owner. In my opinion the will is unquestionably valid, because the document bears a date and we apply the rule that the date of a document proves the intended effective time. Although Rama says this only where the borrower instructed the witnesses to write the date, here the patient himself signed the document after seeing the date. His signature therefore constitutes approval of the dated wording and an admission that the document expresses his own words. A party's admission is equivalent to a hundred witnesses. Indeed, a handwritten signature generally functions by way of admission that the writing is his. Accordingly, the date must likewise be treated as his own declaration. Moreover, when one interpretation would nullify a document entirely, we do not invoke the rule that the holder of the document has the weaker hand; we presume the instrument was drawn effectively. Even if the document's witnesses later testified that they had not heard the date from him, they would not be believed to retract their recorded testimony, unless independent witnesses or the litigant himself established that fact. We therefore construe the gift as though he said: the title from today, and the fruits from one hour before death. One could object that if this were his intent he would not have needed to say "one hour before death," since a gift "from today and after death" already gives the body now and fruits after death. Yet this does not invalidate the document. If his words literally intended the entire gift to begin only one hour before death, then he was plainly unfamiliar with the relevant law. If so, it is equally possible that he mistakenly believed even the fruits had to take effect before death rather than after it. Therefore the will is valid.
In the same seif, gloss: One who consecrates his property, etc., for it is impossible to say the body from today, etc. Ketzot HaChoshen challenges this, asking why it cannot be treated as a condition. In my opinion there is no difficulty. Rashba follows Rabbi Yochanan, who treats such language not as a condition but as a retained portion in the acquisition. Ritva explains that in monetary law such a reservation is like retaining the fruits. Here the giver did not reserve the fruits, so the transaction fails entirely. The Rama may follow this view, or at least regard the dispute between Rav and Rabbi Yochanan as unresolved and therefore rule stringently in each context, leaving property in its presumptive possession.
