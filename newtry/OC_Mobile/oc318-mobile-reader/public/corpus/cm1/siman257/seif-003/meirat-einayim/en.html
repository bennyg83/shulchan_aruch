The fruit addicts until they die:
He even came back and took the father. He came back and took away from the son of the land that he had given to him and that he had never given his son any of the father’s death, but in his life, the fruit and the curse of the father was not the power of the father’s hand to sell as “the fruit of his whole life was not the sale of his father.”
It is appraised for the purchaser. This is the view of the Rif and Rambam. Their approach, as explained in the Derishah, is that only when a father gives to his son do we say that his mind is especially close to the son and that he gave him also all produce attached to the ground whose time for harvesting had not yet arrived. The son therefore owes nothing to the donor's heirs for such produce. But the father cannot diminish the purchaser's rights and favor the son at the purchaser's expense. Whatever grew while the property was in the purchaser's legal sphere is appraised, and the son must pay its value to the purchaser. The alternative opinion cited by Rama holds that the father sold the purchaser only the rights that belonged to the father. The son therefore need not pay the purchaser for produce that grew during the purchaser's period, just as he would not owe anything to the father's heirs had the field remained in the father's hands until death.
