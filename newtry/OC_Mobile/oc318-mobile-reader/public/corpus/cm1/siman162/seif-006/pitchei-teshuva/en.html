Others cannot object. See Magen Avraham, siman 154, end of seif katan 23, who writes that the words of Re'em, part 1, siman 53, indicate that if the synagogue can contain everyone, they may not separate, and Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 41, writes similarly. Responsa Radbaz, part 3, siman 472, explains that this applies where all are united in heart, so that their praise of Hashem rises properly. But if their hearts are divided and reconciliation is impossible, and they are constantly disputing and quarreling, it is better that each group pray separately. In such a case neither a person's own chazakah nor that of his ancestors controls. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 12, letter 3, rules similarly in his case and alludes to this Radbaz. See Responsa Be'er Yitzchak, Orach Chaim siman 24, concerning a city with an established synagogue from which a number of people recently separated to pray independently. They then demanded their share of the old synagogue's income, books, and sacred articles. The questioner ruled that they could claim nothing and could not diminish the old synagogue's rights. Be'er Yitzchak agreed. A minority wishing to establish another synagogue where the old synagogue can contain everyone can be prevented by the majority from separating, as Magen Avraham writes in the name of the Rosh. Pachad Yitzchak likewise cites several authorities that if a new synagogue will cause financial injury to the old one, its construction can be prevented because it cuts off the old synagogue's support. Therefore, if a congregation splits into two merely because of differences of opinion, all consecrated property goes to the larger group, and even sacred property held by the minority is removed from them and given to the majority. If they divide because the building cannot contain everyone, the law is to divide the property according to the number of adult men. This distinguishes the Mabit cited by Magen Avraham, who says that when some necessity requires division, the consecrated property is divided. A genuine necessity such as lack of space differs from an ordinary ideological dispute. In addition, residents of a city may adopt binding communal regulations and the majority can compel the minority. He further raises the concern that transferring consecrated property from the many to the few may itself constitute a lowering of sanctity. Be'er Yitzchak concludes that if, when the minority separated, the majority could have prevented them because the synagogue contained everyone, then even though the majority remained silent at the time, the minority cannot later claim rights in the consecrated property. Even where separation was permitted under the Radbaz because relations had become irreconcilable, the minority still cannot demand a share of the consecrated property. The original association was effectively formed for as long as the synagogue would exist. Partners who formed a partnership while friendly do not automatically dissolve it merely because they later quarrel. The right therefore remains with the majority. See also Chatam Sofer there, letter 4. Magen Avraham further cites Knesset HaGedolah that a congregation can decree that ten men not pray outside the synagogue, both because the authorities may infer from multiple minyanim that the Jewish population is large and because some individuals avoid communal taxes while forming a separate minyan elsewhere. See what I wrote similarly in siman 149, seif 31, seif katan 4. See also Responsa Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat siman 25, concerning a proclamation by the av beit din together with community leaders that no minyan be held in a private home and that everyone attend the synagogue, except after Shabbat and festivals, with a fixed fine for violation. Someone later formed a home minyan because circumstances made him unable to go to the synagogue. Shev Yaakov ruled that the proclamation plainly applied only to one who was able to attend the synagogue but formed a home minyan without necessity. Even if the proclamation had purported to include someone unable to attend, he would not be obligated to obey a rule contrary to Torah law and therefore could not incur its fine. As to one who violated the proclamation repeatedly, he is liable only for the single stated fine, even if he formed many minyanim, because the proclamation did not state a separate fine for each occurrence and no fresh warning was given between them.
