(See there in the gloss: Some say that he may close the entire width opposite his courtyard, etc.) It appears to me that the words of the Ramah cited by the Tur at the end of this siman should be explained as follows. The Ramah discusses an alley in which courtyards open opposite one another. On one side there is an additional courtyard with no courtyard opposite it on the other side, and this is called the innermost courtyard. Beyond that courtyard there are walls facing one another until the alley is closed at its end, but no courtyard opens in those walls. They form only an enclosure: one wall extending from the side of the courtyard, another opposite it, and a third joining them at the closed end. The area between these walls belongs to all the residents of the alley. The Ramah rules that the owner of the innermost courtyard may close the entire width of the alley opposite his courtyard. Once he closes it, no courtyard opening remains beyond his barrier, only an empty area enclosed by walls. Although that area is not his, the residents of the alley can reach it only by passing opposite the entrance of his courtyard, and he may prevent them from doing so. Consequently, they are all there only by his permission. The Tur explains the Ramah to mean that those walls also belong to the innermost owner. Otherwise, had the residents of the alley built them, they could still open another courtyard there beyond the innermost courtyard, and for that reason they would not have lost their right to pass opposite his entrance. The Rivash, however, writes that the innermost owner has already acquired the right to prevent them from later opening a new courtyard, and he may therefore close the entire width of the alley opposite his own courtyard. He adds that even where one courtyard faces another, but the entrance of one courtyard extends farther toward the closed end of the alley, that courtyard owner may close the entire width opposite his entrance. In his view, those opposite the area farther inward have already lost their right there. The Tur plainly disagrees, for he writes that one certainly may not close opposite another person’s courtyard, since today or tomorrow that person may wish to make use of the area. The Tur follows his own position that no resident loses the right to use the portion beyond his present entrance, because he may still open wherever he wishes within his courtyard. This, in my view, is the clear explanation of the Tur and the Ramah. The Ramah does not follow the explanation of Rabbeinu Chananel in the responsum cited by the Beit Yosef, according to which the residents of the alley may prevent the innermost owner from narrowing the alley because the many people entering it would then be forced toward the courtyards. Rather, the Gemara does not address that consideration at all, and should be understood as Rashi explains it, as cited by the Beit Yosef. Although the Beit Yosef writes that the Ramah accords with Rabbeinu Chananel’s explanation, I do not know how to reconcile the words on that basis.
