However, small alleys, etc., the residents of the alley are permitted, etc. See Responsa Pnei Yehoshua, part 2, end of siman 94. He writes that the meaning of the Rosh and Tur is straightforward: residents of the public domain cannot prevent doors from being installed in those smaller alleys. Their ability to object to a neighboring alley is because at times the public domain becomes crowded and people enter there. But where the smaller alleys are places that people do not enter even when crowded, residents of the public domain cannot object. The residents of the alley itself, however, can certainly object. Thus, if the alley residents do not object but residents of the public domain do, the latter cannot prevent the doors. This explains why the Rosh begins in the plural, "however, the small alleys," and concludes in the singular, "the residents of the alley are permitted." The phrase means the residents of the alley opening to the public domain who wish to install doors in those smaller alleys for some purpose. Although they may not install doors in their own alley because the public may enter it when crowded, they may install them in those other alleys. But if the residents of those alleys object, their status is no worse than that of a side street and the doors may not be imposed upon them. This is unquestionably the correct interpretation.
