Maharashach discusses a doorway that had been closed. He suggests one might distinguish between an opening into an alley and an opening into another's courtyard, because the alley is jointly used and the doorway owner has a stronger right there. In his actual case, however, the doorway had never been built shut and had merely remained locked, so he concludes that the right unquestionably remained. Nimukei Yosef shows that even in an alley the doorway owner does not own the body of the alley. Indeed, the alley is a weaker right because the public also passes there. Nevertheless, so long as the doorway posts were not removed, the opening is treated as still legally existing and the user's rights remain. In a courtyard, the doorway owner can actually have a stronger right in the four amot before the opening, especially where the father divided the property. Even where the courtyard was purchased jointly and the right is only one of use, leaving the doorway posts in place preserves the right just as in an alley. According to Rosh, removing the posts can constitute abandonment of the right even in a courtyard. But mere closure without removing them does not.
