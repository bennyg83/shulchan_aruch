To add benches in the synagogue, etc. See Responsa Masat Binyamin, siman 4, concerning a man who had a synagogue seat beside the Aron Kodesh on the north side. After occupying it for more than the years required for chazakah, he died and left the seat to his sons. The community wished to place a menorah next to the seat, arguing that synagogues ordinarily have a menorah on both sides of the Aron Kodesh and that they had not done so previously only because the congregation had been small. The heirs objected that the community could not make their place narrower and more crowded. Masat Binyamin discusses at length the underlying ruling of the Rivash that derives synagogue law from the law of an alley, and notes several distinctions. He concludes that even in ordinary secular uses, where a place is not intended for full use, an individual cannot object merely because traffic increases or the place becomes somewhat crowded. This is true even of a vineyard path that has some use, if it is not full use, and all the more where the area has no independent use. It is certainly so in a synagogue, where the activity is sacred service of Hashem, and increased traffic and crowding are not necessarily detriments but can be advantages, since there is reward for the crowding and also reward for the additional steps. He therefore regarded an individual's objection based solely on increased traffic or crowding as untenable, though he did not wish to decide against great earlier authorities without the agreement of the sages of his generation. In the particular case before him, he wrote that there were conclusive proofs that the heirs had no claim and that even the Rivash would agree. See Responsa Tzemach LaTzedek, siman 94, concerning a synagogue with seats along its four walls and around the bimah, leaving open spaces between the Aron Kodesh and the bimah and on the north and south sides. After the congregation grew, twelve householders could not find seats even to rent. The community wished to add benches in these open areas. People whose seats bordered those areas objected that their passage and the space for their shtenders would be narrower than before. He distinguished the case from the Rivash. Residents of an alley cannot compel one another to install a door, so one resident can also prevent another from installing it. By contrast, members of a synagogue can compel one another to build a synagogue when necessary. Even where there is already a synagogue in the city, if it cannot contain everyone, the others must participate in providing sufficient space. All the more, existing members cannot prevent the addition of benches. The Rivash must therefore have concerned a case where additional construction could not be compelled, such as where seats were still available but people did not wish to rent them at the high price. Here no seats were available even at a high price. In addition, the Rivash concerned one wealthy man with many seats in a corner where others did not ordinarily pass. In this case the open areas were used by the entire congregation to reach their seats, so the adjacent seat holders had no greater claim than anyone else. Their complaint that their shtenders could no longer stand as far out as before was also insufficient. If they had previously occupied more than the proper measure, the congregation could have objected; the reason no one objected was merely that there was then ample room and no harm. Such silence creates no chazakah, both because they are partners and because no chazakah is acquired against communal property. Therefore, if after adding benches they still have proper room to reach their seats and place their shtenders, as determined by beit din, they cannot object. Nor can they demand that the twelve householders rent a separate synagogue. Those twelve might not produce a minyan every day because some travel for business; a new synagogue might also require governmental permission and create concern that the authorities would take notice of the community's size; and the public may not be able to bear the added expenses of a building, chazzan, shamash, lights, and other needs. He concludes that benches may be added so long as the earlier seat holders retain reasonable space, and even the Rivash would agree. See also Responsa Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 33. A congregation had a place in the southeast corner of the synagogue reserved for a groom on his wedding day. Reuven had a seat at the end of the south wall adjoining that place. The groom's place was narrow because the corner of the southern bench prevented him from taking three steps comfortably after prayer. Reuven died leaving adult and minor orphans. Later the seven communal trustees sold the groom's place to Yehudah on condition that a groom could use it on his wedding day, while Yehudah would sit there at other times. They also shifted the southern bench somewhat westward to make the place roomy. Yehudah occupied it for years and then sold his right to Shimon, who likewise occupied it for years and established a three year chazakah. One of Reuven's orphans later claimed that he had been a minor when the community first sold the place, that the sale wrongfully impaired his father's right at the head of the southern bench, and that the occasional use by a groom had formerly left the adjacent area generally open, whereas a permanent occupant now crowded him. He also challenged the movement of the bench. Shimon answered that although the heir had been a minor when his father died, he was already an adult while Yehudah held the place and failed to protest, and therefore had presumably waived his right. He also argued that the community had authority to sell the place and to move the bench, which did not harm the other seat holders. Panim Meirot ruled that if Shimon can prove that the orphan was already an adult during Yehudah's chazakah, the heir lost his claim. Although a purchaser who originally entered while the owner was a minor generally cannot establish chazakah merely by remaining three years after majority, because the minor did not know enough to protest, once the first purchaser sold to a second purchaser beit din advances the purchaser's claim. We therefore claim for Shimon that Yehudah may have possessed a deed or witnesses proving that the heir waived his right after reaching majority. He further writes that even if Shimon cannot prove that the orphan was already an adult before Yehudah, the possession is valid. Since Yehudah bought from the community, we claim for him that the community possessed a deed from the orphan's father authorizing the sale. The Rama's rule that a community does not establish chazakah against private owners does not alter this, for the same authorities also say that individuals do not establish chazakah against the community, because the community is regarded as powerful and able to remove an occupant whenever it wishes. Thus even the father's possession would not necessarily have constituted a chazakah against the congregation. Once the congregation sold the right, we can claim for the purchaser that the congregation had witnesses or a deed authorizing sale whenever it wished. We similarly claim that the congregation had authority to shift the bench somewhat westward. The Rama's rule against adding synagogue benches concerns creating an entirely new place. Here they did not create a new category of seat but merely adjusted an existing arrangement that previously did not require precision because the groom sat there only occasionally. When a purchaser appeared, the community relied on its own right to move the bench slightly, particularly since no actual injury was caused to the other seat holders. Panim Meirot rejected a contrary ruling by another scholar. Panim Meirot further discusses a new synagogue in which the community sold six seats on the east side south of the Aron Kodesh and six north of it, stipulating that the owners of these twelve seats would have priority in choosing the most important places in the women's section. A dispute arose whether the south side purchasers had priority over corresponding purchasers on the north side, or whether the first purchaser on the south should draw lots with the first on the north, the second with the second, and so forth. He ruled that the right side, which corresponds to the south, is generally more important. Therefore the first seat on the south outranks the first on the north. Yet the first on the north outranks the second on the south, and so on in order. He derives support from Berakhot 41b and Tosafot there. This requires comparison with his own ruling in responsum siman 10 concerning precedence among the seven species. See there carefully. See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, Orach Chaim part, siman 29, which I cited in Pitchei Teshuva to Orach Chaim siman 150, seif 5, under the words "where they pray."
Others cannot object. See Magen Avraham, siman 154, end of seif katan 23, who writes that the words of Re'em, part 1, siman 53, indicate that if the synagogue can contain everyone, they may not separate, and Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 41, writes similarly. Responsa Radbaz, part 3, siman 472, explains that this applies where all are united in heart, so that their praise of Hashem rises properly. But if their hearts are divided and reconciliation is impossible, and they are constantly disputing and quarreling, it is better that each group pray separately. In such a case neither a person's own chazakah nor that of his ancestors controls. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 12, letter 3, rules similarly in his case and alludes to this Radbaz. See Responsa Be'er Yitzchak, Orach Chaim siman 24, concerning a city with an established synagogue from which a number of people recently separated to pray independently. They then demanded their share of the old synagogue's income, books, and sacred articles. The questioner ruled that they could claim nothing and could not diminish the old synagogue's rights. Be'er Yitzchak agreed. A minority wishing to establish another synagogue where the old synagogue can contain everyone can be prevented by the majority from separating, as Magen Avraham writes in the name of the Rosh. Pachad Yitzchak likewise cites several authorities that if a new synagogue will cause financial injury to the old one, its construction can be prevented because it cuts off the old synagogue's support. Therefore, if a congregation splits into two merely because of differences of opinion, all consecrated property goes to the larger group, and even sacred property held by the minority is removed from them and given to the majority. If they divide because the building cannot contain everyone, the law is to divide the property according to the number of adult men. This distinguishes the Mabit cited by Magen Avraham, who says that when some necessity requires division, the consecrated property is divided. A genuine necessity such as lack of space differs from an ordinary ideological dispute. In addition, residents of a city may adopt binding communal regulations and the majority can compel the minority. He further raises the concern that transferring consecrated property from the many to the few may itself constitute a lowering of sanctity. Be'er Yitzchak concludes that if, when the minority separated, the majority could have prevented them because the synagogue contained everyone, then even though the majority remained silent at the time, the minority cannot later claim rights in the consecrated property. Even where separation was permitted under the Radbaz because relations had become irreconcilable, the minority still cannot demand a share of the consecrated property. The original association was effectively formed for as long as the synagogue would exist. Partners who formed a partnership while friendly do not automatically dissolve it merely because they later quarrel. The right therefore remains with the majority. See also Chatam Sofer there, letter 4. See also Responsa Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat siman 25, concerning a proclamation by the av beit din together with community leaders that no minyan be held in a private home and that everyone attend the synagogue, except after Shabbat and festivals, with a fixed fine for violation. Someone later formed a home minyan because circumstances made him unable to go to the synagogue. Shev Yaakov ruled that the proclamation plainly applied only to one who was able to attend the synagogue but formed a home minyan without necessity. Even if the proclamation had purported to include someone unable to attend, he would not be obligated to obey a rule contrary to Torah law and therefore could not incur its fine. As to one who violated the proclamation repeatedly, he is liable only for the single stated fine, even if he formed many minyanim, because the proclamation did not state a separate fine for each occurrence and no fresh warning was given between them.
