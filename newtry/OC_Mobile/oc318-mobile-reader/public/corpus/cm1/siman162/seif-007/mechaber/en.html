Five courtyards open into a closed alleyway. All the residents use the section alongside the outermost courtyard, while the outermost courtyard uses only its own section. The second courtyard uses its own section and the outer section, but not the sections farther inward. Thus, the innermost courtyard uses every section of the alleyway as well as its own. Therefore, if the owner of the second courtyard builds a platform opposite his entrance and thereby narrows the way, the outermost resident cannot object, but all the residents farther inward may object because he lengthens their route, since they must go around the platform. A platform means a narrow place about a cubit wide and four or five handbreadths high, on which merchandise is placed for sale and on which people sometimes sit. Rama: Some say that an increase in walking distance is not a valid objection, and an inner resident cannot object to an outer resident unless the inner resident would have had the right to close the alleyway at that point. Where the outer resident has a right to use the alleyway farther inward, an increase in walking distance is not a valid claim. The same rules that apply to an alleyway apply to seats in a synagogue. Therefore, if a wealthy person owns many seats and the congregation wishes to add benches in vacant areas to increase charitable revenue and allow more people to pray, or individuals wish to sit in the aisle, the wealthy person may object on the ground that they narrow or lengthen his route. If there is an established local custom, however, it is followed. The congregation may enact that anyone who owns a seat he does not need must rent it at a rate fixed by them. If the congregation wishes to build another synagogue, or individuals wish to build a synagogue for themselves, others may not prevent them, and one who objects deserves censure. A synagogue may be used only in the customary manner. Therefore, if those seated on the western side wish to raise their seats on a platform above the others, the others may object. But if they raise them only slightly so that they can see, without appearing higher than the others, the others may not object, because one benefits and the other suffers no loss. See below, siman 171, seif 1. If the owner of the second courtyard opens a second entrance to his courtyard between his original entrance and the outermost courtyard, the outermost resident cannot object, because his own right of use extends only outward from his doorway. If, however, the second resident opens the new entrance between his courtyard and the third courtyard, the inner resident may object, because the second resident has no right to use the alleyway farther inward than his original entrance. The same rule applies throughout. Rama: Some say that each resident has a right to open an entrance anywhere along the frontage of his courtyard, whether toward the outer or inner side. He may therefore also object to anyone who seeks to close the alleyway opposite his courtyard, even at a point farther inward than his existing entrance.
