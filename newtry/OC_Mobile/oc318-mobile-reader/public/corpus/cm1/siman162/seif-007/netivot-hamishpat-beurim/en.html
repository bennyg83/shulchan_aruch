Taz correctly objects that the reason is not merely the inconvenience of a longer route. According to this opinion, the inner residents simply have no right of use or passage through that part at all. The inner resident may close the alley across its width up to the outer resident's first entrance. If the objection were only based on increased travel, the inner resident could object even to an opening between himself and the outer resident, since the inner resident also has passage there. The primary point is therefore the scope of the existing passage right.
Therefore, a wealthy person who has many seats. This at first seems difficult because Rivash bases the rule on the claim of increased passage, while Rama previously cited an opinion that increased passage is not a valid objection. Taz's question is also strong: Rashba rejected increased passage only regarding opening an entrance in an alley, but might agree concerning building a platform that narrows the route. Rama can be understood as distinguishing between narrowing the physical route and merely increasing the distance walked. Rashba himself says that even unloading goods in front of one's entrance can be prevented where it blocks passersby and constricts the way. Thus where the new arrangement narrows the passage, everyone may object. Where it merely requires people to walk around a small platform, the matter depends on the earlier dispute. This also explains Rama's wording regarding synagogue seats: if the arrangement narrows the passage, there is an objection according to all; if it merely increases travel, it depends on the dispute. However, this assumes the synagogue already exists. If it burned and the seven good men of the city wish to rebuild it larger and add seats in order to finance construction, they may do so. They could even sell the entire site and build elsewhere. The original seat holders are then entitled only to the value of their prior shares as assessed in the ruin, and if they object that amount can be returned to them.
