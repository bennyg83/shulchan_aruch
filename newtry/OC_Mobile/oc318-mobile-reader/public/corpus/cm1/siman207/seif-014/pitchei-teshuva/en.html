For anyone who says. See Baer Heitev, who cites the Bach and questions his words. In truth, this is an early dispute. Darkei Moshe to this siman, letter 7, cites the Mordechai in the chapter "An Ox that Gored Four or Five" in accordance with the Bach, that asmachta acquires by Torah law, as shown by the fact that a guarantor becomes obligated even though his undertaking resembles asmachta. Darkei Moshe then cites the Mordechai at the end of "The Laborers" that a guarantor is not subject to the defect of asmachta. See Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat on this issue. Ar'a DeRabbanan, entry 1, siman 71, notes that the Bach's position is explicit in Rashi to Shevuot 47a, s.v. "one who plays dice." See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 66, who suggests that this point may underlie the dispute between the Rambam and Raavad in the laws of chametz and matzah, cited by Magen Avraham 441:2, whether asmachta applies in dealings with a non-Jew. One may say that the Rambam agrees that asmachta not involving a party's control is only a rabbinic defect, and the Sages did not institute it for a non-Jew. Still, he could distinguish between a Jew acquiring from a non-Jew by such an undertaking and a non-Jew acquiring from a Jew. Chatam Sofer concludes that most authorities hold that the law of asmachta does apply to a non-Jew, as stated by Magen Avraham.
