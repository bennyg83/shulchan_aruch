Conditions that can be fulfilled. This excludes an impossible condition, in which case the act remains valid and the condition is void. All the laws of conditions are derived from the condition made with the tribes of Gad and Reuven, where fulfillment was possible. From that condition we also derive that the condition must be doubled, the positive formulation must precede the negative, the condition must precede the act, and the condition and the act must concern distinct matters, as was true there. If these requirements are absent, the transaction remains valid and the condition is void, as the Tur writes in Even HaEzer siman 38 and explicitly in this siman, seif 3. The Mechaber also alludes to this immediately below when he writes, "The laws of conditions have already been explained," etc. The Tur, Rambam, and Mechaber mention here specifically the requirement that the condition be possible because that is the subject under discussion: if the conditions are fulfilled, the acquisition takes effect. See the Perishah.
The purchaser's benefit. The Tur explains, for example, where the seller says: "If the field is later seized from you, I will set aside another good field from which you may collect your principal, the appreciation of the field, and its produce." See there.
They are merely words of appeasement. The Rosh explains the matter. Since the purchaser did not repeat the condition that was for his own benefit, this shows that even he did not regard the seller's words and promise as a binding term. Rather, the seller merely wished to calm the purchaser's distress and anger. The purchaser thought to himself: if I repeat the condition, the seller may immediately retract his promise. It is better for me to remain silent, perhaps later he will be ashamed to retract what he promised. But the purchaser in fact resolved to buy even without responsibility. See there. I have paraphrased the Rosh's wording only to clarify his meaning.
Where he initially sold to him without specification, etc. The Tur's wording is different: "For example, at the beginning of the transaction they fixed that the sale would be without responsibility, but the acquisition had not yet been completed, such as where he paid money but no deed had yet been written in a place where a deed is required, or the like. Since at the beginning of the transaction the purchaser agreed to buy without responsibility," etc. Rama speaks more generally here because he is discussing other conditions, regarding which the seller need not accept them unless the purchaser stipulated them. Responsibility is different, because the law is that omission of responsibility is treated as a scribal error and the purchaser need not stipulate it. Therefore one must say that the relevant distinction is not whether the seller mentioned responsibility at the beginning or end, but whether the parties initially acquired expressly without responsibility. This is clear. See the Derishah.
Before the sale was fully completed, etc. If he imposed the condition after the sale was completed, even a condition stated by the purchaser for his own benefit would not affect something already fully concluded. In the Derishah I proved from Tosafos and the Rosh that if at the beginning of the sale the seller stipulated that he was selling specifically without responsibility, then even if immediately afterward, still at the beginning of the transaction, the seller says, "Do not worry; if it is seized from you I will accept responsibility," this too is merely appeasing language if the purchaser did not himself stipulate it and did not repeat the seller's words by saying, "Yes, it shall be specifically as you said, with responsibility."
