The laws governing conditions. It appears that the formal rules of conditions are required specifically when one uses conditional language such as "if." But if he says, "When such-and-such occurs," as in, "When I have money, I will return it to you," the formal requirements of double formulation and the other laws of conditions are not necessary. Such wording resembles a time limitation rather than a condition. One who divorces a woman to take effect after thirty days does not need the formal rules of conditions. Similarly, if he says, "Today you are not my wife, but tomorrow you are my wife," the problem is lack of severance, not defect in formal condition language, as Rambam writes in chapter 8 of Hilchot Gerushin, halachah 9. See Rambam at the beginning of chapter 9 of Hilchot Gerushin, where he distinguishes between saying, "When the sun rises," and saying, "On condition that the sun rise." The same should apply if he says, "Until the sun rises." This is like saying, "Tomorrow you are not my wife," meaning that the act itself is defined only until a time. So too, "until I have money" can be a temporal limitation rather than a formal condition. So long as he did not use conditional language, the formal laws of conditions are unnecessary. Because the principal rules of conditions belong in Even HaEzer, I will not expand on them here. See Otzar Poskim for the contemporary practice that with movable property one generally does not require a doubled condition, whereas with land documents are often drafted according to the conditions of the children of Gad and Reuven. There are also conditions that do not require the ordinary formal rules because they operate as a reservation within the body of the sale itself, such as saying, "On condition that this particular right remains mine." The details of that category will be explained in siman 212.
