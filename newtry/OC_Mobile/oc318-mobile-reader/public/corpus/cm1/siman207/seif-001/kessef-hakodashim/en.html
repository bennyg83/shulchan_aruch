Placating words. One sold a cow to another. After the sale the buyer said, “If you wish to keep it for yourself and not sell it, keep it.” The cow remained in the seller’s house. Even though the seller’s guarded courtyard could theoretically acquire the cow back for him if there were a valid conditional conveyance, this is no stronger than the placating words discussed in siman 207:1, because the buyer alone introduced a condition that benefited the seller by giving him an option. Even if the seller afterward repeated that this should be the arrangement, perhaps it does not help, because had the seller adopted the condition before completion of the sale, the original sale would have been made only on those terms. Once the sale was complete, however, a condition initiated only by the buyer for the seller’s benefit may remain mere placating words, even while the cow remains in the seller’s house. This requires study. Where such a condition is otherwise effective, if the seller initially intended to keep the cow and only later decided to sell, it seems that a genuine initial decision to keep it, without evasion, falls within the condition. The other party may perhaps require him to accept a ban that he is not acting deceitfully, comparable to the law of a neighboring purchaser. If from the outset he intended merely to keep and feed it for a time until it fattened and then sell it, that likely counts as an intention to sell from the beginning. If, however, he sold cheaply because an epidemic was killing livestock in that place, and the buyer told him that if he chose to keep it himself and accept the danger rather than sell cheaply he could do so, then keeping it for several weeks, assuming the risk and fattening it, may perhaps count as keeping it for himself. This requires study. In monetary conditions it appears that bereirah is generally recognized, as in the rule, “When I sell it, I will sell it to you for one maneh.” Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 66 cites Maharit concerning bereirah in monetary law, though I do not now recall his conclusion. Where a statement would ordinarily be mere placating words, perhaps that defect does not apply if the condition also carries a benefit for the speaker himself. Thus, in a lease, if the tenant stipulates that he will pay proportionately each month, although rent is technically due only at the end, the landlord benefits from gradual payment and may have a grievance if it is withheld. The tenant may therefore have intended a real term, although perhaps he intended merely to retain the option of paying in installments and is not legally bound to do so unless his wording imposed an obligation. If the landlord originally demanded a fixed amount in advance and the tenant responded that he would pay monthly, this is certainly not mere placating language, because the landlord agreed not to receive the advance in exchange for the tenant’s monthly obligation. It is a genuine condition. Another case: one bought grain without specifying when he would remove it. The seller demanded that he take it away and clear the premises. The buyer answered, “Although the price has now fallen, do not fear, I will take it. If the price rises, I will allow you to cancel the sale.” Perhaps this is not mere placating language, because the statement induced the seller to leave the grain in his premises in the hope of a possible profit if the price later rose. Since the seller’s courtyard can acquire, once the price rises the courtyard may immediately effect the reacquisition. Although such an arrangement may involve asmachta, a possessor can perhaps plead kim li that asmachta does not apply to an item already in his possession. This differs from compromise, where even the waiving party requires a kinyan sudar because his waiver is uncertain and depends upon the arbitrators’ decision. In an ordinary doubt, the possessor may say kim li that there is no asmachta when the property is already in his hand. Compromise has its own advantage as well, for many authorities hold that even where the object is in hand asmachta remains and an ordinary kinyan sudar does not cure it, whereas a kinyan sudar for compromise is effective. At least the possessor may plead kim li as stated. If the buyer did not retract before the price rose, there remains a question of language. “You may cancel the sale if you wish” sounds like waiver, and waiver cannot ordinarily transfer an object already acquired without language of conveyance. The same is true if he says, “I will cancel the sale for you,” “I will release you,” or “I will take you out of the sale.” These expressions are weaker than, “If you take mine I will waive,” which is cited in the name of Rashba as effective. Still, “you may cancel” could mean that if the seller comes and says that he wishes to cancel, the buyer will then waive or convey the object back. Perhaps the statement can also be understood as rhetorical placating language: “Why are you upset that the grain remains in your house? If the price falls I will not burden you, and if the price rises and you become distressed that you sold too early, then if you actually express such stinginess I will cancel the sale.” The point would be that the seller would be embarrassed to display such resentment after benefiting from the sale if prices fell. If the buyer explains that this was his intent, perhaps he need not swear, although the seller may impose a ban. If the buyer does not offer that explanation, perhaps the statement should not be treated as mere placating words. If he denies making the statement at all, perhaps no oath is required because, even if made, it might have been rhetorical as described. If an oath is shifted to the buyer, perhaps only a ban can be imposed, since this is a matter not naturally remembered precisely and he may later clarify his recollection even after leaving beit din. If the place where the grain was stored was not normally rented out, then even if the buyer said this merely to appease the seller by granting a possible benefit if prices rose, perhaps he may retract from that promise even before Heaven.
