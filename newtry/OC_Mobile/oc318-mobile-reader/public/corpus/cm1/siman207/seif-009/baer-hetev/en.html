Asmachta. The Gemara states: Every stipulation framed as 'if' is an asmachta and does not acquire. Meaning, when a person does not absolutely resolve to sell but says, 'If this event does not occur, the property shall be sold,' we say that he relied on the expectation that the event would occur and therefore the sale would not take effect. Had he known it would not occur, he would not have made the sale dependent upon it. For example, here he assumed that he would have money and could redeem his field within three years. It follows that he did not originally resolve to transfer it absolutely, and therefore the buyer does not acquire. This is unlike the condition of Gad and Reuven, where Moshe Rabbeinu clearly intended to transfer the land absolutely, but imposed a double condition that if they failed to fulfill their term, the land would be taken back. Thus far the Sema. See above, siman 73, seif 17, and Responsa Rashach, siman 177.
From now. Even according to the opinion cited by the Rama in seif 14 that 'from now' alone does not suffice, here it is different because he immediately placed the property in the creditor's hands as collateral and security for the money, making it resemble a sale. The creditor also conferred a benefit upon him. We therefore say that his intent was for a complete acquisition to take effect immediately, subject only to the condition that he could redeem it within three years. So writes the Rosh. Nevertheless, the lender may not consume the produce during those three years, because perhaps the borrower will redeem the field, making the transaction retroactively a loan, and the lender would have consumed the produce as interest. Rashi states this explicitly. Sema.
He was particular. For this demonstrates that he definitively resolved to transfer it. Otherwise, why would he object, since the borrower could still redeem it? Ibid.
Likewise. The Sema writes that this teaches that even though he said it in the form, 'If I do not repay,' nevertheless it is not an asmachta with respect to this consequence, and he cannot satisfy the creditor with a different field. The students of the Rashba, cited by the Beit Yosef, explain it this way. See there.
