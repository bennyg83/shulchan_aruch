If the condition was not fulfilled. See Taz, Yoreh Deah, end of siman 236, who derives a general rule. If one contracts that another must deliver a particular commodity for a market fair, when that is the commercially relevant time, and an accident prevents delivery until afterward, the purchaser need not accept it later. Where, however, the stipulated time is not central to the transaction and an accident prevents timely performance, the underlying transaction is not necessarily void. Taz applies this also to a man and woman who swore to marry by a specified date. Nekudot HaKesef disagrees with this distinction and follows Bach, as does Shach above, siman 21, seif katan 3: whenever the transaction was initially made expressly upon a condition, the counterparty is released if the condition fails, even through accident. We say that the Torah exempts one who is coerced, not that coercion obligates the other party. Later authorities agree with Shach. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer 38:1, likewise writes that where one buys merchandise expressly on condition that it be delivered for market day and the seller is prevented by accident, later delivery does not preserve the sale; he is inclined to rule even to extract the money, even where a formal kinyan sudar had been made. Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 56, discusses Reuven, who gave Shimon an order payable in another country one month later in the currency of that country. Shimon paid Reuven in local red coins at the current rate. When the order reached its destination the city was under siege, payment could not be made on time, the instrument was protested and returned, and meanwhile the exchange rate moved against Shimon. Shimon argued that the entire transaction was rescinded because the order had not been paid at the stipulated time and demanded return of his original money. Reuven wished only to pay the foreign currency amount, locally or at the destination, and claimed that he was excused from market loss by the accident. Beit Ephraim explains that the case depends on the dispute between Taz and Shach. Foreign currency is like a commodity traded in the market. According to Taz, perhaps the exact date was not central because Shimon still needed the funds in that foreign city after the date, unlike merchandise purchased specifically for a fair. According to Shach, since the parties expressly stipulated payment in the foreign place on the specified date and wrote this into the order, failure to pay on time releases Shimon and requires return of his money. The conclusion of the responsum is missing, but its tenor is that, because the case depends on a dispute, one cannot extract from the party in possession.
