If the condition was not fulfilled. One sold a cow and said that at the non-Jew’s house from whom he bought it, where it had been raised, he had seen them milk a full measure of milk from it. The buyer said that he was purchasing in reliance upon this. It was later discovered that the cow gave that amount only because its calf was with it there. The calf remained at the non-Jew’s premises, and now the cow could not be milked properly at all. Although mere disclosure of intent generally does not invalidate a sale of movables, customary monetary law does not require all the formal rules of doubled conditions, and here the purchase was expressly presented as conditional upon the cow giving milk. One might argue that the buyer should have stipulated more precisely because perhaps the milk depended upon the calf, and in rental law where both parties could have known a fact the loss may fall where it falls. Still, the buyer’s disclosed purpose, that he was buying it as a milk cow, can be joined to the condition. He also explained that he assumed that since more than half a year had visibly passed since calving, the cow no longer depended upon the calf. Now that it became known that she still followed the calf for milk, that circumstance may fall within the condition. This requires study. In practice the sale was rescinded by mutual agreement if it would be established that over a full day the cow did not give the agreed measure. More generally, where merchants fix a time for the buyer to take delivery of purchased animals or other movables, and the time arrives without the buyer appearing, if the seller remains in possession it appears that he may cancel the sale. In monetary matters a doubled condition is not required, especially where one seeks to take property from a possessor. Although mere disclosure of intent does not ordinarily suffice for movables, an agreed delivery date is not merely disclosure but an actual term of the transaction. With animals requiring care and food, the fixed time is certainly material. If the buyer delays, he unquestionably must reimburse the seller for feeding and care. The timing is therefore part of the substance of the sale, not an incidental expression of intent. If the seller says he refuses to continue caring for the animals, and it is not easy even for beit din to appoint a caretaker for a fee without turning the matter into a form of returning lost property, there is strong reason to permit cancellation. This is especially so if payment itself was due that day, because merchants may have an urgent temporary need for cash. In engagement conditions, delay of the specified time can dissolve the bond, as discussed in Yoreh Deah 232 and elsewhere. Although the law of one who is repeatedly demanding his purchase money normally requires several conditions before a sale is cancelled, everything depends upon the wording and nature of the parties’ agreement. At least with animals, I incline to say that the seller may cancel. I believe I wrote elsewhere regarding other merchandise; where ongoing feeding and care are required, the time appears to be essential. This requires further study. If, however, the seller says, “I consider myself as though I have already received payment for the food and care,” the sale remains in force, and the seller’s position is stronger. At minimum, money cannot be extracted against this claim. Another case: a seller stipulated that unless the buyer wrote to him by a fixed day instructing him to send the merchandise, the sale would be void. The buyer claims that he orally informed the seller that he stood by the sale. The seller may say, “Until you write to me, I retain the right to retract.” This is all the more so if the oral notice was unwitnessed, but even if witnessed the seller can explain that he specifically required a letter because a signed writing would provide reliable proof and avoid later dispute, especially where he held earnest money or where witnesses would later be unavailable. In one actual dispute, the seller said the agreement was that if the buyer failed to send notice affirming the sale, there would be no sale. The buyer said the agreement was the opposite, that only an affirmative letter cancelling the purchase would dissolve it, while silence left it in force. The seller argued that his version was supported by the parties’ stated purpose: by the arrival time of the mail, called the post, market information would have become known, and the buyer might then choose whichever position left him near profit and far from loss. The seller therefore wished the benefit of that market information to belong to him and fixed the time for the buyer’s letter before the post. A single witness later testified that the buyer had handed him a letter before the post, but travel delay caused the letter to reach the seller only afterward. The expression concerning the post might support the buyer, because if the buyer dispatched the letter before learning the market news, he did not exploit later information, and the fact that the letter arrived after the post should not matter. Even if the oral wording had said that the letter must reach the seller before the post, perhaps the disclosed commercial purpose should control. Yet as long as the letter had not reached the seller, the buyer could still retrieve it from the messenger, even from another city by sending an express rider. Thus the seller may argue that only actual arrival before the post eliminated the buyer’s power to reverse himself. Property cannot be extracted from the seller against that reasoning. If it were established that the condition depended only upon dispatch, the messenger’s testimony that he received the letter before the deadline would create a question of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear. Ordinarily that doctrine does not apply where the claimant knows that the defendant cannot know the fact, as Shach writes in siman 72, seif katan 51. But if the letter bore its date, the defendant could potentially know by reading it whether it was written before the post. Although the witness does not know whether a date was written, the buyer asserts that he certainly dated it. This could place the matter within the doctrine of one who cannot swear. One might object that letters are sometimes antedated, especially where the date benefits the writer, but perhaps a dated letter still makes the matter one the defendant ought to know, or at least one where the claimant alleges knowledge on the basis of circumstances. This requires study. In any event, because it is not proven whether the condition depended upon dispatch or arrival, the claimant bears the burden of proof. Even if one witness testified that the condition concerned dispatch and another that it concerned arrival, it appears that their testimony cannot be combined. Testimonies combine where they establish the same legal fact through differing routes, such as separate admissions, but here the two versions are contradictory descriptions of one condition. This requires further study. Finally, if a sale was conditioned to be retroactively void if payment was not made by a certain date, and the seller thereafter married, perhaps he cannot later say, “I regard myself as though I have received payment,” because his wife’s lien has already attached and he cannot prejudice third parties by that declaration. Nor would he be believed to do so. This requires careful study.
