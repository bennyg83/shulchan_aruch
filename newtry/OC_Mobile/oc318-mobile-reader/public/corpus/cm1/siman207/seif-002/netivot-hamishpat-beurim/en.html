Some say that this applies specifically when he acquired. See Sema, seif katan 6, through his statement that Beit Yosef explains the matter this way. Beit Yosef's position, according to one of his explanations, is that every conditional transaction is an asmachta unless the person says "from now" or the object has already entered the other party's possession. A present kinyan is treated as though the matter were already in the other party's possession. But if no kinyan was made at the time the condition was stated, and the kinyan occurred only afterward, the original undertaking remains an asmachta. Derishah also explains Beit Yosef this way. Beit Yosef, however, gives a second explanation: perhaps only actual chazakah helps, not every type of kinyan. Sema further explains Mechaber by distinguishing whether the seller's principal intent was the transaction itself or only fulfillment of the condition. Perishah's intent is to distinguish between the language "if," which indicates that the transaction depends primarily on fulfillment of the condition, and the language "on condition that," which indicates that the transaction itself is primary. Sema concludes that when Mechaber later says, "unless he tells him, acquire from now," those words do not refer only to the immediately preceding clause. According to this reading, the basic distinction is that the first clause concerns language of "if," while the later clause concerns "on condition that," and certainly "from now" acquires. All of this is strained. In my view Rambam holds that even a kinyan made with future-tense language such as "I will give" can be effective. Ramban, cited by Beit Yosef in the same discussion, says likewise. Beit Yosef in siman 206 cites Ittur that "I will give" is ineffective because it is asmachta. Mahara Aharon Sasson, responsum 133, writes that an ordinary kinyan is presumed to be "from now," and therefore can cure future-tense language such as "I will give," unless the parties expressly made the kinyan itself conditional. Where the kinyan is expressly subject to a condition, we do not presume it to be presently effective, and future-tense language remains ineffective. This is Rambam's intent here. At the beginning of the chapter he says: one who acquired through one of the valid modes of acquisition, but imposed a condition upon the acquisition. Meaning, he sold presently, using language such as, "This field is sold to you on condition that..." In that case the transaction is a present sale subject to a condition. But if he made the kinyan now and said that if the condition is not fulfilled the recipient shall not acquire, Rambam means that he did not use language of a present sale but said, "I will give," or, "I will sell." In that case, even if the condition is fulfilled, the recipient does not acquire because the language is an asmachta-style future undertaking. Rambam explains this in his example. If he sold a house "on condition that you go to such-and-such place," meaning he used language transferring the house now subject to that condition, the sale can be effective. But if he said, "If you go to such-and-such place, I will sell it to you," and the person went and afterward made chazakah, he does not acquire. The original words were only a future promise and asmachta. Even making chazakah after fulfilling the condition does not help to revive that ineffective original promise, because at the time of the original statement there was no present acquisition. Certainly chazakah performed before fulfillment of the condition would not help either, just as a kinyan sudar that has already returned to its owner cannot support a later future acquisition.
