For this is asmachta. Bach sought to explain why the condition of the tribes of Gad and Reuven is effective even though it appears conditional and dependent on future events. He concluded that asmachta is valid by Torah law and invalid only rabbinically, bringing proof from a guarantor. The Shach called this unsupported, noting that a guarantor is exceptional because the benefit of the lender's reliance causes him to obligate himself fully. Yet Bach's basic proposition was already stated by Rabbeinu Natan of Chinon, cited by Mordechai. He writes that asmachta can acquire in consecrated property because asmachta acquires by Torah law, as shown by the Torah source for guaranty, "I will guarantee him." This does not mean that rabbinic invalidation is irrelevant to Torah consequences. Once the Sages ruled that asmachta does not acquire, practical ownership follows rabbinic law. Thus the authorities dispute chametz pledged conditionally before Pesach: even if by Torah law the conditional acquisition might operate, once rabbinic law says the asmachta does not acquire, the chametz can remain the Jew's for practical halachic purposes. This is comparable to Beit Yosef's ruling that kiddushin accomplished through a three party arrangement can have Torah effect because the Sages validly transferred the underlying money. Rabbinic property law can determine the practical Torah ownership result.
