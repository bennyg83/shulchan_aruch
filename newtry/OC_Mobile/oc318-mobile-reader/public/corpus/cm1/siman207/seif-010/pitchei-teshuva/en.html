See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 91, concerning a seat in the synagogue that belonged to an orphan girl and was sold by beit din to R. Zalman. A deed of sale was written, and at its end it stated that after the sale R. Zalman agreed that if, after six years, one of the orphan's guardians or brothers would come and pay him the stated sum, he would be obligated to sell the seat back to the orphan. Subsequently both the purchaser and the orphan died. The purchaser's heir sold the seat to another person, and the orphan's heir then came with the stipulated sum and demanded that the second purchaser return the seat by virtue of the condition. Chavot Yair answers at length that even had the first purchaser and the orphan still been alive, beit din could not have compelled him to accept the money and resell the seat. The record of the beit din sale contained no condition as part of the sale, nor was such a condition made at the time of sale. Rather, only afterward did the purchaser express his willingness, as a voluntary concession, without an undertaking or kinyan. Under the explanations of the authorities to Bava Metzia 65, this is merely verbal inducement and not a binding condition, as explained by the Tur and Beit Yosef in siman 207. Consequently, after R. Zalman's death, even if one were to argue from the wording "obligated" and from the strength of a beit din act that his commitment had been made in a legally effective manner, it was nevertheless only his personal undertaking and was not a condition attaching to the original sale. His heirs therefore need not perform it. This is comparable to one who binds himself by kinyan to sell a particular parcel to another after a year for a fixed sum and then dies, where surely his heirs are not compelled to carry out that sale. He further writes that our case is stronger still because the deed said that if one of the orphan's guardians or brothers would come and pay him. This implicates the rule of "to me, and not to my heirs," and the related question whether wording directed to a particular giver or recipient is exact. The Beit Yosef records several views. The Ramah, citing the Ritva in the name of the Ramah, holds that if either seller or buyer dies, the condition can no longer be fulfilled, because "to me" excludes heirs and "you shall give" may exclude one's heirs. Another view of the Rashba distinguishes between "to me," which is exact, and "you shall give," which is not. The Ritva himself disagrees with the Ramah and maintains that such exactness applies specifically to a get or a gift, but in a sale neither expression is necessarily restrictive, so payment by the seller or his heirs to the buyer or his heirs can suffice. Accordingly, even if R. Zalman's promise had originally been made in a form that legally bound him, once the orphan died and the person tendering payment was not one of the brothers named in the deed, the purchaser could refuse and plead that the restrictive reading should be followed. This is especially plausible because the beit din record stated that all was done for the orphan's benefit, indicating that the arrangement was intended specifically for her lifetime. Certainly after the first purchaser died, his heir could rely on the phrase "to me" and contend that the right was personal to the original purchaser. In the present case even the first purchaser's heir had died, so one would have to extend "to me" not only to heirs but even to purchasers from heirs, a proposition that even the Ritva may not have intended. Moreover, since the purchaser's later concession was voluntary, it resembles a gift, where even the Ritva agrees that personal wording is restrictive. In conclusion, however, there is no need to rest the decision on these nuances. The first purchaser's post-sale expression of willingness, made without kinyan, was void from the outset and amounted only to nonbinding words. Even if one insisted that the language of obligation and the force of the beit din instrument created some personal liability, there is no basis to compel his heir, much less a purchaser from that heir, to carry out what the first purchaser personally undertook after the sale. The second purchaser therefore acquired valid title and cannot be dispossessed. A responsum printed at the end of Torat HaShelamim, siman 7, rules likewise in a nearly identical case. See also Responsa Shevut Yaakov I, siman 165, concerning Reuven, who sold land without his wife's knowledge, while the purchaser agreed in the deed that if Reuven's wife objected the sale would be void. Reuven died, then his wife died, and their son sought to object in her place, claiming that he had inherited her right. Shevut Yaakov explains that although Ketubot 91 shows circumstances in which a son stands in his mother's place, that is where the mother had an independent right, such as a lien for her ketubah. Where her only right arises from a personal condition made at the time of sale, the condition may be understood as applying to her and not to her heirs. He therefore rules that the land cannot be removed from the purchaser in possession, in agreement with Chavot Yair.
