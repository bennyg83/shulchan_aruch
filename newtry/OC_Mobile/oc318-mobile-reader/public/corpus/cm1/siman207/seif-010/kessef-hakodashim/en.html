Seif 15, gloss. He instructed that the document state that they made a kinyan from him before an important beit din. A document provided that if the loan was not paid at maturity, a specified movable in the borrower's house would become definitively vested in the lender, provided that the lender then pay the borrower a specified sum. The document then listed other debts and conditions and concluded that everything had been effected by kinyan sudar, effective from now, before an important beit din. It is obvious that the kinyan sudar also applies to the clause vesting the movable. Since the document states 'from now' and 'before an important beit din,' there is no concern of asmachta. In monetary documents it is not customary to recite every technical form of the laws of conditions, such as the condition of the sons of Gad and Reuven. Only certain long-term instruments affecting land use such language. The practice relies on the view in Even HaEzer siman 38 that monetary conditions do not require the full formulation of the condition of the sons of Gad and Reuven, and the clause is therefore effective. If, however, several years passed and the lender never paid the specified additional sum, it appears that the movable did not vest in him, according to the principle in Yoreh Deah siman 236 that where an act is described in a particular formulation, it must be carried out in that manner. I have discussed elsewhere the dispute there between the Shach in Nekudot HaKesef and the Taz, and the same reasoning applies here. If this note is later produced against an heir who claims that perhaps the decedent paid before maturity, the ordinary rule of staleness should apply: after six years for an iska note and after three years for an ordinary loan, as stated by Misgeret HaShulchan, because the movable remained in the borrower's possession and the document is in substance an ordinary debt instrument. If the movable had been deposited with a third party, perhaps the rule of staleness would not apply where the document did not provide that vesting occurs only upon the lender's additional payment. Where there is a clear explanation why the lender did not take possession, perhaps staleness does not apply even without such a clause; to some extent this resembles a pledge already in the lender's control. This requires careful study. To cure asmachta through kinyan sudar with 'from now' before an important beit din: if he expressly says 'before an important beit din,' two persons may suffice, and even in private if he later does not deny it. If he appears before the leading authority of his city, so that one need not expressly use the phrase 'important beit din,' there should nevertheless be two others with him so that they constitute a beit din. Although for adjudication a single judge accepted by the litigants may suffice, and their appearance before him is a form of acceptance, here the matter may not be comparable to acceptance of a judge. Therefore, if he is alone, the phrase 'before an important beit din' should be stated expressly. Yet whenever two others are present with the leading rabbi, even if they themselves are not qualified judges, the leading rabbi can join two laymen, because the concern with a panel of three is only that none be learned, whereas when the chief rabbi of the city is among them there is certainly one learned member. The Sema, seif katan 50, writes that where the property is deposited with a third party for the benefit of both sides, asmachta does not apply. It requires study whether he intends this as a practical ruling or merely to reconcile the Shulchan Aruch in seif 17 even according to the dissenting opinion in the gloss to seif 11. The Beit Yosef cites two rationales: one is that because it is an asmachta with respect to one party, it is likewise so with respect to the other, which accords with the Sema; the second is that wherever one did not confer a benefit, as in a loan, asmachta applies even when property is delivered to him or to a third party. Thus there is no decisive ruling between these reasons. I then saw that the Taz in seif 17 explicitly explains the case on account of the words 'from now.' His language appears to imply that even where one conferred a benefit, such as a loan, asmachta still applies. This requires study, for the Beit Yosef cited both reasons in the name of the Semag, while the Taz rules like the Rosh. Yet in seif 11 too there is no decisive ruling between the two views. Although the Shach writes there that according to the Maggid Mishneh, depositing with a third party is ineffective according to everyone, his words imply that this is only according to the Maggid Mishneh, while the gloss appears to equate depositing with a third party and direct delivery. The result may therefore remain doubtful. See the introduction to Pri Megadim, Yoreh Deah, concerning successive 'some say' formulations, and the note I recorded there. In a case of doubt one may compare siman 223, seif 2. For practical law concerning property held by a third party, Pri Megadim's method would require examining which opinion is the majority. In siman 223 the first opinion appears primary, since the Beit Yosef cites the Maggid Mishneh as finding it difficult to reconcile the Rambam, while the Rosh and Tur are later authorities and constitute the majority. Where there is no determination whether the original owner retains possession or the property is divided, perhaps all agree that where there is a double doubt the property should be divided. Some great authorities held that money may even be extracted from a present possessor through a double doubt. See Kuntrus HaSfeikot and Pnei Yehoshua at the beginning of Ketubot. Although the accepted rule is not to extract from a current possessor on that basis, perhaps one may remove the force of the original-owner presumption through a double doubt. This requires study. The parties had also used a situmta concerning conveyance of land. Land unquestionably remains in the possession of its owner, because situmta does not operate for land, as the Shach writes in siman 201 in the name of the Bach. On the preceding reasoning, several sugyot require study, and even in the case of exchanging a cow one may have a double doubt, whether the relevant event occurred earlier and whether the values were equal. See Ketzot HaChoshen, seif katan 10, who challenges the Noda BiYehuda. In my view, the Noda BiYehuda's words are fully correct. One who signs a document plainly accepts liability according to the legal force of its wording, as though the formalities had actually been performed. There is no distinction here between a kinyan and an undertaking, because he has already accepted an obligation measured by the force of the admission or oath stated in the document when he knowingly signed it. If a person merely signs a blank sheet and the holder later fills in language of acquisitions and oaths, the matter depends on what the signer knew about customary documentary clauses and reinforcements, as the Shach writes in simanim 41 and 42 and as I have discussed elsewhere. Whether the laws of bereirah apply here requires study. Ketzot HaChoshen, part one, discusses this somewhat, but I do not presently have it before me.
