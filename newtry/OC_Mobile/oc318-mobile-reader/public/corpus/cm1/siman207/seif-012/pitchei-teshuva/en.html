My earnest money is forfeited to you. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 5, was asked whether the common German expression meaning that the earnest money should be forfeited has the same force as saying, "My earnest money is waived to you." He answers that it plainly does. Even if the expression is one of gift rather than waiver, with money the language of gift is effective just as waiver is. Although Shulchan Aruch, siman 253:2, treats the expression "shall fall to so-and-so" as language of inheritance, ordinary speech distinguishes between the simple word "fall," which denotes inheritance, and the idiom "be forfeited," which denotes a gift or waiver. He adds that even if one merely gives an earnest payment in the customary commercial manner, without expressly saying it will be forfeited, contemporary mercantile usage shows that the parties do not intend the earnest money itself to effect acquisition. Rather, the evident intent is that the payment shall be forfeited to the other party if the giver withdraws. It is therefore not the case of "let my earnest money acquire" discussed in siman 190:10. He further discusses whether to rule like the dissenting view cited by the Rama and concludes that the seller may plead that the law follows the Mechaber, since the Rama did not conclude, as he commonly does, that the dissenting opinion is primary.
