See my sefer Mekor Chaim on Hilchot Pesach, siman 448, where I explained that there is a major distinction between an explicit condition and a disclosure of intent supported by a compelling factual presumption. Whenever the transaction later reverses because of an explicit condition, such as a gift "on condition that you return it," a new kinyan is required when the item is returned. Rashba on Sukkah 46 writes that the first sale or gift was a complete transfer. The recipient merely became personally obligated to fulfill the condition. By contrast, where the transaction is treated as limited because of a disclosure of intent together with a compelling presumption, the limitation operates as a reservation in the body of the transaction itself. Even if the disclosed intent and presumption amount to an expectation that the sale or gift will later return, no new kinyan is required at the time of return. There are several practical consequences to this distinction, as explained there.

See above, siman 120. See Shach, seif katan 8, who writes, "See Darkei Moshe, and this requires study." His intent appears to be that Darkei Moshe treats forced acceptance as a dispute among the authorities, while siman 120 appears to hold that according to everyone forced payment is valid. Shach therefore concludes that the matter requires study. In my view the cases can be distinguished. Here the issue can be the seller's condition that when he later gives the buyer the money back, the buyer must resell or return the property. In that setting, if the seller tenders the money even against the buyer's will, the tender is equivalent to repayment of a debt. Everyone agrees that repayment against the creditor's will is valid, as in siman 120. The buyer must then perform the return sale. If he refuses, he is violating the condition, and the original transaction can be undone according to its terms. But suppose the condition imposed on the buyer himself was to return the property gratuitously by a certain time, and at that time he attempted to return it while the original seller refused to accept because he wished the original sale to fail retroactively. Whether forced delivery counts as fulfillment of the condition depends on the dispute in Even HaEzer siman 143 whether fulfillment of a condition can be forced upon the receiving party. Darkei Moshe addresses this second type of case, where the transfer itself is fulfillment of a condition. Siman 120 addresses ordinary repayment. Therefore there is no contradiction. Thus, when the original seller must tender money back to the buyer and that money is substantively repayment, tender against the buyer's will is valid according to everyone. But where the condition itself requires a gift or return delivery to another person, forced delivery is subject to the dispute whether compelled acceptance counts as fulfillment of a condition.
