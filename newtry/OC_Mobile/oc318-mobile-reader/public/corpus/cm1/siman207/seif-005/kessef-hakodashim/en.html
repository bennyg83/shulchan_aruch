Seif 3. And it shall revert to him like land. Where a seller made clear that he urgently needed the money, and afterward the funds became subject to a lengthy attachment that was not imposed by the usual public proclamation, it requires consideration whether this changes the sale of land. If a co-owner sold his share of a house because his partner had sold his own share, and the partner's sale was later cancelled, perhaps he may say that he sold only because he did not wish to live with the purchaser, whereas now that his partner remains in the house as before, and certainly if the partner is his brother, he does not wish to sell. If one said that he was selling his share for the amount for which his partner had sold his share, and the purchaser stated that the partner had sold for a specified sum, but it was later discovered that the partner had received more, this disclosure of intent does not cancel the entire sale. It affects only the price, and he must be paid the same amount as his partner. If one sold a house by kinyan sudar and said that he wanted his wife to be paid in exchange for her kinyan sudar, and she then agreed, but afterward a new circumstance arose by which their son might acquire some right in the house, even though that circumstance had not been contemplated at the time and had not yet begun, this nevertheless appears to be a disclosure of intent capable of cancelling the sale because of the later development. Here the wife's consent concerns the very cause of the sale, because without her agreement there is no domestic peace, and this is unlike a disclosure concerning something that is not causally connected with the sale. Since her refusal is for the benefit of their son, who is also his son, there is no plausible claim that she was coached merely to say that she does not consent, and absent a specific allegation of collusion there is no basis to impose a cherem. By contrast, with respect to payment of a note, the debtor must specifically demand an oath. If one sold his tefillin because he had new ones, and expressly said at the time of sale, 'I am selling these because I have new ones,' and the new tefillin were later found invalid, it appears that there is a clear presumption sufficient to void the sale. Although the Rama writes that mere disclosure of intent does not suffice for movables, tefillin are different according to the Sema's reason, because people readily sell ordinary movables, while a person does not normally sell tefillin unless he has another pair. This is especially so where, in that time and place, tefillin themselves are readily available but competent scribes are not, for then the presumption is particularly strong.
