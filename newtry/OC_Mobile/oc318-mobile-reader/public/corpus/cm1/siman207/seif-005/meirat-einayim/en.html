Likewise, a seller or purchaser who stipulated that the purchased item be returned to him at a fixed time, or when he gives him the money, etc. It appears that the phrase "or when he gives him the money" also refers to the fixed time mentioned just before. The meaning is that either he stipulated that the item be given back as a gift at a fixed time, or that it be resold to him when he returns the money at that fixed time. Until then the field remains completely acquired by the purchaser. Therefore the purchaser may eat the produce and enjoy what he bought. If it is to be returned without payment at the fixed time, it resembles the Syrian form of mortgage, where at the end of the stipulated years the field leaves the mortgagee without further payment. If it is to be returned when the seller repays the money, it resembles a mortgage in a place where the borrower cannot redeem during the fixed term, which the Rosh permits even without deduction, as stated in Yoreh Deah at the beginning of siman 172. Even authorities who prohibit that mortgage would permit this transaction because the money was not given as a loan but as the price of a complete acquisition for a fixed period, no worse than a gift on condition of return. Therefore the ruling concludes that the sale is valid and the property returns as stipulated. This differs from a sale with no fixed period, where the seller stipulates that whenever he brings back the money the purchaser must return the field. Such a transaction is not truly a sale at all, but a loan, and the purchaser may not eat the produce, as the Tur and Mechaber write in the following seif. Both laws come from the Rambam in chapter 11 of Hilchot Mekhirah. This, in my view, is the proper explanation, unlike the implication of the Maggid Mishneh. Rashba also ruled concerning Reuven, who owed Shimon a maneh and sold him land for that maneh by a deed of sale without making chazakah, while Shimon wrote that if Reuven repaid the debt the sale would be void. Reuven later said he had repaid. Rashba ruled that Reuven is believed, and Shimon cannot say, "If the sale deed remains in my hand, what are you doing here?" Since the deed arose only because of the debt, the underlying obligation remains an oral loan.
