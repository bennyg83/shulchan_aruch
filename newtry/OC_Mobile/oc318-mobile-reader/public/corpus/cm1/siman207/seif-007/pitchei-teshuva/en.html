Unexpressed thoughts are not legally effective. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 10, discusses Reuven, who sold his house to Shimon. Levi gave Reuven a bill of exchange for the purchase price on Shimon's behalf, and Reuven accepted it as cash. Reuven and Shimon stipulated that Reuven must obtain governmental permission for the sale within six weeks, failing which the sale would be void, and the bill was deposited with a third party pending fulfillment. Reuven obtained the permit, but at the end of the six weeks Shimon was away on a distant journey, so completion was delayed until Shimon returned. Reuven then sought to complete the sale and Shimon agreed, but Levi argued that since the condition had not been fulfilled on time, the sale was void and his bill must be returned. Chatam Sofer writes that had Levi himself expressly conditioned his giving of the bill on completion of the sale within six weeks, his obligation would have ended when the period expired, even if the delay resulted from unavoidable accident caused by Reuven or Shimon. Levi would never have undertaken to leave his bill outstanding beyond the stated time; the Torah exempts the person prevented by accident, but accident does not impose liability on another. Here, however, Levi made no condition with Shimon. Shimon alone stipulated with Reuven. Shimon could therefore waive the condition or extend Reuven's time, and Levi could not object that he had given the bill only on the basis of the six-week limit, because he never made such a condition. Even though he heard the condition between Reuven and Shimon, his contrary intent was merely unexpressed thought. Even had Shimon made a statement for Levi's benefit that he would return the bill if the sale were not completed within six weeks, while Levi himself gave the bill without conditioning it, most poskim and Rama at the beginning of siman 207 would regard the statement as mere reassuring words rather than a binding condition. All the more so here, where the condition existed only between Reuven and Shimon. Levi's participation in negotiations would at most make him Shimon's agent or broker with respect to Reuven, not turn the condition into one governing Levi's bill. Chatam Sofer adds that even had Shimon not waived the condition and instead contested the sale, the transaction would still not necessarily be void, because the delay was accidental. The essential sale was complete and the deadline functioned as a self-imposed spur. Shimon's absence was a genuine accident, and the law excuses failure caused by accident. Chatam Sofer concludes that Levi has no claim against Reuven, who is not his litigant, and even Shimon has no claim because Shimon's own absence caused the unavoidable delay. The suggestion that Reuven should have deposited the permit with beit din is ineffective, as appears from Rashba cited in Beit Yosef, siman 73. The ruling is clear and without doubt.
