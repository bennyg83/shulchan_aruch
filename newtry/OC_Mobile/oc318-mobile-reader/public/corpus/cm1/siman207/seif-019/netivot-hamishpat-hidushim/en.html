See above, siman 209. Meaning, it is explained there that beit din does not descend to his property, but rather compels him to fulfill his oath. If he dies, the heirs are exempt, and if he obtains release from his oath he is freed from the obligation. This applies specifically where there was no kinyan concerning the matter, only an oath. But if there was a kinyan, except that it was not before an important beit din, the oath reinforces the kinyan, and we say that he certainly resolved conclusively and transferred ownership. Beit din then descends to his property, the heirs are also liable, and even if he obtains release from his oath he nevertheless remains liable. This is unlike siman 209, where ownership could not vest because a kinyan is ineffective for something not yet in existence. Here, however, the kinyan is effective in principle, and it is only because of asmachta that we say he did not resolve conclusively to transfer ownership; once he swore, he certainly did resolve conclusively and transfer ownership (Sema). The Shach and Taz disagree and maintain that even if there was a kinyan not before an important beit din, his obligation exists only by virtue of the oath. This is entirely analogous to the case in siman 208 where one swore to uphold a sale. Likewise, if one obligated himself in a penalty and pays the penalty, the authorities dispute whether he must still fulfill the oath. Mahari Weil writes that the stringency of a handclasp oath was imposed only when it was given to that person himself, since it is like making a covenant, but not when the handclasp oath was given to another person (Shach).
In a document stating that he accepted upon himself. Even where it is known that he did not actually accept it, nevertheless, since he wrote this, he resolved conclusively and transferred ownership. It requires study whether, if he made a kinyan before witnesses, the prevailing custom to write in all documents that the parties accepted under ban and under a severe oath has the same effect as where he instructed that it be written (Sema). See Beurim, for according to the Shach and Taz, the statement in the document that he accepted upon himself under ban does not help.
There is one who disagrees. This is the Rivash, who maintains that in such a case it is not considered an oath (Sema).
In an asmachta involving charity. Although with an asmachta even delivery to an ordinary person is ineffective, with consecrated property it is effective. For the rule that a declaration to the Most High is like delivery to an ordinary person means that wherever delivery to an ordinary person would be effective in some manner, a declaration to the Most High is likewise effective. For this reason, a declaration to the Most High is also effective regarding something not yet in existence, as in siman 209 (Sema).
