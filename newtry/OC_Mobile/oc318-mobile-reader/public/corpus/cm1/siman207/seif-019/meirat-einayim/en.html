A vow, oath, or solemn hand pledge is effective despite asmakhta. Mordechai writes this in the chapter Shor Shenagach Arbaah VeChamishah in Maharam's name, deriving it from vows stated conditionally in Scripture, such as Yaakov's vow and the vow concerning the Canaanite king. Rama refers to siman 209:4, where he writes that one obligated by an oath concerning something not yet in existence may be released from his oath and, if he dies, his heirs need not fulfill it. That ruling is from Rivash, responsum 335. Rama teaches that the same principle applies here. It appears clear, however, that Rama's reference is limited to a case where one merely undertakes by vow or oath and makes no effective kinyan. Mordechai and the Mechaber teach that the vow or oath binds him even though the underlying promise is an asmakhta and no proprietary kinyan was made. But where a kinyan was made upon a matter involving asmakhta and the party also swore to fulfill it, then he and his heirs are bound, and subsequent release from the oath does not undo the monetary kinyan. The oath strengthens the kinyan and removes the asmakhta no less than a kinyan before an eminent beit din. Rivash himself discusses one who made both a kinyan and an oath, and nevertheless says that release from the oath works and the heirs are not bound. But his reasoning depends upon the Geonic view that even a kinyan before an eminent beit din is insufficient without "from now." According to our ruling, following Rabbeinu Tam, Rambam, and others, that a kinyan before an eminent beit din is effective without an express "from now," an oath can likewise strengthen a kinyan and eliminate the asmakhta. Rivash's responsum itself makes this distinction. He writes that according to the questioner's approach, which follows Rabbeinu Tam, perhaps the oath functions like an eminent beit din, analogous to consecrated property, where speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person. But according to the Geonim, who require "from now," even if consecrated property has no law of asmakhta because its declaration is treated as immediate delivery, an oath to pay the excess up to one hundred gold coins is not itself a "from now" acquisition. Therefore, although the party must fulfill his oath, beit din does not descend upon his property for the excess, his heirs are not liable after his death, and if he receives valid release from his oath during life, the personal obligation ceases. This demonstrates that Rama's reference to siman 209 concerns a case with no otherwise effective kinyan. There is no disagreement regarding the three consequences Rivash mentions where obligation rests only on the oath. In siman 209 Rama writes that a kinyan does not acquire property not yet in existence. That is different from the present case, where the property exists and the only defect is asmakhta. An oath may strengthen such a kinyan. This also explains why there Rama did not adopt Rivash's separate rationale based on lack of "from now." The real reason there is that no kinyan can presently take effect upon something not yet in existence. Examine this carefully.
Likewise, if the document states that he accepted upon himself, etc. Meaning even though he certainly did not make such an acceptance orally and the phrase is merely part of the scribal formula. Maharam Padua states this explicitly in responsum 50. He expressed doubt whether it is effective, while Rama rules that it is, comparing it to a document stating that a kinyan was made before an eminent beit din even though we know it was not, which is effective as stated in seif 15. If so, just as there we require at least that the obligor instructed the witnesses to write a deed, thereby accepting all the customary strengthened formulations that scribes use, the same should be required here. This raises a practical question in our time. Anyone who makes a kinyan before witnesses is presumed to intend that they write a fully strengthened document according to customary forms, and those forms routinely state that he accepted the obligation under severe oath and solemn hand pledge. If so, perhaps no doctrine of asmakhta would remain for documents executed by kinyan before witnesses. This requires study.

If one vows something involving asmakhta to charity or consecrated property, it is acquired, as explained in Yoreh Deah. The source is Mordechai in the chapter Shor Shenagach Arbaah VeChamishah. Rabbi Shlomo asked Maharam whether a declaration containing asmakhta is effective for consecrated property. Although speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person, perhaps it should be no stronger than actual delivery to an ordinary person, which does not overcome asmakhta without an eminent beit din. Maharam answered that "speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person" does not mean like bare delivery in one particular form. It means that the declaration has whatever legal strength actual delivery or acquisition would have in an ordinary transaction. He therefore concludes that even if one says, "When this object comes into my possession I will give it to consecrated property," although the case includes both asmakhta and something not yet in existence, he must fulfill the vow. With an ordinary person he could at least transfer the property after it comes into existence by delivery or kinyan. For consecrated property, the earlier declaration itself becomes a vow obligating him to give it once it comes into existence. The same applies to asmakhta. This explains Rama here and in siman 209:4, where he says that vow and oath are effective even regarding something not yet in existence. See what I wrote above.
