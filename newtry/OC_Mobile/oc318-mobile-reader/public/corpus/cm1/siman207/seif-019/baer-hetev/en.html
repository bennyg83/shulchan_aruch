And an oath. See the Sema, who discusses this at length and concludes that according to our ruling, that a kinyan before a distinguished beit din is effective even without saying 'from now,' an oath is likewise effective, as the Rivash answered his questioner. The Bach disagrees and says that the Rivash wrote this only according to the questioner's premise but did not himself accept it. This appears primary, and it is explicit in Responsa Rivash, siman 341. The Taz likewise disagrees with the Sema. See there. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 16; Responsa R. Aharon Sasson, end of siman 64; and Responsa Raanach, siman 66. Mahari Weil, siman 137, writes that were he not afraid to disagree with his colleagues, he would say that a handshake was treated stringently only when the handshake was given directly to the person for whom the act was to be performed, as with Tzidkiyahu and Nevuchadnetzar and with a covenant, but in his case, since the handshake was not given to the betrothed man himself, one should not be so stringent. This is a strong reasoning because the basis of the stringency is that a handshake resembles making a covenant. See Responsa Rama, simanim 49 and 108; Responsa Rashach, part 2, siman 63 and end of siman 144, and part 3, simanim 30 and 105; and Responsa Raanach, siman 34. Shach.
That he accepted. Meaning, even though he certainly did not expressly accept this, it is a standard scribal clause that is routinely written. Responsa Maharam Padua, siman 50, states this explicitly, although he was uncertain whether it is effective. The Rama rules that it is effective and compares it to a deed stating that the party made a kinyan before a distinguished beit din, which is effective even when it is known that no such kinyan occurred, as stated in seif 15. From there it follows that, just as there the obligor must at least tell the witnesses to write the deed, allowing us to say that he accepted whatever formal language the scribes would use, so too here. Yet this requires study. Nowadays, anyone who makes a kinyan before witnesses intends that they write the deed with all customary strengthening clauses. If so, there would seemingly be no law of asmachta in deeds executed by kinyan before witnesses where the scribes write that he accepted everything under severe cherem and a Torah oath. This requires study. Sema.
Acquires. The Mordechai states in the name of Maharam that even if one says, 'When this property comes into my possession I will give it to hekdesh,' although there are two deficiencies, asmachta and property not yet in existence, he is nevertheless obligated to fulfill his vow. The reason is that with respect to an ordinary person, this money could at least be transferred once it comes into existence by delivery or kinyan. Therefore, for sacred property, the declaration made before it exists is effective to obligate him to give it once it comes into existence, because the statement becomes a vow. The same applies to asmachta. See there.
