A vow, an oath, and a handshake pledge. Rivash rules that if a person dies while bound only by an oath, his heirs are not personally required to fulfill his oath. This raises a separate question in the opposite direction. If one swore to give money to a recipient and the recipient died, must the promisor fulfill the oath by giving the money to the recipient's heirs? Mordechai indicates that he must. He cites a dispute concerning one who vowed money to marry off an orphan girl and she died. Rabbi Chaim Kohen exempted the donor because his undertaking was specifically to enable her marriage. Rabbi Avi HaEzri held that since a vow to a poor person has binding force, the heirs acquire the benefit unless the original wording itself shows that the gift was limited to that specific purpose. The dispute therefore turned on presumed intent, not on a rule that an oath or vow can never benefit heirs. Shiltei Gibborim, however, cites Rabbi Yitzchak of Vienna as saying that where the obligation is specifically one of charity, the heirs may not receive because the donor intended a mitzvah, not an unrestricted enrichment of heirs. If the intended poor recipient died, especially where the heirs are not poor, the charitable vow may have no remaining object. That limitation belongs to charity. A personal oath to give to a named person is different. An oath binds regardless of whether the recipient is rich or poor. If the recipient dies, the promisor should therefore fulfill his oath to the recipient's heirs. The law of recognized priestly recipients supports this. A firstborn can take a double share in priestly gifts that were destined for his father, even though the father had not yet physically received them, because the giver may not retract from the small gift. If a small promised gift can be treated as sufficiently established to pass to heirs, all the more a binding oath should have to be fulfilled to the recipient's successors.
If the document states that he accepted upon himself an oath and ban. If another person wrote the document and the signer signed without reading it, and witnesses know that he never read or understood that an oath was included, I do not believe he is bound by the oath. This differs from the monetary rule that one who signs a document is bound by what is written even if he cannot read. In monetary law he is treated as having relied on the scribe and accepted the writing, just as a party can entrust legal credibility to a third party. That is a law of reliance and authorization between people. An oath and ban are matters between a person and Heaven. They do not create a kinyan by themselves. If beit din knows with certainty that the signer never knew of the oath language and never intended to swear, there is no prohibition from which beit din must separate him. The fact that his signature binds him monetarily does not create an oath that never occurred. Noda BiYehudah writes more broadly that by signing one accepts every term, including an oath. In my view that is correct only if the oath language itself creates or strengthens a monetary kinyan. But where the entire legal force of the clause is the religious prohibition of an oath, and it is known that he never read or accepted it, no oath exists. This is especially so regarding future property or property outside his possession, where the clause has no acquisition effect and would rest solely on the prohibition.
