With a gift. In siman 207 it is explained that with a gift, disclosure of intent is legally significant, and even unexpressed intention may sometimes be taken into account. This appears to apply also to movable property, despite what was written earlier that with movable property a mere disclosure of intent does not suffice because he should have stipulated expressly. Regarding customary penalties imposed in business dealings, it requires study whether to classify the arrangement as a gift or a sale. Where the transaction carries expected profit, it appears sale-like. Even if there is no actual profit, one might initially still treat it as a sale because there was at least a possibility of profit. This may be learned from siman 306 regarding a shochet who receives the intestines from validly slaughtered animals as compensation. See Ketzot HaChoshen at the end of siman 227 regarding an error between gold and copper. Our case is not identical to that one, but resembles the case of intestines. Similarly, according to Rav Yosef's perutah benefit, even the possibility of benefit can make one a paid bailee. Chazal also said regarding artisans that through the benefit of being given an opportunity to earn wages he becomes a paid bailee, even if he ultimately loses the wage. This appears to run against the analysis of Ketzot HaChoshen, though perhaps not conclusively. The distinction may be that even someone who is not actually skilled in the work but thinks he is may nevertheless be considered a paid bailee because he anticipates earning from it. He may later learn the craft or subcontract it and retain part of the profit, so there is a real benefit. By contrast, in the case discussed by Tashbetz concerning a goldsmith who misidentified copper as gold, the circumstances appear to have been such that even if the goldsmith had announced it was copper, there was no realistic possibility of profit from buying that piece. The only possible profit existed if it truly was gold. Thus the benefit could not be connected to that transaction at all, unlike the artisan who still has a real avenue of gain. I have not found a decisive proof for this distinction, and perhaps Tashbetz exempted the goldsmith because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Regarding the Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 207, seif 11, based on dina d'garmi, there is no difficulty, because the damage was not certain: even without his statement the owner might have chosen to slaughter because most animals are kosher. What is written there in the name of Beit Yosef in the first answer may similarly mean that the parties never intended a full guaranty secured by the pledge. Had they intended that, the pledge could support a real guaranty. Perhaps this also explains why one cannot infer full reliance merely from the fact that he trusted the expert. This requires further study.
