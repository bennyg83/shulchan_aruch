Movable property. Baer Heitev states that with respect to a purchaser there is no distinction between land and movable property and suggests this from Beit Yosef. Tur, siman 230, however, records a dispute between Rabbeinu Yonah and Rabbeinu Chananel. Rabbeinu Yonah writes that one who buys wine and says that he intends to take it to a particular place remains bound by the purchase even if the wine falls in price before reaching that place. A seller who sells land because he intends to move to Eretz Yisrael may have the sale voided when the move fails, because sellers often sell under pressure generated by some external need. A purchaser, by contrast, voluntarily buys in the hope of profit and assumes the ordinary risk of loss. Rabbeinu Chananel and Ramah disagree and see no distinction between seller and purchaser, though Ramah limits the rule to a case where payment was expressly deferred until arrival. Derishah explains that although elsewhere the Tur says disclosure of intent ordinarily does not affect a sale of movable property, wine bought for resale in a higher-priced market is comparable to land sold because of a special need. Nevertheless, Rabbeinu Yonah still distinguishes a voluntary purchaser from a pressured seller. Beit Yosef appears to decide like Rabbeinu Chananel, and the Tur's placement of his view last may likewise indicate preference for it. Chatam Sofer, siman 70, finds this distinction reflected in Tosafot, Ketubot 47b. He further distinguishes between an event affecting the seller or purchaser and an event affecting the purchased object itself. If a person sold property in order to buy a cow and then could not obtain the cow, or a purchaser bought something for a specific need that later disappeared, an implied condition may affect the sale. But if the transaction was complete and only afterward the purchased cow was found treifah or died, the event occurred in the object itself after acquisition and no implied condition is inferred. He uses this to explain why Tur, when describing a wine investment, first mentions both depreciation and souring, but in the later dispute concerning a purchaser mentions only depreciation: souring resembles the purchased cow becoming treifah and is not governed by implied intent. Netivot later applies disclosure of intent to a wartime flax contract, and Beit Ephraim discusses the same case at length. He cites Rashbash in the name of Rashbetz concerning merchants who bought flax on credit to load onto a ship, but the ship was prevented from sailing; the flax was returned to the original owners because it was clearly purchased only for shipment on that vessel. Maharashdam has a similar case concerning houses purchased in reliance on funds expected from a third party. Netivot suggests that even Rabbeinu Yonah would recognize disclosure of intent for a purchaser of land, because land cannot be moved elsewhere. Thus, if one living in one city buys a house or shop in another expressly because he intends to relocate there, and the relocation later becomes impossible or the local residents prevent him from settling, the sale is void. In my humble opinion, the plain words of Rabbeinu Yonah do not support this. His central reason is that a seller may act under pressure, while a purchaser acts voluntarily. On that reasoning disclosure of intent should not help a purchaser, whether of land or movable property. Derishah likewise implies this, and even Rabbeinu Chananel's acceptance of disclosure for movable purchases need not prove the same for land. Chatam Sofer explicitly writes that, as a rule, an implied condition is not presumed for a purchaser of land because a person is pleased to acquire land even with limited utility. He notes, however, that where there is a risk of losing the land entirely, as in the rule that omission of warranty language is treated as a scribal error because a person does not throw away his money, the presumption is different. Chatam Sofer also discusses individuals who purchased communal residency rights and later objected when the burial society imposed a new assessment for burial plots. He rejects their claim that they purchased only on the assumption that no such charge would be imposed. A communal residency right is itself like acquisition of enduring real property, including a share in the synagogue, mikvah, and communal assets; these purchasers had not mentioned burial when they bought, and there was no universal presumption that communal membership guaranteed burial free of future assessments. In addition, implied intent can void a transaction when the contemplated commercial purpose never began, such as wine bought solely for a market that later collapsed before resale. Once the purchaser has enjoyed the purchased right for a substantial time, however, later circumstances do not undo the original transaction. See also below, siman 292, concerning one who claimed he had purchased goods only in order to seize them for an existing debt, and the authorities cited there. As to Rama's statement that one must formulate a formal condition, Chatam Sofer, siman 102, explains that even according to those who do not require a doubled condition, one must at least use conditional language such as “if I ascend to Eretz Yisrael, the objects or land shall be sold.” Saying merely, “because I wish to ascend to Eretz Yisrael I am selling,” is not the language of a condition; its effect rests only on the Sages' assessment that he otherwise would not have sold, as though he had stipulated. This also explains Rashi's distinction between land and movable property. See Maharibal III, siman 91, on the distinction between the language “if” and the language “because.”
