(Seif 7: The purchaser, of his own volition.) To explain the words of the Ramah and resolve the Tur’s objection, it appears to me as follows. In the chapter Eizehu Neshech we learned: If one sold another a house or a field and said, “When I have money, return it to me,” the purchaser may not consume the produce, because there is no genuine sale, since he is obligated to return it when the seller obtains money. If the purchaser said, “When you have money, I will return it to you,” he may consume the produce. The Gemara asks: What is the difference between the first clause and the second? Rava answers that in the second clause the purchaser said that he would do so of his own volition. A man once sold land to another without a warranty. The seller saw that the purchaser was distressed and said to him, “Why are you distressed? If the land is taken from you, I will reimburse you with the best land, including enhancement and produce.” Ameimar said that these were merely words of reassurance. Rav Ashi said to him: Why are they merely words of reassurance? Because the purchaser is the one who would ordinarily need to stipulate concerning a matter for his own benefit, whereas here the seller made the stipulation. But in the baraita, where it teaches, “When you have money,” it is the seller who would ordinarily need to stipulate. Yet Rava says that the second clause applies only where the purchaser said that he would return it of his own volition. This implies that had he not said “of my own volition,” the purchaser’s stipulation would be effective and he would be forbidden to consume the produce. Ameimar answered: Do not say that the purchaser expressly said “of my own volition.” Rather, the statement is regarded as though he had said “of my own volition.” Rashi explains that the permission above is principally because the purchaser stipulated for the seller’s benefit, so it is not a binding condition at all. On this basis the Tur challenges the Rambam: the statement is not a condition at all. It appears to me, however, that the Rambam did not have in his text Rav Ashi’s formulation, “What is the reason that these are words of reassurance,” but rather the version found in the Rif: “Rav Ashi said to Ameimar, But Rava said that the second clause applies where he said, ‘of his own volition,’” and no more. There is strong proof for this. The Rambam, as quoted by the Mechaber in seif 1, rules alike whether the transferor or the recipient made the stipulation. This shows that it makes no difference to him whether one party stipulates for the other’s benefit. The Maggid Mishneh likewise states this explicitly in the Rambam’s name in chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah. The Rambam therefore necessarily did not have the reasoning, “Why are they words of reassurance?” Rather, he explains that they were words of reassurance because the seller did not speak as a genuine and sincere undertaking, but merely to calm the purchaser’s distress, as the phrase “words of reassurance” is explained in that same sugya regarding the dying woman. According to this, had the seller genuinely and sincerely accepted the obligation upon himself, it would have been effective, as Rashi writes regarding that woman. Rav Ashi therefore asks Ameimar: Rava found no way to permit the purchaser in the second clause of the baraita except where he said “of my own volition,” so that the condition is not binding and the purchaser may consume the produce. Without that, the purchaser would be forbidden. Yet the purchaser’s statement, “When you have money,” was merely reassurance and not a genuine undertaking. The produce should therefore be permitted because there is no condition at all. The baraita’s second clause addresses only whether the purchaser may consume the produce, not whether the condition is enforceable. Why, then, did Rava make the permission depend specifically upon the words “of my own volition”? Ameimar answers that this is indeed the point. The statement is regarded as though the purchaser said, “of my own volition,” and the produce is permitted because there is no condition. The purchaser spoke only by way of reassurance. From this we learn that if the purchaser truly and willingly undertakes to return the property when the seller gives him the money, the condition is binding, and there is no prohibition of interest because he acts from his own choice rather than from an obligation imposed by the sale. In any event, we do not rule according to this conclusion, for in seif 1 we rule in accordance with the Rosh that when one party states a benefit for the other at the conclusion of the sale, it is merely reassurance. Here too, after an unconditional sale, the purchaser stated a benefit for the seller. The Rambam’s view is nevertheless well explained according to what I have written, although it is not the practical halacha.
