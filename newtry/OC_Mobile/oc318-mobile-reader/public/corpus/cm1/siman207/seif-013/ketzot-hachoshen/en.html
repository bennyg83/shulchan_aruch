Even if he gave collateral for the money. Rosh was asked about money won in gambling. Afterward the losing party gave collateral for the amount, later retrieved the collateral, and converted the amount into a loan. Rosh ruled that payment was required. Rivash understood Rosh through the law that a mistaken waiver can still be a waiver in a sale. Thus, although the original gambling obligation involved asmachta, once the loser actually paid, gave collateral, or formally converted the amount into a loan, the earlier asmachta was effectively displaced. Rivash also writes that if a person now creates a new and genuine debt instrument, the new obligation can stand. Yet the original responsa of Rosh require careful reading. Rosh says that if the new kinyan or debt document explicitly says it is for the prior gambling debt, and that gambling obligation was an invalid asmachta, the new kinyan does not help. Only where the new document can be understood as an independent obligation, not demonstrably based on the gambling debt, can it be enforced. Nor do I understand how the mere pledge itself should create a new obligation under the ordinary rule, "there is no maneh here and no pledge here." A pledge is only security for an existing personal debt. If the body of the debtor was never obligated, the property cannot become encumbered as guarantor. The correct understanding of Rosh is different. His responsum says that gambling is not an absolute asmachta in the same way as ordinary conditional obligations. We rule that ordinary gambling can be acquired because neither side controls the outcome and each fully intends to obligate himself. Where they gamble only on credit, beit din may still refuse to extract because of the asmachta concern, but the underlying obligation is not treated as robbery in the same way. If the loser voluntarily pays, the recipient may keep it. Therefore, when the loser gives collateral for the gambling debt, the pledge can attach because the person's body has a real underlying obligation, even though beit din would not have extracted payment without the collateral. Once the pledge has attached, returning it in exchange for the debtor's renewed promise leaves a valid obligation to pay. This explanation fits Rosh's own wording, where he says, in effect, that gambling asmachta is acquired in principle and is not treated as theft, even though beit din does not ordinarily extract the money. It also explains why the pledge can work despite the rule requiring a prior personal obligation. The result is limited to gambling, where the asmachta is incomplete and the participant fully enters the risk because he too hopes to gain. In an ordinary asmachta, where no personal obligation ever takes effect at all, giving collateral does nothing. There is no personal debtor and therefore no security interest. This is, in my view, the correct reading of Rosh.
Acquired by both of them. My son, the distinguished Rabbi David, asked how the board can be acquired when it belongs jointly to both parties. In Perek HaSefinah it is explained that a jointly owned courtyard does not enable the partners to acquire from one another unless the goods are measured and placed into the purchaser's own container. Tosafot in the first chapter of Kiddushin likewise states this. If so, how can one partner acquire from the other through a courtyard that belongs to them both? At present this requires study.
