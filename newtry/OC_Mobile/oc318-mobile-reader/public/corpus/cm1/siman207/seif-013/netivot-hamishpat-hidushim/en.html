Even if the people there were willing to sell and the agent negligently failed to buy, he is nevertheless exempt, because the initial undertaking itself was an asmachta and never created an obligation:
"If I leave it fallow and do not work it." For example, a sharecropper accepts a field and stipulates that if he does not cultivate it he will pay the owner's loss. If he leaves it fallow, he is liable because he pays only the actual loss and the undertaking is not exaggerated. Sema. See the Beurim. The same applies where the other party suffers no loss and the promisor likewise incurs no disproportionate loss, such as sale of movables at their fair value at the proper time of sale under a condition entirely within the seller's control. That is not exaggerated and the acquisition is effective. But sale of land, or movables before their ordinary time of sale, is considered exaggerated because people do not normally sell then, and the acquisition is ineffective. Where the commitment is not exaggerated, even if he fails to fulfill the condition because of a common unavoidable event, he remains liable, and with a kinyan the obligation is effective:
He transfers to the purchaser subject to a condition. Every conditional arrangement can involve asmachta. Therefore, where the condition depends on something entirely outside his control, such as rainfall, it is not an asmachta, similar to gambling. Where it depends entirely on his own control, such as sale of land even at fair value, or movables before their normal time of sale, the commitment is exaggerated and ineffective. Movables sold at fair value at the proper time of sale involve no exaggeration and the transaction is effective. Where the condition depends on others or on the purchaser, it is an asmachta in every case:
But if it is not entirely within his control. Specifically with purchasing wine, which people ordinarily sell, he may rely on the expectation that they will certainly sell to him. Nevertheless, because the matter is not entirely within his control, we say he does not fully commit himself, for had he known they would refuse he would not have imposed the penalty upon himself. Gambling is different, because there is no independent circumstance upon which he can reasonably rely. Therefore he fully commits to the stake. Sema:
On credit, we do not extract. Although the gambling arrangement is not necessarily an asmachta, it still does not create a fully enforceable debt collectible through beit din. Sema. See the Beurim. Even if another valid kinyan occurred in a manner that would transfer the money, it still does not help unless they are gambling with money already set aside and removed from the giver's control, so that no later collection is required. The dissenting view holds that the gaming board itself must belong to both players, so that the board, functioning as their shared courtyard and domain, immediately acquires the money for whichever one wins. Sema. See the Beurim. According to this view, even another kinyan together with money set aside is ineffective if the board is not jointly owned, because so long as the money has not entered the winner's domain, collection is treated as judicial extraction that cannot be compelled unless the arrangement was made before an important beit din.
