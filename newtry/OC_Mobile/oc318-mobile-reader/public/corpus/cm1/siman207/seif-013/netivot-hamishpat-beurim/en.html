With ready money. This opinion necessarily follows the first rationale cited by Sema in seif katan 33: even where an asmachta has some form of acquisition, beit din does not extract money under it. This view does not require the gaming board itself to belong jointly to both players, so there must have been some other kinyan. If there was a valid kinyan, why should it matter whether the money was physically prepared or merely owed on credit? The answer must be that even a formally acquired asmachta is not extracted by beit din unless the money has already left the loser's possession. This limitation applies specifically to gambling-type asmachta, where the person exaggerates a contingent undertaking. But in an asmachta concerning something fully within one's control, without exaggeration, the acquisition can be enforceable even through beit din, as explained above regarding "I will pay the best value." It further appears that even according to the second opinion, which requires the gaming board to be jointly owned because it follows Sema's second rationale, the same principle remains: although an asmachta can acquire in some sense, beit din does not ordinarily extract money under it. That is why those authorities require specifically that the board belong to both. An ordinary independent kinyan would not suffice, because so long as the money has not entered the winner's domain, extracting it would require beit din to remove property from the loser. But if the board belongs to both, the money immediately enters a jointly controlled domain from which the eventual winner receives it without a new act of judicial extraction. Proof appears from Tosafot in Sanhedrin 25, who write that the board must belong to both; if they play on credit, even a kinyan does not help unless they make the kinyan before an important beit din, as in Nedarim. At first glance their proof from Nedarim is difficult. Nedarim deals with a true asmachta, whereas gambling is said not to be asmachta according to the opinion they are explaining. Their intent must be this: even where an asmachta creates some acquisition, ordinary beit din still does not extract money under it. A kinyan before an important beit din is stronger and can support actual extraction. Nedarim proves that such a beit din can enforce even a true asmachta. Tur and Mechaber in seif 15 likewise write without qualification that an asmachta acquired before an important beit din is binding, implying that even extraction is possible. Accordingly, Tosafot's requirement that the board be jointly owned is not merely to create a kinyan. Even if some other complete kinyan occurred, they still require the board to be joint so that the money enters the winner's effective domain immediately, without reliance on later judicial extraction. This also resolves the question I raised above in siman 176, seif katan 2, how a jointly owned board can acquire for one partner from the other when a jointly owned courtyard ordinarily does not allow partners to acquire from one another. Here the purpose is not that the board alone creates the full kinyan. There may already be another kinyan. The joint board is needed so that once the result is determined, the money is already in a domain from which the winner can take without a new act of extraction. Rama did not repeat Tosafot's concluding clause that an important beit din suffices even when the board is not jointly owned because he relied on seif 15, where he states generally that kinyan before an important beit din works for asmachta.

Specifically when it is not within their control. Rama writes this to answer Tosafot's question in Bava Metzia 74 on Rabbeinu Tam's explanation of earnest money. He distinguishes that earnest money is different because the outcome is within the promisor's control. There remains Tosafot's second question: the Gemara compares gambling to the law of a nazir, yet in the nazir case there is no concept that, because each wants to acquire, each fully resolves to transfer. In my view the issue can be explained through Shach, seif katan 18. Shach cites Bach's view that d'oraisa an asmachta does acquire, and calls the statement almost prophetic. In truth, Shach overlooked that this is Mordechai's position, cited in Darkei Moshe to this siman. Shach asks: if asmachta acquires d'oraisa, why does the Gemara regarding a guarantor need the special reason that, in consideration of the lender trusting him, the guarantor fully resolves to obligate himself? Mordechai can answer that the rationale of "he fully resolves to obligate himself" helps only if d'oraisa the kinyan is valid and the defect of asmachta is merely rabbinic. Such a strong evidentiary circumstance can overcome the rabbinic concern. But if d'oraisa an uncertain asmachta is no kinyan at all, the psychological reason that he fully resolves to obligate himself cannot by itself create a missing Torah kinyan. Accordingly, the dispute in Sanhedrin can be understood as follows. The opinion that a gambling asmachta does not acquire holds that asmachta is invalid d'oraisa. Therefore the rationale that because each participant wants the chance to acquire from the other he fully resolves to transfer does not help. The opinion that gambling does acquire holds that asmachta is valid d'oraisa and was invalidated only rabbinically. In gambling, because each participant wants his own chance to win, he fully resolves to transfer if he loses. That strong circumstance defeats the rabbinic asmachta concern. The Gemara then properly challenges this from the nazir case. There, if asmachta were valid d'oraisa even when contingent on uncertainty, one of the participants should become a nazir, as Tosafot explain. The fact that neither does shows that asmachta is invalid already d'oraisa. If so, the gambling rationale of "because he wants to acquire, he fully resolves to transfer" cannot cure the defect. The Gemara answers that the nazir case follows R. Yehudah in the name of R. Tarfon, who holds that nazir status was given only through a definite and explicit undertaking. The failure of nazir there therefore does not prove that ordinary monetary asmachta is invalid d'oraisa.
