Likewise, conditions that people make between themselves, even before witnesses and in writing, such as, "If this happens," or, "If you do this, I will give you one hundred," or, "I will transfer this house to you; but if it does not happen or you do not do it, I will not transfer or give it," do not effect acquisition even if the condition is ultimately fulfilled. Anyone who conditions, "If this happens," or, "If it does not happen," has not fully resolved to transfer ownership, because his mind still relies on the uncertainty whether the condition will occur. Rama: Some say there are three categories in the laws of asmachta. First, where performance is not in his control and depends on others, such as, "Buy wine for me from a certain place, and if you do not buy it you will owe me such and such," the condition is always an asmachta because the sellers may refuse, and it does not take effect. Second, where performance is within his control and the penalty is not exaggerated, such as, "If I leave the field fallow and do not work it, I will pay from the best of my property," it is not an asmachta and is binding. But if he exaggerates and says, "If I do not work it, I will pay one thousand zuz," it is an asmachta and is not binding. Therefore, when a seller transfers property on condition, "If you do such and such for me, you will acquire," it is an asmachta because fulfillment of the condition is not in the seller's control but in the purchaser's. Third, where the outcome is not in his control at all and does not depend on others, such as gambling, where he does not know whether he will win, some say that because he nevertheless accepted the condition, he fully resolves to transfer on account of the uncertainty. This applies when they play with money placed ready before them. If they gamble on credit, however, one cannot collect what the loser owes. Tur in the name of Ri. Some say that even if he gave a pledge for the money, it is ineffective, because where no money obligation exists the pledge cannot create one, as explained above in siman 190, seif 9. When money is placed ready on the gaming board, it is permitted and there is no asmachta. Mordechai, chapter Zeh Borer, contrary to those who prohibit gambling in every form. Hagahot Mordechai there. Some say it is permitted only when the board on which the money lies is jointly owned by both players. Tosafot there; Beit Yosef in the name of Ri. See siman 370. It is called asmachta only when the principal obligation itself depends on a condition. If a person obligates himself to another and says, "If he is alive, I will give it to him, and if not, I will give it to his heirs," this is not an asmachta, because in all events he is obligated to give it. The same applies to similar cases. Beit Yosef in the name of a responsum of Rashba. Some explain that gambling is not an asmachta because both parties condition reciprocal obligations and each may lose; because each wishes to acquire, he fully resolves to transfer. Therefore, if two people make a wager with one another, the obligation is binding if they made a kinyan, provided the outcome is likewise not within their control. If it is within their control, it is not binding, as explained regarding an earnest deposit below in seif 11. Tur and Mordechai, chapter Eizehu Neshech, in the name of Rabbeinu Tam. Even regarding gambling some disagree, which is why other explanations are required for gambling and for engagement penalties discussed below in seif 16.
