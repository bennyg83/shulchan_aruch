Shin) There, halachah 6. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this is a general rule learned from Bava Metzia 66b, from Rava's statement that any acquisition dependent on an "if" does not acquire. The Rashba, in a responsum cited by the Beit Yosef, was asked about Reuven, who owed Shimon one hundred and sold him land for that amount, writing a deed of sale, but Shimon did not take possession. Shimon then wrote to Reuven that if Reuven repaid the amount he owed him, the sale would be void. Reuven later claimed that he had repaid Shimon. The Rashba ruled that Reuven is believed, and Shimon cannot say, "Why is your deed of sale still in my possession?" Since the payment was not given on account of the sale but only as an oral loan, possession of the sale deed is not proof. See Sema, seif katan 6.

Alef) The Rosh writes in Responsa, rule 66, responsum 18, concerning one who won one thousand gold coins from another through gambling and afterward the loser made a kinyan chalipin obligating himself to pay the thousand. If he said, "I transfer to you the money to which I became obligated," or even made an unspecified kinyan where it was known that the obligation arose from gambling, he is exempt. In rule 72, responsum 11, the Rosh writes that if he later gave collateral for this obligation, then even if the collateral was subsequently returned, the amount becomes an enforceable loan and he must pay.
The authorities cited as the second view hold, to the contrary, that the principal case of asmachta is where performance is fully within his control and he exaggerated the penalty, for then one may say that he relied on his ability and truly intended to fulfill it. Where performance is not entirely within his control, their reasoning differs. This distinction is recorded by the Darkei Moshe in the name of the Ramban. According to the first view, however, a matter outside his control is itself asmachta. See Sema.
