Likewise, conditions that people stipulate, etc., "if such-and-such occurs," etc. See the Perishah, seif 14, where I wrote that even if he immediately made a formal kinyan on the matter, so long as he revealed that his principal intent was not to transfer ownership but only to secure fulfillment of the condition, it is considered an asmachta. See there and the Sema, seif katan 6, where I discussed this at length.
"Buy wine for me from such-and-such place," etc. Meaning, it is inexpensive in that location. Therefore he did not fully resolve to accept the penalty, because the undertaking depends specifically on buying there, and perhaps the sellers will not agree to sell to him.
"If I leave it fallow and do not cultivate it, etc." Meaning, one accepts another's field as an aris or renter to plow and sow it and obligates himself: "If I do not work it properly, I will pay you the loss from the best of my property." If he later fails to perform the required work, he must pay, because he did not accept an excessive or exaggerated obligation.
But if it is entirely beyond his control, etc., then he certainly fully resolves and transfers despite the uncertainty. Specifically where one can say that he relied upon an expected event, such as buying wine from a particular place where wine is regularly sold, can we say that he trusted it would certainly be sold to him. Even so, because it is not within his control, we say that he did not fully resolve to transfer if he fails; had he known they would refuse to sell, he would not have promised the penalty. A dice game differs. There is no external event before us upon which he can be said to have relied when making the promise. Therefore we say that each player fully resolved to transfer the money to the other if the other wins.
But if they play on credit, etc. The Rosh writes in Responsa, klal 66, siman 8, that one person won one thousand zuz from another in gambling, and afterward the loser made a kinyan chalipin obligating himself for the thousand zuz. The Rosh ruled that he must pay and there is no asmachta, provided the kinyan does not state that it arose from gambling. He writes similarly in Responsa, klal 72, that if the loser afterward gave collateral for it, even if the collateral was later returned, the amount became a loan obligation upon him and he must pay.
We do not extract it from him, etc. Meaning, although it is not an asmachta because he fully resolved to transfer, as explained, there is nevertheless no complete enforceable debt that beit din can collect. In the Derishah I wrote that some explain the reason as follows: even where there is no asmachta, every transfer requires a kinyan. When the money lies on the gaming board, that placement itself is the acquisition, because the board is lent or transferred to both players and whoever wins has the board acquire the money for him immediately, like his courtyard or domain. When they play on credit, there is no such kinyan.
Specifically where it is likewise not within their control, etc. It appears that these authorities do not distinguish between something wholly beyond his control and something partially beyond his control as explained above. To the contrary, they hold that the principal case of asmachta is where the matter is fully within his control and he exaggerates the undertaking, because one can say he relied upon his ability to perform and intended to do so. Where the matter is completely beyond his control, that rationale is absent. I wrote this approach in the Derishah, seif 17, and Darkei Moshe likewise cites Ramban to this effect. This is what Rama means by "as will be explained concerning earnest money below in seif 11": there it is considered an asmachta precisely because the choice whether to complete or cancel the sale is entirely within his control. According to the first view, by contrast, something outside his control is even more strongly an asmachta.
And even in this some disagree. Meaning, even when both elements are present, namely that the parties stipulate reciprocal penalties and the matter is not within their control, these authorities still consider it an asmachta. Rama's statement that "therefore they needed other reasons concerning gambling" refers specifically to these last dissenters. According to the immediately preceding opinion no additional reason is needed, because reciprocal stipulations in a matter outside either party's control suffice. Rama's mention of penalties in shidduchin, however, refers to both views, because according to both, a matter within the person's control ordinarily creates an asmachta. Why then are engagement penalties, which are within the parties' control, not treated as asmachta? Rama concludes that the reason will be explained below in seif 16: because one who breaches humiliates the other party, the stipulated sum is not regarded as exaggerated. It will also be explained there that the Spanish sages structured engagement penalties in a manner that avoids asmachta entirely. See there.
