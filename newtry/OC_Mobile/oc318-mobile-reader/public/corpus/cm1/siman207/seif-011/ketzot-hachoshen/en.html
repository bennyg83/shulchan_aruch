One who gives earnest money. The Tur records a responsum of Rosh. Reuven feared slaughtering his cow lest it prove tereifah. Shimon told him, "Slaughter it. If it is tereifah I will pay you such and such for the meat, and if it is kosher such and such." Shimon deposited collateral with a third person. After the cow proved tereifah, Shimon saw the loss and retracted. Rosh ruled that no purchase had been completed. There had only been words. The collateral did not complete the transaction, both because money does not acquire movables and because collateral without an underlying debt does not create acquisition. Beit Yosef asks why Shimon should not be liable as a guarantor. Reuven slaughtered on his instruction and otherwise might have sold the cow alive. He answers that a guarantor fully obligates himself because the lender relies upon him, while here Shimon supplied collateral and the reliance structure was different. Alternatively, Shimon did not enter as guarantor at all but as a purchaser; once no valid sale was completed, the attempted purchase has no effect. One can explain Rosh even more directly according to his own view in Kiddushin. A person who says, "Give a maneh on a rock," or, "Give a loaf to a dog and I will become obligated," is not liable under guaranty where the value reaches no human recipient. The same principle appears in the dispute whether saying, "Throw a maneh into the sea and I will obligate myself," creates liability. Here the instruction to slaughter the cow resembles throwing value away rather than extending value to a borrower. There is therefore no ordinary law of guaranty. I later found this explanation also given by Rabbi Ozer.
A more difficult question is why Shimon is not liable under the law of direct causation for the loss. Reuven slaughtered the cow because of Shimon's instruction. Rosh elsewhere rules that if a person tells an artisan, "Make this item for me tonight and I will take it tomorrow," and the artisan does so, but the customer then refuses because he purchased elsewhere, the customer must reimburse the artisan's loss. This is like workers who travel and find no work, where the employer must pay because they lost the day through his instruction. Sema similarly rules that if Reuven promises to lend Shimon and tells him to pay the scribe for preparing the documents, then retracts, Reuven must reimburse the scribe's fee that Shimon spent relying on him. Why should the cow case differ? Perhaps because most animals are kosher. Shimon reasonably assumed the slaughter would reveal a kosher animal. The harmful result was therefore an unusual accident. Even a direct tortfeasor can be exempt for a sufficiently unavoidable accident. Because the probability favored a kosher result, Shimon's instruction may not count as negligent causation of the loss. The point nevertheless requires further study.
