But if it is entirely beyond his control. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 34, concerning a commercial arrangement customary when merchants send goods to another country. One method is that the carrier receives a percentage fee and accepts responsibility for the goods, giving the merchant a promissory note for their value. If the goods arrive safely, the note is returned; if they are lost through mishap, he pays the amount of the note. Another method is that the carrier does not accept responsibility; a third party does so, gives the merchant a promissory note for the value of the goods, and receives a percentage as compensation for the risk, while the carrier separately receives his fee. The question was whether such an assumption of liability is legally binding or is merely mercantile custom, since it might seem to be asmachta and was not made by kinyan before an eminent beit din. Beit Ephraim rules that the first arrangement, where the carrier himself accepts responsibility, is unquestionably binding. He takes the goods into a place of danger and they are lost through his agency, so even without an express undertaking he may be liable, as in Bava Metzia 94a regarding one who leads entrusted property to a place frequented by wild beasts or robbers. Certainly an express stipulation can increase a custodian's liability, since a paid or unpaid custodian may condition himself to bear the liability of a borrower. Where a separate person accepts responsibility, one might initially regard it as asmachta. Yet although ordinary guaranty itself has an asmachta-like element, the guarantor becomes obligated because the creditor relied on him. Here the basis is stronger than in an ordinary guaranty. Beit Ephraim compares the matter to Responsa Rivash, siman 308, concerning an undertaking dependent on a ship's safe arrival, and concludes that this guarantee is effective when the goods are delivered, even according to the Rambam. Indeed, the guarantor may be viewed not merely as an ordinary guarantor but as the primary obligor, because the merchant releases the goods only in reliance on his note, and the carrier who takes possession acts in effect as his agent. Moreover, under the prevalent practice the guarantor gives an unconditional promissory note for a sum of money, without mentioning the goods. The arrangement can therefore be understood in the form of the Spanish sages' device described in siman 207: he presently obligates himself for the amount of the note, subject only to a condition that if the goods are returned safely the note is forgiven. Until then the note remains fully effective, with no defect of asmachta. Thus the Jewish mercantile custom is valid Torah law, and in any event established commercial custom itself has legal force. In siman 35 Beit Ephraim discusses a related case. After giving the promissory note, the guarantor learned that the route had become especially dangerous and attempted to withdraw, asking the merchant to recover the goods from the carrier. The merchant tried but was told that the goods were already underway and could not be returned. They were eventually lost. The guarantor argued that he had warned the merchant, while the merchant responded that extraordinary efforts to retrieve the goods were not his responsibility because the guarantor's note was in his possession and the burden lay on the guarantor. Beit Ephraim rules for the merchant. Since the guarantor originally accepted every risk until the goods would be delivered to their owner, he cannot free himself merely by shifting to the owner the burden of forcibly recovering them from the carrier. Especially where the merchant did make reasonable efforts and persuasion failed, the guarantor, at whose direction the goods had been released and who gave the note securing them, had to undertake any further coercive effort. If he declined to do so, he caused his own loss.
