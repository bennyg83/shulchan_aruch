After he obligated himself, they make a kinyan from the creditor, etc. The reason the Spanish sages used two acquisitions and two documents rather than write everything in one document, for example, "Reuven owes Shimon one hundred, and if such-and-such occurs or Reuven does such-and-such, Shimon hereby waives the debt from now," is that this would appear to be an asmachta. Since the release is written alongside the obligation, it appears as though Reuven said, "I owe you one hundred if I do not do such-and-such." For this reason Rama writes in the gloss that in some places they prepare two complete promissory notes and place them with a third party, etc. Even using the Spanish method, Reuven, who holds the waiver document if he performs the condition, could in theory produce it even if he failed to fulfill the condition and say, "You see that this debt was originally placed upon me only if I failed to fulfill such-and-such, and at the time I expected to fulfill it." This is clear.
"This debt is waived from now," etc., until "and this is our practice." These are Rambam's words. Although according to Rambam "from now" alone removes the problem of asmachta, as the Tur and Mechaber write in seif 14, one could still ask why this two-document method was needed. Even if one says that they did not make the kinyan from the obligor that he would be obligated from now, but only from the creditor that he would waive from now, why did they not simply arrange a kinyan from the obligor that he accepted the obligation from now, just as they arranged it with the creditor? Then two documents would not be needed. One can answer that they structured it this way as an additional safeguard, as I wrote in the Perishah. There is no need for Beit Yosef's answers that most penalties concern money, which cannot itself be acquired by chalipin, or that a specific object is required for the kinyan to attach to. Both answers are forced. Ordinarily, one who obligates himself monetarily undertakes liability in value, not in specific coins that cannot be acquired through chalipin. And when he obligates himself for a fixed sum, there is no more specific object of obligation than that. A person can even obligate himself regarding something not yet in existence, as written at the end of this siman and in siman 60. The Levush follows Beit Yosef's explanation. See there.
