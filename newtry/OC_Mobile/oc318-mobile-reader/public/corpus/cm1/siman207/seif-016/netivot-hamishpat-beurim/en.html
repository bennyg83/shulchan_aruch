They place both documents with a third party. The arrangement is that Reuven gives Shimon an unconditional debt instrument obligating himself, and Shimon then deposits it with a third party and says that if Reuven fulfills the condition, the debt is waived and the document is to be returned. Shimon then makes a corresponding document. Where the documents are placed with a third party rather than delivered directly to the beneficiary, each depositor must say at the time of deposit, “Acquire this for so-and-so,” because with a gift the word “give” is not automatically equivalent to “acquire.” Afterward each may state that if his fellow fulfills the condition, he waives the obligation. Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 56, says similarly. If fulfillment of the condition becomes impossible through ones, there is room to question the Spanish sages’ arrangement. Since the obligor first undertook an unconditional debt and only the beneficiary’s later waiver was conditional, perhaps the rule “the Torah exempts one who is compelled” does not apply and the debt remains. Alternatively, because the entire device was instituted only to avoid asmachta, perhaps it should be treated like an ordinary conditional obligation and ones should exempt. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah, rules that in this construction the obligor remains liable even where fulfillment became impossible through ones. The unconditional obligation must be stated expressly. If the parties first discussed all their conditions and only afterward made an unspecified kinyan, we presume the kinyan was made subject to the original conditions. Sema’s statement that two kinyanim and two documents are used raises no question regarding the need for two kinyanim; the later waiver itself also requires a kinyan, as Taz explains. Two separate documents, however, are not indispensable if witnesses know that the first obligation was expressly unconditional. If one tells the obligor in advance, “Undertake the debt unconditionally and he will afterward waive it conditionally,” that too is problematic, because the obligor can say he undertook only in reliance on the promised conditional waiver; the first kinyan is therefore effectively conditional. Sema also cites Beit Yosef that many penalties are monetary and money is not acquired through chalipin. This is difficult: even an unconditional transfer would then not help. If the obligation is framed as a personal debt, which can bind even regarding money, one can likewise formulate the conditional undertaking in an effective manner according to Rambam’s view that “from now” can cure asmachta. Taz’s discussion on this point is likewise difficult.
