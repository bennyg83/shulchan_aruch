Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes in his gloss to the Shach that this answer is insufficient. Even without the oral witness saying, 'I acquired it,' if he merely testifies to the loan, the borrower still should not be believed to say that he repaid, because there is one witness in the document and one oral witness, and there is no migo of claiming that the loan never occurred, since that claim would be contradicted by the two witnesses taken together. As to the Shach's underlying explanation, that since the witness says the debt was not repaid the borrower must swear to contradict him, this follows the Shach's approach in siman 75, seif katan 43, where he appears to accept Rabbeinu Yitzchak's answer that in the case of the silver ingot the claimant is not believed through a migo because the alternative claim would require him to swear against the witness. Therefore, where his present claim also contradicts the witness, he may rely on the migo. Rabbi Akiva Eiger adds that this appears from the Ran in Ketubot in the episode of the shepherd. The Ran asks that the claimant should have to swear against the shepherd. Why did he not ask more strongly that the claimant should not be believed to say, 'I seized it during the owner's lifetime,' through a migo that he could have claimed purchase, since a purchase claim would require an oath against the shepherd and would render him one who is obligated to swear but cannot, and therefore must pay? It must be that because his present claim also contradicts the shepherd, the migo remains available. However, according to Tosafot's second answer, also cited by the Shach in siman 75, seif katan 43, whenever a party does not contradict a witness under oath, the witness is treated as two. Thus, even if the witness also says that the debt was not repaid, since on the fact of the loan the borrower does not contradict him, the witness is treated as two on that point, and consequently the borrower is not believed to claim payment. Examine this carefully. See Netivot HaMishpat on this subject.
