[" Another witness in writing, etc. The sequence of the Mechaber’s ruling is as follows. There are three distinct cases. First, one witness in writing and one witness orally, without a kinyan, combine according to everyone to prevent the debtor from claiming, ‘This never occurred.’ Since they directly contradict that claim, their force is no less than that of two oral witnesses who contradict him. But if he claims, ‘I paid,’ the matter depends on the dispute in the following seif. According to the latter view there, under which a debtor who claims payment is believed with a heset oath because a single witness in a document is treated not as full documentary testimony but as oral testimony, the same is true here. Although the two witnesses combine, their combined evidence has only the status of oral testimony, and the debtor may claim payment. Second, if the oral witness says, ‘I effected a kinyan from him,’ the two combine to make the loan a documented loan even for purposes of preventing the debtor from claiming payment, even according to the latter view in the following seif. Since the oral witness says that he effected a kinyan, and we rule that an ordinary kinyan is presumed intended for writing, it is as though both witnesses wrote a document, and the debtor cannot claim payment. Nevertheless, it is not a full document for collection from encumbered property, because the two forms of testimony are not equal: one witness is in writing and the other testifies to a kinyan. Third, if the oral witness says, ‘The document was delivered in my presence,’ then it has the status of a full document and may be used to collect from encumbered property. The Rosh, cited by Beit Yosef, explains the reason: it is regarded as though there are two witnesses to delivery, because regarding the witness signed on the document we presume that the document was also delivered in his presence. This means simply that the document was presumably delivered before that signed witness too, as Ir Shushan writes. We do not presume that a kinyan was made before him, because many documents are written without kinyan. This is the proper sequence of the Mechaber’s words. It is contrary to Sema, who removes the Mechaber’s ruling from its plain meaning and explains Rambam and the Mechaber’s statement that where the oral witness says, ‘I effected a kinyan,’ the two combine to make the loan a documented loan, as meaning that it may even be collected from encumbered property. Sema is forced into great difficulty in explaining the Mechaber. The matter is clear, however, that the Mechaber intends what I have explained. He states explicitly in Kesef Mishneh, chapter 8 of Hilchot Edut, that Rambam’s precise language, ‘the two combine to make it a documented loan and he cannot say, I paid,’ implies that the status of a documented loan is created only to prevent the debtor from claiming payment, but not to collect from purchasers. Although an ordinary kinyan is presumed intended for writing, that applies when the kinyan was made before two witnesses. Here the kinyan was made before only one. The Tur, by contrast, writes in siman 51 in Rambam’s name that it becomes a full documented loan, but Kesef Mishneh concludes that his own reading is more accurate. I later saw that Ir Shushan explicitly writes as I do regarding the middle case, that one does not collect from encumbered property, and explains the reason. Sema asks: if so, why is the second oral witness needed at all? Rambam himself holds in the next seif that even when only one witness is signed on a document, the debtor cannot claim payment, even with an oath. Bach raises the same challenge against Kesef Mishneh. In my view there is no difficulty. A practical difference arises if the written witness later comes and contradicts the debtor’s claim that he paid, or if the written testimony explicitly states that the witness knows the debt was not paid. With only that witness, the debtor would swear against him. But when the oral witness says, ‘I effected a kinyan,’ the two combine to create the status of a documented loan, and the debtor cannot claim payment. Moreover, one who studies Rambam himself in chapter 4 of Hilchot Edut sees that the primary purpose of the ruling is to teach the law of joining different forms of testimony. As to the Mechaber, there is independently no difficulty, because he is teaching that even according to the latter view in the following seif the debtor cannot claim payment, as I explained. Sema further writes that the Tur at the beginning of this siman cites Rambam only as saying that the two combine and the loan becomes a full documented loan, without adding the Mechaber’s phrase that the debtor cannot say, ‘I paid.’ But Kesef Mishneh himself cites the Tur and rejects his reading based precisely on Rambam’s need to add the phrase ‘and he cannot say, I paid.’ Sema also asks that the Mechaber himself rules at the beginning of siman 39 that if witnesses testify that a kinyan was made, one may collect from encumbered property even if no document was written, and he finds it forced to say that here it is unknown whether the kinyan was also made before the signed witness. Yet Kesef Mishneh himself gives that distinction, and it is not forced at all. Sema further writes that Rambam needed to add, ‘and he cannot say, I paid,’ because sometimes one may collect from encumbered property and nevertheless the debtor can claim payment, as the Tur writes in the name of Tosafot in siman 39 regarding an admission before beit din. In my view this was an error. The Tur there states that with movable property, from the time of admission beit din may write, etc., and that Tosafot explain the rule that an admission before beit din is like a documented loan specifically for collecting from purchasers whenever it is known that he did not pay, or he admits that he did not pay. If he claims that he paid, however, he is believed. Therefore beit din may not write the admission unless they know, through witnesses or his own admission, that it was not paid. Sema understood this as meaning that even while the debtor can claim payment, collection from purchasers remains possible. This is not so, as I have already explained at length above in siman 39, seif katan 21, and in siman 79, seif 12, seif katan 6. Rather, the meaning is that the admission has the status of a documented loan for collection from purchasers only where it was known at the time that he had not paid, or he expressly admitted that he had not paid. Then beit din may write it, and collection from purchasers begins from the time of the writing. Certainly, once beit din wrote his admission, he can no longer claim that he paid afterward. But if beit din has not yet written his admission and after leaving beit din he claims that he paid, he is believed, and he likewise cannot collect from purchasers. All this is clear. This also appears to me the primary practical ruling. Rashbam and other poskim hold that even a kinyan does not permit collection from encumbered property unless a document is written, as Beit Yosef cites at the beginning of siman 39, and the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef in Get Pashut also imply this, as Beit Yosef cites in siman 50, seif 12. Although most poskim agree that a kinyan permits collection from encumbered property even without writing, here, where only one witness testifies to the kinyan, the case is weaker and one should not collect from encumbered property. This appears correct to me. See Responsa Maharash Kohen, book 3, siman 35; Responsa Rama, siman 110; Responsa Mahari Beit Levi, siman 52, p. 116; and Responsa Maharit, siman 71, concerning one oral witness and one written witness:"]
