And he cannot say, "I paid." Shach follows Kesef Mishneh that for this purpose the matter is treated like a written loan: the borrower cannot claim payment. Nevertheless, according to them, one does not collect from encumbered property, because the witness signed on the document did not participate in the kinyan and therefore cannot combine with the witness to the kinyan for purchaser-facing collection. Sema objects that Rambam already holds in seif 2 that even a document bearing only one witness prevents a payment claim. Shach and Gidulei Terumah answer that Rambam's reason there is the rule that where the defendant does not contradict the single witness, he becomes liable to swear and, being unable to swear, must pay. If, however, the witness who testifies in writing to the loan also comes before beit din and says that he knows the debt was not repaid because he never left the parties, then the borrower can contradict him concerning repayment. The ordinary unable-to-swear rule would then no longer apply, and the borrower could claim payment. With the second witness to the kinyan, however, the two join and the borrower cannot claim payment. In my view this added explanation weakens rather than strengthens the case. What difference does it make that the borrower can contradict the witness concerning the added assertion that payment never occurred? Regarding the core fact of the loan he does not contradict him. The witness is therefore treated like two regarding that fact. Remove the added statement that payment did not occur. Would the borrower have been believed? Certainly not. How can the witness lose force merely because he added more testimony? His silence would then be stronger than his speech. Moreover, once his written testimony has established the loan, he cannot later retract or weaken its force by adding oral testimony that incidentally gives the borrower a point of contradiction. In truth there was no need for this strained answer. Many practical distinctions exist. For example, the witness may testify that the defendant owed rent, produce, land-derived obligations, or documents, and the defendant may say, "I returned them." In such matters there may be no Torah oath, so the unable-to-swear doctrine does not apply. Yet if the written witness joins with a witness to the kinyan, two witnesses exist and the defendant is not believed to say he returned or repaid. Another possibility is that a single witness supports the defendant's claim of payment. According to Ramban at the beginning of Bava Metzia concerning one custodian who transfers to another, a supporting witness prevents the rule of unable-to-swear from arising. But two witnesses are not displaced by one supporting witness. I discuss further distinctions below in siman 69. Even though Kesef Mishneh's view can thus be defended, the primary interpretation appears to be Sema's: Rambam means that the witnesses combine even to collect from encumbered property, as Tur states explicitly. Beit Yosef notes a variant reading, but why should we prefer that over Tur's carefully transmitted text? More importantly, Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 9, section 2, law 2, expressly cites Rambam as saying that such testimony has the full status of a document both regarding encumbered property and regarding a payment defense. His testimony to Rambam's text and meaning is decisive. There is also a strong proof from the Gemara. If a witness in writing and a witness to a kinyan did not combine for encumbered property, the Gemara could have answered Amemar's difficulty from the analogy to a tied document very simply: just as an improperly witnessed tied document does not collect from encumbered property, so too an ordinary document with one written witness and one kinyan witness does not. It is no answer that the cases would not be perfectly identical, because Abaye himself explains the Mishnah through one witness in writing and one orally, where the analogy is likewise not exact. Rosh too interprets Abaye's case as affecting collection from encumbered property. Evidently the Gemara does not accept such a limitation regarding one written witness plus one kinyan witness. If Rashbam is read as holding that a tied document with only two witnesses is defective principally regarding encumbered property, the proof is even stronger. Sema also properly explains why Rambam had to state expressly that the borrower cannot say, "I paid," even if the witnesses allow collection from encumbered property. One might have thought that purchaser-facing collection follows because beit din does not plead payment for purchasers where repayment is uncommon, while the borrower himself, if he loudly claims payment, might still be believed. Rambam teaches that he is not. Shach rejects this because he treats the two issues as inseparable, but above in siman 39 I showed from Tosafot and Rosh that they need not be identical. There is an additional reason why Rambam had to state the payment rule here. Some Geonim, cited by Rif in the second chapter of Ketubot, hold that if a long time passes after witnesses saw a kinyan and the creditor did not ask them to write a document, the claim is weakened and the debtor may be believed to say he paid. Even those who disagree might concede that where the creditor deliberately accepted a document signed by only one witness and did not have the second witness sign, the circumstances appear suspicious: perhaps he was repaid in the meantime and therefore did not pursue a full document. Rambam therefore teaches that even here the borrower is not believed. This also answers Sema's question from Rambam's rule concerning a document with one witness. There, the inability to claim payment rests on the Torah oath mechanism. Here, once the two witnesses combine for the kinyan, the claim involves a property lien and there is no Torah oath mechanism of that kind. If the Geonic view were accepted, the debtor might have been believed precisely because the kinyan witnesses were not converted into a regular two-witness note. Rambam teaches otherwise: the two witnesses combine, the purchaser-facing lien stands, and the borrower cannot claim payment. This reading is clear and correct.

This law, together with Rama's gloss, has left me uncertain, and its source in Rosh at the end of Perek Get Pashut deserves careful examination. Rosh writes that if the witness testifying orally says that the document was delivered in his presence, the document is valid even for collection from encumbered property, because it is regarded as though there were two delivery witnesses. We presume that the witness whose signature appears on the document was also present at delivery. Rosh proves this from the rule according to R. Elazar that when a document comes before us with witnesses signed upon it, we collect even though we do not know that it was delivered in their presence, because we assume that since they signed it, it was also delivered before them. At first glance, Rosh's certainty that the signed witness was also a delivery witness appears to follow his own position in Perek HaMegarresh that with deeds of sale, gift, and loans, delivery witnesses are required. If the document cannot be valid without them, we presume the signatories acted properly and did not release the document for use until they were present at its delivery. Thus, where only one witness is signed, we likewise presume that he was present at delivery, and together with the oral witness there are two delivery witnesses. But if one holds that delivery witnesses are not required at all and signatures alone suffice, there is no basis to presume that the signed witness also attended delivery. Why should we extract property from purchasers on the basis of an unnecessary and uncertain event? Rosh himself writes in the second chapter of Gittin that a borrower often borrows privately, and the whole concern in the sugya that a document might be written in Nissan and delivered only in Tishrei assumes that it may be handed over privately. The rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant should prevent our inventing delivery witnesses merely because one signed witness appears. Rosh's proof actually points in the opposite direction. According to R. Elazar, where delivery witnesses are essential, the fact that the document is in the recipient's hands and signed by proper witnesses allows us to presume that the required delivery occurred properly. Rosh says explicitly regarding gittin that because the get is in the woman's possession and signed, we assume it was delivered validly before witnesses, since everyone knows matters of marriage require two witnesses. But according to R. Meir, who does not require delivery witnesses, why would we assume the unnecessary presence of such witnesses? There is no reason. This creates two difficulties with Beit Yosef. First, he codifies Rosh's rule without mentioning any dispute. Yet in Even HaEzer siman 133 he presents first, in accordance with Rif, Rambam, and their followers, the view that a get can be valid through signature witnesses alone even according to R. Elazar. If so, there is no basis to state here categorically that a signed witness was certainly present at delivery. Second, Beit Yosef explains Tur as saying that if the signed witness explicitly states that he was not present at delivery, the two do not combine, and Rama follows him. But this too does not fit the view of Ran and Tosafot that the signatures themselves can function as delivery evidence even if the signatories did not physically see delivery. According to that approach, the signed witness can still count in the legal framework even if he says he was absent from the literal delivery. One might suggest that Beit Yosef's Even HaEzer ruling does not rely on Rif and Rambam but rather on Ran's explanation that once the document is signed and in the recipient's possession, we have an evidentiary presumption equivalent to delivery witnesses. Tosafot in Bava Batra 165 also say that a signed witness can be treated like a delivery witness because his signature proves the borrower delivered the document under circumstances involving the lender's instruction. But this is strained. Beit Yosef there plainly follows Rif and Rambam. Moreover, if the signed witness can count constructively as a delivery witness even when not physically present, how can Beit Yosef and Rama say his express statement that he did not witness delivery prevents combination? There is another difficulty. Rosh's phrase, "you should know, for according to R. Elazar," sounds as though he is introducing a new proof rather than merely applying the same premise that delivery witnesses are required. More fundamentally, Bava Metzia asks why we are not concerned that a document was written in Nissan and delivered in Tishrei, causing unlawful collection from earlier purchasers. If according to Rosh a loan note is invalid without delivery witnesses, then there are only two possibilities. If the borrower might deliver it privately without witnesses, the document is invalid even if delivered on the same day; the antedating problem is secondary. If we assume the borrower will deliver it properly before witnesses, then those witnesses will recognize the date defect and refuse to participate. Why is the Gemara concerned with an antedated lien at all? Abaye answers that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the lender immediately. Yet Abaye himself follows R. Elazar that delivery witnesses create the document. If a note can be written for the borrower while the lender is absent and the signatories then leave, who guarantees that the borrower will later obtain proper delivery witnesses? If the answer is that the lender will not lend without such witnesses because he knows the document is otherwise invalid, then again the delivery witnesses will notice an antedating problem. Ran raises a parallel question regarding gittin. It therefore appears that when Rosh writes in Perek HaMegarresh that "we require delivery witnesses" for sale, gift, and loan documents, the word "require" is not literal. He means that delivery witnesses are legally effective in those documents, against the view that only signature witnesses can operate in monetary instruments. He does not mean that signature witnesses can never suffice. Rosh says explicitly in the first chapter of Gittin that R. Elazar requires delivery witnesses only for instruments whose legal effect is created by the document itself, whereas instruments serving merely as evidence do not require them. The same must be true of ordinary loan documents and sales whose acquisition occurred independently by money or kinyan. This also resolves Tosafot in Ketubot 20, who ask why a witness may write testimony in a notebook and later testify from it. If every evidentiary note required delivery witnesses, their question would be misplaced where the writing remained with the witness and was never delivered. Clearly an evidentiary document does not require delivery witnesses. How then can Rosh presume that the signed witness was also present at delivery? Tosafot in Bava Metzia 20 say that Abaye's rule that signing witnesses acquire rights for the lender is itself a rabbinic enactment. Without that enactment, witnesses could never safely sign before actual delivery, because they would have to remain until they saw the instrument handed over. Rosh may hold that this enactment applies where a normal two-witness document exists, because commercial necessity and prevention of closing the door before borrowers justified the rule. But a document signed by only one witness is uncommon and does not create the ordinary lending security. Chazal did not extend the special enactment so far. We apply the principle, "do not add beyond the enactment." Therefore, one witness who signs cannot simply hand the instrument to the borrower and depart. He must remain until the lender is present and the document is delivered. That makes him a delivery witness in fact. Rosh's proof from R. Elazar now fits. In a class of documents where proper delivery is required for validity, the fact that qualified witnesses signed allows us to presume they acted properly and remained involved through delivery. Similarly, where a single witness could not lawfully hand the document to the borrower under the ordinary signing-before-delivery enactment, we presume he acted properly and was present when the lender received it. One might object that with a single signed witness there is no purchaser-facing collection anyway, so why is there any antedating concern requiring him to remain? Tosafot themselves, however, discuss R. Asi and Abaye in a context of one witness signed on a document. Why should Abaye's doctrine matter there if one witness may always hand the note to the borrower? The answer must be that witnesses can sign separately. If one witness signs and hands the incomplete note to the borrower, the borrower may later obtain a second signature and deliver the completed note to the lender at a later date, creating an antedated document. It is not enough to say the second witness will refuse to act improperly. The integrity of the document's entire testimony cannot be left to the diligence of a future second witness. Each witness bears responsibility that the written testimony cannot be used wrongfully. Therefore even the first witness may not hand it to the borrower unless the lender is present, absent Abaye's special acquisition rule. This explains Shulchan Aruch. Since he does not rule broadly like Abaye that signing witnesses acquire for the lender, a single witness should not write and deliver a non-kinyan note to the borrower alone. The context here plainly concerns such a note. Therefore the signed witness is presumed to have been present at delivery, and he joins the oral witness who testifies to that delivery. One problem nevertheless remains. Beit Yosef says the signed witness is believed if he expressly says the document was not delivered in his presence. How can he be believed to say that he himself acted improperly by handing the instrument to the borrower without the lender? If our presumption of his presence at delivery rests on the rule that proper witnesses act lawfully, his later statement should not be accepted to destroy the validity of the document, any more than signatories to a get are ordinarily believed to say that the required delivery witnesses were absent. Perhaps Beit Yosef follows Shach's position above in siman 43, seif katan 16, that witnesses are believed even when their handwriting is established elsewhere to say that they erred regarding R. Asi's rule and wrote in Nissan though delivery occurred only in Tishrei. If so, the witness could also admit that he improperly released the document to the borrower and did not witness delivery. Even though such an admission is outside the expected course of proper conduct, it may be accepted. But this issue is disputed. I explained above in siman 43 at length that many authorities hold that even if witnesses who admit such an error are not disqualified as people, they are not believed to turn the document into an antedated instrument once their signatures are established. If so, here too the signed witness should not be believed to say he was absent from delivery. This may be why Rama's gloss does not say, "if the signed witness says it was not delivered before him." Instead he writes, "but if it was delivered before only one witness," meaning that beit din independently knows that fact. For example, the oral witness testifies that he alone witnessed delivery and that before then the document had been in the borrower's hands. In such a case it is established that there was only one delivery witness. Rama avoids deciding whether the signed witness's own admission is enough. Perishah treats his credibility as obvious; to me it is very doubtful for the reasons stated. Rama's careful wording supports this uncertainty. After all this, I remain uneasy because I have not found earlier authorities explicitly discussing every point developed above. One whose understanding of Rosh and Mechaber is complete should judge the matter. Gidulei Terumah raises another question. If Rosh assumes the signed witness was also present at delivery, then the two witnesses saw the delivery together. Why does Rosh say that according to the Sages who disagree with R. Yehoshua ben Korchah they do not combine because it is separate testimony? They were present at the same delivery. The answer emerges from careful reading of Tosafot. According to those Sages, it is not enough that the witnesses happened to witness the same event. Each must know that the other also saw it and must testify in beit din accordingly. Tosafot ask, if one witness is in writing and the other orally testifies to seeing the loan, why should this necessarily be separate testimony? It could be that the written witness wrote, "before me and before so-and-so, the other witness, the loan occurred," while the oral witness testifies, "before me and before the signed witness the loan occurred." Tosafot specifically require each to attest that the other also witnessed. They do not say merely that both testify to the same loan. Evidently, under that opinion, two people who happened to be present do not count as having "seen together" unless each knew of the other's observation and says so. Therefore, even if the document was delivered in the presence of the signed witness, he did not testify that he knew the oral witness was also present, and the oral witness did not necessarily testify that the signed witness saw. The testimony can remain legally separate. There is therefore no difficulty.
Each of which bears only one witness, and so forth. Beit Yosef and Rama understand Tur and Rosh according to a text reading: "But two documents, each bearing only one witness, and each delivered before one witness, do not combine." They were troubled by the fact that in a single document we presume that the signed witness was also present at delivery. They therefore explain that here it is known that the signed witness was not present at the document's delivery. Two separate one-witness documents therefore cannot combine for collection from encumbered property. Bach cites Maharshal's reading: "But two documents, each bearing only one witness, or delivered before only one witness." According to this reading, the case includes either two documents each signed by one witness, or two unsigned documents each delivered before one witness. There is no need for the strained assumption developed above that the signed witness definitely was not present at delivery. Maharshal's text is more straightforward. A difficulty arises for Beit Yosef's interpretation from the Gemara's discussion of Amemar concerning a simple and a tied document. Why did the Gemara not say that the Mishnah excludes precisely a case of one written witness together with one delivery witness who do not combine? Under Maharshal's reading, such a case is not readily available: as explained above, a signed witness is ordinarily also treated as a delivery witness, so one written witness plus one delivery witness effectively yields two delivery witnesses. And the Mishnah compares defects within a single instrument, whereas Maharshal's case concerns two separate instruments. But under Beit Yosef and Rama, the exact configuration does exist. Why not use it to explain Amemar? One might answer that it would not be fully analogous to the tied document. In a tied get lacking the required number of witnesses, the defect remains even where it is possible that the signatories were also delivery witnesses; otherwise every tied get could be saved through delivery witnesses. According to R. Elazar, an ordinary get with no signatures can indeed be valid through delivery witnesses, so the Mishnah's special requirement of three signatures in a tied get must mean that the normal presumption of valid delivery differs depending on how many signatories appear. Perhaps with three signatories we presume proper delivery, while with only two we do not. Then an ordinary document with one written witness and one oral witness could analogously be defective because we do not presume the signatory also witnessed delivery. Gidulei Terumah may have intended something like this. Still, it does not fit Rosh's own proof. Rosh argues that if the Yerushalmi's rule were accepted, Amemar should have said that the Mishnah excludes one written witness and one oral witness when the single signature stands alone on the note. Why is that not too dissimilar from a tied document? Moreover, in a tied document, if only two of three signatures are authenticated, the instrument certainly does not become valid merely because perhaps the two authenticated witnesses also saw delivery. Gittin 82 requires proper validation of the prescribed signatures. If complete analogy were required, Rosh's question on Amemar would fail from the outset. Evidently the comparison need not be exact in every detail. Another answer is available according to what I wrote above. Where the signed witness was not present at delivery because he improperly handed the document to the borrower before the lender appeared, there is no purchaser-facing publicity. Customers can say, "Bring proof when the note reached the lender." One witness to delivery cannot extract money from them. Furthermore, the signatory himself did not create publicity by signing because the instrument remained in the borrower's hands, as Rosh writes in Bava Metzia concerning a found loan document. Therefore the Mishnah would teach nothing new by saying such a document does not collect from encumbered property. Its defect is obvious. This can defend Beit Yosef's interpretation, though Maharshal's text remains cleaner and preferable.
To collect from encumbered property. Sema writes that above in siman 45 Tur expresses uncertainty whether two documents, each bearing one witness, can combine, implying that Tur is uncertain even about collection from the borrower himself. Sema explains that perhaps Tur is concerned for Raavad and those who hold that a single written witness has no evidentiary force at all and cannot combine. Here, then, the definite rule concerns only encumbered property, while collection from free assets remains doubtful. This appears entirely incorrect. If Tur seriously entertained Raavad's view as a practical doubt, how could he state at the beginning of the present discussion that one witness in writing and one oral witness combine? According to Raavad, the written witness is nothing because it is testimony from writing. One cannot answer that there the signed witness is presumed also to have witnessed delivery. That would not help: if the only testimony now before us is his writing, Raavad still regards it as impermissible written testimony. The Yerushalmi itself, which Raavad treats as primary, speaks of validating the handwriting of one witness and not the other. Clearly mere presumed presence at delivery does not solve the written-testimony problem. Rosh expressly rejects the Yerushalmi's limitation and rules that one written witness is not disqualified as "from their writing." Tur follows Rosh. Why would he then remain doubtful out of deference to Raavad? Moreover, Sema's explanation does not even solve Tur's earlier doubt in siman 45. Even according to Raavad, one could forge an additional half-line alongside each witness and then add another signature. If the second signature cannot be authenticated, the instrument would still contain one authenticated written witness and could have some force under the very rule Sema attributes to Raavad. The only way to avoid the problem would be to say that each witness extended his signature to the very edge of the page so that no second signature could be added, but Tur never states such a limitation. The correct explanation of Tur's doubt in siman 45 is what Shach writes and what I explained there at length: where there is one witness opposite another and both stand only on a single line, there is an inherent defect visible in the document, and the question is whether they combine in that abnormal structure. Apart from such a defect, Tur has no doubt that two one-witness documents can combine to collect from the borrower. His uncertainty has nothing to do with Raavad's rejected position. Shach's understanding there is correct.
