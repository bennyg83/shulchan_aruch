They combine. Their joining prevents the debtor from saying, "This never happened." With one written witness he can take an oath contradicting that witness, whereas with two witnesses joined together he cannot. According to the opinion stated in seif 2 that one may plead payment even against a single written witness, he may likewise plead payment when two such witnesses are joined, because the result is only an oral loan and the borrower is believed to say he paid. Sema and Shach. See what I write in seif 2.
And he cannot say, "I paid." The clear intent of the Shulchan Aruch is as Shach explains. Beit Yosef states explicitly in several places that for this purpose the joined testimony has the law of a document, so the borrower cannot plead payment. Yet it does not necessarily permit foreclosure of encumbered property, because the two witnesses are not both in writing or both connected through one kinyan. One might ask what novelty remains according to Rambam, who is understood in seif 2 to hold that even against one written witness the borrower cannot say, "I paid." See Tumim. The practical difference can arise where the written witness testifies to an obligation that does not independently generate a Torah oath. Against one written witness the debtor could then say he paid, because the doctrine of one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear does not apply. Here, once the two witnesses join, he is not believed to plead payment under any circumstance. Nevertheless, in Tumim I conclude that the primary halachah follows Sema: he cannot say he paid and the creditor may also collect from encumbered property. There had been room to think that although collection from encumbered property is possible, payment should still be believed because the arrangement is unusual. We might even infer interim repayment from the fact that the creditor obtained one signature but not the second, suggesting he was paid in between and therefore stopped seeking a second signature. And if there were only one written witness, there would be no Torah oath here because collection from encumbered property makes it a land-secured claim, so the rule of inability to swear would not apply. The law therefore teaches that payment is not believed in any form. There are effectively two witnesses, like a document signed by two witnesses, and the combined proof is complete. Rama writes in responsum that the same applies not only where the witness himself made the kinyan, but where he testifies that he saw the kinyan. See responsum 110. In my view such testimony helps regarding collection from property presently held by the borrower, but not from encumbered property. The same distinction concerning payment follows the rules above in siman 39:5 and Shach at the end of siman 46.

If it was delivered only before one witness. See Tumim. If the witness signed on the deed was also the delivery witness, the case is straightforward. But if a different person was the delivery witness, I remain uncertain whether the signed witness is believed to say that there was no valid delivery, or whether the matter must be established independently through witnesses. Rama therefore wrote generally, "If it was not delivered," meaning where the lack of valid delivery is known. Maharshal takes a different approach. He holds that the witness signed on the document is presumed to have been a delivery witness as well, and therefore emends Tur to say that either two documents bearing separate signatures, or two delivery witnesses alone, do not join in the relevant way. See Tumim. I wrote there that his approach appears more convincing and correct.
