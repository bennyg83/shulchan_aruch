One witness in writing. See Shach, seif katan 1. Kesef Mishneh writes that although an ordinary kinyan stands for writing, this applies when the kinyan was made before two witnesses. Here it was made before one witness. At first this seems difficult. If such a kinyan is not regarded as intended for writing, why should the debtor not be believed to say he paid? Even with two witnesses, where the transaction was not intended for a written deed, payment can be pleaded. Kesef Mishneh's intent is that a kinyan before two witnesses is ordinarily intended to generate a two-witness document. That publicity can create collection against encumbered property. A kinyan before one witness is at most intended to produce a one-witness writing; even if two separate witnesses each saw the same kinyan at different times, if neither knew of the other, each publicizes only a one-witness matter, and purchasers do not understand that there is a full two-witness lien. Therefore the two separate writings do not combine to reach purchasers. Shach's rule in siman 39 that two witnesses to a kinyan at different times can reach encumbered property must mean that each knew the other also witnessed the same kinyan, such as where the obligor told each, 'The same kinyan I make before you I also made before so-and-so.' Similarly, if the obligor tells two witnesses separately to sign a document, they should not create one two-witness instrument unless he told each that he is to sign together with another whom the obligor is also appointing. Otherwise he instructed each only to make a one-witness document. Nevertheless, the debtor cannot simply plead payment where he knows that two witnesses were instructed to produce writings. Once he knows there are two separate one-witness instruments outstanding, the usual concern, 'If it was paid, why are your writings still in his hands?' can apply. This is why Beit Yosef rejects Raavad's doubt and holds that the outstanding writings can defeat a payment plea even if they do not reach purchasers. Shach adds a practical difference where the written witness later testifies that he knows payment did not occur. Urim VeTumim asks why adding this statement weakens rather than strengthens the testimony. The point follows one explanation of the Rabbi Abba case: so long as the defendant does not swear against a lone witness, the witness functions like two. If the defendant does not deny the original loan, the one witness can therefore stand as two regarding the borrowing, and the debtor's payment defense becomes a migo against testimony. Under the alternative explanation, that the defendant merely fears taking an oath against the witness, this consequence does not follow because he must swear in any event. Urim VeTumim resolves Sema's question by saying the combination matters for land and land produce, where there is no Torah oath and therefore no rule of one who must swear and cannot. Even if the written witness only proves the loan, the debtor may still have a migo to deny authorizing the writing, just as an unauthenticated document can be answered with payment through a forgery migo despite other proof of the loan. Shach's phrase, 'or he explicitly wrote that he knows it was not paid,' must mean that the document records an admission by the debtor at a later date that the earlier loan remained unpaid and that he then instructed the witness to write it. If the witness simply writes on his own, 'I know it was not paid,' that is unauthorized testimony from writing. Finally, Shach explains the phrase, 'I acquired,' as meaning, 'a kinyan was made before me.' Although Sema called this strained, Kesef Mishneh explicitly interprets it that way. There is no difficulty that the second witness does not testify that two witnesses were present at the kinyan. Every ordinary two-witness document ultimately depends on each witness separately, and documentary law itself joins their acts into one instrument. Rama's responsum explains that because the essential force of documents is rabbinic, even one person's disclosure can suffice for the relevant publicity mechanics when combined in the form prescribed by the Sages.

The document was delivered before me. Shach explains that we presume delivery also occurred before the witness who signed the document, creating two delivery witnesses. This follows Rosh. Urim VeTumim objects that Rosh held a document is valid only through delivery witnesses, so the signers had a duty not to sign until they saw delivery. Under our ruling that signed witnesses themselves can validate a document, or that signature witnesses function as delivery witnesses through the document's possession, they need not see delivery at all. All the more so under the doctrine that signed witnesses acquire for the recipient, a document may be written for the borrower without the lender present. There is no difficulty. The presumption that a document was delivered at the time of writing is not based only on a rule that the witnesses performed their duty properly. It is a broader factual presumption: the overwhelming majority of documents are written for immediate use and are delivered immediately or very soon after writing. Tosafot in Gittin speak of a presumption that a get was delivered on the day it was written even though a get may be written for the husband while the wife is absent. Likewise, documents of sale can be written for the seller though the buyer is absent, yet a document that later appears is ordinarily presumed delivered near its writing. This is a majority-based presumption, not merely a duty-based presumption. It can therefore operate even where the witnesses were not technically required to see delivery. The phrase 'on the day it was written it was delivered' is itself not exact; the presumption is that delivery occurred immediately in proximity to the writing. In places where hours are written, the same logic applies to the hour. This also explains why a witness can later say, 'It was not delivered before me.' Such a statement concerns what occurred after the signing and after the document left his hand. It is not a retraction from his signed testimony. Even the original signers themselves can testify to such a later fact, just as they may testify that the debt was later paid or forgiven. There is therefore no need to say, as Urim VeTumim suggests, that the witness is believed only because he can claim he erred about a rabbinic rule. The statement is ordinary testimony concerning an event subsequent to signing.
