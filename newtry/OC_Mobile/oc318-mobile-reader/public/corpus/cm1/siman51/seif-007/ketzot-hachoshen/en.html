A document that was delivered. In Perek HaNosei the Gemara discusses one who says to another, "I owe you a maneh." Rabbi Yochanan obligates and Reish Lakish exempts. The Gemara establishes the dispute where he says, "I owe you a maneh in a document." Rashi explains that he wrote it in his own handwriting and delivered it before witnesses. Rava compares the matter to a guarantor whose undertaking appears after the witnesses' signatures. Tosafot says that according to Rashi the guarantor must have written, "And I am guarantor," and delivered it before witnesses, and Ben Nanas and Rabbi Yishmael dispute whether this wording functions as an admission or not. Tosafot asks why the guarantor does not become obligated through the instrument itself together with delivery witnesses. Tosafot answers that this is not a sufficient obligation document because the guarantor's name is not actually written. His handwriting alone does not show from the document itself that he was the person who delivered it. Therefore he cannot become bound through it merely by delivery. This proves that even according to the view that delivery witnesses effect the document and the document need not otherwise be self identifying in every respect, where the principal party's name is absent and the writing itself does not establish that he delivered it, the document is ineffective. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 129 similarly rules that a get lacking the names of the husband and wife is invalid even according to the view that delivery witnesses effect the divorce, and cites Tosafot in Gittin. Some later authorities read that as only rabbinic. Tosafot in Perek HaNosei, however, implies a Torah level defect, because its reasoning is stated in ordinary monetary law as a substantive failure of the document.
