Since he cannot swear, he pays. This is Rambam's view. Even if one accepts that a debtor may claim payment against his own handwriting because a person may leave such writing in the creditor's possession, he does not ordinarily leave a formally signed one witness instrument after payment. A migo that he could have denied the entire matter does not help, because this is the precise structure of Rabbi Abba's rule: one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay even though he has a migo. Rashba, however, writes in responsum 1090 concerning a document containing one witness where the borrower claims payment that according to the view allowing a payment claim against personal handwriting, the same applies here, and according to the stricter view it does not. Rashbam goes even further. In Bava Batra 161, where Abaye discusses combining one written witness and one oral witness, Rashbam explains that the borrower can still say, "I paid," because once the instrument was not made in the standard form required by the Sages, the borrower may not have considered it important enough to demand its return. This is striking because Rashbam himself is among those who do not allow a payment claim against personal handwriting. He apparently treats a one witness document as weaker than personal handwriting, whereas Rashba treats them alike. Knesset HaGedolah engages in a lengthy discussion and asks why the borrower should not be believed through a migo: had he denied everything, he would take a Torah oath, whereas now with a payment claim he would take only hesset and be exempt. He suggests that perhaps Rashba does not allow a migo from a Torah oath to a rabbinic oath. I am astonished. How does migo help here at all? This is exactly Rabbi Abba's rule. Tosafot in Bava Batra already asks why the snatcher is not believed through a migo and answers, among other explanations, that even with the migo he remains one obligated to swear but unable to swear, and therefore must pay. The same answer applies here. If "I paid" were independently a valid claim against this instrument, he could be believed. But if it is not independently a valid claim and survives only through a migo of denial, that migo cannot defeat the rule of inability to swear. The entire elaborate discussion is therefore unnecessary.
