And some say that even. Meaning that the debtor takes a rabbinic oath. Certainly, if he denies the transaction entirely, he is believed with such an oath, because one witness in a document is legally ineffective and constitutes written testimony outside court. The Yerushalmi, cited by the Rosh, says that even two documents, each signed by one witness, are ineffective. Since one written witness does not obligate an oath, the defendant swears only as on an unsupported oral claim. The same principle applies to two witnesses for monetary liability. The Gemara's comparison of one witness in an ordinary document to one witness whose invalidity is biblical proves that a single written witness is not treated like an oral witness. Rav Acha Gaon likewise rules that the invalidity is biblical and that property seized on its basis is recovered. The first opinion is the Rambam's, who rejects the Yerushalmi and follows the Behag. On his view, two witnesses in two documents certainly combine against a denial, and a plea of payment does not require their combination. The rules cited above in simanim 44 and 45 concerning staggered signatures follow the second opinion. According to the Rambam, the testimonies could apparently combine even against encumbered property.
(Collected note) And some say, etc. The Maggid Mishneh questions the Rambam: even if one written witness creates a valid document, why should it be stronger than two oral witnesses? Nor can one rely on the argument, 'Why is your document in my hand?' because the same argument would then defeat a plea of payment against a handwritten obligation. Since a handwritten obligation collects only from unencumbered property, the debtor is not necessarily careful to retrieve it, for he could sell all his assets. The same applies here. Gittin chapter six shows that the debtor's own handwriting is stronger than one witness.
