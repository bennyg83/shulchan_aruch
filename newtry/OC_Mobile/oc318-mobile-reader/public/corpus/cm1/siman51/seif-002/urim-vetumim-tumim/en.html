Shach rejects this view, but Rosh in responsa, at the end of klal 55 and again in klal 67, law 4, explains the position favorably and does not question it or cite a dissent. This strongly suggests that Rosh agrees with Rambam. I also prove below in the rules of migo from another passage in Rosh that he accepts this principle. A responsum of Rif cited by Nimukei Yosef and Rosh in the first chapter of Makkot provides further evidence. A document was signed by two witnesses, one of whom was a relative. The unrelated witness remembered the testimony and had not known of the other's disqualification. Rif validated the document through the single qualified witness. Later editors already asked: if the witness remembers the event, remove the document and let him testify orally. But Rif's point was precisely to preserve the status of a document with one witness. That matters where the defendant admits the original debt but says, "I paid." If the witness testifies only orally, there is no document and the borrower can claim payment. By validating the writing as a one-witness document, Rif prevents that payment defense according to Rambam. In the actual case, once the defendant denied the loan entirely, he became liable to swear and the practical need for the document disappeared; nevertheless Rif first taught the legal principle that the writing itself retains documentary force. At one point I thought Rif might instead follow Tosafot's question in the case of R. Abba's ingot: why is the defendant not believed to say, "I snatched my own property," through a migo that he could have denied snatching and then sworn against the witness? Tosafot answer that a person is afraid to contradict the witness because the witness and the opposing litigant could later disqualify him from taking oaths. Perhaps that concern would not apply where the witness is in writing and no longer remembers, because after the defendant swears falsely there would be no one able to testify later that he swore falsely. That could explain why Rif insists the surviving witness remember the event. But according to Rambam's principle, this whole question is unnecessary. Whenever the defendant does not contradict a single witness concerning a fact that obligates an oath, the witness is treated as two regarding that fact. Therefore a claim of payment against the one-witness document is no longer merely a migo problem. Once the defendant does not deny the underlying loan, the written witness stands like two and the debtor cannot use a migo against what is treated as two-witness evidence. Why, then, does Rif require the qualified witness to remember the testimony? This supports the rule I discussed above in siman 46: a single witness whose handwriting is not independently established must remember the underlying event. Rambam requires memory more broadly. Some authorities limit the requirement to one witness. Here the witness's handwriting may be authenticated elsewhere, but our reliance on him regarding whether he knew of the relative's disqualification creates a migo in his favor. If he says, "I did not know he was related, but I no longer remember the transaction," he could have said, "I did know he was related," which would destroy the whole instrument. Where the witness has such a migo, even established handwriting does not overcome his lack of memory. With only one witness there is also no ordinary rule that a migo cannot defeat two witnesses. Therefore the document is worthless unless he remembers the testimony. This fits Rif precisely. Rambam himself follows the same conceptual line. He holds that documentary evidence is rabbinically validated in many contexts as an enactment to prevent closing the door before borrowers, as I explained above in siman 28. The Gemara, according to Rosh's interpretation, shows that one written witness can be valid and can combine with another. Rambam could ask: why did Chazal override the Torah concern of testimony from writing for a one-witness document? There is little concern of closing the door, because people rarely lend on the strength of one witness. Even if the lender is not afraid the borrower will deny the loan, since the borrower would then have to swear against the witness and people are reluctant to do that, the borrower could simply say, "I paid," and walk away. The lender would have gained little protection. Rambam therefore concludes that one cannot claim payment against such a document. Because the single written witness creates an oath obligation regarding the original debt, and the defendant does not contradict that witness on the debt itself, he becomes one who cannot swear and therefore must pay. Once this is the law, people do have reason to lend against one-witness writings, and the rabbinic validation of written testimony serves the commercial purpose that justified the enactment. The two rulings support one another. From all this it appears that Shach's rejection is not decisive. The weight of Rambam, Rif, and Rosh supports the contrary view. The matter should therefore at least be treated as an unresolved dispute of law, and money cannot be extracted from a possessor against this substantial position.
