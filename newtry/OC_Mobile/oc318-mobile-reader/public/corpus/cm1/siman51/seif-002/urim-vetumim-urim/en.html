He is one who is obligated to swear, etc. Meaning: had he denied the one witness and said, "Nothing ever happened," he would have been obligated to swear against him. The general rule is that where one witness could obligate a Torah oath and the defendant does not contradict him, that witness has the force of two witnesses for the relevant matter. A document signed by two witnesses prevents a plea of payment because we say, "Why is your document still in his hand?" The same logic can apply to a document signed by one witness where that witness has this enhanced force. Since the lender took the trouble to have even one witness sign, the borrower too would not ordinarily leave the instrument in the lender's possession after payment. See Tumim, where I explain that Rif and Rosh appear to support this, so the Shach's preference for the contrary "some say" is not decisive. The matter remains an unresolved monetary doubt. The Shach further says that if the one witness later testifies explicitly that he knows the debt was not paid, even Rambam would agree that the borrower must swear against him and can thereby be exempt. I disagree. Why should the witness's added statement worsen the borrower's position? Had the witness remained silent, the borrower would already lack credibility to say he paid under this approach. I also conclude that if the borrower says he paid and another one witness supports him by testifying to payment, then even Rambam agrees the borrower is believed. One witness supporting the debtor stands against the one witness on the instrument, and we should not extend the exceptional force of the latter beyond necessity. All this concerns claims for which one witness would generate a Torah oath. Where the underlying matter carries no Torah oath, the borrower is plainly believed to say he paid, because the rule of one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear does not apply.
He said to me, “I am a lawyer who says that I will be faithful to Hashem, even if I say that the seven I need to swear.”
For even if he claimed that he paid, etc. The fact that one witness can sometimes have the force of two does not itself prevent a plea of payment. Even against two witnesses a borrower could ordinarily say he later paid. What blocks the plea in an ordinary document is the presumption, "Why is your document still in his hand?" That presumption is strongest in a formal instrument collectible from encumbered property. Here, where the document signed by one witness does not have that full force and the borrower could even deny the transaction entirely subject to an oath, he may not be so careful to retrieve it after payment. Therefore under this opinion he remains believed to say he paid.
