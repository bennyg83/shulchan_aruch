If he remembers the testimony. Taz in siman 36 writes that signing is like seeing the event and the beit din's later adjudication is like the formal giving of testimony. Therefore, if beit din already ruled on a document signed by valid and invalid witnesses, the valid witnesses cannot later testify orally because they have already joined the invalid witnesses in both sight and testimony. Before the document reaches beit din, however, he allows them to testify orally. This is difficult. If a document containing invalid signers is itself void, it was never 'judicially examined,' so its arrival in court should not create valid testimony. If it nevertheless is treated as judicially examined despite the defect, then the testimonial act should be fixed from the time of signing, not only when the document reaches court. Taz's statement is best understood only according to Rosh, who validates written testimony from a witness capable of speaking. Rama's responsum explains that under that view written testimony does not become formal testimony until the writing reaches beit din. Thus, once beit din adjudicated on the written statement, the witnesses had indeed joined in the act of giving testimony. Before that point the valid witnesses can still testify orally. But Taz's further statement that signing itself is like the moment of seeing is still difficult. If the valid witnesses originally saw the event without the invalid persons and were qualified at that moment, why should later signing together corrupt the earlier observation? The principal law is therefore that even after beit din has seen the defective document, the valid witnesses may testify orally, because we do not rule like Rosh that ordinary written testimony is valid. The writing itself never constituted a completed testimony for us. If, however, valid and invalid witnesses were already joined at the original observation, Shach can hold that the testimony was spoiled from the outset and cannot later be repaired. This resolves an apparent contradiction in Rosh. In one responsum he says valid witnesses can later testify orally only where the invalid persons were not with them at the time of seeing; elsewhere, in a will case, he appears to permit later oral testimony even though they were present together. The distinction is that in the latter case the document itself, absent proof of intentional testimonial joinder, is treated as though the valid witnesses state that the relatives or disqualified persons merely came to observe or signed for honor. The valid witnesses can be believed regarding that circumstance. But where it is known that all sat together for the purpose of signing as witnesses, their later signature strongly demonstrates that they intended to join as witnesses already at the time of observation. Then the entire testimony is invalidated. The result is this: if the original testimony was invalidated through actual joinder of valid and invalid witnesses at the time of seeing, the valid witnesses cannot later repair it orally. But with an ordinary document where people merely happened to be present together and there is no proof of joint testimonial intent, the valid witnesses' oral testimony remains available.
