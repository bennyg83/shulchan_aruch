But with oral testimony in such a case, and so forth. This wording deserves scrutiny. Rif rules like R. Yehudah that where part of a testimony is invalidated, the whole is invalidated, yet in Gittin he rules like Rava regarding a document that functions as both a get and a gift, dividing the effects: for the divorce aspect no validation is required, while for the gift aspect validation is required. The Yerushalmi links these issues. Rashba in his chiddushim to Gittin, citing Ramban, answers that document validation is different because the defect does not lie in the underlying testimony itself. Ran writes similarly, and Baal HaIttur appears to agree. Mordechai in the first chapter of Gittin, however, cites Rabbenu Yoel with another answer: the concern in written documents is that people may confuse the case with other documents and come to validate a relative as a witness. Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Makkot says likewise. Mordechai then notes that according to this explanation one can distinguish a written document from oral testimony, because with oral testimony there is no danger of confusing a relative's written signature with a valid one. That distinction works only according to Rabbenu Yoel's answer. According to Ramban, Rashba, Ran, and Baal HaIttur, there is no special reason to distinguish writing from oral testimony. The same conceptual rule about partial invalidation would apply wherever the underlying testimony remains intact. Responsa Rivash, siman 195, appears to follow his teacher Ran in discussing an agreement made concerning two scribes where one of the scribes participated among those approving the agreement. Rivash considers whether the doctrine that partial invalidation nullifies the whole applies, even though the issue is the substance of the agreement rather than the writing itself. This may explain why Mechaber omitted Rama's distinction. Rama, however, can reasonably rule leniently. Many authorities already hold more broadly that even in written documents we rule like R. Eliezer and do not invalidate the whole testimony in this fashion. At minimum we should not extend the stringent view beyond its clearest setting. Therefore Rama adopts Rosh and Mordechai's explanation that the written-document stringency is due to possible confusion, a concern absent from oral testimony. Ritva's responsum cited above in siman 37, regarding charity administrators who have discretionary benefit in a distribution, also appears consistent with this approach.
