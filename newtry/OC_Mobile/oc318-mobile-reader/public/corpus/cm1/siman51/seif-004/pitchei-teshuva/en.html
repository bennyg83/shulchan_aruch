He is believed with an oath. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 78, writes that according to this opinion it is obvious that even if the document contains a clause of credibility, the borrower is believed to say, 'I paid.' This is unlike a debtor's authenticated handwriting containing a credibility clause, regarding which no authority disputes that he cannot claim payment, as stated in siman 69, seif 2. There we say that had he paid he would not have left the writing in the creditor's hands, because with a credibility clause he has no other defense once his handwriting is authenticated, and payment is a weak claim while the document remains with the creditor. A document signed by one witness is different. Even if it contains a credibility clause and is authenticated, the borrower may have been unconcerned about leaving it with the creditor because he could still deny the witness and say that the matter never occurred. This resembles an unauthenticated document signed by two witnesses, where he is believed to claim payment through a migo that he could have claimed forgery. There is no substantive difference between a migo of forgery and a migo of denying that the transaction occurred. Avodat HaGershuni further writes that even the Rambam, the first opinion here, who holds that a borrower is not believed to say he paid against a document with one witness, may have said so only when the document was drafted in the plural, 'Before us, the witnesses,' but only one witness ultimately signed, or when two witnesses signed and one was later found to be a relative or otherwise disqualified. If from the outset the document was written to be attested by only one witness, even the Rambam may agree that the borrower can claim payment. The Maggid Mishneh records an interpretation of the Rambam in this manner. This also resolves the question of the Bach and Sema, seif katan 2, why an additional oral witness would ever be needed.
