It is like a potsherd. The simple meaning is that it does not even retain the force of testimony by one witness. Beit Yosef says this in Bedek HaBayit, and Bach agrees. Shach, however, argues at length. Why, he asks, should a document signed by one valid witness and one relative be worse than a document signed by many witnesses, among whom some are relatives? In the latter case we can say the relatives signed merely to fill the space and the qualified witnesses remain valid. Why not say the same here, treating the relative's signature as mere filler and preserving the qualified signature as one witness? Since Rif also says that where only two witnesses signed and one is related the document is internally false, Shach understands Halachot Gedolot and Rif as sharing one view. He therefore explains the phrase "like a potsherd" only to mean that it is not a valid two-witness document; the qualified signature may still retain the status of one witness. Alternatively, he limits the complete invalidity to a case where it is known that the witnesses sat together intending to sign as a unit. The second suggestion has no basis. Rambam states the law without qualification. If complete invalidity depended upon proof that they deliberately joined as witnesses, he should have said so. The first suggestion is also difficult in light of Rosh's responsum codified below in seif 5, where the qualified witness must come before beit din and testify orally; his signature alone does not suffice. Shach tries to answer that there too it was known they sat together to testify. But that leads back to the contradiction Sema already raised between different rulings of Rosh in klal 59. In one responsum Rosh says the qualified signer may testify orally and join another witness; at the end of the klal he invalidates the qualified witnesses together with the relative. Sema answers that the latter follows Rambam, who invalidates once the witnesses intended to testify and actually testified, while the former follows Rosh's own view requiring both intent at the event and appearance in beit din. Shach objects that signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony was investigated in beit din, so if they intentionally sat to sign with the relative, both conditions are fulfilled. That objection is correct, but it does not support Shach's reading. The actual case of the will in Rosh involved one witness outside while two signers inside heard the same testamentary instructions. We cannot invent two different wills to say the qualified witness did not see the event together with the relative. The true resolution is different. Rosh holds that where many witnesses sign and some are relatives, we may sometimes say the relatives signed merely for honor or to fill space. But if the last two signatures are relatives, the document is invalid because it can be validated through the relatives, as explained above in siman 45, seif 12. If ten signatures appear and one relative signs at the very end, the danger of validating through improper witnesses itself can render the instrument forgeable. All the more so in a document with only two signatures, where one of the two required signatories is a relative. The instrument can be validated through a disqualified signature and therefore is fundamentally defective. Ramban questions whether this concern applies outside a tied document, where three witnesses are required, because where extra signatures appear on an ordinary document people understand they are surplus. But where an ordinary document contains exactly the two signatures needed for validity, even Ramban should agree. It closely resembles a tied document: the relative occupies a position necessary for the document's appearance of validity. The document is therefore like one capable of falsification, and is worthless as a document even if the relative signed merely as filler. This explains Rosh's will responsum. The qualified witness's testimony itself was not personally disqualified merely by hearing the will in the same setting. Perhaps he did not intend to join with the relative as a witness at the critical moment, according to Rosh's rule requiring both conditions. But the document as a written instrument was nevertheless invalid because it bore only two signatures and one was disqualified, creating a document that could be validated improperly. Therefore Rosh required the qualified witness to testify orally. At the end of the klal, where the witnesses actually sat to sign together as a testimonial unit, their testimony itself was invalidated as well. There is no contradiction. This also makes it unnecessary to say, as Shach does, that Halachot Gedolot preserves the written force of the valid witness in every such two-signature case. The better reading is that the first view, which treats the whole document as a potsherd, has strong support from Rosh and perhaps Rambam as well. Hagahot Ashri cites Or Zarua that where one witness is in writing and one orally, if the written witness's handwriting is independently established they combine at least to create an oral-loan level claim, and the borrower can say, "I paid." But if the handwriting is not established and the witness himself says, "this is my handwriting," the writing is nothing. Gidulei Terumah asks why self-validation should differ from external validation. The answer follows what I explained above in Rambam's approach. Where handwriting is not established independently, a single witness must remember the testimony. Or Zarua may not require this with two witnesses, but he can require it with one, as Tosafot offer in one explanation and as Hagahot Mordechai indicates. Here we must be dealing with a witness who does not remember the event orally; otherwise there would be no need to combine his writing with the oral witness, since he could simply testify orally and make two live witnesses. Therefore, if the handwriting is not authenticated elsewhere and he merely identifies it as his own, his lack of memory leaves the writing worthless. If the handwriting is independently established, the writing can have evidentiary force without his present memory. This removes the difficulty. It also supports Shach's position in his responsum concerning Rama's gloss above in siman 46, seif 10, that a single written witness differs from two. Here Or Zarua's words clearly fit that distinction. Still, regarding the present issue of one valid and one related signer, I see no clear basis to reject the first view that the document as such is completely invalid.
Its law is like that of a document, and so forth. This is the view of Halachot Gedolot. Based on what I established above regarding Rosh, who requires the signed witness in certain cases to testify orally, Tur's statement, "and so wrote my father Rosh," cannot mean that Rosh agrees with Halachot Gedolot in every detail. It means only that Rosh agrees with the fundamental proposition that one witness in writing is valid testimony and is not rejected as testimony from writing, contrary to Raavad. That is also why Rosh cites Halachot Gedolot in Perek Get Pashut. Ma'adanei Melech, however, notes that Halachot Gedolot's wording, "we impose an oath on the basis of this witness who signed," may imply that the witness must come and testify orally. If so, the apparent dispute between Rambam and Halachot Gedolot disappears, and Tur's statement that Rosh agrees with Halachot Gedolot can be read literally. But if Halachot Gedolot requires oral testimony, how can Rosh and Tur cite him as proof that one written witness has legal force against Raavad? Perhaps the proof works as follows. If it were established that the two signers intentionally joined as witnesses and one was disqualified, the qualified witness could not simply save himself by testifying orally, because he saw and joined with the invalid witness and the combined testimony would have been nullified. Halachot Gedolot must therefore be saying that we presume the relative signed merely for honor or to fill space, so the qualified witness remains individually valid. That presumption itself makes sense only if one written witness has some legal utility. Otherwise, why would the lender first obtain a meaningless one-witness writing and then add a relative merely to fill space or for honor? It would be a potsherd from the outset. Particularly since Halachot Gedolot holds that where many witnesses sign and a relative appears, the relative can be treated as filler only in certain positions, the concept of "filling" presupposes an otherwise meaningful instrument. Thus his ruling implies that one written witness has legal standing. Rosh's final comment that the valid witness is not disqualified through the relative because "he left space" may be Rosh's own addition, not Halachot Gedolot's exact language. If they intentionally joined in a single testimonial act, the disqualification would apply under the rule that witnesses are treated as though examined in beit din. Nevertheless, where the valid witness himself comes before beit din and says he knows the relative did not intend to testify and signed only for honor or to fill space, his statement can be accepted. The valid witness could, if he wished to lie, create another written proof or otherwise shape the testimony; his oral clarification therefore explains why his own written testimony remains usable. Thus Halachot Gedolot may still require oral testimony from the qualified witness while simultaneously proving that a one-witness writing is not inherently worthless. In any event, we do not find a clear and decisive dispute against the first view above that in an ordinary two-signature document containing one relative, the document itself is invalid unless the circumstances of the relative's signature are affirmatively clarified. Shach's attempt to establish the latter view as primary is therefore unpersuasive.
