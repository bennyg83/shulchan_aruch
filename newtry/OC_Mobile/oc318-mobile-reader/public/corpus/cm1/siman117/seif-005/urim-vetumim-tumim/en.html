If he made his slave an apotiki, etc. The Maggid Mishneh writes that the underlying loan must be documented by a written instrument, although the designation of the slave as an apotiki need not itself be written and may be made orally. The Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Kamma writes that if one made his slave an apotiki "in a document," the creditor can collect even from heirs. Perhaps he too means only that the loan itself is documented. The Bach, however, cites Tosafot in Bava Batra, Perek Chezkas HaBatim, who write: "Even if Rava holds that slaves are like land, he specifically states apotiki to teach that this works whether by writing or orally." This appears to show that no document is needed at all. It would be strained to say that Tosafot mean, as the Maggid Mishneh does, that the loan itself is written and only the apotiki designation is oral, and that the novelty is that a purchaser cannot say, "I did not know of the apotiki and assumed you had unencumbered property," because the slave's apotiki status has publicity. That is not the natural reading of Tosafot. However, Knesset HaGedolah already notes that Tosafot were driven to this formulation if slaves are treated like land, whereas if they are movables the analysis differs. More fundamentally, Tosafot follow their own position in Bava Kamma 11, s.v. "for example," that under strict law even a slave made an apotiki would not be collectible from heirs because lien liability is not d'oraisa. The reason the creditor can collect the slave from purchasers is its publicity, and once the Sages allowed collection from purchasers they did not distinguish and allowed collection from heirs as well. Thus for Tosafot the entire rule depends on publicity rather than on an intrinsic lien in the slave. If so, it indeed makes no difference whether the apotiki was written. An oral designation can be public enough to permit collection from purchasers, and the same rabbinic extension then reaches heirs. This works even if a slave is treated as movable property, and all the more if treated as land. But we do not rule like Tosafot on the underlying point. As I wrote at length above in siman 107, we follow the Rashba: if movables together with land are expressly subjected in a written instrument, the creditor can collect them from heirs because an actual lien has attached. He ordinarily cannot collect those movables from purchasers because the lien lacks publicity. The Rashba proves this from the very statement that if an ox is made an apotiki it cannot be collected from a purchaser because it has no publicity. The implication is that the lien itself may exist and would matter against heirs, where publicity is irrelevant, even though purchasers are protected. According to that approach, the creditor's position against purchasers can never be stronger than his underlying position against heirs merely because there is publicity. If an oral apotiki designation creates no enforceable lien at all over a slave treated as movable property, publicity cannot manufacture the missing lien. We do not accept Tosafot's special rule that publicity alone, even without a substantive lien, allows collection from purchasers. Thus, if slaves are movables, an oral apotiki designation is insufficient unless some written lien already attached to the slave. This follows directly from the Rashba's proof. If an oral statement, "My movables together with land are subject to you," were enough to create the substantive lien, then those movables should be collectible from heirs even without any written instrument, because heirs need no publicity. Yet the Rashba's rule specifically concerns a written lien over movables and land. The oral statement itself does not suffice. Similarly, an oral slave-apotiki cannot create a collectible right merely because the slave's status later becomes public. I therefore find the Maggid Mishneh's formulation difficult if understood literally. If he holds that the oral apotiki itself creates a substantive lien in the slave, why is any written loan needed? A valid lien plus publicity should be at least as strong as a field sold before witnesses, which collects from purchasers because the transaction is public. If, however, the oral apotiki does not create a substantive lien, how does it help that the general loan is written? The slave is treated as movable property and is not automatically included in a land lien. A written loan without a written clause subjecting the slave should not reach it. The better reading is that the written instrument includes a lien over movable property with land, or specifically includes the slave among the secured property. Such a clause creates the substantive lien and therefore works against heirs. Ordinarily it still lacks sufficient publicity to defeat purchasers. If the debtor additionally designates this particular slave as an apotiki, even orally, that unusual designation becomes public. The combination of a substantive written lien and the publicity of the apotiki allows collection from purchasers. This is also how the Rosh's language should be understood. The Rosh's own position requires explanation because in Bava Kamma he appears to agree with Tosafot that collection from purchasers can follow publicity even where collection from heirs would not otherwise exist. Why then does he require the apotiki to be written? The answer appears in a responsum of the Rosh, siman 86, law 11, where he writes that "the slave puts out a voice." At first sight that phrase is puzzling. The voice of the slave himself should not count like the publicity of two witnesses. Even one witness recorded in a document does not generate sufficient publicity to permit collection from purchasers. Why should purchasers be expected to rely on rumors spoken by the slave? The Rosh means that when the lien is written in an instrument, the witnesses ordinarily may not publicize a movable-property clause because such matters remain private. But if this particular slave is publicly designated as an apotiki, the slave himself spreads the fact, so the witnesses no longer treat the matter as private and the public is alerted to investigate. Purchasers who hear the slave's publicized status must inquire, and inquiry will reveal the written lien from the witnesses. The slave's "voice" therefore does not replace the document; it activates the publicity of the already documented lien. This reconciles the Rosh's rulings and explains why the written instrument is still necessary. The same reading should be given to the Maggid Mishneh. The document establishes the actual lien over movables or the slave, while the apotiki designation may be oral because its function is to create publicity. The unusual character of making a particular slave an apotiki naturally becomes known, as the Sema also reasons. But an oral apotiki without any underlying written lien does not itself create the substantive security where slaves are treated as movables. There is strong support from Tosafot in Kiddushin. The Gemara discusses a father who has only five selaim of encumbered property and five selaim of unencumbered property, while both he and his son require redemption. Rabbi Yehudah holds the son's redemption takes priority in a certain arrangement. Tosafot explain that the encumbered five selaim are property the father designated to the kohen as an apotiki, while the unencumbered five belong to the son. Later purchasers therefore cannot say, "We left you property from which to collect," because the son's property was never subject to the father's redemption obligation. The Maharsha asks why an apotiki is needed. The Gemara assumes according to Rabbi Yehudah that an obligation written in the Torah is treated like an obligation written in a document, and a written obligation ordinarily collects from purchasers. Why add an apotiki? The answer begins with Kiddushin 13. Rav Pappa says that an oral loan does not collect from purchasers because, although lien liability may be d'oraisa, an oral loan lacks publicity. Tosafot ask that Rav Pappa elsewhere holds lien liability is not d'oraisa and answer that the present case concerns an obligation written in the Torah, which has Torah lien force like a written obligation, but still may fail against purchasers for lack of publicity. Thus even if pidyon haben is treated as written in the Torah, that legal status alone does not create the necessary publicity. Tosafot therefore establish the property as an apotiki, which supplies publicity. Without the principle that a Torah-written obligation creates the substantive lien, however, the apotiki's publicity alone would not help. This is exactly our distinction. The underlying obligation must first have lien force; the apotiki supplies the missing publicity. In Bekhorot the Gemara may conclude that Rabbi Yehudah does not generally treat a Torah-written obligation as a written document, but the Tosafot in Kiddushin explain the particular alternative according to the view under discussion. Their words are coherent. One might ask why Tosafot did not simply establish the five encumbered selaim as an explicit apotiki made from the father's own property, which would itself prevent the purchasers from saying other property was left. That would require an explicit apotiki so strong that payment is limited to that land. Tosafot elsewhere treat such an apotiki as nearly acquired by the creditor and, in our earlier discussion, even allow an apotiki creditor to take enhancement beyond the debt. It would then resemble payment through land in a setting where the Torah requires monetary redemption rather than land. Tosafot therefore prefer an ordinary apotiki whose function is publicity rather than substantive conversion of the property into the kohen's own asset. We return to the practical halachah. A written foundation is required. The Baal HaTerumot and Tur also indicate that a slave is treated like land for this limited purpose of publicity, not that it is stronger than land in creating a lien without a document. Land itself does not collect from purchasers merely because someone orally declares it security without a valid lien structure. The slave's advantage is that, despite being movable property for many laws, its specific apotiki status becomes public; it is not that oral words alone create a security interest stronger than land. This also resolves a problem I raised above in siman 39 concerning Tosafot in Bekhorot. Tosafot begin with two questions and answer through the dispute of Rav Pappa concerning whether a Torah-written obligation is treated like a written document. One could still ask, according to an alternative explanation in which Rabbi Yehudah agrees that an ordinary oral loan does not collect from purchasers and the dispute is instead about another rule, why a Torah-written obligation should reach purchasers. According to what I have written, it does not do so by itself. It reaches purchasers only when joined with the publicity of an apotiki. Thus the two elements work together: substantive lien force from the written or Torah-written obligation, and publicity from the apotiki. There is, however, a further question in the pidyon haben sugya. The Mishnah says that after thirty days the child is presumed redeemed. If so, purchasers should be able to plead that the father already paid some kohen. Even if the father admits he did not redeem, his admission cannot harm purchasers, as the poskim repeatedly rule. The Ran raises a similar point in other lien contexts, and above in siman 39 the Sema and Shach dispute the effect of a judicial ruling where repayment is later claimed. Were it not for Tosafot's explanation, I might say that the father sold the property privately without witnesses. As against the kohen he would be believed that a sale occurred because he has a migo that he could say he already redeemed through another kohen. As against the purchasers he would be believed that he did not redeem because he has a migo that he never sold the property at all. This would coordinate the competing claims. But that cannot be Tosafot's case. They ask that if the kohen had already collected from the unencumbered property for the son's redemption, he should return it or purchasers should recover from him. Under the private-sale construction the kohen could simply say, "I do not believe your admission that these people are purchasers or that you had not previously redeemed," because their rights would depend entirely on unverified statements made after his collection. It therefore appears that Tosafot assume a public sale. How then is the repayment plea answered? If the property was made an apotiki, the apotiki itself may place the claim in a status where it is presumed unpaid absent a receipt, because the designated security remains outstanding. The Mishnah's general presumption that after thirty days redemption occurred concerns an ordinary case without such an apotiki. This may be the solution, although Tosafot in Bekhorot do not expressly mention the apotiki as the Maharsha notes. Another possibility follows from the fact that Tosafot must deal with a no-warranty sale, as I wrote above in siman 39, because otherwise the arrangement would not be sensible advice for preferring the son's redemption at the expense of other claimants. The main question is whether the kohen would later have to return what he collected. One can construct the timing so that the father sold immediately when the son's thirty days ended, while the witnesses remained continuously aware that no redemption had occurred, and then the father died. The father therefore could not have paid unnoticed, and the son would have no reason to pay from his own pocket because the kohen can collect from no-warranty purchasers of the father's property. That construction works where the father sold. It is much harder under Tosafot's alternative that the son himself sold inherited property. Tosafot prove the relevant land was inherited from the father, and an heir generally cannot sell inherited land until reaching the required age. One cannot plausibly say witnesses remained continuously with him for many years to establish that he never redeemed himself. This may be why Tosafot labor to show that the property was inherited from the father: it supports their first answer, that the father himself was the seller, rather than the alternative in which the adult son later sold. I am surprised the later authorities did not emphasize the basic repayment question. Tosafot in Gittin 51, s.v. "or perhaps," say there is no authority who holds an ordinary oral loan collects from purchasers. One might have challenged them from Rabbi Yehudah in Bekhorot according to Rashi. Tosafot elsewhere already address that difficulty. Their answer that a loan can have special publicity does not automatically solve pidyon haben, because the father normally redeems his son and the public may assume payment occurred. The obligation may therefore lose practical publicity through the ordinary expectation of redemption. This requires further study. The practical conclusion nevertheless remains: for a slave apotiki to be collectible from purchasers under our accepted halachah, there must be an underlying written lien. The specific apotiki designation may be oral if it creates the requisite publicity, but publicity alone cannot substitute for the substantive lien.
