Because it has publicity. The Gemara in Bava Batra 44 states that if one makes his ox an apotiki and sells it, the creditor does not collect from the purchaser, but if he makes his slave an apotiki and sells him, the creditor does collect, because the latter has publicity whereas the former does not. The Baal HaTerumot explains that a slave is like land and that the fact he was made an apotiki becomes known. At first glance this might suggest that because a slave is legally like land in several respects, he is treated like land here as well. That is difficult, for if so, even without being made an apotiki he should be collectible from purchasers as land is. Moreover, legal classification as land does not itself create publicity, and the Gemara should then have said simply that one is like land and the other is not. Rather, the Tur's precise language resolves the matter: when a slave is made an apotiki, even an ordinary one, the creditor collects because the matter becomes known, and in that respect the slave resembles land. Thus it is the apotiki designation that creates publicity, and once there is publicity the law resembles land, whose collectibility from purchasers is likewise based upon public notice of its liens. Possibly the slave himself spreads word that his master designated him, or perhaps because a slave is significant property, an apotiki designation made before witnesses naturally becomes known. In any event, only after he is made an apotiki does this publicity arise. Without that designation, he is not treated as land for this purpose. In general, a slave is treated as land only for Torah laws where that classification was given, not automatically for every monetary rule.
