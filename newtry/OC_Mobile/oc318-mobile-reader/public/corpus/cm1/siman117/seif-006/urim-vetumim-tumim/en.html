He collects his debt, etc. In Gittin, Perek HaSholeach, the Tanna Kamma and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel dispute one who destroys another person's lien. The Tanna Kamma exempts him and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel obligates him. The Gemara explains that they dispute liability for damaging another's lien. The Rambam rules like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that he is liable. A difficulty is raised from Bava Kamma 33. If one slaughtered an ox that was subject to payment as a tam, the Gemara says that according to the view one is liable for destroying another's lien, the slaughterer must compensate the injured party for the reduction caused by slaughter. Yet in Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, chapter 5, the Rambam does not state this liability. See Yam Shel Shlomo and below in siman 407, as cited by the Shach. It appears that the Rambam understands liability for destroying a lien as a rabbinic penalty, because the act is fundamentally indirect damage. The Yerushalmi likewise implies this. He then applies Tosafot's reasoning at the beginning of Bava Batra concerning a vineyard partition: where the wrongdoer is harming his own property at the same time, there is less need for a penalty designed to deter deliberate indirect harm, because a person is not ordinarily expected to damage himself merely in order to harm another. A tam ox is half the owner's property and half subject to the injured party's claim. Slaughtering it reduces the owner's own half just as it reduces the injured party's half. There is therefore no reason to impose the special penalty for destroying another's lien. The Rambam consequently follows the anonymous baraita that exempts him in that case, while still ruling generally that one who destroys another's lien is liable. Examine this carefully. The Raavad disagrees and holds the halachah does not follow Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. He says the rule concerning one who burns another's debt instrument is no proof, because there the wrongdoer performs a direct act against the other person's property. It occurred to me that the Raavad may rely on the Yerushalmi, which explains Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel as a penalty analogous to Rabbi Meir's rule concerning an instrument containing ribbit, where Rabbi Meir penalizes the holder and allows collection of neither principal nor interest. Since we do not rule like Rabbi Meir, we likewise need not rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel in an ordinary case of lien damage. Yet where the person who destroys the lien personally benefits from doing so, even the Sages may impose liability. This resembles an antedated document or a case where interest was capitalized into principal, where the Sages also invalidate or penalize because otherwise the wrongdoer has an incentive to repeat the act. Thus, if one destroys another's lien without personal benefit, the Raavad may exempt him, while if he profits from the destruction he is liable even according to the Sages. This resolves an apparent contradiction in the Raavad. Above in siman 104 the Tur cites him as ruling that if an heir subjects his father's assets in a way that prevents the father's creditor from collecting, the heir must compensate the father's creditor as one who destroys another's lien. The commentators ask how this accords with the Raavad's gloss here exempting one who destroys another's lien. According to what I have written, there is no contradiction. In the heir's case he directly benefits, because he uses the father's property to satisfy his own debt. Since the destruction enriches him, everyone agrees that he is liable. This is clear.
