Monetary consecration does not displace a prior lien in the same way as bodily sanctity. Rashi and Tosafot explain Rava's principle that consecration, chametz, and emancipation displace liens as referring primarily to a sanctity that cannot be redeemed, such as altar sanctity or a personal konam. Once such sanctity takes effect, it does not later fall away merely because a creditor comes to collect. Monetary consecration is different because it can be redeemed, and the lien can therefore reemerge. Rambam takes a different position and says that even monetary consecration can displace the lien temporarily. When the consecrated land is redeemed and becomes ordinary property in the purchaser's hands, the prior creditor can then collect from the redeemer. Raavad disagrees and says monetary consecration does not truly displace the lien at all, and that the small redemption payment is required only so that people should not say consecrated property leaves without redemption. The conceptual distinction depends on whether monetary sanctity can fall away by itself. Tosafot says bodily sanctity, once it takes effect for even a moment, does not fall away without an appropriate act. Monetary sanctity can fall away through redemption and is therefore weaker than the creditor's prior lien. This also explains fields returning at Jubilee: a purchaser's temporary ownership can end at Jubilee because his right was temporary, while bodily sanctity does not simply disappear. Rashi's own approach is somewhat different. He says that even according to Rava, who holds that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward, encumbered property is already not fully "in the debtor's possession" for purposes of ordinary sale or monetary consecration. The debtor owns it but the creditor's lien removes full control. Bodily sanctity, however, can take effect even on property not fully in one's possession. This reading distinguishes altar sanctity from monetary consecration at the very threshold of whether sanctity can attach.
There is substantial reason to say that monetary consecration itself does not displace even a movable lien. Mishneh LaMelech assumed that dedication of movables should be no better than sale, since an ordinary creditor cannot follow movables after sale. Tosafot in Arakhin, however, expressly leaves this as a doubt and suggests the opposite: even though a sale of movables defeats the creditor's claim, monetary consecration may not. The underlying reason is that the rabbinic removal of liens from sold movables was enacted for commercial stability. Consecration is not an ordinary private sale. Rashba also compares consecration to abandonment and writes that a creditor's lien can survive abandonment more strongly than ordinary transfer. This supports the view that even movables subjected to a debt may remain reachable after monetary consecration. If so, an oral loan may likewise be collected from property dedicated with monetary sanctity. Rif's ruling concerning a deathly ill person who consecrated his property and then said, "Give so and so the maneh I owe him," can be read in its simple sense. Even without a written document, the debt can be paid from the monetary consecration when the decedent clearly directs payment. Ramban reinterprets Rif because he assumed an oral loan cannot collect from encumbered property, but if monetary consecration is unlike sale, Rif's plain language is entirely coherent. This appears to be the better explanation.

Rama writes that if a debtor forbids his property to the creditor by konam, he cannot thereby defeat the lien unless he forbids the property to everyone. The source in Tosafot is that the Sages strengthened the creditor's lien against a private konam designed solely to defeat him. In my view a general konam is not necessarily stronger. We rule that a general konam, one that prohibits an object to everyone, has redemption. It therefore resembles monetary consecration rather than bodily sanctity. If monetary consecration does not displace a prior lien, a general konam should not either. The private konam of the type "my work is forbidden to you" has no redemption and would, by strict law, resemble bodily sanctity. The Sages nevertheless strengthened the husband's or creditor's lien so that one cannot intentionally use a private konam to destroy it. The result is that neither form of konam should ordinarily displace the lien: the private form because the Sages strengthened the lien, and the general form because it is redeemable and therefore no stronger than monetary consecration. Tosafot's reference to garments spread over a corpse is different. There the object becomes prohibited to everyone through a nonredeemable prohibition that, once it takes effect, does not fall away. That can displace a lien. The same is true of true bodily sanctity. The language of Rama suggesting that a general konam itself displaces the lien therefore requires study.
Some authorities disagree and hold that even a prohibition directed only against the creditor can be effective. Sema reads the dissent that way. According to what I wrote, the principal case for this dissent is indeed a private, nonredeemable konam. A general konam, being redeemable, should not be stronger than monetary consecration. Maharit further distinguishes between forbidding specific property to the creditor and forbidding one's own personal benefit. If the debtor says merely, "My benefit is forbidden to you," the creditor's existing right to collect is not necessarily something the debtor can prohibit, because one cannot forbid to another what is already legally that other's right. This is comparable to marital relations where one spouse cannot use a vow to remove an existing obligation to the other unless the law specially recognizes the prohibition. Accordingly, even the dissent should not be read as saying that every verbal prohibition defeats a creditor's lien. The effective case is one where the object itself can receive a genuine nonredeemable prohibition and no rabbinic strengthening blocks it.
