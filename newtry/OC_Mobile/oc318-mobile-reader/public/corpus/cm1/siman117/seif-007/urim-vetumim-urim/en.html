Like sanctity of the object itself. The Mechaber follows Rashi and Tosafos, who hold that sanctity attaching to the object itself removes a prior creditor's lien, whereas mere monetary consecration does not. Nevertheless, even monetary consecration must be redeemed for a minimal amount, so that people should not say that consecrated property left consecration without redemption. See Tumim. Rambam disagrees and holds that even monetary consecration removes a prior lien. I concluded that even according to Rambam, nowadays the creditor himself may redeem it. The practical difference in Rambam's view concerns an apotiki. See there.
With the intention that it be buried with him. This requires an actual act, such as spreading the garment over the corpse. Mere designation and preparation do not prohibit it, because we rule that designation alone has no legal effect.
The creditor redeems it for a small amount. See Tumim. Yam Shel Shlomo holds that this must be a recognized redemption amount, such as four zuz or a similar publicly meaningful amount. I wrote in Tumim that with monetary consecration there is no need for such stringency.
And some disagree. The first opinion holds that the Sages strengthened a creditor's lien in such cases, because otherwise every debtor could consecrate property and thereby defeat his creditor. That concern does not apply to a garment spread over a corpse, which becomes prohibited to everyone. There is no reason to think people would ordinarily destroy their own property merely to defeat a creditor. The dissenting opinion does not recognize such strengthening of the lien. See Tumim. I concluded that with movable property that was not expressly encumbered by note, everyone agrees with the dissenting view. Nevertheless, the debtor is compelled until he regrets the vow and seeks release from it. This is the view of Ran and Maggid Mishneh.
