Like sanctity of the body. The Mechaber follows Tosafos and Rashi, who hold that when we say consecration removes a lien, this refers to intrinsic sanctity, whereas monetary consecration does not remove the lien. The Mishnah in Arachin teaches that if one consecrated property while owing a ketubah or another debt, the redeemer redeems it on condition of paying the creditor. The Gemara asks why redemption is needed at all and answers that it is because of Rabbi Eliezer, lest people say that consecrated property leaves consecration without redemption. According to Tosafos this is clear. The Mishnah concerns monetary sanctity, which does not remove the lien, so the Gemara asks why any redemption is needed and answers that it is required only because of appearance. Rambam, however, holds that even monetary consecration removes a lien, although after redemption the creditor's lien revives. Therefore the redeemer initially redeems the property for a small amount, because he knows that the creditor will afterward seize it. The Tur cites both views and rules like Tosafos. Kesef Mishneh, Lechem Mishneh, and Gidulei Terumah understand Rambam as reading the Gemara's question differently: why should an outsider redeem the property? Let the creditor himself give a perutah to the Temple treasury, redeem it, and keep it against his debt. The Gemara answers that because of appearance the redemption must specifically be performed by another person. Gidulei Terumah asks from Pesachim, where the Gemara says that when the borrower consecrated property, the creditor came and redeemed it, apparently showing that the creditor himself can redeem. It is strained to say that "creditor" there is not literal.
It appears. The true difference between an outsider and the creditor is this. The creditor gives only one payment, so people could say that consecrated property was released without proper redemption and merely through a perutah. An outsider, however, effectively pays the full redemption value: one perutah goes to the Temple treasury and the balance goes to the creditor. Ultimately the entire value has been paid. This works under the law of the Mishnah, when consecrated property had to be redeemed for its true value. Nowadays, when it is permitted initially to redeem property worth one hundred with one perutah, there is no distinction between the creditor and anyone else. Everyone redeems for a perutah. Accordingly, nowadays there should be no practical difference between Tosafos and Rambam. According to everyone redemption is required even for monetary consecration, and after the perutah redemption the creditor collects his debt. Even the creditor himself can perform the redemption. Why then did the Tur cite the dispute and rule like Tosafos? The answer is that there is a significant difference whether the lien remained continuously in force during consecration or was removed and only revived after redemption. For example, with an apotiki the creditor can collect appreciation on the ground that "my land appreciated," as discussed above in siman 115. If the borrower consecrated the apotiki, it appreciated while in the Temple treasurer's possession, and it was later redeemed, then according to Tosafos, who says the lien never disappeared and redemption was required only because of appearance, the creditor can say that his land appreciated and take the appreciation. According to Rambam, however, the apotiki lien was removed by consecration, and only the general debt revived after redemption. The creditor has no right to the appreciation that arose while the property was consecrated. There are many other practical differences. It is even possible that after redemption the creditor can be removed with money, because the specific apotiki lien was broken by consecration and only the debt itself returned. This is why the Tur's ruling matters.
For a small amount. Arachin 29 relates that a man consecrated his property, and Rabbi Yehudah told him to take four zuz and throw them into the river, following Shmuel's rule that consecrated property worth one hundred can be redeemed upon a perutah. The Gemara asks why even four zuz are required rather than one perutah and answers that it is for publicity. Rambam therefore rules that four zuz are required. Rif, however, in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah rules that a perutah is enough. Ran asks how Rif can ignore the Gemara's requirement of four zuz. It appears that Rif understood that the publicity was not required because people might suspect a Jew of misappropriating consecrated property without redemption. Jews are not suspected of such sacrilege. Rather, that case concerned property declared cherem. There was room to think the owner intended a cherem for kohanim, as Ulla indeed said that had he been present he would have given it all to kohanim. Rabbi Yehudah therefore ordered four zuz thrown into the river in order to publicize that an unspecified cherem goes to Heaven rather than to kohanim. Where the person expressly consecrated to Heaven, no such publicity is needed. Therefore Rif omitted the four zuz requirement. Maharshal nevertheless rules like Rambam that four zuz are needed. But with monetary consecration according to Rashi and Tosafos, where the consecration never removed the creditor's lien and redemption is required only because of appearance, everyone can agree that one perutah suffices. The Gemara's four zuz ruling concerned a cherem that took effect by Torah law.
And some disagree. In my opinion, regarding movable property that was not expressly subjected in a document, everyone agrees that the borrower can prohibit it by a vow. Tosafos's entire basis for saying otherwise is the Yerushalmi, which asks how a person can prohibit by konam what is needed for his wife's support and answers that her support is rabbinic, whereas a creditor's lien is Torah law, since we rule that property liens are of Torah law. The Sages strengthened the creditor's lien, as Rashba writes explicitly in responsum 618. But movable property has no Torah lien. The borrower can sell it, and after his death ordinary movable property is not subject to the creditor at all. There is therefore no lien upon the objects themselves, only a personal obligation resting upon the borrower, whom beit din can compel to pay. Why should a konam not take effect on the objects? There is no property lien here for the Sages to strengthen. Beit din can instead compel him until he regrets the vow and seeks release, as Ran and Maggid Mishneh write. The case of the garment spread upon a corpse proves nothing. There the garment was the wife's own property that she had brought into the marriage. If the husband sold it she could recover it. Her lien upon that specific property was therefore strengthened by the Sages. Tosafos consequently needed to distinguish whether the prohibition was directed only against the creditor or against everyone. But with the borrower's own movable property, the dissenting view is correct according to everyone.
