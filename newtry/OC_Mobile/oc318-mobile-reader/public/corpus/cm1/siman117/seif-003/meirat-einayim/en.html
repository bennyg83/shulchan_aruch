He made his ox an apotiki and sold it. Rashba writes that the same applies if he gave it as a gift: the creditor cannot collect it from the recipient.
The creditor does not collect it. Under strict Talmudic law this assumes that the movable property was not also encumbered through agav with land. Even where such an agav lien existed, later custom nevertheless developed not to collect sold movables because of market protection, as explained in siman 60 and at the end of siman 113.
Even if he made it an apotiki in a written document. Movable property is not treated as property over which a lasting documentary lien can operate publicly, because the purchaser takes the item into his own house and there is no continuing land-type lien memorialized by the document.
Even if the purchaser knew, etc. The Tur gives the example that the purchaser had been a witness to the loan. Even an explicit warning by the lender not to buy the borrower's movables does not help, because no enforceable lien against purchasers took effect on the movables at all.
