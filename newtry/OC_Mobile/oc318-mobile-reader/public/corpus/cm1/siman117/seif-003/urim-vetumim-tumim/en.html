If he made his ox, etc. The Sema cites a responsum of the Rashba that the same rule applies if the ox was given as a gift, because a gift and sale have the same status. The Bach, however, cites Mordechai HaAruch that a creditor may collect from the recipient of a gift because the recipient suffers no purchase-price loss. There was no need to rely only on the Mordechai, for Tosafot in Ketubot 49b, s.v. "he and his wife," explicitly say that the rule that an ox made an apotiki and then sold cannot be collected from applies specifically to a sale. If it was given as a gift, the creditor collects it. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 109, s.v. "borrower," were uncertain, but in Ketubot they decide the matter. The law is therefore at least doubtful. The reason for Tosafot's distinction requires explanation, because in most contexts a gift is treated like a sale regarding encumbered property. Tosafot appear to follow Rashi's interpretation of the rule that consecration, chametz, and emancipation can remove an asset from a lien. Where one made an ox an apotiki and then consecrated it, Rashi says specifically bodily sanctity removes the lien, not mere monetary consecration. The case necessarily concerns an apotiki without a general lien over movable property together with land; otherwise there would have been no need to formulate it as an apotiki. Yet even then Rashi says monetary consecration does not remove the creditor's right. If a gift were no stronger than a sale, monetary consecration should at least be comparable to a gift and defeat the lien. Rashi therefore implies that the creditor can collect an apotiki from a gift recipient. Rashi's proof is likely from the Mishnah in Arakhin concerning one who consecrated his property while owing a ketubah or debt. The Gemara there makes clear that the debts remain attached, so a redeemer redeems in a manner that permits satisfying the creditors. Tosafot state that "his property" includes both land and movable property. Why should movable property remain subject to the creditor after consecration if an ordinary gift would remove it? This indicates that consecration, which resembles a gift, does not necessarily cut off the creditor's apotiki claim. It is strained to force the Mishnah into a case of an express lien over movables together with land. The Tur does write such a lien, but Rashi evidently found it forced to add that unstated detail. The Ran and Tosafot in Nedarim likewise follow Rashi that the rule concerns an ox made an apotiki and that only bodily sanctity removes the lien. This supports a distinction between gift and sale. One cannot prove the contrary from Bava Batra, where the Gemara says that one who sold a field cannot testify concerning it but one who sold a cow or garment can. Why did it not distinguish within movables: if he sold the cow he may testify because even an apotiki creditor cannot collect after sale, but if he gave it as a gift he may not testify because an apotiki creditor could collect? The answer is that the Gemara specifically teaches the field case because one might otherwise have thought the seller may testify there as well. If he still owns land, the creditor can collect from him; if he owns none, what difference does the testimony make? And concern for being called a wicked borrower would seem to apply equally toward the purchaser. The Gemara therefore teaches the special rule that a no-warranty sale changes that calculus. A gift of a cow would not present the same issue, because one who gave it away has not taken the recipient's money and does not incur the same concern. The Gemara's wording therefore does not resolve our dispute. The practical rule remains doubtful.
