To a creditor, or to a woman for her ketubah. This follows the baraita, 'One who designates his field, etc.' Rif explains in a responsum that it concerns an ordinary apotiki, as is also stated in the Jerusalem Talmud at the end of Sheviit, and as explained below.
But all this, etc. This is derived from the Gemara's specification, 'and a river swept it away.' Therefore, although the creditor may collect from another place even while the designated field remains in the debtor's possession, as Ran writes there, the law is stated to teach that the debtor cannot remove the creditor from the designated property by offering other property. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's statement there, that a woman does not collect, etc., expressly indicates that she cannot be removed to other property, and the same applies to a creditor. According to the first Tanna as well, when a woman or creditor collects from other property, that is because the designated field was sold, as the Jerusalem Talmud explains: one who encumbers a field to his wife and then sells it, if she wishes she may collect from other property. The colleagues asked whether she should not collect only from encumbered property. Rabbi Yose answered, 'Unencumbered property stands before her, and you say she should collect from encumbered property?' The mishna concerns a case where he did not say, 'You shall have payment only from this field.' But if he said, 'You shall have payment only from this field,' she collects only from it. Thus, even though the case concerns an ordinary apotiki, absent the sale she would have had to collect from that field. It also appears that even the creditor may not collect elsewhere, contrary to Ran. Ran's words are drawn from Rashba's novellae there, based on the Tosefta in Ketubot: Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says that one who designates a field as an apotiki for a woman's ketubah and sells it to another places the choice in her hands; she may collect from the purchaser or from other property. But no proof follows, because that rule is specifically where it was sold. On the contrary, the expression 'the choice is in her hands' implies this limitation, for otherwise what novelty would there be in saying that she may collect from the purchaser when she could in any event collect elsewhere? Rashba too speaks only of a sale. The plain sense of the Bavli, 'and a river swept it away,' likewise supports this, as does the case where he sold it, as explained above. The Jerusalem Talmud there further says that a deed containing a property lien is subject to shemittah, for Rabbi Ba said in the name of Rav: if he designated land for the debt, shemittah releases it; the exception applies only where he designated, etc. Bava Kamma likewise states that if one made his slave an apotiki and sold him, the creditor collects from the slave, just as he collects from orphans. See citations 40 and 62.
Only with money. This applies even to an explicit apotiki, as the Tur and all the authorities write. Only with respect to purchasers does the first chapter of Bava Metzia state that they cannot remove the creditor from an explicit apotiki, and the same is true of orphans, as explained above in siman 115. But the debtor himself may remove the creditor by paying money. Proof follows from Gittin, where, concerning a master who freed a slave, Rav says that the first master is subject to the law stated there, in accordance with Rava. Tosafot explains that the case concerns an explicit apotiki. Yet if the debtor could not remove the creditor even by money, Rava too would agree that collection takes effect retroactively, as stated where the Gemara says, 'Since he had money, etc.' Tosafot there and in the chapter HaManiach explain that if one consecrated the property, the consecration is effective because the debtor can remove the creditor with money. Thus consecration displaces a lien only where the debtor can remove the creditor, and consecration and manumission are compared. Certainly this is so for an ordinary apotiki, since even a purchaser may remove the creditor, as Bava Metzia states. The Gemara therefore had to establish the other case as an explicit apotiki.

He made his field, etc.; but, etc. Tosafot in Bava Batra 45b writes that an explicit apotiki is like a sale without warranty. See also Tosafot, Bava Metzia 14b. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 43, disagrees and writes that even if the field is discovered not to belong to the debtor, the creditor does not collect from other property, based on Tosafot's difficulty in Bava Batra. This is the basis of the opinion introduced as 'some say.' See citation 40.
He made his field, etc. This is the second baraita there, and the ruling follows the first Tanna. Rif explains in a responsum that the case concerns an ordinary apotiki. The Jerusalem Talmud in Sheviit likewise states: one who encumbers a field to another and then sells it, Rabbi Acha says the sale is effective temporarily, while Rabbi Yose says it is not effective even temporarily. It then teaches: one who writes a field as an apotiki for a woman's ketubah or for a creditor's debt and sells it, the sale is effective, but the purchaser must take account of the risk. The mishna concerns one who said, 'You shall have payment from this field'; their dispute concerns one who said, 'You shall have payment only from this field.' The cited baraita is the first Tanna's view. The Jerusalem Talmud in Yevamot similarly discusses property of a wife's melog or tzon barzel estate and distinguishes a permanent sale from a temporary one. It then derives the rule from the baraita concerning an apotiki for a ketubah or debt, where Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says that a sale of a field designated for a ketubah is ineffective because a woman should not have to pursue litigation in the courts. Rif cites this briefly. Hence the ruling, 'and when he comes, etc.; when does this apply, etc.' Ran and Maggid Mishneh explain that even an ordinary apotiki differs from other property in two respects. First, a permanent sale does not prevent eventual collection from it. Second, even a temporary sale does not prevent collection if no unencumbered property remains, even though property sold later remains available. This is the meaning of, 'if he does not find,' and of the Jerusalem Talmud's warning that the purchaser must protect himself. See Baer HaGolah and Tosafot, Bava Metzia 14b. Tosafot's difficulty can be resolved because, as explained above, even an ordinary apotiki is collected first. It may therefore be an ordinary apotiki, and the creditor collects the improvement only through an after-acquired-property clause. Only with an explicit apotiki does the creditor collect improvement as an unauthorized improver, as explained in the Gemara. See also Tosafot, Bava Batra 157b. The case must concern an ordinary apotiki, because the baraita explains the mishna in Gittin that one does not collect for consumed produce or land improvement from encumbered property. The Gemara explains consumed produce in the case of a robber, because an ordinary creditor does not collect produce. If the case concerned an explicit apotiki, however, a creditor would collect produce according to Rosh and the Tur, as in the law of one who enters another's field without permission. The Jerusalem Talmud likewise says that unencumbered property stands before her, and only where he said, 'You shall have payment only from this field,' does she collect exclusively from it. The dispute between Rabbi Acha and Rabbi Yose is therefore within the first Tanna's position, and the law follows that Tanna. See citations 40, 62, 115, 133, and 241.
But most authorities, etc. Rambam also agrees to this rule. He speaks only of an ordinary apotiki and adds that a permanent sale remains effective until the moment of collection, even where the apotiki was explicit, and certainly where it was ordinary. These authorities explain the Jerusalem Talmud's statement, 'but if he sold them permanently, etc.,' as referring only to slaves of a wife's tzon barzel estate. With an apotiki, even an explicit one, there is no distinction between temporary and permanent sale. The Jerusalem Talmud's dispute whether the sale is effective temporarily concerns a permanent sale, and the dispute is whether the sale is void immediately or remains effective until collection. Ramban writes that because we do not know which opinion is authoritative, the sale remains effective until collection. Their dispute concerns an explicit apotiki, as explained there. The additional rule, that the creditor collects from it even where other encumbered property exists, follows from the Jerusalem Talmud's statement that if he said, 'You shall have payment only from this field,' he collects only from it, even though unencumbered property is present.
(Supplement) But most authorities, etc. Nimukei Yosef uses this principle in the first chapter of Bava Kamma to resolve the case of Reuven who sold with warranty. He asks how the creditor may collect from the purchaser if the debtor retained unencumbered property, and answers that the property was made an explicit apotiki. See citation 92.
