He collects from the other property, etc. The designation was mistaken from the outset, because the creditor never had any right in this field, since it did not belong to the debtor. If, however, the field did belong to the debtor but was later taken by an earlier creditor, this creditor cannot return against the debtor, because he stipulated that payment would be only from this field, and payment could have been obtained from it had the debtor had funds with which to satisfy the earlier creditor.
If he does not find unencumbered property, he collects it from them. This is the language of the Rambam in chapter 18 of Hilchot Malveh. Rama's gloss, stating that most commentators disagree, necessarily assumes that the Rambam's later case, where one made his field an apotiki and then sold it, includes an express apotiki, and that even then the Rambam permits collection from the purchaser only where no unencumbered property exists. The other commentators, according to this reading, hold that with an express apotiki the creditor collects from that field even where other unencumbered property exists. After careful examination, however, this cannot be the Rambam's meaning. The Rif writes in a responsum that the baraita stating that one collects from other property concerns only an ordinary apotiki, while with an express apotiki the creditor collects from that field even if other property exists. The Rambam generally follows the Rif, and there is no reason to assume disagreement here. Moreover, immediately before this ruling the Rambam discusses the case where the debtor stipulated that payment would be only from this field, namely an express apotiki. He then begins a separate clause, 'If he made his field an apotiki and sold it,' which strongly indicates that this separate clause concerns an ordinary apotiki. Otherwise he could simply have continued, 'and if he sold it.' The Maggid Mishneh also infers from the Rambam's words 'if he does not find unencumbered property,' rather than 'if there was no unencumbered property at the time of sale,' that even if the debtor later sold his other property, the creditor may collect this apotiki from its purchaser. The Maggid Mishneh derives this from the Yerushalmi's statement that the purchaser must look out for himself, and that statement is explained there regarding an ordinary apotiki. It therefore appears that the Rambam limits the condition concerning lack of unencumbered property to an ordinary apotiki. With an express apotiki, he agrees with the Rif that the creditor collects from that field even where the debtor still has other property. The Rambam omitted this because he generally confines himself to the language of the Gemara. On this point his view agrees with the other commentators. Their dispute concerns only his concluding distinction between a temporary and permanent sale, which he derived from the Yerushalmi. The other commentators explain that distinction only in connection with the preceding law of property brought into marriage. Some have forced Rama's words into a different reading, saying that Rama too understood the Rambam as speaking only of an ordinary apotiki, and intended to disagree only with the Rambam's statement that a permanent sale is ineffective. They argue by an a fortiori inference from an express apotiki. I do not find this satisfactory, because Rama's language does not support such an interpretation.
