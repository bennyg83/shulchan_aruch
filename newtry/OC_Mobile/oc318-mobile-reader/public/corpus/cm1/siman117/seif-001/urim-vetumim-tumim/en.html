But so long as it remains in his possession, etc. See Urim. Where the creditor's ordinary right is to intermediate-quality land but the designated property is inferior, or the reverse, everyone agrees that he may insist on receiving the legal grade to which he is entitled rather than being bound by a merely ordinary apotiki. There is a proof from the end of Ketubot. The Mishnah says that where two people lent money to one another, each collects from the other. The Gemara asks according to Rabbi Shimon, who holds that we do not perform pointless reciprocal collections and who appraises property according to general standards, so that there would be no difference whether one debtor owns intermediate land and the other inferior land. The Gemara labors to find a case where reciprocal collection matters. Why not simply establish it where one debtor made ordinary property an apotiki? Then one creditor would receive the intermediate-quality land to which he is ordinarily entitled, while the other would receive only the inferior apotiki, or conversely one would receive intermediate land and the other superior land designated as his apotiki. Necessarily, an ordinary apotiki does not alter the grading rules of intermediate, inferior, and superior land. Reciprocal collection would still be pointless. The Gemara could not establish the Mishnah as an explicit apotiki, however, because then Admon's argument, "If I truly owed you, why did you borrow from me?" would fail. If the first lender's property was expressly designated with the stipulation that payment could come only from it, the creditor could not compel him to pay cash. Although the debtor may choose to redeem the apotiki with money, the creditor cannot force cash payment contrary to the stipulation. The debtor could therefore genuinely have needed cash and borrowed. If the second lender made an explicit apotiki, then the first lender acted even more reasonably: his claim reaches all the other's property, while the other's claim reaches only one designated field and may even be lost if that field is swept away. Thus the Mishnah cannot be forced into an explicit apotiki. An ordinary apotiki, however, would have been available if it changed land grades. Since the Gemara does not use it, the rule stated above is established.
And some disagree, etc. See Urim. With respect to encumbered property, the creditor certainly cannot tell the purchaser, "Go instead to later purchasers who bought afterward." This resolves Tosafot in Bava Metzia 15 and several difficulties I raised above in siman 115. Tosafot ask how the baraita's statement that principal is collected from encumbered property can occur, since a purchaser should say, "I left you other property from which to collect." They answer that the field was an apotiki. Tosafot then ask why the Gemara can infer that an after-acquired-property clause is effective, since it could simply establish the baraita as an apotiki. This language seems puzzling: they had already been forced to say it was an apotiki. The Maharsha similarly asks how the Gemara can cite the baraita as support for Shmuel's rule that a creditor takes enhancement if the baraita necessarily concerns an apotiki. According to the present distinction, all is clear. Tosafot need not mean an explicit apotiki, which is uncommon and creates other problems discussed above. They can mean an ordinary apotiki. Later purchasers may exist, but the first purchaser cannot force the creditor to go to them. Tosafot's question concerned principal from encumbered property, not enhancement from unencumbered property. Thus the baraita can initially involve an ordinary apotiki and still prove that an after-acquired-property clause works and that an ordinary creditor takes enhancement. With an ordinary apotiki the creditor cannot say, "My own land was enhanced." He still needs an after-acquired lien for the enhancement. Tosafot therefore ask: once we have already introduced an apotiki to solve the principal issue, why not go further and establish it as an explicit apotiki? That is the meaning of their words, "establish it as an apotiki." This reading is precise. It also gives a clean explanation of the Gemara's initial statement that the creditor is owed only the value of the land and not the enhancement, a formulation that troubled many commentators because no creditor may take more than the amount of his debt. Even at the initial stage the case concerns an ordinary apotiki. The creditor may have an additional debt, but only part of his claim is secured by this apotiki. As to the unsecured balance, the purchaser could ordinarily direct him to later purchasers. Nevertheless, once the creditor has lawfully entered this field to collect the apotiki debt, he can take the enhancement toward the remaining debt as well, because he is already in the process of collection and need not be redirected. For example, the creditor's apotiki claim may equal only the principal value of the field, while additional sums are owed under a separate unsecured claim. Once he collects the field, he uses the enhancement toward that additional debt. He does not thereby take more than the total amount owed to him. Because the enhancement is not itself part of the apotiki, he must reimburse the purchaser's expenses; as to that additional claim he had no special lien on this field and the purchaser should not lose his expenditure. Why may the purchaser not still say, regarding that additional debt, "Go to the later purchasers"? The answer is that the rabbinic rule directing a creditor away from one purchaser toward another applies when he is seeking to initiate collection. Where he is already in lawful possession for collection, we do not compel him to relinquish property subject to his general lien and pursue someone else. This reasoning works only if the creditor takes the field itself and merely pays the purchaser money for his protected share. He is then in possession. If the purchaser instead keeps a portion of the land itself, the creditor cannot be called possessor of that portion, since the purchaser has held it all along. This is why the Gemara must infer that the purchaser is removed with money rather than retaining land. It then asks whether that monetary removal rule is accepted by all authorities. At the conclusion the Gemara instead establishes the case as an explicit apotiki, where the special rules apply directly. In my view this interpretation is correct and avoids the many forced explanations offered elsewhere.
And it was discovered that the field had not been his. This is Tosafot's view. The Ramban and Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 43, disagree. Their proof is from Shmuel's rule that if Reuven sold a field to Shimon without warranty, Reuven may not testify on Shimon's behalf because he has an interest in preserving the field for his own creditor. The Gemara asks: if Reuven has other land, the creditor can collect from him; if he has none, what difference does this testimony make? The commentators ask why the Gemara did not simply answer that the field had been made an apotiki. From this they infer that even where the field is an apotiki, if it is later found not to have belonged to the seller, the seller does not reimburse the apotiki creditor, just as a purchaser without warranty cannot recover. Otherwise there would still be a practical difference, since the creditor could seek payment after the true owner takes the field. Tosafot say the Gemara could indeed have answered this way, though that is somewhat strained. See what I wrote above in siman 37. It may be explained according to Rav Pappa, who says the case is one where the purchaser recognizes that the property is like the seller's donkey, meaning he knows it was not stolen and is properly the seller's. According to Rav Pappa, Shmuel's case involves a purchaser who recognizes the seller's title. If the true owner nevertheless later takes the field, the seller could still defeat an apotiki creditor's claim by showing that the seizure was wrongful. The seller has one witness who can join him in testifying for the purchaser, as the Rashbam explains, and the purchaser himself also knows the facts and can testify once the field has already been taken from him and he is no longer interested in preserving it. The two can therefore establish that the true owner wrongfully seized the field. In such a situation the apotiki creditor cannot complain that his designated security was lost through the seller's act; the seller can say, "Here are witnesses that the claimant wrongfully deprived me," which is comparable to the field being swept away. At minimum there is insufficient basis to extract other property from the seller. This may be Tosafot's intention when they say the Gemara could have answered with an apotiki, except that the resulting discussion would work only according to Rav Pappa. The Gemara therefore gives an answer that works according to everyone. In any event, this issue has nothing to do with Rav Zevid's statement that even if property is discovered not to have belonged to the seller, a purchaser without warranty has no recourse. There the purchaser expressly accepted a no-warranty sale. Here no such express condition was made. We merely treat an apotiki as though it were already acquired by the creditor for certain purposes. Even an ordinary purchaser without an express no-warranty stipulation may recover when title fails. Giddulei Terumah combines two distinct doctrines, and on that basis concludes that Tosafot reject Rav Zevid in favor of Rav Pappa. According to him, the Shulchan Aruch's rulings would conflict, because we rule like Rav Zevid. In truth the two rules are unrelated.
But if he sold it as an absolute sale, etc. The Maggid Mishneh explains that this is a mistaken sale: had the purchaser known the field was an apotiki, he would not have bought it, and the defect goes to the substance of the transaction. This is strained, because the Rambam rules, as cited below at the beginning of siman 226, that even when definite adverse claimants appear, the purchaser cannot withdraw once he has taken possession. The Yerushalmi suggests a better explanation. An apotiki is treated as though the field itself is already acquired by the creditor, subject only to the debtor's right to redeem it. This explains why the arrangement is not invalid as an asmachta. If so, the debtor cannot sell the field as an absolute permanent sale, because to that extent he is selling something that is not fully his. A sale cannot take effect partially as a permanent conveyance and partially remain subject to another's ownership. Thus, if he purports to sell it absolutely, the transaction is defective from the outset and falls away when the creditor comes to seize it. If, however, the debtor says that he sells only until the creditor comes to collect, so that the purchaser's right is expressly temporary, the transaction resembles a lease for a term rather than an absolute sale. The objection that one cannot sell property belonging to another does not arise in the same way. The Rambam therefore distinguishes these cases because he views the apotiki itself as already acquired by the creditor, subject to redemption. The Yerushalmi in Shevuot and Yevamot provides substantial support for this reading. The Sema suggests a different distinction: perhaps only an ordinary apotiki, which has less publicity, creates a mistaken sale, whereas an explicit apotiki is public and the purchaser cannot claim mistake. But the Yerushalmi expressly says the opposite structure: with an explicit apotiki everyone agrees that the debtor cannot make an absolute sale, while the dispute concerns an ordinary apotiki. The Sema's explanation therefore cannot stand against the source.
