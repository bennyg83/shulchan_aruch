The law of one who designates his field or slave as an apotiki. It contains seven seifim:
One who designates his field as an apotiki for a creditor, or for a woman for payment of her ketubah, by writing to them, "From this property you shall collect," and a river washes it away, may have the debt collected from his other property, including property that has been sold. However, so long as the designated field remains in his possession, he cannot discharge the creditor with other property, only with money. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 18 of Hilchot Malveh, and Ran. Others disagree. Tur, seif 1. If he stipulated that payment could be made only from this property, the creditor may not collect from other assets. If he sold it to another and this creditor comes to seize it, the purchaser cannot remove the creditor by paying money, since the debtor stipulated that payment would be only from this property. Tur, siman 146. Rama: If he made the field an explicit apotiki, saying, "You shall have payment only from this," and it is discovered that the field was not his, some say that the creditor may collect from his other property. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 6, part 3, citing Tosafot. But if an earlier creditor seized it from him, he has no recourse against the debtor. Ibid. If one designated his field as an apotiki for his creditor or for a woman's ketubah and then sold it, the sale is valid. When the creditor comes to collect, if he finds no unencumbered property, he may seize this field. This applies when the sale was only temporary, but if he sold it as an absolute permanent sale, the sale is not valid. Rama: This is the Rambam's view. Most commentators disagree and hold that even when unencumbered property exists, the creditor may seize an explicit apotiki, and if it was sold, the sale remains effective until the time of seizure. This is the primary ruling. Tur, Maggid Mishneh chapter 18, and Beit Yosef.
