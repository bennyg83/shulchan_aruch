One who impaired the document. Beit Yosef cites Baal HaTerumot that if the creditor's son, rather than the creditor himself, admits partial payment, he need not take the special oath of one who impairs a document, because "the enactment was made for him and not for his son." Darchei Moshe and the Sema cite this. The Shach asks how to understand it. If the debtor says that he paid the creditor's son and the son admits receiving part, why should the son differ from the original creditor? The concern that one who pays is careful but one who receives payment may not be precise applies equally. If the son says only, "my father told me that part was paid," Rabbeinu Yerucham says he must at least swear that his father did not leave instructions contradicting the claim. The Shach therefore understands the Yerushalmi source to mean something else: if the creditor himself impaired the document and then died, we do not apply the rule that a person cannot transmit to his heirs a claim that required his own oath. The Bavli likewise says, "Give no addition to the rule of Rav and Shmuel," and excludes the case of a creditor who impaired his document and died. According to that reading the statement contains no new rule about the son's own partial admission, which is why later codifiers omitted it. The Yerushalmi as printed can indeed bear only the Shach's reading. But Baal HaTerumot itself cannot. In gate 2, discussing the Geonic oath imposed upon a debtor that he has no assets, Baal HaTerumot says that if the debtor dies and his son says that the father left him nothing, the son does not take that oath, because the Geonim enacted it "for him and not for his son," just as the Yerushalmi says concerning an impaired document. If the Yerushalmi meant only that the creditor's heirs do not lose the document because the deceased could no longer swear, what proof would there be? In that case the heirs may still have to swear whatever oath is within their ability. The special rule merely prevents total loss of the claim when the father's personal oath became impossible. Here the son can swear perfectly well that he inherited nothing. Why should that oath disappear? Nor was there any need for Baal HaTerumot to cite the Yerushalmi if he meant the ordinary Bavli rule, which is explicit. It therefore appears that Baal HaTerumot had a different Yerushalmi text or interpretation. Variant Yerushalmi texts are common and many passages quoted by early authorities are not found in our printed editions. The intended distinction may be as follows. Where the original debtor pays his creditor partially, the Sages imposed the special oath upon the creditor because "the one who pays is careful, while the one receiving payment is not always precise." The debtor is personally subordinate to the creditor and may be reluctant to insist immediately on return of the document or a receipt when the creditor delays, so the Sages protect him through the oath. But the debtor's son does not stand in the same relationship of personal subordination to the creditor. The heir is not himself the borrower's servant; only inherited assets stand responsible for the father's debt. If he pays, he should insist on the document or receipt. Failure to do so is his own loss, and the special enactment of "one who impairs his document" need not apply. The same conceptual distinction explains the Geonic oath. The original debtor is personally obligated to pay and therefore the Geonim imposed an oath concerning lack of assets. His son has no personal debtor status. He pays only from inherited property, and if there is no inheritance he has no personal payment obligation. The enactment was therefore not extended to him. This gives coherent meaning to Baal HaTerumot. Nevertheless, practical law should not be newly established from it. No other major authority clearly codifies a distinction between father and son in the law of an impaired document. Baal HaTerumot himself does not repeat it in its natural place. The Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe did not codify it in the Shulchan Aruch, and Even HaEzer implies that if heirs themselves impair a ketubah after the woman's death, they would ordinarily have to swear absent an exemption. The printed Yerushalmi likewise supports only the narrower rule that we do not say the deceased transmitted an uncollectible oath burden. We therefore should not create a practical father and son distinction. The only accepted difference is that the rule "one cannot transmit an oath to his sons" is not extended beyond its stated cases.
Even if there were witnesses, etc. Whether partial payment made before witnesses removes the special oath, and whether a creditor who carefully accounts down to a perutah removes it, are unresolved questions in the Gemara. Ran and Rosh therefore rule that the creditor may collect only after swearing. Still, the Mechaber's unqualified wording requires some thought. I wrote above that where a claimant who ordinarily swears and collects has already seized the property and refuses to swear, beit din may not seize his assets but can place him under a ban until he swears. Here, because the underlying issue is itself unresolved, if the creditor has already seized he can say that the halachah follows the side of the Gemara's doubt that no oath is required. According to the Mechaber, who often allows even post doubt seizure, it would be especially difficult to ban him for refusing an oath that may not exist. One must say that the Mechaber does not permit a "kim li" claim regarding the existence of a required oath. Yet in my discussion of the general rules of "kim li" I concluded that most authorities do recognize such pleas even regarding oaths. The point therefore remains somewhat difficult.
