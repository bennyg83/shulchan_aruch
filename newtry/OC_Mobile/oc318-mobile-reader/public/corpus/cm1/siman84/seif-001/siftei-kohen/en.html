One who admits partial payment, etc. Darkei Moshe quotes Beit Yosef in the name of Baal HaTerumot that if the creditor’s son admits partial payment, the son need not swear, because the enactment was made for the creditor himself and not for his son. Sema repeats this. In my humble opinion, the ruling requires analysis. If the borrower says that he paid the son and the son admits receiving part, why should it matter whether the father or son impaired the note? The reason for the oath is that a person is careful when paying but not equally careful to exact a receipt. That reasoning applies to the son no less than to the father. At first glance one might explain that the borrower claims the father told the son that part had been paid. Ordinarily the heirs need not swear that their father did not instruct them unless the borrower demands an oath, just as where there was no impairment, as is explicit in the chapters HaKotev and Kol HaNishba’in and is explained below in siman 108, seif 5. One might therefore say that the same applies when the son admits partial payment. However, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 26, part 3, rules that even in such a case the heirs collect only with an oath, as Beit Yosef briefly cites below at the end of siman 108, seif 8. I will quote his words there.
A careful reading of Baal HaTerumot shows that he did not state this as a rule in gate 21, where he discusses an impaired note. Rather, in gate 2, part 1, he addresses teachers who wished to require heirs to swear the geonic oath that they have no property, just as the borrower himself must. He rejects them, writing that since this later oath is a rabbinic enactment, the enactment extends only as far as the Sages enacted it. He cites the Yerushalmi regarding an impaired note: when the father impaired it, the son does not swear, because the enactment was made for the father, not for the son. Had Baal HaTerumot meant that a son who himself admits partial payment, or who says his father told him part was paid, collects without an oath, he should have stated that rule in gate 21 among the laws of an impaired note. I also examined the Yerushalmi in HaKotev and found no such rule. It says only that when the father impaired the note and died, his son does not lose the claim, because the enactment was made against the father and not against the son. The meaning is that we should not say that because the father impaired the note and then died, the heir either loses entirely or must swear that he knows with certainty that the balance was not paid. The Yerushalmi does not discuss a son who himself impairs the note. This is why Baal HaTerumot did not cite the rule in gate 21. He relied there on the Babylonian Gemara’s rule that when one who impaired his note dies, his heirs collect without the special oath. The Yerushalmi states: “An heir whose father impaired his note collects without an oath. In this respect the son’s power is greater than the father’s, for the son collects without an oath while the father collects only with an oath.” Rabbi Elazar says that the son nevertheless takes the heirs’ oath that his father did not instruct them, did not tell them, and that they did not find the note among paid notes. Rabbi Hoshaya asks whether this baraita follows Beit Shammai, who say that a sotah whose husband died before she drank collects her ketubah without drinking. Rabbi Yose answers that there the reason of Beit Shammai is that she can say, “Bring my husband and I will drink.” Here, by strict law even the father should not swear. The Sages imposed an oath on him, but the enactment was made for him and not for his son. Once he died, the matter returns to Torah law. The Yerushalmi means as follows. Rabbi Hoshaya asks whether the baraita allowing the son to collect without the special oath follows only Beit Shammai, since in the sotah case they treat a note standing for collection as already collected and therefore let her recover despite the doubt. Beit Hillel do not. Rabbi Yose answers that the sotah case differs, because the woman is subject by Torah law to the drinking procedure, and Beit Hillel hold that a note standing for collection is not regarded as collected. Here, however, by Torah law even the father would collect without an oath. The oath is only a rabbinic enactment, and Beit Hillel agree that the enactment was not extended to the son. Since neither the Yerushalmi nor any other posek distinguishes a son’s own impairment from the father’s, and Baal HaTerumot never intended such a distinction, it appears to me that when the son admits partial payment he must swear. If the borrower says, “I paid you the entire debt,” and the son says, “You paid me only part,” the son must take the oath applicable to an impaired note even if the borrower did not expressly demand an oath. Likewise, if the borrower says, “I paid your father everything,” and the son says, “My father told me that you paid only part,” the son must take the heirs’ oath that his father did not instruct him otherwise. Ordinarily, when heirs collect from the borrower himself, they need not take that oath unless the borrower demands it, as is clear from HaKotev and Kol HaNishba’in. Once the son impairs the note, however, he must swear even without such a demand. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Meisharim, Netiv 26, part 3, writes: heirs who collect from heirs collect only after swearing that their father did not instruct or tell them otherwise. If heirs collect from the borrower himself and impair the note by saying, “Our father told us that part was paid,” or if one witness testifies after their father’s death that it was paid, or if they collect from encumbered property, they collect only with an oath in every case. Heirs collecting from heirs likewise collect only with an oath in every case. This is the correct ruling.
