One who impairs his document. From the rule that a lender who admits partial payment of his own document must swear, some infer that we do not use a migo to extract money or even to exempt from an oath. There is room to say, however, that no true migo exists here. Had he brazenly denied receiving anything, the debtor would likely have demanded, 'Swear to me,' and he would then have been obligated to swear even without admitting a defect in the document. Now that he admits receiving part, perhaps we may judge him favorably as speaking because of some old loan or another reason. In any event, ordinary people instinctively impose an oath when a lender admits that his document was partially paid, because the force of 'why is your document in my hand?' has been weakened. That may explain why the debtor does not separately demand an oath. But according to Rav Chisda bar Rav Huna's suggestion that the oath is a Torah oath for purposes of reversal, as Rashi explains there, a migo would have relevance, and the presumption that a person does not brazenly deny a debt may not apply in the same way. This requires further study.
