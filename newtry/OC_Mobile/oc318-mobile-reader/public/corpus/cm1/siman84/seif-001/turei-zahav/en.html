In the Shulchan Aruch, seif 1, “one who impairs his note” The Beit Yosef cites Baal HaTerumot: “But one who impairs an oral claim certainly faces a heset oath by the borrower, who is then exempt, because the claimant has no note and the burden of proof rests upon him.” The Beit Yosef says this is obvious. In truth, one must explain what Baal HaTerumot intended to teach. It appears that he teaches that the borrower is exempt through a heset oath alone. One might otherwise have thought that he is exempt only through an oath resembling a Torah oath, just as the creditor who collects on an impaired note must swear a stringent oath. Baal HaTerumot therefore teaches that heset suffices. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, explains instead that the rule of an impaired note applies only to a witnessed note. With a handwritten acknowledgment, which cannot be collected from encumbered property, there is no law of an impaired note requiring the creditor to swear and collect the balance; rather, the borrower takes a heset oath and is exempt, even according to the Ramah in siman 69, who holds that one cannot claim payment against a handwritten acknowledgment. These words are astonishing. Baal HaTerumot holds that one may claim payment against every handwritten acknowledgment, which is therefore equivalent to an oral loan even without impairment, as the Beit Yosef cites in his name in siman 69. Moreover, according to the Ramah, a handwritten acknowledgment is fully equivalent to a note as against the borrower. Its inability to collect from encumbered property is explained only by its lack of publicity. Since the borrower cannot claim payment, it is a complete note lacking only a credibility clause, and the creditor should swear and collect the balance. Wherever a plea of payment is unavailable, the instrument is a complete note in the creditor’s hand, and he swears and collects. For this reason, it appears to me that with a mamrani note, where by local practice payment cannot be pleaded even without an express clause, the concept of impairing the note does not apply. The debtor must first pay, and may then sue the creditor for having been paid twice, whereupon the creditor takes a heset oath. If local practice grants such credibility only when written expressly, then the instrument certainly has the full law of an impaired note. The same applies to any handwritten acknowledgment lacking a credibility clause in a place where, under the Rama’s ruling in siman 69, the availability of a payment plea depends on the judge’s assessment. If the creditor admits partial payment, he must swear and collects the remainder. This appears correct to me.
