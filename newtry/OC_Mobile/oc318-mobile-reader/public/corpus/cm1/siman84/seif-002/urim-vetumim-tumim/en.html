Unless he pleads, etc. The Sema raised two questions. First, why did the Mechaber rule without qualification like the Rambam that within the term the lender need not swear, contrary to the view of Rav Hai Gaon cited last by the Tur? Second, why does the Mechaber say that if the borrower pleads, "Swear to me," the lender must swear even within the term? What is his source for this, since nothing of the sort is found in the Tur or Rambam? The Shach agreed with the second question as well. It appears that one question answers the other. The Beit Yosef understood the Tur's statement that Rav Hai Gaon requires an oath even within the term as referring to a case where the borrower specifically pleads, "Swear to me." Otherwise Rav Hai Gaon's statements would contradict each other. The Baal HaTerumot, at the end of Shaar 21, cites Rav Hai Gaon that only one who seeks collection from encumbered property must swear even within the term. He explains that even if the borrower admits the debt, an oath is nevertheless required because we are concerned about collusion. Therefore, even within the term we are concerned about collusion. This shows that where the borrower's admission would be effective, such as one who admits partial payment and similar cases, we rely upon the presumption that a person does not pay within the term to exempt the claimant from an oath. The Giddulei Terumah and Shach raised this contradiction. The Beit Yosef therefore understood Rav Hai Gaon as follows. When the borrower says, "Swear to me," even though ordinarily a person does not pay within the term, once we see that he paid part, the presumption is weakened, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote. Since the borrower specifically demands an oath, we require it, as the Sema explains. Certainly where one witness testifies to partial payment, the presumption is likewise weakened. But beit din does not impose the oath on its own, because the presumption arising from the admission of partial payment is counterbalanced by the presumption that a person does not pay within the term. Rav Hai Gaon's statement concerning collection from purchasers, where he gives the reason just mentioned, refers to a case where the purchaser did not specifically demand, "Swear to me." Even so, beit din itself raises the claim on the purchaser's behalf, because the concern is collusion. Whether he personally raises the claim makes no difference. If he does not raise it, we raise it for him. By contrast, in the case of one who admits partial payment and similar cases, the debtor must himself demand, "Swear to me," and only then is the oath imposed. Thus Rav Hai Gaon's statements do not contradict each other, and his language is well understood. Since the words of Rav Hai Gaon are received tradition, and the Rambam does not explicitly state the contrary, because he says nothing at all about a case where the debtor demands an oath in a case of partial payment or one witness, the Mechaber ruled in accordance with both the Rambam and Rav Hai Gaon. This explains his ruling. Upon examining the Gemara, it appears that there is proof for all of this. The Rambam's basic rule, that one who admits partial payment need not swear within the term, derives from the Gemara's discussion of Rav Papa's statement that if the debtor says, "Swear to me," we require an oath. The Gemara asks, "What difference is there between this and one who admits partial payment on his deed?" This is difficult. The Mishnah's ordinary case of a woman who admits partial payment of her ketubah is within the term, because the ketubah is not collectible during the husband's lifetime before divorce. Yet in the case of "Swear to me" no oath is required within the term, as the Ramban, Rosh, and other decisors write. If such an oath were required, we would impose it for heirs as well, because the oath taken when collecting from heirs is precisely the oath of Rav Papa, since we plead for heirs whatever their father could have pleaded. Thus there would be a major practical difference: one who admits partial payment must swear even within the term, whereas one facing only the plea "Swear to me" does not. The Taz similarly wrote this. One cannot answer that the Gemara's question is directed only to Rav Papa, who at the beginning of Bava Batra follows Abaye and Rava in holding that a person sometimes pays within the term. This would also resolve Tosafot's question in Shevuot 41, s.v. "and what," that the Gemara should simply have contrasted the two mishnayot. The distinction could be within the term, while the Gemara's question would concern Rav Papa alone. But Tosafot there write that even according to Abaye and Rava, where the obligation may never mature at all, a person does not pay within the term. The Mishnah of a woman who admits partial payment of her ketubah concerns precisely such a case, since she may die and the ketubah may never become collectible. There is therefore no room for that answer. The Taz also suggests that the Gemara's question is why this oath was not listed in the Mishnah. Apart from the fact that the authorities already wrote that it is included in the Mishnah's law of collection from orphans, as the Baal HaTerumot explains in Shaar 21, one can also say as above that the ordinary ketubah case is within the term, and this itself explains why the Mishnah specifically lists the case of admitted partial payment. Necessarily, even one who admits partial payment need not swear within the term unless the borrower demands it. The Gemara can therefore properly ask what difference there is between the two rules. On the other hand, there is a difficulty from Ketubot 87. The Mishnah says, "I have no vow or oath against you," etc. The Gemara asks what oath is meant. Rav Yehudah in Rav's name says that it refers to an oath concerning an appointment as administrator made during the husband's lifetime. Rashi explains that the exemption does not cover the oath of one who admits partial payment, because when the husband exempted her he meant an oath that arises by itself, not one she caused through her own conduct. Rav Nachman says that it refers to one who admits partial payment, and Rashi explains that it includes that oath as well. The Gemara asks: this is understandable according to the view that it refers to admitted partial payment, because she anticipates that perhaps she will need money, take part of her ketubah, and ask him to write that she need not swear. But according to the view that it refers to an administrator, how could she know that he would appoint her as administrator? Because of this difficulty the Gemara concludes like the view that the exemption includes admitted partial payment. This is difficult according to both opinions, and especially according to the view that the Mishnah concerns an administrator, which is forced into the difficulty noted by the Gemara. If that opinion holds that the exemption does not cover an oath caused by her own act, why not explain the Mishnah simply as exempting her from the oath arising if the husband later says, "Swear to me that I did not give you pledged funds and did not pay you"? That oath is not caused by her conduct at all. The trust clause could plainly apply to it, which would fit the Mishnah far better and avoid saying that she anticipated either needing money or being appointed administrator. This is especially strong because the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 21, part 4, explicitly writes that this oath is an oath of the Mishnah. Necessarily, the trust clause is relevant only after divorce, since she is not exempted against the husband's heirs. When could there be concern that she received payment or pledged funds? After divorce. But she need not condition at the time of marriage regarding that eventuality, for the Mishnah speaks of a condition made at marriage, as Tosafot there write. At the time of divorce she can simply say, "Give me my ketubah immediately, or exempt me from an oath, or do not pay me except before beit din." The Rosh explicitly says the same in the first chapter of Bava Metzia in the discussion of one who pleads payment against an obligation imposed by beit din. Thus the ordinary claim about payment arises at divorce, when the ketubah is still within its term, and no oath is required merely because the husband says, "Swear to me." Therefore the Gemara cannot explain the trust clause that way and must establish it in another case. Yet the difficulty remains: how can the Gemara establish it as the oath of one who admits partial payment? That too must concern events before divorce, for after divorce she can issue the warning just described and has no reason to secure an exemption already at marriage. But before divorce the ketubah is within its term. The Gemara's language that she anticipates perhaps needing money and taking some of her ketubah itself implies that the time for collecting the ketubah has not yet arrived. One must therefore say that she fears that she may admit partial payment and that her husband will then demand, "Swear to me." In that situation she must swear even within the term, and this is why she seeks an exemption from him in advance. The Shulchan Aruch's ruling is therefore compelled. This also resolves the Baal HaTerumot's objection to those authorities who hold that a general clause of trust, without expressly stating "whether all or part," does not exempt one who admits partial payment, but only from the ordinary oath arising when the debtor says, "Swear to me." The Mishnah says merely, "I have no vow or oath against you," yet the Gemara says that it exempts her from the oath of admitted partial payment. According to their position this seems difficult. But as explained, the Mishnah cannot refer to an ordinary oath, because the case is within the term, and after divorce she need not make such a stipulation already at marriage. Therefore the Gemara must establish it as exempting her from the oath of admitted partial payment, for otherwise the clause would have no application in the context of the Mishnah. This is satisfactory. There is another proof for the Beit Yosef. Ketubot 87b asks whether a woman who reduces the amount of her ketubah must swear. Does this have the same status as one who admits partial payment, etc.? Tosafot and the Maharsha explain that although the reason for the oath of admitted partial payment is that one who pays is precise while one who receives payment may not be fully precise, which does not apply where she merely reduces the original amount, perhaps she is actually admitting partial payment and is using the reduction as a stratagem to avoid the oath. This is difficult. What stratagem does she gain by making a false statement that her husband knows to be false? He knows that he wrote the full amount and paid it. He can simply respond by demanding, "Swear to me," forcing her to swear just as in an ordinary case of admitted partial payment. What has her stratagem accomplished? I wrote similarly above at the beginning of the principles of migo. I add that according to this proposed stratagem she may actually lose money. Suppose her ketubah was truly two hundred, and according to the husband he paid the full two hundred. She admits receiving one hundred, but deceitfully says that she received nothing and that the original ketubah obligation itself was only one hundred. If the husband demands, "Swear to me that I did not pay you the full two hundred," what can she do? If her assertion is true, she can swear according to it, but then she will necessarily swear that she received one hundred. She has admitted in beit din that the ketubah was only one hundred, and a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. Her entire admitted ketubah has therefore been paid, and she loses the remaining one hundred. If she cannot swear that way because she actually received two hundred, then by gilgul shevuah the husband can require her to swear whether she received an additional one hundred beyond what she says is due. Even if she is willing to forfeit the first one hundred, she must still swear regarding the second one hundred, as explained below in siman 94. If she admits receipt, she must return one hundred to the husband as money received in error, since by her own binding admission the ketubah was only one hundred while she received two hundred on account of the written ketubah. What stratagem is there? It harms rather than helps her. One cannot say that she may retract because she is moving from one exempting plea to another. Apart from the fact that this principle does not apply here, she may have made an unequivocal admission, and one cannot retract such an admission, as the Shach wrote above in siman 80. Thus the Gemara's concern for stratagem is difficult unless the entire discussion concerns a ketubah still within its term, as explained above. An ordinary woman who admits partial payment within the term must nevertheless swear if the husband specifically demands it, in accordance with the Beit Yosef's ruling. But a woman who merely reduces the original amount has no oath of admitted partial payment. If the ordinary "Swear to me" oath also does not apply within the term, there would be no oath at all, so her stratagem would indeed accomplish something. This is satisfactory. This also resolves Tosafot's question, as explained by the Maharsha, why with an admission of less than a perutah we suspect a stratagem, while with a simple reduction of the ketubah we do not. According to what I wrote, the entire case concerns the term before payment is due. After the term there is no meaningful stratagem, because the husband can demand, "Swear to me." Within the term, although there is ordinarily a presumption that a person does not pay early, that presumption is weakened where we actually see that some payment was made, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote. Thus where she admits actual partial payment, even of less than a perutah, it is reasonable to suspect a stratagem. The fact of some early payment has weakened the presumption. But where she says that she received no payment at all and merely reduces the original amount, while the debt is still within the term, how can we say that she actually received payment and is acting deceitfully? The presumption that a person does not pay within the term supports her assertion that she was not paid and that the document was originally written in error. How can we suspect a stratagem against such a presumption, upon which we rely even to extract money and even from heirs? We cannot compel ourselves to assume that she lies and was secretly paid contrary to such a strong presumption. This is straightforward. After the term there is no need to fear the stratagem because the husband can demand an oath in any event. Tosafot's question is therefore well resolved. One should not infer that after the term an admission of less than a perutah would no longer trigger the oath of admitted partial payment because the concern for stratagem no longer applies. First, the Sages did not distinguish within their enactment. Once they established the oath, they applied it generally. Second, in the ordinary partial payment case there is no monetary loss caused by the alleged stratagem, whereas in the reduction case there can be such loss, as explained. This is precise. The rulings of the Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch are therefore built upon firm foundations of Torah, like a polished mirror, and the proofs above bear faithful testimony to their correctness. There is also some proof that when the debtor says, "Swear to me," he can require an oath even within the term in the case of admitted partial payment or one witness. Otherwise, why are postdated deeds valid? Suppose one borrowed in Nisan and fixed a three year term from that day, while the witnesses dated the deed in Tishrei. When Nisan of the third year arrives, the actual term has expired, and the borrower may begin paying, either partially or in response to one witness, because he understands the loan to be due. Later the lender produces the postdated deed. According to the written date, all the borrower's pleas would concern payment within the term, since three years from Tishrei have not yet passed. If no oath could be demanded within the apparent term, the borrower would have no meaningful protection. Necessarily, if he says, "Swear to me," the lender must swear even then. Thus there is no reason to invalidate the postdated deed, because nothing prevents the borrower from demanding the oath. This is precise. There remains a question concerning an oral loan made before witnesses for a fixed term, where the borrower pays part within the term. Is he then believed to say that he paid the remainder within the term as well? Here the Maggid Mishneh's reasoning certainly applies: once we see that he paid part within the term, the usual presumption has been weakened. Perhaps the Rambam's rule that the claimant nevertheless need not swear applies specifically to a written deed, because the deed itself supports the lender. The entire oath is only to appease the debtor's mind, so even a slight countervailing rationale can defeat the rabbinic oath and restore the basic rule that the lender collects on his deed. But with an oral loan, to extract money from the party currently holding it requires a complete presumption. Once that presumption has been weakened by admitted partial early payment, as the Maggid Mishneh says, how can money be extracted? This requires study. The words of the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 21, part 2, paragraph 4, can be understood precisely this way. He writes that the law of one who admits partial payment exists only with a loan evidenced by deed, where the claimant swears and collects. But with an oral loan, if the lender admits partial payment, the borrower certainly takes a hesset oath and is exempt, because there is no deed supporting the lender and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. All the commentators wondered at this. Even without the lender admitting partial payment, an oral borrower may take hesset and be exempt. Why should the borrower's position become any weaker merely because the lender admits receiving part? The Beit Yosef explained it as a loan established by kinyan without a written deed. The Darchei Moshe and Bach already challenged this. The Bach suggested that it concerns the borrower's handwritten acknowledgment according to the view that payment cannot be pleaded against it. The Taz correctly objected that the Baal HaTerumot holds that payment can be pleaded against a handwritten acknowledgment, and according to the contrary view it has the law of a deed, so that even after an admission of partial payment the borrower would not simply take hesset and be exempt. According to what I wrote, however, the Baal HaTerumot's words are clear. He speaks of an oral loan made for a fixed term, where the borrower claims payment within the term. With a written deed, even if the lender admits having received part within the term, the presumption remains sufficient and the lender need not swear, because the deed supports him. With an oral loan, however, once the lender admits receiving part within the term, the presumption is weakened. Money can no longer be extracted from the borrower, and the ordinary rule applies that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. The borrower therefore takes hesset and is exempt. This is straightforward. One should not object that at the beginning of Bava Batra the Gemara labors to explain the Mishnah stating that up to four cubits of a courtyard wall the builder is presumed to have paid. It asks: if the obligation is already due, this is obvious, and if it is still within the term, why should he be presumed to have paid? Why not answer that the case is one where he paid part of the construction expense, and therefore he is believed to say that he paid all of it even within the term, since we see that he paid part early? First, it would be strained to establish an unqualified Mishnah in such a special case. Second, perhaps this reasoning applies only to a definite debt, where the presumption that a person does not pay within the term is not overwhelmingly strong. But where the obligation might never arise at all, the presumption that one does not pay early remains strong enough to extract money even if he paid part, because a person does not surrender money while the obligation remains doubtful. Partial payment does not establish that he paid everything. In the wall case, it was possible that he would never become liable at all, as Tosafot write that one could have asked, "Perhaps he will never build." This is why the Gemara did not give that answer, and all is satisfactory.
