One witness testifies, etc. The Kesef Mishneh, on Rambam, Hilchot Ishut chapter 24, writes that this applies specifically where the husband himself makes a definite claim and brings one witness supporting him. But if he does not know that payment was made except from the witness's statement that it was paid, he cannot impose the oath of the Mishnah. The Shach objects that since we rule that a claim based upon one witness is treated as a definite claim and can obligate a Torah oath, the same should apply to an oath of the Mishnah. He also says this is evident from the Rashba's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 72, new matter 22, that even where the claimant himself is uncertain, the opposing party must swear. In truth, the Rashba is not such a strong proof. The principal rationale of the Beit Yosef, as it appears to me, is that here the claimant possesses a deed supporting him. What can one witness accomplish against two witnesses? The Sages imposed an oath merely to appease the debtor's mind. Such appeasement is needed when the debtor makes a definite claim, but if the debtor himself does not know whether he paid, there is no need to appease his mind. This appears to be the Beit Yosef's reasoning. Accordingly, there is no proof from the Rashba. There the case did not involve a deed. A custodian did not know the value of the lost article. The owner said it was worth a dinar, while the borrower did not know. The strict law would have required payment because he could not swear, but one witness testified that the object was worth only a shekel. The Rashba ruled that the owner must swear. There no deed supports the owner, so the witness's testimony plainly converts the matter into a definite evidentiary claim requiring an oath to contradict him. Here, by contrast, the lender holds a deed supporting nonpayment, equivalent to two witnesses, and the statement of one witness has no standing against two. At first glance, this question appears to depend upon a dispute among the authorities. The Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 21, part 5, paragraph 1, asks why there is any need for the special oath arising from one witness, since the debtor can in any event say, "Swear to me." He answers with the Gemara's distinction that the one witness oath applies even when the debtor did not specifically demand it. If the Shach were correct, there would be no question at all from the one witness case, only from the case of one who admits partial payment. The one witness oath is needed precisely where the debtor does not assert payment with certainty. In such a case he cannot say, "Swear to me," because he has no definite claim. Yet the Sages' enactment concerning one witness could still impose an oath. The fact that the Baal HaTerumot asks the question suggests the Kesef Mishneh's view. Furthermore, Tosafot and the Rosh in the chapter HaKotev explain the Gemara's statement that where one witness testifies that the ketubah was paid, the oath is rabbinic, and that if the husband is clever he can bring her to a Torah oath. They explain that one practical difference is reversal of the oath, since with the rabbinic oath she can reverse it upon him, while with the Torah oath she cannot. This is difficult. Why should he first pay her and then sue her? Extracting money afterward may be difficult, and no one knows what the future will bring. He might instead say, "I do not know whether I paid you." Even if he previously asserted with certainty that he paid, he can later say, "I no longer remember clearly whether I paid or not. I am now making my claim only on the basis of the witness." Then how could she reverse the oath upon him when he himself says he does not know? There is a qualification. According to the Maharam's question on Rashi, why did Rashi not explain the cleverness as involving reversal of the oath, given that Rashi holds that where a person cannot swear and must pay, the opposing party must first swear? If the woman in the Torah oath case said, "I do not know," she would be unable to swear and would have to pay, but the husband would then have to swear, so he would still not escape the reversal in practical effect. One must therefore say that Maharam holds that once she has said in beit din, "I know," she is not believed later to say, "I remembered that I am uncertain." The same would then apply to the husband, and the objection falls away. Still, absent that approach, the Kesef Mishneh's position is the deeper answer. This would be an even better strategy than paying first and then suing. Necessarily, according to the Kesef Mishneh, in such a case no one witness oath exists at all if the litigant himself claims only doubt. It is strained to answer that where he makes a definite claim supported by the witness, the woman will not swear because she fears that afterward he and the witness may combine to disqualify her, as Tosafot write in Bava Batra in the case of the piece of metal of Rabbi Abba. She would therefore reverse the oath, whereas if he himself pleads uncertainty and relies only on the witness, she can safely swear to contradict the witness and need not reverse it. The unqualified language of Tosafot and the Rosh does not imply such a distinction. Moreover, whether a litigant and one witness can combine to disqualify a person is itself unresolved as a matter of law, as discussed at length above in siman 34. Their words therefore point toward the Beit Yosef's view. The Rambam's own language likewise supports the Kesef Mishneh. According to the Tur's version, the Rambam writes that a husband cannot impose an oath upon his wife when she collects her ketubah on the ground that she committed adultery merely because another person told him so; he can do so only if he claims that he personally saw it. This clearly indicates the Kesef Mishneh's interpretation. Although the Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 115, seif katan 32, explains "on the word of another" as meaning a relative or otherwise invalid witness, the phrase naturally means any other person. The Rambam speaks without qualification. If he meant only a relative or invalid witness, the rule would be obvious. The Beit Shmuel is surprised that the Rama, who holds in Choshen Mishpat that a person may impose an oath after being informed by a relative or invalid witness, did not gloss Even HaEzer accordingly. There is no difficulty. After payment, he may impose a hesset oath on that basis. Before payment, while she holds her ketubah deed, according to all opinions he cannot require an oath on the statement of a relative or invalid witness. Even a valid witness imposes the special oath only because the Sages instituted it to appease the debtor's mind. We should not extend that enactment to relatives or invalid witnesses. The Even HaEzer case concerns collection before payment. The Rama therefore properly added nothing. The evidence thus clearly indicates that the Kesef Mishneh correctly understood the Rambam, whose language was copied by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. The Beit Yosef did not invent this distinction himself. On the other hand, the Ramban in Chazakat HaBatim, the Baal HaTerumot in Shaar 19, Tosafot, the Ran, and especially the Ritva to Ketubot indicate the contrary. They ask concerning the incident of Avimi, who claimed that a payment should be applied to another debt, that one witness nevertheless testified to payment, making it the case of Rabbi Abba's piece of metal, where inability to swear should cause payment. The Ritva answers that because the creditor still holds his deed, the one witness creates only the rabbinic oath imposed when one witness says the deed was paid, and regarding a rabbinic oath we do not say that inability to swear causes payment. The Ramban in the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 19, likewise holds this way. See what I wrote above in siman 58 at length. In that incident the borrower did not personally see the payment, as the Ramban explicitly writes in Chazakat HaBatim, but relied upon the witness. Nevertheless all these authorities conclude that the lender is subject to the rabbinic oath of one witness testifying to payment, even though the borrower himself pleads only uncertainty. This is decisive evidence that they disagree with the Kesef Mishneh. Rashi too appears to have held this way, in the passage that troubled the Maharsha. But the saintly Maharam of Rothenburg preceded him in addressing the issue, as cited in Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Shevuot chapter 11, letter 5. There, regarding Rami bar Hama's view that the oath is from Torah law, Rashi explains that the practical difference is reversal of the oath. Yet regarding the statement, "If he is clever, he brings her to a Torah oath," Rashi explains the difference as whether the oath must be taken while holding a sacred object. Maharam was forced into a strained explanation, as I wrote above at length in siman 87. According to what I have written here, Rashi's words are entirely clear. Rami bar Hama holds that the oaths of one who admits partial payment and one against whom a single witness testifies to payment are Torah oaths. They are stated in the same clause of the Mishnah as the oaths concerning collection from orphans, encumbered property, and in the creditor's absence, which everyone agrees are rabbinic. It would be extremely strained for the oath mentioned regarding partial payment and one witness to require holding a sacred object while the other oaths in the same clause do not. The Mishnah groups them together, implying that the character and severity of the oaths are alike. Accordingly, if Rami bar Hama says the first two are Torah oaths requiring a sacred object, he must maintain that even the rabbinic oaths in that Mishnah require a sacred object. Rashi therefore could not explain the practical difference in Rami bar Hama's statement as whether a sacred object is required. He instead explains a consequence unique to the Torah oath, namely that it cannot be reversed. That issue does not arise in the other cases involving orphans, purchasers, or collection in the debtor's absence, so there is no inconsistency in grouping them together. Likewise, when Rami bar Hama counts the one witness case as a Torah oath, Rashi does not separately explain the practical difference, relying on his earlier explanation that the difference is reversal. The earlier objection, that the debtor can merely plead uncertainty on the basis of the witness and thereby prevent reversal, is not difficult. If he is clever enough to do so, that strategy indeed works. Rami bar Hama, however, is stating the legal rule for the ordinary case where the debtor himself pleads payment with certainty and one witness supports him. One might otherwise think that the claimant can reverse the oath. Rami bar Hama teaches that it is a Torah oath and cannot be reversed. By contrast, when Rav Papa says, "If he is clever," he is affirmatively giving the debtor strategic advice. There the earlier question is valid: if the point were merely to avoid reversal, the better advice would be to plead uncertainty on the basis of the witness and escape reversal that way. Rashi therefore explains that the difference Rav Papa seeks is not reversal but holding a sacred object. Once Rami bar Hama's position is rejected and the entire Mishnah is understood as involving rabbinic oaths, the Sages can be understood as not requiring a sacred object for any of them. Thus the oaths listed together truly share the same character, all being rabbinic and not requiring a sacred object. Rashi's words are clear and refined. Accordingly, as a matter of practical law, this is a dispute among the authorities and cannot be decisively resolved, especially since Even HaEzer siman 115, together with the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, rule according to the Rambam as explained by the Kesef Mishneh.
His heirs do not collect, etc. Most commentators leave the responsum of the Rosh and the Mechaber's ruling in difficulty. How can they rule that where one witness testified that the deed was paid, or the creditor admitted partial payment, and the creditor then died, the rule that one cannot bequeath an oath to his children applies so that the heirs cannot collect? The Gemara expressly says concerning one who admitted partial payment and then died, "apply Rav and Shmuel's rule but do not extend it." The Mechaber himself rules this way below in siman 108, and so does the Rosh in his halachic rulings. The Sema labored below in siman 108 to resolve the contradiction by a strained interpretation. The Shach correctly writes that it is preferable to leave the Rosh and Mechaber in difficulty rather than force such great difficulties. I have seen, however, that the complaint is not against the Rosh alone. The Baal HaItur preceded him, as cited by the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 14, part 2, paragraph 6, writing: "We do not find that where one admits partial payment on his deed his heirs swear the heirs' oath and collect except in a case of orphans collecting from orphans." At first glance this is strange and inverted. Regarding one who admits partial payment, the Gemara says not to extend Rav and Shmuel's rule, whereas "orphans from orphans" is the very case of Rav and Shmuel's principle that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children. The Rivash, responsum 483, likewise writes that many authorities hold that even with an impaired deed or where one witness says it was paid, if the claimant dies, his heirs do not collect. This has already been questioned from the Rosh's responsum, siman 82, because the Gemara expressly says, "do not extend it." See there, where a strained resolution is offered. In responsa of Maharam, Prague edition, siman 407, he cites the Behag as ruling that where the lender dies and his heirs produce the deed, the borrower swears and is exempt, because a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children. Rabbenu Tam objects that Rav Papa's oath arising when the borrower says, "Swear to me," is not within the category to which the rule of not bequeathing an oath applies. Moreover, even regarding one who admits partial payment the Gemara says not to extend Rav and Shmuel's ruling. Despite Rabbenu Tam's objections, we are obligated to labor to reconcile the Behag, whose words are received tradition, so that they do not directly contradict an explicit Gemara. I therefore examined the Rosh's halachic rulings and found that they too require analysis. In Shevuot, concerning Rav and Shmuel's rule that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children, the Gemara says that the matter depends on the dispute in the case of Rabbi Abba's piece of metal, whether one who cannot swear must pay. If we apply the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, Rav and Shmuel's ruling cannot stand. Tosafot ask what connection there is between the two. They explain that Rabbi Abba holds that where a Torah oath is required, inability to swear causes payment. But where a claimant swears and collects, the oath is rabbinic. We do not say that inability to take that rabbinic oath causes him to lose and prevents collection. Therefore Rav and Shmuel's principle that one cannot bequeath the oath does not apply. According to Rav and Shmuel, however, even with a rabbinic oath inability to swear causes loss. Tosafot then ask why the lender's heirs should lose the entire debt, since the borrower's heirs also cannot swear, as both sides are heirs. At least half should be lost by the borrower's heirs. They answer that Rav and Shmuel do not accept the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. They say only that a claimant who comes to collect but cannot take his required oath loses. Therefore the lender's heirs lose everything. See there. According to this, if the holder of a deed is disqualified from swearing, the opposing party should not swear and be exempt, because we rule like Rabbi Abba. Tosafot in Ketubot 88, s.v. "he brings her," as explained by the Maharsha, state that this law depends upon Rabbi Abba. If inability to swear does not cause loss in a rabbinic oath, why should a disqualified holder of a deed lose merely because the opposing party is prepared to swear? This is straightforward. Yet if the Rosh follows Tosafot's explanation, how can he rule in the chapter HaKotev, as the abridged rulings there expressly state, like the Rif that when the holder of the deed is disqualified from swearing, the opposing party swears and is exempt? There is also another difficulty. The abridged rulings say that where the claimant alone is disqualified, the opposing party swears and is exempt, but where both parties are disqualified, the claimant collects on the deed. The Maharsha also understands Tosafot in Ketubot this way. At first glance this is difficult either way. If inability to take a rabbinic oath causes the claimant to lose, he should lose even where both are disqualified, just as elsewhere when both are disqualified the person obligated to pay must pay. If inability to take the rabbinic oath does not cause loss, why should the claimant lose where only he is disqualified? His case should not be treated as though he were liable to pay. It appears that where the claimant on a deed is disqualified and the opposing party swears, the claimant's loss is not the ordinary legal consequence of inability to swear. It is a penalty imposed upon the disqualified claimant. But where both are disqualified, there is no basis to apply that penalty. Since we rule that one who cannot swear must pay, where the ordinary law would not make the claimant lose, from where do we know that the Sages penalized him when the other party is equally disqualified? In the Mishnah the ordinary law would itself have imposed payment because the obligated party could not swear. Thus there is no basis for the same penalty here. This is clear. Accordingly, the Rosh follows his own approach and does not accept Tosafot's answer to the earlier question. In his rulings in Shevuot he gives a different answer. He too asks that if Rav and Shmuel hold that inability to swear causes loss, then certainly with a Torah oath inability should cause payment. He answers that the primary oath is placed upon the borrower to swear that he does not owe because he paid. When the borrower's heirs cannot swear that their father paid, we should therefore say that because they cannot take their oath, they must pay. This is Rabbi Abba's position. Rav and Shmuel, however, do not hold that inability to swear causes payment. Therefore, although the borrower's heirs cannot swear, they are not obligated to pay. The claimant's heirs, who hold the deed, then lose because they cannot collect it without taking their own oath. Thus according to the Rosh, even Rabbi Abba could agree that inability to take a rabbinic oath causes a claimant to lose. The reason the claimant does not lose in the case of heirs is that the primary oath lies upon the borrower's heirs, and their inability to swear creates a payment obligation. This explains the Rosh's ruling concerning a disqualified claimant. Where the borrower is alive and can swear, even with a rabbinic oath the claimant who cannot swear loses, because the borrower himself takes the oath that he paid. This applies according to all opinions, even Rabbi Abba. But where both are disqualified, the primary oath lies upon the borrower, and his inability to swear requires him to pay rather than causing the claimant to lose. This is correct and clear. See what I wrote above in siman 82, that it is not entirely compelled that the Rosh himself distinguishes between one disqualified claimant and two disqualified parties exactly as the abridged rulings attribute to him. According to what is explained here, however, it is clear how the Tur derived that distinction from his father's words in chapter Kol HaNishba'in, where the Rosh gives the reason that according to Rabbi Abba the claimant does not lose because the borrower's heirs themselves cannot swear. This is clear. Yet according to this a new difficulty arises. How can the Gemara say that where one admits partial payment on his deed and then dies, "apply Rav and Shmuel's ruling but do not extend it"? The reason we do not follow Rav and Shmuel is that the halachah follows Rabbi Abba. But according to the Rosh, Rabbi Abba disagrees with Rav and Shmuel only because the primary oath is upon the borrower's heirs, who cannot swear, and therefore must pay. Here the borrower is alive and is prepared to swear. In such a case, even Rabbi Abba should agree with Rav and Shmuel that the claimant who cannot take his rabbinic oath loses, just like a disqualified claimant where the defendant can swear. How then can this be described as a case where we do not extend Rav and Shmuel's rule? Rabbi Abba himself should agree with them. At first glance this is a serious difficulty for the Rosh. We must therefore say that the Gemara's case of one who admits partial payment and dies, where it says not to extend Rav and Shmuel's ruling, concerns the same class of dispute as their original case, namely one where the borrower also cannot swear because he too has died before collection. The Gemara abbreviates when it says merely that the lender admitted partial payment and died. The borrower must likewise have died afterward. The discussion is tied to the dispute between Rav and Shmuel and Rabbi Abba, and that dispute concerns precisely a situation where the borrower has died and his heirs cannot swear. While the borrower remains alive there is no dispute: according to all opinions he can swear and thereby be exempt. Thus the Gemara's phrase "one who admits partial payment on his deed and dies" must be understood as including that the borrower later dies as well, though this is omitted because it is evident from the subject of the discussion. The matter then truly depends upon Rabbi Abba's dispute. Rabbi Abba holds that since the borrower's heirs cannot swear, inability to swear causes them to pay. Rav and Shmuel reject that principle, so even in this case the lender's heirs would lose. The Gemara says not to extend their exceptional rule, meaning that Rabbi Abba's law is accepted outside the special oath imposed on heirs, which is particularly stringent, to the point that according to Abba Shaul even a trust clause does not override it. In other cases the halachah follows Rabbi Abba: since the borrower's heirs also cannot swear, they must pay. This is satisfactory. The Gemara's wording is abbreviated, for after saying that the creditor admitted partial payment and died it should have added, "and afterward the borrower also died." But such abbreviation is entirely normal where the omitted circumstance is understood from context. The Rosh's rulings are therefore consistent with his law concerning a disqualified claimant as well. If the borrower can swear, the claimant who cannot take the required oath loses. If the borrower cannot swear, the borrower must pay. This is also exactly what the Baal HaItur meant when he wrote that we find heirs of one who admitted partial payment swearing the heirs' oath and collecting only "from orphans against orphans." He means that if the borrower remains alive, the lender's heirs do not collect merely by taking the heirs' oath, because the borrower can swear and is exempt. Only when the borrower has also died, so that the present collection is by heirs from heirs, do we say not to extend Rav and Shmuel's ruling and instead follow Rabbi Abba, requiring the borrower's heirs to pay because neither side can take the original oath. The phrase "orphans from orphans" does not mean that the borrower died before the lender. It means that by the time collection occurs both sides are represented by heirs. This is satisfactory and clear. The Behag likewise holds this way. He rules that if the lender dies while the borrower remains alive and the lender's heirs produce the deed, the borrower swears and is exempt because the lender cannot bequeath the required oath to his children. Rabbenu Tam's objection from the case of admitted partial payment is answered, because there both sides are ultimately deceased and the borrower is no longer available to swear. Here the borrower remains alive. This is clear and correct without any difficulty. The Rivash's statement that with an impaired deed or one witness testifying to payment, if the claimant dies we apply the rule that a person cannot bequeath an oath, likewise refers to a case where the borrower remains alive. This is satisfactory. The Rosh's responsum also clearly concerns a living borrower who seeks to swear and cries out insistently that he paid. Therefore the principle "do not extend it" has no application, and the borrower swears and is exempt. This is exactly what the Mechaber intends here, where the borrower is alive. Below in siman 108, by contrast, the Mechaber discusses the case explained above, where the lender admitted partial payment and died and the borrower then died as well. There we say not to extend Rav and Shmuel's rule. There is therefore no contradiction at all. These points are clear to one who examines the sources, and in my humble opinion the earlier commentators left me room here to clarify the matter.
