One witness. In Perek HaKotev Rav Papa says that if a husband is clever he can transform the wife's rabbinic oath into a Torah oath by paying the ketubah again before one witness and then combining that witness with the earlier witness, treating the first payment as a loan. Rav Shisha son of Rav Idi objects that the two witnesses do not testify to the same payment. The final arrangement is to pay the ketubah before both the first and second witnesses and then treat the first payments as a loan. At first glance the initial proposal is difficult. If by combining the witnesses he proves that he paid twice, why does Rav Papa say he should place "those first monies" as a loan rather than the later payment? Both payments are supported only through the combined testimony of one witness from each event. The answer follows Tosafot's explanation of the practical difference between the Mishnah oath and the Torah oath. If the woman is suspected concerning oaths, under the Mishnah oath she can sometimes collect without swearing, whereas once the matter is converted to a Torah oath the opposing party can swear and collect or be exempt according to the applicable rules. Raavad writes that the shifted oath for a suspected party applies only where the other party did not know at the time of the transaction that he was dealing with a suspected person. If he knowingly dealt with one, he cannot create for himself an automatic right to swear and collect. The Shach indicates Tosafot can agree. Accordingly, the husband cannot make the later payment the independent loan claim, because by the time he makes that payment he already knows the woman is suspected, and he paid again precisely in order to create the Torah oath mechanism. He cannot then invoke the shifted oath based on a transaction knowingly made with a suspected party. The earlier payment, however, may have occurred before he knew of her suspected status. That earlier payment can therefore be treated as the loan claim. This explains why Rav Papa specifically places the first monies in the category of loan and also resolves Tosafot's related question about combining the witnesses.
Within its term. One may ask: whenever two witnesses can establish a monetary fact, one witness should create a Torah oath. If two witnesses can testify that a document was paid even within its term, why should one witness not obligate the creditor to swear despite the presumption against early payment? A one witness oath is the counterpart of two witness monetary proof. In monetary law a single witness can testify even against a presumption, just as one witness can require an oath against a possessor despite the ordinary presumption that whatever is in a person's hand is his. The answer is that the ordinary oath imposed on a creditor when one witness says the document was paid is not a Torah one witness oath in the standard sense. A debt instrument carries a land lien, and Torah oaths do not apply directly to claims involving land. Moreover, this is an oath imposed upon one who seeks to collect, not a defendant's ordinary Torah oath. It is fundamentally a rabbinic oath instituted to satisfy the opposing party's concern. Since the whole oath is only a rabbinic measure, the Sages were lenient within the due date where the strong presumption favors nonpayment. If, however, the litigant cleverly restructures the case so that a genuine Torah one witness oath arises, for example by paying before two separate witnesses in the manner described by Rav Papa, then the within term presumption cannot remove the oath. A one witness Torah oath is like two witnesses for money, and an ordinary presumption does not defeat that testimony. This also explains the Rosh's proof that a supporting witness can exempt from an oath in partial admission. In the case of one who says the document stated only two sela while the lender claims five, the written document supports the defendant and can exempt him from the partial admission oath. That does not show that every evidentiary presumption can defeat a one witness oath. A supporting witness has the same witness structure as the witness imposing the oath. A mere presumption against early payment does not. Thus if one actual witness testifies that a payment occurred within the due date in a configuration that creates a true Torah oath, the debtor or creditor cannot escape simply because of the presumption. Rav Papa himself holds that people may sometimes pay within term, which is why Tosafot had to identify other practical differences in the sugya. But even according to the ordinary halachah that early payment is uncommon, a genuine Torah witness is stronger than that presumption.
