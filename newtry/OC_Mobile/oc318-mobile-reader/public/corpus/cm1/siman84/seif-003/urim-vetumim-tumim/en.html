Payment written between the lines, etc. The Shach sought to explain the responsum of the Rosh as speaking specifically where the borrower admits that, with his knowledge, the notation between the lines was written there at the time of payment. But if the borrower claims that the lender wrote it between the lines without his knowledge in order to avoid impairing the deed, such a notation is ineffective, for the borrower can say that the lender acted deceitfully to avoid the law governing one who admits partial payment on his deed. The Shach writes that there is apparently proof for this from Bava Batra 172. His intent is that the Gemara says that if a lender wishes to divide a deed for one hundred into two deeds of fifty, the borrower can prevent him, because when the borrower later pays fifty, the original deed will thereby become impaired. According to the Rosh, however, even if the deed remains for one hundred, when fifty is paid the lender can simply write the payment between the lines. The Shach's question is strong, but his answer does not suffice. If the lender is intent upon avoiding the status of an impaired deed, then even with the larger deed, when the borrower pays fifty the lender can write the payment on it with the borrower's knowledge and arrange for witnesses to attest to that fact, so that the borrower cannot later deny it. Indeed, even without witnesses, if the borrower admits that it was written with his knowledge, the deed would no longer be impaired. Would the Sages establish a protection for the borrower by ensuring that the deed becomes impaired only if the borrower denies the truth and says that he did not know of a notation that he actually knew about? That would make truth disappear. What practical distinction is there in this explanation? Moreover, one who examines the Rosh's responsum, klal 68, siman 16, will see clearly that he never says that it was known to have been written with the borrower's knowledge. He simply rules that such a notation does not constitute impairment of the deed. I would have said as follows. If the borrower holds a receipt for the payment, and the same payment stated in the receipt is also written between the lines of the deed, then the borrower can properly claim that the lender wrote the notation on his own in order to avoid impairment of the deed. Otherwise, why would both devices be needed, both a notation between the lines and a receipt? The principal proof of payment is the receipt, and the notation between the lines is ineffective even if written with the borrower's knowledge, because it is superfluous and regarded as absent in light of the receipt that proves the payment. But where the borrower holds no receipt and the payment is written between the lines, there is a presumption that this was done with the borrower's knowledge. Why would a person pay without any proof at all? Perhaps the lender will die and, Heaven forbid, the borrower will lose recognition of the payment. Necessarily he relied upon the notation between the lines, and that notation is the principal evidence. This is straightforward. Therefore, in the Rosh's responsum, where the borrower had no receipt and the payment was merely written between the lines, we plainly assume that this occurred with the borrower's knowledge and the deed is not considered impaired. By contrast, in the Gemara in Bava Batra, if the borrower specifically wants a receipt, the lender must give him one. The borrower may say, "I do not rely upon a notation between the lines. Perhaps it will be scraped away without the erasure being recognizable," especially according to those who validate an erasure between the lines, or he may cite similar concerns. Once the lender gives him a receipt, the notation between the lines accomplishes nothing at all, and the deed is indeed impaired. If the borrower possesses a receipt, writing the same payment between the lines neither adds nor detracts. Thus there is no difficulty. The real difficulty is from the Gemara's question whether a woman who reduces her ketubah has the same law as one who admits partial payment. As explained above in the name of Tosafot and the Maharsha, the uncertainty is whether she may be using the reduction as a stratagem. But what stratagem is there? She could simply write the payment between the lines of the ketubah. There is no receipt in the husband's possession, for if there were a receipt, how could she deny the payment and reduce the original ketubah amount? Necessarily there is no receipt. If so, writing the payment between the lines should be effective and remove the status of an impaired deed. It appears that the Rosh's responsum itself provides the distinction. He explains that a notation between the lines does not make the deed impaired because where payment was made before witnesses, one can say that witnesses happened to be available for the first payment but were not available for the second. By contrast, writing between the lines was always within the borrower's power to arrange. One must understand this. If so, how can an impaired deed ever exist? If the borrower paid and took no receipt, what prevented him from requiring the lender to write the payment between the lines? Necessarily, where nothing was written between the lines, the borrower can excuse himself by saying, "It never occurred to me to have the payment written between the lines," as stated in the responsum attributed to the Ramban, siman 100. But once one payment was actually written between the lines, he has plainly considered this method. Therefore any later payment should likewise have been written there. Accordingly, if the borrower says that he paid in Nisan and that payment was written between the lines, and then claims that he also paid again in Iyar, while the lender denies the second payment, the Rosh properly says that the borrower is not believed. If he made the Iyar payment too, he should have had that payment written between the lines as well. But if the borrower says, "Before the Nisan payment, I had already paid you in Adar. At that earlier time I did not think of writing payments between the lines and no witnesses happened to be present. When I later paid you in Nisan, you denied the earlier payment, so I refused to pay again unless you gave me a receipt. We ultimately agreed that you would write the Nisan payment on the deed, and that was done. The earlier payment was not written because you had already denied it," his position is different. The same must be true where he holds a witnessed receipt for a later payment but claims that he made an earlier payment without a receipt. The decisors speak generally of impairment of a deed. Why do we not say that when he took the later receipt he should have included the earlier payment? Necessarily, as I wrote, he can answer that by the time of the later payment the lender had already denied the earlier one, so he secured a witnessed receipt at least for the later payment. Thus the Rosh's case, where the borrower claimed that he made another payment after one had already been written between the lines, is well understood. The Rosh says he is not believed, because that later payment too should have been written there. In the case of a woman who reduces her ketubah, however, her alleged stratagem may be based on the fear that even if she writes one payment on the ketubah, her husband may deceitfully say that he also made an earlier payment. She would then be required to swear. She therefore instead reduces the original amount. This is satisfactory. This is what appeared to me as a way to uphold the Rosh's words, but the matter still requires study. One who examines the Rosh's responsum there will see that he writes that scribes have a practice, when noting payment on the reverse of a deed, to state that the money was given to the lender as a new loan rather than as payment, so that the deed will not be treated as impaired. The Rosh says this is unnecessary and only an extra precaution, because a payment written between the lines does not render the deed impaired. If his intent were as I suggested, the scribal wording would still have practical value, because it would prevent the borrower from claiming that an additional payment had been made before the notation. The notation itself would be effective only against a claim of a later payment. Since the Rosh says the wording makes no difference, this implies that he recognizes no such distinction. I therefore return to what I wrote initially: the true distinction is whether the borrower possesses a receipt. Without a receipt, the notation between the lines is treated as made with his knowledge and is effective. With a receipt, the notation adds nothing. As for the difficulty from the woman who reduces her ketubah, perhaps the Rosh does not accept Tosafot's explanation that the Gemara's entire uncertainty is based on possible stratagem. He may instead follow the Ritva, who explains that the uncertainty is whether the Sages made no distinctions and required an oath whenever the claimant does not demand the full amount written in the deed. Alternatively, one can say that the Gemara needed the reasoning that a payer is precise while a recipient may not be precise because otherwise we might simply infer from the fact that the borrower trusted the lender enough not to take any receipt that perhaps he made additional payments without receipts as well. That reasoning does not help where payment was made before witnesses or against a receipt, for there the borrower did not rely upon the lender. The rule that the recipient may be imprecise is needed for those cases. By contrast, in the case of a woman who reduces her ketubah, the Gemara says that the original document was written on trust. Her husband trusted her with a document stating the larger amount, although she could have demanded the entire sum. It may therefore be that he likewise trusted her regarding repayment and did not take a receipt. That itself may be the Gemara's uncertainty, without any need for a theory of stratagem. Nevertheless, Tosafot explicitly explain the matter in terms of stratagem, which supports what I wrote above. Apart from the Rosh, we also find no other author supporting this rule, as the Shach observes. Therefore this law remains in need of study.
In the borrower's handwriting. This implies that writing by another person is invalid. This is difficult. These words come from a responsum of the Rosh, and the Rosh follows his own view that an erasure between the lines is invalid because we are concerned that something had originally been written there and was later erased. He therefore requires that the notation be in the borrower's own handwriting, which removes that concern. But according to the Mechaber's ruling above in siman 44, that an erasure between the lines is valid and we are not concerned that something had been written there and erased, there should be no need here specifically for the borrower's handwriting. One must say that according to the Mechaber, the reason an erasure between the lines is generally valid is that it is uncommon to write a notation of payment between the lines. Here, however, we actually see that a payment notation was written there. We therefore may properly be concerned that an earlier notation recorded payment of a larger amount, that it was erased, and that a smaller payment was then written in its place. See responsum attributed to the Ramban, siman 100, regarding this. Therefore, specifically the borrower's handwriting is required.
