A third. Maharshal writes that this applies specifically to land or a deposit, where a kinyan can validly transfer the authorized representative's share as a full acquisition because the property is legally within the principal's possession, or to a documented debt where he transfers his share by writing and delivery. In other matters, where the kinyan works only by rabbinic enactment for purposes of agency, transferring a share of the money itself does not help. The Shach explains: Although one can appoint an agent even concerning property that cannot be transferred to the agent, if we say he appointed him as an agent, how can he litigate over the entire claim when we see that the principal gave him actual ownership only of one third? But regarding land or a deposit, we can say that he indeed appointed him as agent for the whole claim and separately transferred one third to him as a full acquisition. This is the primary ruling.
The whole. The reason is that a fractional description such as one third or one quarter is part of a phrase that necessarily refers to the entire debt. Once the principal authorized him to sue for the entire debt and also transferred one third of it to him, he has sufficient legal connection to litigate regarding all of it. Sema, and this is the primary ruling. Maharshal likewise concludes that if the representative was given one third and agreed to litigate only over that third, he need not give the principal any portion of what he recovered from that third. If the document says, "one third of whatever you recover from the debt," this resembles transferring the future fruits of a palm tree, where no kinyan presently takes effect. If, however, he transfers one third of the debt itself, the acquisition is valid. In the former case the defendant need not litigate with him, though if the defendant does litigate and the representative recovers money, he acquires his share because even with future fruits of a palm tree, once seized they are not removed from the possessor. The Shach says that this aspect of Maharshal, that the defendant need not litigate when the representative is given one third of whatever he recovers, does not appear to agree with the Sema above. He suggests that Maharshal refers to a loan debt. Even if the note is transferred by writing and delivery, saying "one third of whatever is collected" still resembles future fruits of a palm. The Sema, however, refers to land or a deposit. There, even if he transfers a share of whatever will be recovered, the underlying property is actually his and is expected to come, so it is not comparable to future fruits of a palm, and the defendant must litigate with him. See siman 209, seif 5. Shach.
