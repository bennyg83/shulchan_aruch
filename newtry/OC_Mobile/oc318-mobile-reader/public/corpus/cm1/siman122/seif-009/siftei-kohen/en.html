Who comes to bring the claim. This entire seif is the language of Rambam. Bedek HaBayit writes that Rambam's words apply only when the partners are plaintiffs, not when they are defendants, as Mordechai writes in the name of several great authorities. He therefore sees no place for the Rosh and Tur's ruling that the claimant may immediately descend to the absent partner's property. In my view, however, their ruling is well founded. The reason given by those authorities is that the absent partner may say, “I remained silent because I hoped he would never sue me.” That reasoning applies only where collection is still required from the partner who did not appear. They therefore follow Ramah cited by Tur in siman 176:31, who rules that if two partners own land and one is sued concerning that land because of a debt of their seller, or because witnesses say the seller stole it, and the claimant is placed in possession, the absent partner cannot overturn the judgment, since the appearing partner acted as his agent. This applies specifically to property that does not require collection from the absent partner, such as land, an animal, or a slave already under the control of the appearing partner. Thus, regarding land, the Rosh's ruling stands: the appearing partner acts for the other and the claimant enters the land immediately. The absent partner cannot demand new time to relitigate. Those great authorities, Ramah, and the Rosh all follow one approach. This is unlike Bach, who understood Ramah as disputing them and the Rosh. Rama below, siman 176:25, also clearly indicates that they do not dispute the Rosh and Tur, and Sema there writes that all agree with Ramah. The Mechaber himself also cites the Rosh and Tur there.
He claims the whole amount. Above, at the end of siman 77, Tur and Mechaber rule that if the other partner is in town, hears of the case, and does not appear, the defendant must pay the entire amount to the partner who sued. The same appears in siman 299. There it is clear that if it is not known that the other partner heard, even though he is in town, the defendant should not pay the appearing partner until the absent one is notified. Tur and Mechaber do not make that distinction here because actual payment requires certainty that the other partner heard and failed to appear. Without such certainty, payment is withheld lest he did not hear, later litigate, and the defendant be unable to recover what he paid. Here, however, the defendant must litigate even where it is merely presumed that the absent partner heard. If the partner later proves that he did not hear, he may relitigate, and no irretrievable loss results. If it is definitely known that he did not hear, even though he was in town, the defendant need litigate only concerning the appearing partner's share. Baal HaTerumot and the Mechaber themselves distinguish there, even where the partner heard, between litigating and paying. Although the defendant must litigate concerning the whole, he pays only half to the appearing partner and deposits the other half with beit din. Thus, saying that the partner acts as agent concerns the litigation, not necessarily payment of the entire amount. The same applies here.
The other partner cannot. Sema explains that where the absent partner was not in town, even if we do not know that he has a different argument, the judgment is not binding upon him because he may have had a defense that the appearing partner did not raise. This also affects procedural time, since he receives a new period fixed by beit din for payment, and time to enter litigation. In my view this explanation is strained. The language copied by the Mechaber is Rambam's, and Rambam disputes Tur's ruling that even when the partner was in town he may later reopen the case if he has other arguments. Bedek HaBayit states this explicitly. The Mechaber's statement that the matter will be further explained in siman 176:25 and 26 means that there he records the dissenting view of the Rosh and Tur. As to the law, Baal HaTerumot, Rashba, Ran, and Raavan all appear to follow Rambam. Raavan states that an oath imposed in the litigation of one partner binds all, but if the defendant was exempted without an oath, the absent partner may say that had he been present he would have argued more and caused liability. This applies only when he was not in town. If he was in town, the appearing partner certainly acted as his agent and the defendant is fully discharged. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise writes that the absent partner may later assert what he would have argued only when he was not in town. If he was there, he should have appeared, and his failure to do so shows consent to his partner's conduct. This also appears to be Maharam's view.
Therefore, the defendant has. Tur agrees with Rambam rather than the Rosh, as do Baal HaTerumot, Ran, and apparently Ritva. This is the primary ruling, unlike Sema, who inclines toward the Rosh. Semag writes that if the other partner is in town, both must appear. Bach adds that at times the defendant may say to the appearing partner, “Your partner would not dare advance such false claims against me,” and asserts that Rambam and Tur agree. I do not understand this. Rambam and Tur clearly dispute Semag, for their words show that where the partner is in town the defendant cannot postpone the case for any reason and must litigate the entire matter with the appearing partner. Ran, Baal HaTerumot, Mordechai, and Hagahot Ashri likewise state that Semag's position is unlike Maimonides. Maharam of Tiktin and Maharam's responsum also indicate that Rashi and Maharam dispute Semag. Since the partner was in town, he should have appeared, and the defendant could not initially say, “I will not litigate until I hear the others' arguments.” This is the correct ruling.
