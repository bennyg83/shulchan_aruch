If the land produces fruit. Sema writes that even if no fruit is presently attached because it was already harvested, the husband may litigate concerning the land itself so long as it is expected to produce in future years. According to Sema, Rav Hai excludes only land that never produces; Rosh allows litigation even over that, unless both spouses admit that the defendant owns the fruit for several years and only the corpus belongs to the wife, while the defendant claims the corpus as well. Sema says Terumat HaDeshen supports Rosh against Rivash. Sema’s reading of Rav Hai is not compelling. Rav Hai’s plain language requires fruit presently on the land. Terumat HaDeshen indicates that this is also the view of Rambam, Semag, and Mechaber, and Bedek HaBayit reads Rambam like Rav Hai. Sema’s reading of Rosh is more persuasive. Rosh says that even if land assigned to the wife produces no fruit, the husband still has an interest because it can be sold and the proceeds invested in fruit-producing property; moreover, the underlying money had originally been intended to yield income. That reasoning applies even to land incapable of producing. Bach limits Rosh to land that will later produce and argues that wholly barren land is not collectible by a creditor. In my view, that is incorrect. The rule above, siman 108, means only that where the debtor has ordinary intermediate land, the creditor must receive that instead of barren land; it does not mean that barren land is immune from collection. My reading also resolves Tur’s contrast with Rav Hai. Under Bach’s reading one must force Rav Hai to disagree even where the husband litigates over cash, though Rav Hai’s words do not imply this and Terumat HaDeshen says that even Rav Hai permits litigation over cash. Sema’s assertion that Terumat HaDeshen proves Rosh against Rivash is also difficult. Terumat HaDeshen does not decide between them. It cites Rav Hai, Rambam, Semag, and Rosh, then explains that even those requiring fruit on the land exclude only a case where the wife’s property must first be sold and replacement land purchased. Ready cash is itself equivalent to fruit-bearing land because land producing immediate income can be purchased at any time. This does not contradict Rivash, who says merely that there were not yet fruits because the vine was not laden. Rivash may follow Rav Hai and require presently attached fruit. Raavan appears to agree, and Terumat HaDeshen may concede this regarding actual land. Terumat HaDeshen further says that even where the wife inherited other property that must be sold and converted into income-producing land, if she is present but refuses both to litigate and to authorize her husband, the husband may litigate without authorization. Otherwise a relative could collude with her, retain the property, and permanently deprive the husband of the usufruct enacted for him by the Sages. The Gemara’s rule requiring authorization where the husband does not “descend to the fruits” means, as Rashi explains, a case where he has no present claim to any fruit at all. For example, if the decedent had already sold the fruits to the possessor for several years, the husband presently has no practical interest and needs authorization to litigate over the corpus. But whenever he has any present usufructuary interest, he may litigate concerning that interest without authorization. This is the true intent of Terumat HaDeshen. It does not adopt Rosh against Rav Hai, Rambam, Semag, and Rivash. It establishes only that all agree a husband may litigate over his own existing right to fruits, and that ready money is treated as presently productive because it can immediately be invested. Rama in Even HaEzer, siman 85, likewise appears to require fruit presently on the land when the dispute concerns the land itself.
And no authorization is required. This implies that if she objects, he cannot litigate, as is also explicit in Responsa Mabit, part II, siman 164. Although Beit Yosef writes below, siman 176, in the name of Ritva, that a husband requires no authorization regarding his wife's property because his hand is like hers, and may even make a claim orally so that she cannot restrain him, Ritva appears to speak of produce that belongs to the husband. In such a case she certainly cannot object, as stated in the preceding seif katan in the name of Terumat HaDeshen. Even were that not the plain implication of Ritva's language, I cited Rashba and Ran in Even HaEzer 84:14 that she may object regarding the corpus of the land. Ritva's words are independently difficult. If the reason were simply that his hand is like hers, why does the Gemara say that where the land has no produce he needs authorization, while where it has produce he does not need authorization because once he descends to litigate concerning the produce he may litigate concerning the entire land?
