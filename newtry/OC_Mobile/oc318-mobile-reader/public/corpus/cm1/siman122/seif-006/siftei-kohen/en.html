For this is what is written in a power of attorney. Beit Yosef asks that if the clause obligating the principal for all litigation expenses was omitted, the attorney should have even greater power to keep the entire recovery. He answers that the practical difference is where the attorney did not keep the money but delivered it to the principal: without the clause, the principal need not reimburse expenses. Darkei Moshe and Sema cite this. In my view, the question does not arise. The attorney can retain the money only if he claims it was transferred to him absolutely. If he admits he is merely an agent but seeks reimbursement, he cannot keep the recovery merely because the expense clause was omitted. The principal need not reimburse expenses unless the attorney claims that such authority was granted. Sema’s reliance on a migo likewise presupposes a dispute: the attorney says he was authorized to spend, and could instead have claimed an outright transfer. If he makes no such claim, no reimbursement is due under the document. Maharshal nevertheless rules that even without the express clause, the principal must reimburse reasonable expenses because the authorization was plainly accepted on that understanding. This includes incidental expenses beyond any fixed fee. As a matter of custom, all agree that expenses must be paid even if the standard clause was omitted, because one who executes an instrument intends the customary terms of that jurisdiction, as explained above, siman 61, seif 5.

Sema writes that even where the principal conveyed the claim incidentally with land, and all the more under modern forms where such language is standard but both parties intend only agency, the attorney cannot keep the recovery. Beit Yosef, however, writes that where title was conveyed through land the attorney may retain it. Darkei Moshe rejects this, reasoning that the decisive factor is the evident intention of a power of attorney: the appointee was made an agent, whether or not the form contains an incidental transfer through land. At first glance, Beit Yosef and Sema follow their earlier positions regarding whether such an authorization survives the principal’s death. Yet Baal HaTerumot proves that even where the authority survives death, the attorney cannot keep the recovery. The transactions involving Rabba, Rav Safra, Rabbi Abba, and Rav Pappa were forms of authorization effective against the risk of death, but the appointee remained an agent and could not retain the property. A different rule applies where the principal made an unqualified, complete transfer and the instrument mentioned no authorization. If the principal later claims that it was only an authorization and the recipient denies this, asserting an outright gift or sale, the recipient may retain the property. Baal HaTerumot cites Ri Migash to this effect. The Gemara likewise states that only because the document says, “Whatever litigation you undergo I accept upon myself,” do we infer agency and prevent the appointee from keeping the recovery. Tur, Mechaber below, and Maharshal agree.

And Reuven wants Shimon to give him his money. This means the money Shimon collected in another city from funds belonging to Reuven. Reuven now demands immediate payment there and refuses to wait until Shimon returns to Reuven’s city, although their original agreement contemplated later payment there. Shimon prevails because he accepted the funds on that basis. Terumat HaDeshen explains that had the money still remained with the original depositary, Reuven could revoke, since coin is not acquired by symbolic exchange. But once Shimon actually received it, Reuven cannot recover it from him contrary to their agreement. This resembles a purchaser who takes possession of future date-palm produce: although the initial sale was ineffective, once the purchaser seizes the produce it is not removed from him. The same principle applies to a debt instrument delivered without all required formalities. This ruling assumes that Reuven initially consented that Shimon could take the money for himself at that location when he chose.
