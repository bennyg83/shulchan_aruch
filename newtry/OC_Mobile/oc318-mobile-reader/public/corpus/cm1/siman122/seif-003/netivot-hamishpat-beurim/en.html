Lest he die. See Shach seif katan 5, who rules in accordance with Itur. The Gemara does not fear merely that the principal may die, but rather that heirs may later object that the agent exchanged the funds for inferior coins. Itur explains the distinction. With loan money or a specifically identifiable deposited object, death alone does not revoke the agency, for an agent is no worse than a guardian appointed by the father for his minor heirs. With deposited money that bears no identifying mark, however, there is concern that inferior coins were substituted. The father might recognize his own coins, while the heirs would not, and he did not appoint the agent to act against the heirs in such a dispute. Itur uses this to explain why the Gemara records two separate disputes, one regarding movable objects and one regarding deposited money. Shach then asks from siman 340, where Rama rules that if the principal dies it is uncertain whether the agency is revoked, while here the rule seems certain. In my view there is a distinction. When the lender says "send it through so and so," the Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia 98 explains that the borrower's discharge is not based on agency but on arev, like one who says "give a maneh to so and so and I will become obligated to you." If so, even death or revocation does not undo the debtor's discharge. In the classic arev case, if the promisor revokes privately without the payer's knowledge and the payer nevertheless gives the money in reliance on the original instruction, the promisor remains liable, because the payer expended money on his word. The same is true if the promisor dies without the payer's knowledge. By contrast, where the lender appointed an agent before witnesses to receive payment, the creditor did not expend anything in reliance on the borrower. The borrower's exemption rests solely on the fact that delivery to an authorized receiving agent is like delivery to the creditor himself. If the creditor died or revoked the agency, the agent's hand is no longer the creditor's hand and the borrower remains liable. The uncertainty in siman 340 reflects two approaches in the Shitah Mekubetzet as to whether "send through him" works by arev or by agency. According to the agency approach, death or revocation cancels it; according to the arev approach, it does not. Here, however, with an agent appointed before witnesses, death or revocation certainly cancels the agency. Rama's view is therefore primary, as Tumim also concludes. But where the instruction was merely "send through him," the principal view is that it operates by arev and the borrower is exempt even as against the heirs. Tumim's attempt to explain siman 340 through the rule of one guardian who transfers to another is difficult. Once the lender dies, the matter falls before the heirs, and the heirs can say that they do not trust the person whom their father trusted.
