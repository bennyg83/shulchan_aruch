"If he does not want to give him a seat, he will not be sent to him."
He said to him, “He who is a messenger and an angel, that he is called to him, and that he shall be saved.”
If it appears to beit din. Terumat HaDeshen writes that if the person seeking delay acts from quarrelsomeness, jealousy of the defendant, or merely to frighten him into paying money, his demand carries no weight. In all such matters, a judge has only what his eyes perceive.
The reason is. Baal HaIttur and Baal HaTerumot hold that the defendant must deliver the property to the appointed messenger. Although the principal can revoke the agency, one may presume not only that a messenger performs his mission but also that the principal remains faithful to his appointment. Maharam ibn Sasson therefore writes that one in possession may rely upon the authorities who exempt him when he did not know of revocation. The same authorities do not fear the principal’s death. Maharshal agrees and cites Rashi’s statement that Rabbi Abba was elderly, implying that ordinary persons are presumed alive. Rosh also appears to hold that death alone does not permit delay, since he mentions only possible revocation. As a matter of law, even possible revocation should not allow the defendant to refuse. Rif’s statement that one who does not wish to pay a messenger appointed before witnesses is not compelled can be read differently. It applies where the defendant asserts a substantive defense or counterclaim, for then the messenger cannot litigate without a power of attorney containing “Go, litigate, and acquire for yourself.” Maharshal similarly limits the defense “You are not my litigant” to a defendant who has some claim, counterclaim, or complete denial. Baal HaTerumot cites Rif in this manner and afterward rules that mere concern about death or revocation does not justify delay. Thus Rif need not be forced into Rosh’s interpretation.
He will be liable to pay. I have already explained that several authorities exempt the defendant because death is not ordinarily suspected. Some hold that even if he knew the principal had died, payment to the attorney is effective, because death does not revoke a power of attorney. This is the view of Baal HaIttur, Baal HaTerumot, Maharshal, Raavan, and Hagahot Ashri. Maharshal nevertheless says that after the principal’s known death the defendant may refuse because the heirs might revoke the attorney. Mahari Weil similarly writes that although the authorization remains effective against third parties, an heir who is himself the litigant may revoke it. Regarding a messenger appointed before witnesses without a written authorization, Baal HaIttur and Baal HaTerumot also exempt the defendant even if he knew of the principal’s death, comparing the messenger to a guardian appointed by a father for his minor heirs. Beit Yosef’s statement that Baal HaIttur concedes liability upon death is therefore incorrect. The incident of Rabbi Abba proves no such thing; the concern there was that heirs might challenge the quality of the coins exchanged. Mordechi, Hagahot Ashri, and Maharik remain uncertain whether death revokes ordinary agency. Maharshal and Raavan hold that a messenger appointed before witnesses loses authority upon known death. Mechaber, Rosh, Ramah cited by Tur, Rabbenu Baruch, Ritva, Tosafot, and Agudah appear to hold that death revokes even a written authorization. Nevertheless, Tosafot and Agudah say that if the depositary paid before learning of the death, he is exempt, because beit din would then have presumed the principal alive and instructed payment. A written authorization accompanied by a genuine transfer of title is different, as explained below.
However, if it is known that he died. At first glance this appears to mean that even where the principal transferred title through land, the defendant who knowingly pays after death is liable. In my view, that cannot be correct, and no authority holds it. If death defeats the transfer when known, it should also defeat it when unknown, just as ordinary agency is revoked according to Mechaber and Rama. Careful reading of Rosh’s responsum shows the opposite: when the property was genuinely transferred through land, the principal’s death creates no road-risk liability, for the attorney acquired it. Where no such transfer occurred, the authority rested only on agency, which ends at death. This is explicit in the newer text of Rosh’s responsum and in Tur: once the principal transferred the property through land, the attorney acquired it, and the heirs do not take it even if the principal died. Sema’s attempt to limit this to an unknown death is strained. The ordinary presumption of life would exempt the defendant even without a transfer; the transfer must therefore matter even where death is known. Rama’s wording, “If it is known that he died, he should not give it to the messenger,” is best read as practical advice. Since the property will ordinarily belong to the heirs, the defendant may refuse lest they revoke the attorney. Rama does not say that if he nevertheless pays he is liable. Maharshal uses the same formulation even while holding that death does not revoke the authorization. This applies to a business investment or deposit that can be acquired through land. A loan cannot ordinarily be acquired incidentally with land, even if such language is written, and its authorization remains based on agency; it therefore follows the ordinary rules of revocation at death. Movable property transferred by valid symbolic acquisition has the same status as money on deposit transferred through land. Several later authorities agree that where title was genuinely transferred, payment is effective even after known death. Their contrary readings of Rosh result from an incomplete text.
