And the reason, etc. See Urim. In truth, the Rosh in his rulings gives the reason that perhaps the sender will revoke the agency. A practical difference arises where one sends through his only son, who is his heir. There is no concern that the sender may die, because if he dies the money has been delivered to the heir's own property, but there remains a concern that he may revoke the agency. Conversely, with an authorization that does not include acquisition of the property itself, there may be no concern of revocation, yet there is concern of death, for what does it help that he cannot revoke if perhaps he dies? The Tur gives both reasons, perhaps he will revoke and perhaps he will die. Thus the question still remains: what does mere authorization accomplish if there remains a concern that he may die? It therefore appears that the Tur holds that death is not common and by itself is not a concern. The Sema's distinction between a get and monetary matters is strained, as the Sema himself notes that many disagree. Revocation likewise is not common, because there is a presumption that a person will remain faithful to his word. But when both concerns combine, they create a substantial concern, as we find in many places. This is the Rosh's intent. The Rosh asks why agency should not suffice, since everywhere a person's agent is like himself. At first glance this is difficult, because the Gemara expressly gives the concern that the sender may die. But that itself is the question: why should we be concerned for death when it is uncommon? The Rosh answers that there is an additional concern of revocation. With two concerns we are apprehensive. This is also why the Tur needed to give both reasons. There is no need for Rashi's explanation, cited by the later authorities and the Rashba in his chiddushim, that the Gemara was concerned for death because the sender was elderly. According to what I have written, there is no distinction. Two combined concerns are sufficient. Therefore, with an authorization, since there is no concern of revocation, the remaining concern of death alone does not trouble us. This is precise.
Perhaps he will die, etc. The Baal HaTerumot, Gate 50, part 2, law 4, cites the Rif that if the debtor does not wish to give the money to the agent, we do not compel him, for he can say, "You are not my litigant." The Baal HaTerumot writes: from all this we learn that an agent appointed before witnesses to receive money may receive it if the debtor wishes, but if the debtor does not wish, he need not give it, because the agent is not his litigant. Yet later, in law 6, the Baal HaTerumot investigates at length whether there is concern that the sender may revoke the agency or die, and concludes that we are not concerned at all. This would seemingly imply that the debtor must give the money, contradicting his ruling in law 4 following the Rif. The Gidulei Terumah already noted this and did not resolve it. The Shach, because of this difficulty, seeks to innovate a distinction: the statement "You are not my litigant" applies when the debtor has a dispute or claim against the lender or depositor, because there is a difference between the principal himself standing in beit din, where he may be reluctant to make a false claim, and an agent. But where there is no dispute, since we are not concerned for revocation or death, the debtor must give it to the agent. These words are strained. The concept of an existing dispute appears in the laws of authorization, but what relevance does it have to an agent appointed before witnesses? Especially since the Baal HaTerumot expressly speaks of such an agent even where there is no dispute. There is no need to elaborate because the difficulty is evident. The contradiction can be resolved differently. The Rif's basic ruling is certainly correct that the debtor can say, "You are not my litigant." The Baal HaTerumot's discussion of death and revocation makes a difference where the agent originally delivered money to the depositary or borrower and said, "So and so sent you this money as a loan or deposit," and afterward the owner appointed that same person before witnesses to collect it. In such a case the holder cannot say, "You are not my litigant," because he originally received the money from this person's hand. Whichever way one looks at it, if he was an agent to deliver it, he should likewise be treated as an agent to retrieve it. Yet if we were concerned that the sender may have revoked the agency or died, even here the holder could not be compelled to pay. Therefore law 6 teaches that we are not concerned for those possibilities. Alternatively, the Rosh asks how the debtor can say, "You are not my litigant," since we generally rule that a person's agent is like himself. Rashi, according to one version, writes that if the agent seizes property belonging to the debtor we take it from him because he is one who seizes on behalf of a creditor. This must mean that his seizure disadvantages another, namely the debtor, because until payment the debtor can use and trade with the money. The Rosh rules in Bava Metzia that one who seizes for a creditor where this disadvantages others is ineffective even if the creditor appointed him as an agent. We see that in such a case the principle that a person's agent is like himself does not apply because the agency harms another. According to this, with a sealed deposit that the debtor may not use, or with a loan where payment benefits the borrower by protecting him from currency changes or the like, so that payment is advantageous rather than detrimental to him, there is no concept of harming another. The debtor therefore cannot say, "You are not my litigant," because a person's agent is like himself. It was then necessary to teach that we nevertheless do not suspect revocation or death. Thus, where payment is detrimental to the debtor, he can say, "You are not my litigant," and there is no question from ordinary agency because agency does not operate to another person's detriment. Where payment is not detrimental, agency ordinarily suffices, and the Baal HaTerumot adds that we do not invalidate it because of possible revocation or death. This resolves the matter.
He will be liable to pay. The Shach in seif katan 5 lists many authorities who, in his understanding, hold that even if the sender dies the debtor is exempt. In truth he has mixed together two different cases: an agent appointed before witnesses, and one who comes with an authorization but without acquisition of the property itself. Regarding an ordinary agent appointed before witnesses, most authorities distinguish between revocation, where the sender himself was negligent by revoking, and death, regarding which all agree that the debtor is liable, with the exception of the Baal HaItur alone, who is a lone opinion against the majority. The disagreement concerns an authorization. The Raavan and Hagahot HaRosh hold that death does not revoke it, and many authorities disagree. But regarding an ordinary agent appointed before witnesses they agree, as is clear to anyone who examines the sources. The Shach further asks that below, at the end of siman 340, the Rama cites the Mordechai and Hagahot HaRosh in the name of Mahariach in Bava Metzia: if a borrower was told, "Send it," but before he sent it the lender died, it is doubtful whether the borrower remains liable for its loss. Here, however, the Rama rules without qualification that he is liable because the agency was revoked by death. The Shach leaves the difficulty unresolved. Mahariach himself also appears difficult, because Hagahot HaRosh in the chapter HaGozel, in his name, implies that death terminates agency. It appears, however, that Mahariach agrees that death terminates the agency. His doubt concerns a different issue. Even if we disregard the agency, the borrower may still be exempt as a guardian who transferred the object to another guardian. The owner cannot say, "I trusted you but not him," because he himself said, "Send it." Tosafot raise precisely this question regarding an agent appointed before witnesses and answer that the case involved the agent taking the item through a dangerous place. Otherwise no formal agency is needed at all. Even if the second guardian is inferior, the first guardian was a borrower, and the entire basis of liability would be negligence. There is no negligence where the owner told him to send it. All the more so according to the Shach's conclusion at the end of siman 291 that most authorities hold that where the owner ordinarily deposits with the second person, the first guardian is exempt even if the second level of guarding is inferior. Thus there is plainly a substantial doubt in the case at the end of siman 340. Here, however, the discussion concerns a loan, or a deposit carried through a dangerous place as Tosafot explain. In such a case the agency plainly terminates upon the sender's death, and the money or deposit remains at the borrower's or depositary's risk. This is clear.
However, if it is known that he died, etc. The Shach in seif katan 6 argues at length and cites a newly published responsum of the Rosh. After recounting the incident of Rabbi Abba in the chapter HaGozel, the Rosh writes: "We see that only where he transferred ownership of the property itself are we not concerned that he may have died. This concerns liability for loss on the road. Since he transferred ownership of the property itself, the recipient's acknowledgment of receipt is effective as though it had reached Rabbi Abba during his lifetime." The Shach explains that even if it is known that Rabbi Abba died, this makes no difference, because the transfer is treated as though it had reached him while alive. I am astonished how these words can be read into the Rosh. The Rosh's version of Rabbi Abba's case concludes that the money was a loan, as he writes expressly in the chapter Merubah. With a loan, transfer of ownership of the underlying property is not legally effective. Thus the agency certainly terminates, as the Shach himself rules that agency over a loan terminates at death. The Rosh's responsum is expressly discussing Rabbi Abba's incident and infers its rule from there. We must therefore explain the Rosh to mean only that we are not concerned in advance that he may die. If he actually dies, the agency terminates, exactly as the Rama writes. Consequently the Rosh provides no proof for the Shach. Nevertheless, logic tends toward the Shach's substantive position where the subject is a deposit and the principal actually transferred it through a full and effective acquisition. How could death revoke a completed acquisition? Thus the practical ruling requires study, and the party in possession can invoke kim li, as is known.
