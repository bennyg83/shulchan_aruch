Seif 2. (And the agency is cancelled.) Even according to the opinion in siman 122 that if a creditor cancels an agency to collect repayment the debtor may nevertheless be discharged after paying the original agent, a different rule may apply when a person sends an agent to borrow money for him. If the principal cancels the agency before the money reaches him, perhaps all agree that the agency is cancelled. The lender should have considered that the principal could revoke the borrowing authority, whereas in repayment the debtor who paid the apparent agent can be regarded as an innocent victim of circumstances. A person's own unavoidable loss does not automatically impose liability upon another. Even if the principal's original appointment caused the lender to act, that causation may be only indirect, and the lender may have been expected to consider the possibility of revocation. According to another explanation, however, the lender may have relied on the principal not to revoke, which could support liability. It also appears that cancellation need not be made before witnesses, except in contexts such as a get where the Sages imposed special formal requirements. The recommendation that revocation be done before witnesses may merely be practical, so that the principal can later prove it. The same principle may apply to a promissory note written payable to bearer. If the maker cancels the authority before the note is delivered in the manner contemplated, no liability should arise merely from the existence of the writing, because the obligation was intended to take effect only upon proper delivery. All the more so if he says that he never appointed an agent at all, but wrote the note intending to hand it over personally, and another person took it without authority. Mere silence while someone takes the note may not equal express appointment. If he expressly tells the person to take it, however, his statement itself may create the obligation, as in siman 40. The holder may sometimes possess a migo to claim that a loan was actually made, but whether such a migo can extract money depends on the nature of the instrument. With a witnessed note carrying a lien, the document may strengthen the claim. A mere handwritten instrument is different. An acknowledgment instrument can raise a further question. If it records a kinyan sudar and is of the type whose obligation takes effect from execution, perhaps the maker became obligated from the time of writing to whichever person would ultimately present it. Although this appears to involve bereirah because the identity of the creditor is determined only later, perhaps the liability can arise immediately with the later holder merely clarifying who was entitled under it. This requires further study.
