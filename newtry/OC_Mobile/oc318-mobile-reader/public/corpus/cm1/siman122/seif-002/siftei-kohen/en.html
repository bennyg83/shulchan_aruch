The defendant is exempt. Tur, citing Baal HaIttur, explains that the defendant may tell the creditor: “I relied upon you to maintain your appointment. Just as a messenger is presumed to perform his mission, the principal is presumed to stand by his word. You should not have revoked it, and I should not lose because you changed your mind.” Sema, citing Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, says that even if the messenger knew of revocation, the defendant is exempt if he did not know. In my view, they say this only where the messenger produced a written authorization, because the defendant can answer, “Give me what you hold, and leave the authorization with me.” He paid on the strength of the document. Without a written authorization, exemption rests solely on the presumption that the principal maintained the agency. Once the messenger knew of revocation, that presumption is gone, and payment to him was not authorized. The defendant is then liable.
Some say that he is liable. Maharshal rules like Mechaber that where the principal revoked the agency without informing the defendant, the defendant is exempt. The defendant could not know, and no greater unavoidable circumstance exists. Otherwise the enactment would be useless, because every principal could secretly revoke before the messenger arrived and shift the risk to the defendant. This reasoning also shows that if the messenger knew of the revocation, the defendant is liable. The concern that the enactment would be defeated no longer applies, and the messenger’s knowledge should have led him to investigate. If he deceived the defendant, the messenger caused the loss. This also explains why, in the later case of a written authorization, Maharshal invokes the document itself rather than mere reliance on the principal’s word: that case includes even a messenger informed of revocation. As a matter of law, Mechaber and Maharshal are correct. The dissenting view attributed by Rama to Raavad and Rosh is not established. Bach explains Rosh as exempting the defendant, and Baal HaTerumot shows that Raavad too exempts where the principal revoked, because the defendant relied upon the principal’s agency and witnesses; Raavad disagrees only regarding death.
