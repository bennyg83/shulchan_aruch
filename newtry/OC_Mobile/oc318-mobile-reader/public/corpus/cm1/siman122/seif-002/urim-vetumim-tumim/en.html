The defendant is exempt. This applies even if the agent knew of the revocation, so long as the defendant did not know, according to the Sema. The Shach challenges him, but his words are not compelling. The Baal HaTerumot explains the rule by saying that the defendant can argue, "I relied on your having sent him and on your trustworthiness, and you were negligent by retracting." If so, what difference does it make whether the agent knew or not? On the contrary, the defendant can say, "I relied on your reliability and on the agent's reliability. Since he knew that the agency had been revoked, he should not have taken the property from me." This is simple. Admittedly, the Sema is troubled because in seif 3 the Rama writes regarding revocation of an authorization that if the authorized agent alone knew of the revocation, the authorization is likewise void. The Sema attempts to distinguish between money and litigation, saying that regarding money the defendant can say, "What loss did I cause you?" This is insufficient. The rule comes from the Rosh in the chapter Merubah. The Rosh writes that if the authorized agent did not know, which implies that if he did know the authorization was void, and the Rosh concludes: "Likewise, if the defendant gave him money and it was lost through unavoidable accident, he is exempt." Thus even regarding money the Rosh requires that the agent did not know, and if he knew the defendant is liable. This directly contradicts the Sema, who holds that with money the defendant is always exempt. See Derishah. It therefore appears that the issue depends on a dispute. The Baal HaItur and Baal HaTerumot hold that even ordinary agency cannot effectively be revoked. Their reasoning must be as stated above: the defendant relied on the sender's reliability and the sender was negligent by revoking. According to that reasoning, it makes no difference whether the agent knew, so long as the defendant did not know. The Rosh, however, holds that an agent appointed before witnesses can be revoked. Evidently he rejects that rationale, because the debtor was not obligated to pay the agent and therefore should have guarded against the possibility of revocation. Only with a formal authorization does the Rosh hold that the debtor need not fear revocation, because the principal took the trouble to execute the authorization. Otherwise there would be a danger of manipulation: the principal could secretly revoke, wait to see whether the agent prevailed in litigation, and if he lost then reveal the revocation. Therefore the Rosh holds that revocation is ineffective where the authorized agent did not know. But if the authorized agent did know, there is no such concern of manipulation. Why would we suspect that the agent himself would participate in a deception? The remnant of Israel does not act dishonestly. Thus the matter depends on the underlying dispute. The Rama states this rule consistently with his earlier citation of the Rosh that ordinary agency can be revoked. According to the Mechaber's position, however, the matter does not depend at all on whether the authorized agent knew. This is precise. The Shach in seif katan 11 likewise holds that the Rambam disagrees here and discusses the matter at length. In my view no lengthy discussion is required: according to the opinion that ordinary agency cannot be revoked, the agent's knowledge of revocation is irrelevant. This appears simple.
