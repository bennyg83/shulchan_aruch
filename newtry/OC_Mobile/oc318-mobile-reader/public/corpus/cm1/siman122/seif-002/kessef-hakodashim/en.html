(I later found further material.) I wrote elsewhere concerning one who gives a promissory note to an agent with instructions to obtain a loan upon it, whether the maker can revoke the authority without informing the agent. There is some indication that he cannot, by analogy to an ordinary guarantor. A guaranty can become binding when the creditor later advances the money in reliance on it, and the guarantor cannot necessarily revoke privately without notifying the creditor. Yet the cases may differ. A guaranty operates because the creditor gives money on the guarantor's word. A bearer note entrusted to an intermediary may instead depend upon the exact authority granted to that intermediary. If the maker tells the intermediary to return the note, and the intermediary nevertheless borrows money upon it, the intermediary acts against the principal's instructions. Even though the note says that the maker is obligated to any holder, the maker relied on the intermediary not to deliver it except as authorized. An agent who violates the principal's instructions can be treated as a wrongdoer. This conclusion is particularly strong where the lender never received any direct appointment from the maker and relied solely on the intermediary. The presumption that an agent performs his agency, and the related presumption that a principal does not revoke the agency, are not strong enough by themselves to extract money against the principal's definite claim of revocation. All the more so where the agent himself admits that he was instructed to return the note, or there is a witness to that instruction. I discussed a case in which a person gave an unnamed promissory note to an intermediary to use as payment for merchandise. The seller refused to accept it, and the intermediary then borrowed money on the note from someone in another city. If the maker had already told him to return the note, the intermediary had no right to borrow upon it. The maker's obligation to any holder does not eliminate the restriction placed on the agent who was entrusted with the instrument for a particular purpose. The intermediary's possible migo, that he could claim he lawfully borrowed on the note and that commercial market protection should apply, does not itself impose liability on the maker. A migo ordinarily does not extract money. Even where a document supports a migo, that is usually because the document carries an existing lien and is treated in some measure as already collected. Here no obligation had necessarily arisen before unauthorized delivery. Nor is it enough that witnesses merely identify the maker's signature if they do not testify that he instructed them to execute a binding obligation. The document may have been entrusted conditionally. Indeed, by the intermediary's own account the maker trusted him, and that trust may have included a restriction that the note be delivered only to a specified person. Commercial custom or secular law governing negotiable instruments may create additional questions. If the governing law requires a holder of another's bill to possess supporting written authority, that practice may affect the analysis. I have not determined the precise custom concerning a bill delivered through an agent rather than sold by the original lender. One might seek an analogy to market protection for one who takes a pledge from a thief who is not publicly known as such. Yet an extension of an enactment is itself doubtful, and one who seeks to extract money under such an extension bears the burden of proof. Market protection was ordinarily enacted to prevent taking money away from the innocent acquirer, not necessarily to authorize extracting money from the original owner. The matter requires further study.
