And the reason is, etc. See Shach seif katan 4, who explains Baal HaTerumot by saying that the defendant can say "you are not my litigant" only when he has substantive defenses to raise. Tumim suggests a practical distinction where the present agent originally delivered the loan or deposit to the borrower or custodian, and afterward the lender or depositor appointed him to recover it. Tumim argues that in such a case the borrower cannot tell him "you are not my litigant," since he originally received the property through that agent. This is not correct. Once the original delivery was complete, the agent's first mission ended, and the borrower became obligated only to the lender, not to the agent. He can certainly tell the agent, "you are not my litigant." Tumim's distinction between a loan and a deposit is likewise incorrect, for Baal HaTerumot expressly extends the rule even to a loan. Nor is it correct to say that with a loan the agent is seizing for a creditor to the detriment of others and therefore the agency fails because the money must be used. Against the borrower himself this is not called harming others, as is clear in Gittin 13. Rashi's language concerning seizing for a creditor reflects the rule that such seizure is ineffective even where no one else is harmed if there is no current risk of loss, as explained in siman 105. It therefore seems that Baal HaTerumot's ruling has practical force in cases of concern for death or revocation. For example, the borrower no longer wishes to retain the loan and remain a servant to the lender. Shach in siman 74 rules that even in the lender's absence the borrower may deposit the money with beit din. Since the lender's agent is present, beit din compels the borrower to give it to that agent, because absent concern for death or revocation he cannot insist "you are not my litigant" merely in order to keep the money. That defense is available only when he wishes to retain the money longer. Another practical distinction is where he does not actually raise the defense. The language of siman 66 seif 11 indicates that he must specifically plead "you are not my litigant." If he does not, he must pay the agent because there is no concern for death or revocation. Ramban has already answered the Rosh's statement in Bava Kamma that a person's agent is like himself. The claimant must nevertheless be the owner in order to sue. Since a loan or stolen property cannot simply be transferred to the agent so as to make him owner of the underlying property, the defendant may say, "you are not my litigant," because the agent is not the owner of the thing being claimed.
