He is exempt. Beit Yosef writes that even if the agent knew that the sender had revoked the agency, if the defendant did not know, the defendant is exempt from liability according to the Rif and Rambam. Only where an authorized representative litigated and lost does the effect depend upon the representative's knowledge of the revocation, as Rama writes in seif 3, because making the defendant litigate again is not such a serious loss. This is the Sema's explanation. The Shach responds that Beit Yosef said this only where the person appeared with a formal power of attorney. There the defendant can say, 'Give me what is in your hands, and keep this authorization,' and therefore he is exempt because he delivered the money on the strength of the authorization. In the present case there is no power of attorney, so that rationale does not apply. The only possible basis for exemption is the presumption that a principal stands by his word. Once the agent knows that he was revoked, that presumption is gone, he is no longer an agent, and the defendant who delivers the money to him without a power of attorney is liable.
That he is liable. The Shach writes that Maharshal rules like the Mechaber. Although a sender can revoke his agent, the defendant had no way to know this, and there is no greater unavoidable circumstance. Moreover, if the defendant were liable, the Sages' enactment would be ineffective, since every principal could secretly revoke the agency before two witnesses in his own location before the deposit reached the agent, thereby shifting all responsibility to the defendant.
