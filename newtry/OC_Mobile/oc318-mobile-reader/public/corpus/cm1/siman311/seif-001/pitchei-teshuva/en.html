For there is a substantial objection, etc. See Shach, who concludes that ordinarily two elements are needed. But where the agreement was for unspecified wine and this particular ship, perhaps even if the wine itself did not sink the owner may refuse another ship. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Sechirut, chapter 5, law 3, appears to agree, and Ketzot HaChoshen likewise accepts that even the dissenting view in the Tur disputes only substitution of one type of cargo for another, but certainly not substitution of one donkey for another or one ship for another. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, however, in his novellae at the end of Bava Metzia, disagrees. He cites the Tur in siman 310, who states that wherever the owner must provide another donkey, such as where he said "a donkey" without specification, or even "this donkey" and its value is sufficient to rent another if it dies, then if the renter can obtain another animal cheaply he cannot say that he will rent it for himself. Thus even with "this donkey" the renter must accept another donkey. Rabbi Akiva Eiger therefore explains that the special objection in the case of a sunken ship exists because both the cargo and ship were lost and neither party can complete performance, so one side necessarily loses rental value. If only the donkey is lost and another can be supplied, or only the wine is lost and replacement wine can be supplied, no party suffers an actual loss compared with full performance, and one cannot profit merely by asserting personal preference. He concludes clearly against Shach and Sema. See Hagahot Ashri.
