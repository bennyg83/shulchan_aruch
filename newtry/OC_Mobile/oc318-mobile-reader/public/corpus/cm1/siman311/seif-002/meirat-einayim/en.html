There is a substantial insistence upon this particular ship. This is the Rambam's language in chapter 5 of Hilchot Sechirut. His intent appears to be as the Tur writes in this siman in the name of some authorities and the Rosh: One who rents "this donkey" can ordinarily be given another donkey equally good, and one who rents for a specified burden can ordinarily substitute another burden equal in weight and volume. The condition becomes material only when the circumstances give a real advantage to insisting upon it. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this is also the Rambam's and Rashba's view. The Beit Yosef cites a fuller version of the Rambam's explanation: when one says, "Give me my donkey," that does not mean that from the outset no substitution was possible. There, when there is no material difference, what difference does it make whether this donkey or another? But where the wine sank and the person gains from insisting upon his condition, the condition becomes enforceable. The meaning is that whenever the original burden still exists, the renter may ordinarily substitute another comparable burden. It would be the trait of Sodom for the owner to insist without any real interest, since in any event he must carry a burden of the same size. But once the original burden no longer exists, the law does not force the owner to accept a substitute burden when he now has a real advantage in insisting on the exact condition. This resembles the Rosh's rule regarding brothers dividing fields. If one brother has a field adjoining the common property, the Rambam sometimes compels an advantageous division because of the trait of Sodom, while the Rosh holds that where the other brothers have a legitimate financial advantage they may insist upon their right. Only where there is no real ground for insistence does the trait of Sodom apply. So too here. The Rambam says that "here the ship sank and he gains through his insistence." He ties the enforceability of the condition to the disappearance of the ship and the resulting benefit to the party insisting, not merely to the fact that the wine itself was lost. Likewise, below regarding one who rents a ship and unloads halfway but finds another renter for the rest of the route, the Rambam says that the owner should accept the substitute renter if the number of household members is comparable, invoking "Do not withhold good from its owner." The same principle applies here. The Ir Shushan explains the Rambam differently, saying the renter can insist on this ship because he chose it as especially safe, or the shipowner can insist on this particular wine because it was especially light or packed in smaller barrels. These explanations do not fit the Rambam's actual reasoning. If this particular ship truly was safer, its sinking itself disproves that supposed advantage. The correct explanation is the one I have written.
