This is difficult because there was initial negligence regarding loss and the end was an unavoidable theft. Ordinarily, if the later accident resulted from the initial negligence, liability can remain. Tosafot's distinction can be explained. If one places money in a reed hut that is vulnerable to fire, the only way to avoid the later fire is not to place it there at all. Had he not done so, it would also have been safer from theft. Thus the original negligence created the setting in which the later accident occurred. By contrast, guarding one's own house with a door unable to withstand an extraordinary wind can be adequate against ordinary risks even if it is not adequate against thieves. Here, however, the finder returned a living animal into the owner's house or garden, a place in which the finder can do nothing further because it belongs to another person, and the place itself is not fully guarded. His exemption rests only on the expectation that the owner will see the animal. Because it is alive, there is a real possibility that it will leave before the owner notices it. The finder therefore had no permission to leave it there in that fashion. Had he not done so, it is possible the later theft would not have occurred. The theft is therefore attributable to the original negligence, and he should be liable.
