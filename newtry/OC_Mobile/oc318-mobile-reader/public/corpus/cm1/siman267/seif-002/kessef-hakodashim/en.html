They shall be given to the finder. When a finder sells a lost animal or other lost property that consumes value without producing benefit, the proceeds are placed in his hands and he bears liability for unavoidable loss. This may be a special enactment of Chazal designed to encourage finders to act diligently. If so, we do not concern ourselves that the owner might prefer that the finder not use the money, even though some depositors deliberately place tied money with a custodian precisely so that it may not be used. Perhaps the owner's presumed consent follows from a principle akin to "this one benefits and the other does not lose," so that an idiosyncratic objection is disregarded. In the case of a lost object there is also no one else charged with tending it. A custodian who sells deposited property to prevent loss may be different. It may be preferable not to place the proceeds freely in his hands, particularly where he and the depositor might later dispute the costs of feeding the animal. Although selling the animal itself is an act of returning lost property, the custodian is not literally a finder, and the rabbinic encouragement extended to finders may not apply to him. If the special permission to use the proceeds exists only to reward those who return lost objects, one should be more concerned in an ordinary deposit that the owner does not want another person using his money. Money placed into business is difficult to recover in exactly the same form and can readily become consumed or tied up, approaching the serious problem of misappropriated funds. Selling on credit, whether a lost object or a deposit, also requires careful judgment. If no cash buyer will pay the same price, the credit buyer must be so reliable that the extra credit price genuinely compensates for the risk. Otherwise one may not exchange secure property for an uncertain debt.
