I do not understand his reasoning throughout that discussion, especially his statement that the question there is obvious because Tosafot do not accept this ruling. It is clear that the words of the Ir Shushan are correct and that the Rambam, Tosafot, and Tur agree in every respect. Tosafot write regarding the shearing of an ox's tail: If it is worth a peruta, the rule is obvious; if it is not worth a peruta, it is excluded from the obligation of return. They answer that the verse is needed to teach that one must shear it at the proper time even though it is not worth a peruta when sheared. The Rosh and Tur likewise write that the reference to an ox teaches the shearing of its tail, meaning that one must attend to the improvement of the lost property by shearing the tail at the proper time so that it will regrow, and all the more so the wool of a sheep. The Ir Shushan's words are based on these authorities and merely explain Tosafot and the Rosh. His explanation is compelled by careful reading. The Rambam's statement that this is learned from 'you shall return it to him,' interpreted as 'see how you shall return it,' does not appear at the beginning of chapter 14 concerning the shearing of an ox's tail. Rather, in chapter 13, law 11, he writes that one must inspect and examine the lost property so that it not deteriorate or perish on its own, as stated, 'you shall return it to him,' meaning, see how you shall return it. Thus, this verse teaches that the lost property must not suffer loss. This is an explicit Mishna and baraita at the end of folio 28 in Perek Elu Metziot, where the verse teaches not to feed a calf's value to other calves. The Rambam does not mention that one must actively improve the lost property. He writes only at the beginning of chapter 14: Why did the Torah specify an ox and a sheep? To require return even of the sheep's wool or the shearing of the ox's tail, although it is of little value. His words may also be explained as the Ir Shushan explained Tosafot and the Rosh, namely, that one must shear it even when the current clipping is worth less than a peruta so that it later improves and regrows. One might have thought that this clipping need not be returned, despite coming from property originally worth a peruta, because it was lawfully shorn. The verse teaches that it must be returned because it came from property worth a peruta. The Nimukei Yosef explicitly supports the Ir Shushan. He writes that although the tail clipping is not worth a peruta and items below that value are later excluded from return, that exclusion applies only where the whole item lacks a peruta's value. If the principal lost property was worth a peruta, he must return what comes from it. The Ran writes this. Tosafot further infer that since a verse was needed for the tail clipping even though it is not worth a peruta, one learns that the finder must attend to the improvement of the lost property and shear its tail when the time arrives so that it will grow and be shorn again. Otherwise, an item below a peruta would not require return, while an item worth a peruta would obviously require return. The Rashba writes likewise. This is precisely the Ir Shushan's explanation. His statement in the following seif, that once Scripture teaches that the finder must care for the lost item the Sages said that he must inspect it so it not deteriorate, should be understood as referring to the verse 'you shall return it.' He did not need to repeat the citation. Since the extra mention of an ox teaches that he must even improve the lost property, it follows all the more that he must prevent its deterioration. His next words, that he must inspect it, mean that the Sages explained how such inspection is performed, as he continues with the case of finding clothing. See there and examine carefully.
