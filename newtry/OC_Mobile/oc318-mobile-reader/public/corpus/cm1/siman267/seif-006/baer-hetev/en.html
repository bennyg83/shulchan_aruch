And some say. This opinion does not dispute the explicit statement of the Gemara and authorities that once deceivers became common, the Sages instituted that a claimant must bring witnesses that he is not a deceiver. Rather, it holds that even to a confirmed deceiver one returns an object upon a highly distinctive identifying mark, such as an unusual measurement or weight. According to this view, originally ordinary people were given lost objects even on the basis of weak marks such as color, which are not truly distinctive. Consequently, even now, if a person brings proof that he is not a deceiver, or is established as a Torah scholar, the object is returned to him on an identifying mark such as length or width, as stated in the following seif. According to this, the 'some say' cited by the Rama also refers back to the end of the preceding seif concerning a deceiver. So writes the Sema. The Shach challenges him and rejects his entire explanation, writing that the Rosh proves explicitly that even originally an ordinary person was not given an object based merely on color, but only on identifying marks such as measurement, weight, and the like. A deceiver, and nowadays anyone subject to this concern, is not given it even on such marks, but only on an exceptionally distinctive sign that is independently sufficient, such as a hole beside a particular letter.
