(Seif 6, and some say that a highly distinctive identifying mark is effective.) This is Rosh’s view. It is difficult to explain the Mishna according to him, for the Mishna implies that even highly distinctive identifying marks are ineffective for a fraudster. The Mishna says: “He stated the lost article but did not state its identifying marks,” which the Gemara establishes as referring to its highly distinctive marks, “he should not return it.” It then says that even if a fraudster gives an identifying mark, it is not returned to him. The baraita likewise states: “Originally, anyone who lost an article would give an identifying mark and it would be returned to him. When fraudsters increased, they enacted that he must bring proof.” Rosh writes: “But with a highly distinctive identifying mark, it appears that we return it to every person.” Yet the baraita speaks only of the type of identifying marks that previously sufficed, and previously they returned only upon highly distinctive marks. It appears to me that according to Rosh a highly distinctive identifying mark helps only against the general presumption that an unspecified person may be a fraudster. If it is known that he is a fraudster, even such a mark is ineffective. Rosh therefore writes that with a highly distinctive mark we return it “to every person,” meaning where there is no specific proof of fraud but only the general presumption. The dispute between Rambam and Rosh is thus whether a highly distinctive mark overcomes the general status that fraudsters have become common. Rosh holds that it does. Rambam holds that the presumption is so strong that an identifying mark does not help, as though the claimant were certainly fraudulent. All agree that for a known fraudster even a highly distinctive mark is ineffective. As to the baraita, one may explain it as follows. Originally, before the Mishna’s enactment represented by “he stated the lost article,” ordinary persons received lost property even upon marks that were not highly distinctive. When fraudsters increased, the Mishna ruled that one does not return upon a non-distinctive mark until the claimant brings proof that he is not a fraudster. A highly distinctive mark, however, itself serves like proof that he is not fraudulent. For a known fraudster, no identifying mark suffices. The Mechaber therefore properly writes first, in seif 5, that a fraudster does not receive the article even with a highly distinctive mark. This is according to all opinions, and the Rama adds nothing there. In seif 6, which concerns only the general presumption of fraud, the Rama writes that some say a highly distinctive mark is effective. It is effective against the presumption, but not for a known fraudster. The Sema, seif katan 8, understood the matter differently, but his explanation is highly forced, both in the sequence of the Rama’s words and in the underlying sugya. In my view, a mark is certainly ineffective for a known fraudster.
