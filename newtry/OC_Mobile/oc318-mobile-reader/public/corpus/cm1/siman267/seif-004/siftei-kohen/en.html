Until he provides distinctive identifying marks, etc. See Sema, seif katan 7, where he includes being long or short and weight among the identifying marks, and he writes similarly in seif katan 9. This is an error, for being long or short is not an identifying mark at all. This is explicit in the Maggid Mishna and the Rosh and is also evident from the Gemara. The Sema further writes that an example is saying that there is a hole beside a particular letter. It appears to me that this depends on the dispute between the Rambam and the Rosh cited by the Tur, which is the dispute reflected in the alternative view brought by the Rama in seif 6. As a matter of law, since the Rosh's language is not conclusive, it may be understood as referring to ordinary people in the present era. Even if one grants that the plain meaning of the Rosh includes a person known to be fraudulent, the plain language of the Rambam indicates that nothing is returned to a fraudulent claimant on the basis of any identifying mark, but only through witnesses. The Maggid Mishna states that this is the view of the Rambam and Raavad, and the Bach explains the Rambam this way. Hagahot Ashri in the name of Or Zarua, Perek Elu Metziot, likewise implies that today even an ordinary claimant receives nothing through any identifying mark unless witnesses establish that the article is his. It is possible, however, that the Raavad holds that even a fraudulent claimant receives the item if he gives a fully conclusive identifying mark. The Raavad therefore comments on the Rambam's words, 'until he brings witnesses that it is his,' by saying, 'a fully conclusive identifying mark,' meaning that such a mark is equivalent to witnesses. The Bach also explains the Raavad this way. It is further possible that the Raavad understood the Rambam himself this way, as Migdal Oz writes that the Raavad's language is unclear, either because he calls a fully conclusive identifying mark 'witnesses,' or because he means that even such a mark does not help. I therefore conclude as follows. With a fully conclusive mark, such as a hole beside a particular letter or the like, the item is returned today to an ordinary person even without witnesses that he is not fraudulent or that the item was his. With an ordinary significant mark, such as measure or weight, it is returned only if he brings witnesses that he is not fraudulent, or if he is a Torah scholar. If he is known to be fraudulent, however, the item is not returned even upon a fully conclusive mark, and he must bring witnesses that it is his. It also appears that the status of a Torah scholar applies today for this purpose. Although the Sema above, siman 262, was uncertain whether the status of a Torah scholar applies today regarding lost property, that uncertainty concerns a case where by strict law the item is not returned to any person. Here, by strict law it is returned to everyone, and the stricter procedure was instituted only when fraudulent claimants proliferated. Thus, being a Torah scholar is sufficient even today to remove him from the presumption of fraud. This appears correct. The Bach's discussion of Tosafot, and his objections to them, are entirely incorrect. His first question, how Tosafot could have assumed that an item is returned to a fraudulent person through identifying marks, does not arise. Even according to the view that the garment itself is announced, the Gemara may initially have meant that the claimant is investigated through his marks to determine whether he is fraudulent. In all events, an item is returned only through identifying marks, even to one who is not fraudulent, and when he supplies them this itself indicates that he is not fraudulent. Furthermore, at the stage where the Gemara seeks to prove that identifying marks are of Torah authority, it follows the view that the lost item itself is announced, while the view that the garment is announced follows the interpretation that 'investigate him' means through witnesses. The Bach also writes that Tosafot's original question has no basis, but this is astonishing, for his own answer is precisely Tosafot's second answer. Tosafot's formulation is better. According to the Bach, the phrase 'investigate him through identifying marks' would mean that once he supplies marks the item is given to him without concern that he is fraudulent. According to Tosafot, it means that one investigates whether he is fraudulent, because supplying marks establishes that he is not fraudulent, while marks do not help one who is already known to be fraudulent, as the Mishna states. The Bach's further view, that even a nonfraudulent claimant receives nothing without a fully conclusive mark because ordinary identifying marks are only rabbinic, is also entirely incorrect. In my opinion, he overlooked the Gemara in Perek Shnayim Ochazin, Bava Metzia 18b, and Perek Kol HaGet, Gittin 27b, where Rav Ashi says that the case is one who states that there is a hole beside a particular letter, specifically beside that letter, but not merely that there is a hole. Rav Ashi was uncertain whether identifying marks are of Torah or rabbinic authority. This proves explicitly that with regard to lost property, all agree that a fully conclusive mark is not required. The same is evident in Perek Elu Metziot, where the Gemara says that one may alternatively explain that identifying marks are rabbinic and that the dispute there concerns whether valuation is a significant identifying mark. Thus, all agree that lost property does not require a fully conclusive mark. This was undoubtedly Tosafot's intent when they wrote that the later passage does not mean a fully conclusive mark. Although I wrote that the Rambam may understand it as fully conclusive, that is not the primary view. See the Rosh in Perek Elu Metziot, who discusses whether Rava holds that identifying marks are of Torah or rabbinic authority. I do not understand why all this discussion is needed. Even if Rava held they were of Torah authority, the Gemara in Shnayim Ochazin and Kol HaGet states that Rav Ashi was uncertain, and we plainly follow Rav Ashi against Rava. The Semag, positive commandment 74, folio 153a, also writes this.
