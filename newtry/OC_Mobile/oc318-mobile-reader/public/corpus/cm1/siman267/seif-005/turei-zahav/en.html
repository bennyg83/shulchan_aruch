(Seif 10, and some say that the opposing claimant.) The Tur writes: “Even if one witness testifies that he saw it fall from him, the testimony of one witness is as though nonexistent. However, my father Rosh writes that the opposing claimant must swear,” and so forth. The word “even” implies that all the more so where the witness testifies that the object had belonged to the claimant, the finder can respond, “I purchased it from him.” This is difficult, for the finder would then be one who is obligated to swear but cannot, and must pay. Had two witnesses testified that it belonged to the claimant, it would be returned to him, as stated in seif 9. With one witness, the finder should therefore be obligated to swear under siman 75. According to Rosh, one must answer that this is not the ordinary case of one obligated to swear but unable to do so, because even two witnesses would not extract the property by their testimony alone. Their testimony must be joined with the presumption that the article fell from that person. The rule that one who cannot swear must pay applies only where the witnesses themselves establish the monetary liability. Beit Yosef cites such reasoning in the name of Rashba in siman 75, seif 25. It requires study whether the cases are comparable, and Rambam disagrees there with Rashba, as Beit Yosef notes in that responsum. Moreover, if the witness has power to impose an oath, why did the Gemara need to state any special rule about one witness? In what way is this case different from any other? The implication is that the Gemara teaches that here one witness is entirely disregarded. It is further difficult because a Torah oath against one witness does not fit here. In Ketubot, HaKotev, regarding one witness who testifies that a ketubah was paid, Rava explains that the oath is not a Torah oath, because all Torah oaths are taken in order to avoid payment. Here too the claimant who swears does not receive anything after the oath. The property remains set aside. We do not find that the Sages enacted an oath for such a case. It therefore appears to me that one should not impose an oath merely because of this witness. The Gemara’s wording likewise supports this: “One witness is as though nonexistent.”
