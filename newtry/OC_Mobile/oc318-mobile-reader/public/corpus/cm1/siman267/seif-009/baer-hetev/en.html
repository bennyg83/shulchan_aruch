Identifying signs. Even highly distinctive identifying signs. So writes the Rosh, and his words imply that he means even the most distinctive signs. This also appears from Maharshal in Chochmat Shlomo. What Maharshal writes there, however, that Rashi appears not to agree, is not compelling to me. Rather, when Rashi says, 'Even if you say identifying signs are of Torah law,' he states the greater novelty: even if identifying signs are of Torah law, witnesses are still superior. His meaning is that if identifying signs were not of Torah law, then even an extremely distinctive identifying sign that is effective would be effective only by logic, since nothing concerning signs is mentioned in the Torah. It would then be obvious that witnesses, who are explicitly mentioned in the Torah and are also logically stronger, are superior. But even if identifying signs are of Torah law, so that even a moderately distinctive sign has Torah status, one might have thought it equivalent to witnesses. We are therefore taught that witnesses are superior. Once witnesses are superior to a moderately distinctive sign, they are likewise superior to an extremely distinctive sign, because if signs are of Torah law there is no distinction in this respect.
