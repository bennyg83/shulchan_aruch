If he acted on his own judgment, he fulfilled his duty. See Baer Heitev, who refers to Responsa Rashach and Maharam Lublin, siman 12. See Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 152. Maharam Lublin dealt with a traveler who deposited his property with a householder and died without any known heir. Maharam Lublin disputed another rabbi and held that beit din should remove the property from the householder and appoint a guardian, or, if the householder is wealthy and reliable, require security so that the principal remains available until heirs appear or until Eliyahu comes. The guardian is not appointed to generate profit for an elderly absent owner, but only to preserve the property so that the principal may eventually be restored. Chatam Sofer writes that Maharam Lublin's proofs are compelling, although She'elat Yaavetz, volume 1, siman 142, disputed him. Chatam Sofer himself initially writes that there appears to be no need to remove the property from the holder at all, especially since it is extremely unlikely that heirs will ever be able to prove their status where none were known during the deceased's lifetime. Such property resembles money with no claimant, and Heaven may have awarded it to the present holder. If he wishes to act piously, he should use some of it for the deceased's soul, which is also a form of restitution. See there, and this will be cited below in siman 333, seif 5, seif katan 8. In another responsum there, end of siman 129, he discusses a widow who died with no known paternal heirs. Two years before death she made a deathbed will to a maternal relative, but later retracted it and the beneficiary waived any right, though the document remained in his possession. After her death, the sons of her maternal brother seized her movables, claiming that their grandfather had given her the dowry she brought into marriage and therefore they had priority. The holder of the old will claimed the estate under that document. Chatam Sofer rules that the document holder has no claim, because beit din knows that she retracted it, and the estate remains in the possession of her Torah heirs wherever they may be. The possessors' claim based on their grandfather's dowry is likewise worthless. He then reviews the dispute between Maharam Lublin and She'elat Yaavetz. He finds the Yaavetz's proof from siman 146, seif 9, inconclusive, and adds that even Yaavetz spoke only where the property came into the holder's hands lawfully, not where relatives seized it after death. He therefore rules that the possessors must provide adequate security, with an entry made in the communal register that if heirs become known the property will be taken from them and delivered to the heirs. If they are not reliable enough, the community should appoint a wealthy guardian and require sufficient security for eventual payment to the heirs. See there. The matter still requires study.
