And one who flees because of mortal danger. Whenever he had no opportunity to give instructions, beit din is obligated to attend to his property so that it not be lost.
Relatives fit to inherit. There are two reasons to place the property specifically with relatives. First, if an unrelated sharecropper is appointed, perhaps his entire intention will be to maximize produce so that he can take his share like other sharecroppers, even if doing so exhausts the land, because the owner is absent and cannot supervise. A relative fit to inherit will not damage the land, because he thinks perhaps the owner has died and the land will become his. Even if the owner is alive and returns, according to Tosafos the relative retains all the produce, and according to the other views he at least receives a share in the produce and improvement like a sharecropper. Second, since there is a real possibility that the owner died, why should our protective enactment damage the relative and deprive him of what may be his inheritance? It is better to place the land with him and let him consume the produce than to place it with someone else.
Until it becomes known, etc. Meaning, if it becomes known that the owner died, everything belongs to this relative who entered the property.
Or until the owner returns, etc. Then the relative who worked it is compensated according to the value of his sharecropping, as the Mechaber concludes, and thereafter the owner takes the land back. See the Perishah.
Some of them are not agricultural workers. The Tur says they do not personally work land, but have their own land worked through others. The novelty is that even if the other relatives likewise own land and place it with sharecroppers whom they supervise, the property is nevertheless given to relatives who personally work their own land.
Specifically in the improvement. Meaning, the physical increase in value of the land itself, apart from the produce.
If they heard that he died, etc. When they did not hear that he died, there is concern that the relative will maximize produce and weaken the land, thinking that today or tomorrow the captive may return and take the land from him. The Sages therefore enacted that even if the owner returns, the relative receives a share in the improvement like a sharecropper. This gives him reason not to exhaust the land, since he retains a share in the improvement. If they heard that the owner died, however, the relative entered believing the land was his, so there is no concern that he will damage it. If the owner later unexpectedly returns after the relative worked and improved it, the relative receives no special share of the improvement. His law is like a husband who spent money improving the nichsei melog of his adult wife and she later died: what he consumed he consumed, and what he spent he spent. See the Perishah.
Why do they not always appoint an administrator, etc.? Meaning, why place the land with a relative who will take all or part of the produce for himself according to the various opinions, leaving it in his hands without ongoing supervision until the owner returns? Sometimes the relative may not manage it properly. Why not appoint an administrator, who would in turn hire a sharecropper, supervise him so the land is not damaged, divide the produce at harvest, and preserve the owner's portion safely, while also ensuring the land is not harmed?
Because beit din is not obligated, etc. The Tur cites the Rosh: beit din will not find someone willing to perform this task. A person agrees to become administrator for orphans as a mitzvah, but not for competent adults. Therefore beit din is not commanded to search, since such a person is not ordinarily available. Rambam and the Mechaber use the Gemara's phrase that "we do not appoint an administrator for bearded men," meaning adults. The reason is as the Rosh explains, that suitable administrators are uncommon.
There is no greater arrangement than this. Meaning, if one can find such an administrator, the owner's share of the produce will be secure and the land protected from damage.
Nevertheless, the heir may object. The Rosh and Tur explain that since beit din has no obligation to search for an administrator because such a person is rarely found, the heir has a right to enter the property himself rather than have it placed with an administrator through a sharecropper. He may therefore protest that beit din should not deprive him through an arrangement that is unusual and not imposed upon them as a duty.
