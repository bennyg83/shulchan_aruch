We do not remove them from their possession. See Sema, seif katan 1, who cites Maggid Mishneh's strained explanation of Rambam. Beit Yosef is also surprised that Rambam omitted the explicit Talmudic rule that even if they heard that the owner was on his way back, one who descends is considered diligent and gains. In my view Rambam's words can be understood differently. When beit din places relatives into the property because the owner's death was not heard, beit din conditions their entry: they may consume the produce while the owner is absent, but if he returns, we deduct what they would have received as ordinary tenants and recover the excess. But where they heard that the owner died and the relatives descended in the belief they were inheriting, we do not remove the produce already consumed from them at all. Therefore Rambam had no need separately to write the law that even where one heard the owner was approaching, the person who descended is diligent and gains. It is included in his phrase "we do not remove from their hands," meaning beit din does not take any of the consumed produce from them until the owner himself actually appears and takes what is his. As to selling the body of the land, no rational purchaser would rely on such a sale. A person cannot sell what is not truly his. If someone buys from these supposed heirs, he has caused his own loss. When the owner returns, he can recover his land from the purchasers. Land cannot be hidden, so beit din need not protect against such sales. The heirs will ordinarily limit themselves to consuming produce, which they are legally entitled to do. There is another possible intent in Rambam. When beit din appoints someone to manage absentee property, it appoints specifically a fit person, as Rama says: if some relatives understand agriculture and others do not, the agricultural ones are preferred. Certainly if an unsuitable relative entered first, beit din can remove him and appoint the more suitable one. But where the parties heard the owner died and relatives descended as heirs, whoever entered may remain. Beit din does not intervene because the situation already appears to be inheritance. This explains the baraita's language, "one who descended," in the past tense.
