Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 133, cites Maharam Alshich, siman 37, who reasoned that the rule barring one from seizing for a creditor applies only where others suffer an actual loss, not where one merely prevents them from earning a profit. Rabbi Akiva Eiger notes that this resembles Ramban's distinction, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet to the first chapter of Bava Metzia, between seizing for a creditor and lifting a found object for another. Ramban says that found property is merely a lost opportunity to others and therefore is not called prejudicing them. Tosafot, however, explain the law differently, saying that one who lifts a found object for another acquires it because he could have acquired it for himself. This implies that Tosafot do regard even found property as a prejudice to others. Rabbi Akiva Eiger suggests that Maharam Alshich may have meant only that, in the creditor context, where the underlying property itself is not being taken from the other creditors and only an external profit is being prevented, that is not called prejudicing others, whereas with a found object everyone has an equal claim to the very object and the acquisition deprives them of it. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 54, raises a further doubt where the seizure caused no prejudice at the moment it occurred, but between the seizure and delivery to the creditor a new obligation to others arose. He bases the doubt on Pnei Yehoshua to Gittin 9b, where Rashi and Rif state that a slave is not freed until the bill reaches his hand because immediate freedom might create prohibitions for him before he knows of his changed status. This suggests that even when an acquisition is presently wholly beneficial, a detriment that will arise before delivery may prevent acquisition on the principle that one may confer a benefit without a person's presence but may not impose a detriment. Chatam Sofer therefore questions whether the same applies when a later prejudice to other creditors arises after the initial seizure but before delivery.
