Since, if he wished, he could acquire for himself. See Shach, seif katan 2. The principal ruling is like Tumim, who disputes the Shach and brings clear proof from Rashi in Bava Kamma 71, beginning with the words regarding authorization, where Rashi states that the practical consequence is that he may seize half. If the Shach were correct, he should be able to seize the entire amount because he can acquire half for himself. Tumim, however, does not give a satisfactory reason to answer the Shach's objection from two people who lift a found object together. In my opinion there is a clear distinction. Only where, if one does not acquire for his fellow through this lifting, he himself will also fail to acquire his own share, does his acquisition extend through the entire object. Thus, when two people lift a found object together, if the other does not acquire his share, the first does not acquire his share either. There we can say: since he acquires for himself, he acquires for his fellow as well. But one who seizes for a creditor acquires his own share even if his fellow does not acquire, all the more so with money, which is treated as already divided. His right therefore does not extend into his fellow's share at all, and we do not say that since he acquires for himself he acquires for his fellow. Ketzot HaChoshen cites partners who stole and says they are liable because of this principle even though agency cannot apply, since there is no agent for a transgression. In my opinion, that case proves the opposite. Tosafot there reject Rashi and write that if one partner stole on behalf of himself and the other, the other is exempt because there is no agency for a transgression. Only where both partners themselves lifted the stolen object are they liable. Ketzot HaChoshen asks how this can be, since even when both lift it, the acquisition of each should seemingly depend on the principle that because he acquires for himself he acquires for the other. The answer is clear. The Gemara asks, regarding two who lift a found object, that each portion should be treated as though it were resting on the ground, and answers either through the principle that because one acquires for himself he acquires for his fellow, or because one who lifts a found object for another acquires it for him. If one lifts a found object intending to acquire only half, surely he acquires that half even without anyone lifting the other half. Otherwise, if an object belongs jointly to two people and one owner abandons his share, no one could acquire that share because acquisition would apply only to half the object, which is absurd. Rather, the Gemara's question is that lifting requires the whole object to be raised by the acquirer's force. When another person holds the other half, that half is as though it rests on the ground or on a pillar with respect to the first. The Shitah likewise explains that because one who lifts a found object for another would not acquire for him, the other's hand would be like the ground as to the first, like a garment one end of which rests on a pillar. The answer is that one who lifts a found object for another does acquire it for him, so the whole object is raised by both, one to acquire his own share and one to acquire for his fellow, or through the principle that because he acquires for himself he acquires for his fellow. Since this lifting is effective to acquire his own share, it also serves as a valid lifting for his fellow. Therefore, with a competent adult and a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, because the latter's lifting cannot acquire for himself, his half is like one resting on a pillar and the competent adult does not acquire either. According to what I have written, if a competent adult alone lifts the entire found object intending half for himself and half for a deaf mute, even though the deaf mute does not acquire his half, the competent adult acquires his own half because the entire object was raised by his force and he intended to acquire that half. The deaf mute does not acquire his share even though the competent adult lifted it for him and one might wish to say that since he acquires for himself he acquires for the other. That very fact proves that this principle does not apply in such a case, for his right did not spread through the whole object: even if the deaf mute does not acquire, he still acquires his own half. The principle applies only where both lift it and his acquisition extends through the whole object, as explained. Accordingly, when two people lift an object, even if one is not capable of agency, so long as he is capable of acquisition for himself, such as where the second person is a non-Jew, or where agency would be for a transgression, such as partners who stole and both lifted it, he acquires despite the lack of agency, because the basis is that since he acquires for himself he acquires for the other. But when one person lifts an object for himself and his fellow, the fellow does not acquire by that principle for the reason explained, but only through agency. Therefore, where the fellow is not legally capable of agency, such as a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, or where it is agency for a transgression, such as one who steals for his fellow, the fellow does not acquire. Thus, however one formulates the matter, Tosafot write that one who steals for his fellow does not cause his fellow to acquire because there is no agency for a transgression, even in a case in which the principle that one acquires for himself might seem relevant. The same therefore applies where seizure prejudices others. Since agency is ineffective where others are prejudiced, the seizure does not work for the other creditor even where the seizer himself acquires a share.
