If the borrower told another person, "Acquire this for so and so," the recipient can acquire on that creditor's behalf even where other creditors are thereby disadvantaged. Maggid Mishneh cites two proofs for Rambam's rule. His first proof is from the law that if Reuven owes Shimon one hundred and gives Levi one hundred saying, "Take this to Shimon for the debt," Reuven cannot retract. That proof is not sufficient, because there no other creditor is being harmed. Lechem Mishneh therefore says the real proof is from Gittin, where the Gemara distinguishes ordinary seizure for a creditor from a case in which the owner says, "Give," and treats the latter as effective. In my view even that proof is not clear. The Gemara's case concerns an owner instructing that property be given to the person whose right is thereby established. If the creditor himself says, "Acquire for me," that is one thing. But where the borrower says, "Acquire for this creditor," while the act harms other creditors, the Gemara does not expressly teach that the borrower's instruction overcomes the rule against seizing for one creditor at the expense of others. The correct basis of Rambam's ruling is conceptual. Where a stranger acts only because he wishes to help one creditor collect, we say he cannot seize where the act harms other creditors. But the borrower himself may dispose of his movables as he wishes, because movables are not independently encumbered to the creditors in the same way as land. He can give his own property as a gift to anyone he chooses. Therefore he can also transfer it to a particular creditor through another person's acquisition on that creditor's behalf. The act is not treated as an improper seizure for one creditor, because the borrower is exercising dominion over his own movables. There is a close proof from the law of tithes. A person who has lent money to a kohen or Levi can arrange that terumah or maaser be separated for that recipient, and Shmuel explains that the owner can transfer the gift to him through another person. Even though selecting one kohen necessarily disadvantages others, the Torah gave the owner the right to choose the recipient. Because the choice belongs to the owner, he can make that choice through another person's act of acquisition. The same applies to a borrower regarding his movables. Were it not for concern about departing from the established reading, I would even suggest that Rashi's words, "since the creditor did not appoint him as agent," refer to the borrower rather than to the lender. The term used there can denote either the debtor or creditor. On this reading, Rashi means that if the borrower appoints the person to transfer the property, the transfer works, while appointment by the creditor alone does not help. This would also explain why Rashi does not use the agency rule in the case of lifting a found object for another, and it accords with Rashi elsewhere, where an authorization from the creditor does not make seizure of disputed movables effective. The later commentators who tried to reconcile Rashi by distinguishing cases where the agent could have acquired for himself do not fit the plain wording as well as this reading.
