A bailee who seized, etc. The Sema holds that a bailee may retain the depositor's property for the depositor's creditor even where there is no anticipated loss at all. Although we concluded above that an ordinary person may not seize without anticipated loss, a bailee is different because the property is already in his hands. There is in fact an even simpler reason. The deposited property is subject to the creditor by Rabbi Natan's rule, and when the depositor has no other assets the bailee may deliver it directly to the depositor's creditor. See what I write in the following seif katan. Since the property itself is subject to that creditor, the bailee plainly may retain it for him. This differs from an ordinary outsider whose property is not itself subject to the creditor and who can therefore be told, "What connection do you have to this matter that you should seize for my creditor when no loss is anticipated?" One should not object from Maharam's answer, cited in the Rosh in the first chapter of Gittin, concerning one who says, "Take one hundred for so and so." If the expression "take" is not itself equivalent to acquisition, the sender can retract. Maharam asks why the messenger is not at least like one who seizes for a creditor, and answers that the case is one where no loss is anticipated. The Rosh accepts this answer. Yet once the sender has delivered the money to the messenger, the money is in the messenger's custody and should resemble a deposit, which according to the Sema may be retained even without anticipated loss. The answer is that the Gemara's case there concerns a situation in which the creditor is not present, as is implied by the Gemara's language requiring the messenger to assume responsibility. If the creditor were present, he himself could receive the money.
Where the creditor is absent, all agree that one may not retain the money for him without anticipated loss, and the case is therefore clear. However, there is a difficulty from the Ramban's responsum, siman 118, which rules that an administrator cannot retain money belonging to orphans who have reached majority in order to pay their creditor, but must give the money to the orphans and allow them to pay. It is strained to distinguish between an administrator and a bailee, and the responsum appears to involve a creditor who is present in town. It therefore seems that the right of a bailee to retain deposited property applies only after the creditor's due date has arrived. Before the due date, all agree that he may not retain it. The Rosh in the first chapter of Gittin disagrees with Rabbenu Peretz, who distinguishes between a debt that is due and one that is not due, only where anticipated loss exists. In such a case the Rosh permits seizure even before maturity. Where no loss is anticipated, however, what basis is there to seize before the agreed due date? The due date was not established without purpose. Proof may be brought from the end of Ketubot, where two people owe one another, one debt due after five years and the other after ten. The rule is that each debt is collected at its own time. The fact that one debtor already holds money ultimately owed by the other does not give him a stronger right than a bailee holding another's property. Thus, before maturity seizure is ineffective when no loss is anticipated. The Ramban's responsum can therefore be understood as involving a debt not yet due. Otherwise, if the creditor's debt were already collectible, he could in any event proceed against the administrator directly.
It does not become movables in the hands of his sons. One should not object from the end of Ketubot, where two people owe one another and one dies. If the survivor then transfers movables to the deceased creditor's heirs, the Gemara says that he cannot collect his own debt back from those movables because movables of orphans are not subject to creditors. At first glance, why should his own possession be weaker than that of a bailee who seized property for the creditor, where the property does not become ordinary movables of the heirs? The answer is found in the chapter HaKotev, in the incident involving the grandmother and a bundle of documents. To prevent property from becoming ordinary movables of orphans, one must say and act during the debtor's lifetime, "I am holding this for the creditor." In our case the bailee seized during the debtor's lifetime on behalf of the creditor, so the property had already left the category of ordinary movables of the heirs. In the Ketubot case, by contrast, the surviving debtor did not declare during the other party's lifetime that he was retaining the money for his own debt. Only after the creditor died did he seek to treat the money as seized, and at that point seizure no longer helps. If, however, he had clearly manifested during the creditor's lifetime that he was retaining the money for his own claim, he would indeed be able to remove it from the heirs.
And if the bailee delivered it, etc. The Rashba, commenting on the statement in Bava Kamma that the property is subject to the claimant by Rabbi Natan's rule, writes that if a bailee or debtor wishes to pay the depositor's own creditor directly, he may do so because the property is subject to that creditor under Rabbi Natan's rule. The Raavad also appears to hold this way. The Shach infers from the Rashba's unqualified language that this permission applies even where the depositor himself has other assets. Although Rabbi Natan's rule is generally invoked only when the intermediate creditor has no other assets, the Shach argues that even where he does have assets the underlying lien still exists, and direct payment is therefore permitted if the bailee wishes to make it. In truth, one who studies the Rashba's novellae will see that his statement expressly comments on the Gemara's case in which the guardian delivered the property to the injured party because "it is subject to you under Rabbi Natan's rule." That Gemara necessarily concerns a debtor who has no other assets. The proof that Rabbi Natan's rule is limited to such a case comes from the Gemara itself, which initially establishes the case as one where he has no assets and afterward says, "rather," without abandoning that factual assumption. The Rashba's permission for the bailee to pay the creditor therefore refers precisely to a case in which the depositor has no other assets. The Beit Yosef understood it this way. He challenges the Ramban's ruling that if a bailee delivered the deposit to the depositor's creditor he is exempt, asking that this is merely a case of paying another person's debt without authorization. The Giddulei Terumah and Knesset HaGedolah ask why the Beit Yosef did not raise the same challenge against the Rashba, whom he cites as allowing direct payment even initially, whereas the Ramban merely says that after the bailee has paid he is not liable. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. The Rashba speaks only where the depositor has no other assets, exactly like the Bava Kamma case from which he derives the rule. There Rabbi Natan's lien applies, so direct payment is fully authorized. The Ramban, however, according to the correct reading cited by the Beit Yosef from the Baal HaTerumot, writes: "If the bailee went and delivered it to the depositor's creditor, he is exempt, because he can say to the depositor: had I returned it to you, the creditor would have collected it from you or from your other assets; and where you have no other assets I am likewise exempt by Rabbi Natan's rule, as stated in the chapter Shor SheNagach." Thus the Ramban discusses both cases. Where the depositor has other assets, the bailee's defense is only that the creditor could have collected from those assets; where he has no other assets, Rabbi Natan's rule directly applies. This also explains why the Rashba permits payment initially while the Ramban speaks only after the bailee has already delivered the money. The Rashba's case is one of no other assets, where the bailee may pay directly from the outset. The Ramban's broader ruling includes a depositor who has other assets, and in that case he does not authorize the bailee to pay initially, but merely protects him from liability if he already did so. The Beit Yosef therefore properly challenges the Ramban from the rule of one who pays another's debt without authorization, because the Ramban includes the case where the depositor has other assets. The matter is clear and correct.
For even if he had returned it, etc. The authorities ask from the ruling below in siman 125. There the Tur and the Mechaber, following the Baal HaTerumot, shaar 50, discuss a debtor who sent money through an agent to pay his creditor. The agent retained the money because that creditor himself owed money to the agent. They rule that even if the creditor admits the debt to the agent, the sender can compel the agent to deliver the money to the creditor, because until the creditor actually receives it the sender has not been discharged from his own debt. This applies where the creditor has other assets, so Rabbi Natan's rule does not operate. That ruling appears inconsistent with the present rule that even where the depositor has other assets, a bailee may retain his property on behalf of the depositor's creditor. With respect to the Baal HaTerumot and the Mechaber, one might distinguish as follows. A deposit remains the depositor's property wherever it is located. The depositor's creditor therefore has an underlying lien on it. Even with movables there is some form of lien, as the Rashba explains. The reason creditors do not collect movables from heirs is not the total absence of a lien, but that the creditor did not rely on those assets. All the more so when movables are already in another person's hands, where the lien is stronger, as Tosafot write in Bekhorot and in the chapter Yesh Nochalin. See what I wrote at the end of siman 85. By contrast, where A owes B and B owes C, but the conditions for Rabbi Natan's rule are absent, C has no lien at all on A's debt to B. A debt owed to B is merely prospective property relative to C, and prospective property is not subject to a lien. The Rashba at the end of Kiddushin writes that apart from Rabbi Natan's rule, even if the creditor's document expressly includes movables and land so that the creditor may collect movables from heirs, he still cannot collect a debt owed to the debtor by a third party, because that receivable is merely prospective property. Accordingly, in the agency case the agent has no legal hand to appropriate the money for his own claim, and the sender can demand it back. This distinction could explain the Baal HaTerumot and the Mechaber. It cannot, however, explain the Tur, because the Tur holds that one who pays another person's debt without authorization is entitled to reimbursement. If so, even if the agent's retention did not operate through Rabbi Natan's lien, why has the sender not been discharged? The agent can say, "I paid your creditor's debt on your behalf, which he admits owing me, and you are therefore obligated to reimburse me." It therefore appears necessary to read the Tur more carefully. Why do the Tur and the Mechaber not simply say that the creditor can continue to collect from the sender, leaving the sender to recover from the agent? Rather, if the sender himself is satisfied that the agent retain the money, the creditor has no claim against the sender on this basis. This follows from the present rule that a bailee may retain property for the depositor's creditor, and all the more so according to the Tur, who entitles one who paid another's debt to reimbursement. When the Tur says that "so long as the agent has not given it to the creditor, the sender is not discharged," he means that the original debt itself has not been extinguished as a matter of setoff. If the creditor sues the sender, the sender may answer, "You owe me a corresponding amount because I paid your debt," so each now has a claim against the other. We hold, however, that where two parties owe one another, each debt is independently collectible. See above in siman 85 for the many practical consequences. The sender's instruction to the agent was not merely to create a counterclaim, but to discharge him from his debt to the creditor so that he would no longer stand as a debtor to that creditor. If the agent retains the money, that purpose has not been fulfilled. The sender may therefore revoke the agency and demand return of the money. The money remains his because the agent has not completed the mission for which it was entrusted. The agent cannot answer, "I have my own claim against your creditor," because the sender never subjected these funds to the agent, Rabbi Natan's rule being inapplicable. This is why the Tur emphasizes that "so long as he has not given it to him, the sender has not been discharged." Had the sender been discharged, the agency would have been completed and he could not revoke it. Since he remains legally indebted, he can revoke the agency and recover his money. This interpretation depends on the sender's own will and resolves the apparent contradiction. It appears correct for practical halakhah. The Perishah answers that here the depositor's other assets are overseas, so Rabbi Natan's rule still applies. This is strained and contrary to the Beit Yosef's intent. The Shach answers that below the sender originally did not know that his creditor had other assets, but that is surely a scribal inversion, for if he did not know of other assets the third party lien would more readily apply. In my view the explanation above is preferable.
