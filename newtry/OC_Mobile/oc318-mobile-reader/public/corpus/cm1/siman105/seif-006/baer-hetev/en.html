To seize it. The Sema writes that this applies even where there is no imminent loss to the creditor, or where the creditor is not present, circumstances in which ordinarily one cannot seize property for another. Here, however, since the deposit is already lawfully in the bailee's possession by permission of the debtor, he may retain it for the creditor's benefit when doing so does not prejudice others. Therefore the Mechaber concludes that if the creditor is not present, the bailee cannot continue holding it on his behalf even if the depositor admits owing that creditor. But where the debtor is not financially secure, the bailee's retention is no worse than ordinary seizure of property not already lawfully in his possession. Since the debtor admits the debt, he certainly need not return it. The Shach cites the Bach, who explains instead that the case is one of potential loss, but the bailee does not wish to accept responsibility for the property. He therefore must return it to the debtor, and when the creditor later appears and establishes liability, the property is treated retroactively as having belonged to the creditor. See the Shach's lengthy discussion.
Exempt. The Sema writes that this differs from one who pays another's debt without authorization in siman 128. There, the argument, "I could have persuaded the creditor to waive or reduce the debt," is enough to prevent the volunteer from imposing liability on the debtor, but not enough to extract property already in the volunteer's hands. The Shach writes that, based on his lengthy proof in siman 86 that the lien of Rabbi Natan applies even when the original debtor owns other property, the bailee here becomes directly obligated to the creditor under that lien. He is therefore a litigant vis-a-vis the creditor, as though he himself owed the money, unlike the volunteer payer in siman 128. Accordingly, the Shach concludes that even if the depositor later seizes property from the bailee because the bailee paid the depositor's creditor, that property must be taken back from the depositor. This is the primary ruling. See Responsa Maharashdam 143, 209, and 217; Responsa Maharit 124; Maharashach II, 199; and Mabit III, 202.
To pay. The Tur's wording is: "Had I returned it to you, he would have collected from you or from your other assets." This requires study in light of siman 125, seif 3. The Shach suggests that there the sender, when handing property to the agent, did not know that the debtor had other assets. Here, by contrast, the bailee knows with certainty that the debtor owns other property.
