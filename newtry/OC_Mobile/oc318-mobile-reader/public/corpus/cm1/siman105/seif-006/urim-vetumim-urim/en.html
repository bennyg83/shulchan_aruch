A custodian who seized, etc. Even where there is no risk of loss, since the property is already in his possession he may retain it for the depositor's creditor. Independently, it is also subject to that creditor through the rule of Rabbi Nasan. See Tumim. It appears that the creditor's loan must already have reached its due date. Before the due date, all agree that he may not seize it.
If the creditor is not present, etc. Bach holds that where there was a risk of loss, but the custodian did not wish to accept responsibility and therefore returned the property, it nevertheless does not become ordinary movable property in the hands of the depositor's heirs. This requires study, because Maharshal, as Bach himself cites him, does not appear to hold so, and the position is not persuasive.
And if he delivered it, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that if the debtor has no other property and the funds are therefore directly subject to his creditor through the rule of Rabbi Nasan, the custodian may even initially deliver them to that creditor. If the debtor has other property, the custodian should not initially do so. If he did, however, the depositor has no claim against him.
For even if he had returned it, etc. Although this resembles paying another person's debt without authorization, and the rule of Rabbi Nasan does not apply here because the debtor has other property, that limitation applies where the payer seeks reimbursement from the debtor, who can say, 'Who told you to pay my debt?' Here, however, the payer is already in possession. See Rama below in siman 128. See Tumim, where I reconciled this with the Mechaber in siman 125 seif 3.
