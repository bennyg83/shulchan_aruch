A bailee who seizes the deposit in order to hold it, etc. I proved that this applies even where an ordinary third party could not seize because there is no imminent loss to the creditor, or the creditor is not presently before us. Since the deposit was already lawfully in the bailee's possession through the debtor, he can hold it for the creditor so long as he does not thereby injure others. The conclusion, that if the creditor is absent he cannot continue withholding it merely for the creditor even where the debtor admits the debt, concerns a case with no present danger of loss: the creditor can later seize it from the debtor, and there is no special concern of Shemittah or of the property becoming ordinary movable property in the heirs' hands. Even if the bailee returns it, his prior seizure can retain certain legal consequences once he informs beit din that he held it for the creditor. This resembles return of a pledge, which remains effective for Shemittah and inheritance consequences even after temporary return to the debtor.
But where the borrower is not financially secure, the bailee's lawful possession is no weaker than an ordinary seizure. If the debtor admits the debt and the creditor would suffer a loss from return, everyone agrees that even an ordinary seizure can be effective, all the more so a bailee who already lawfully holds the object.
If he transferred it through agav, etc. Meaning the depositor transferred his right in the deposit. It cannot mean physical delivery, since the item remains in the bailee's hands. He would have had to, etc. Meaning the depositor would have had to pay the creditor. Even according to those who rule that one who pays another's debt without authorization cannot recover from the debtor, and even if he redeemed the debtor's collateral must return it without charge because the debtor can say, "Had you not paid, I might have persuaded him to waive the debt," that rule only prevents the volunteer from extracting money from the debtor on the strength of such a weak claim. Where the debtor seeks to take property already held by the person who paid, we do not use that speculative possibility to remove property from the possessor.
