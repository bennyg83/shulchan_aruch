Regarding a custodian who seized the deposited property for his own creditor, the Sema cites Rabbeinu Yerucham: Reuven sent an agent to collect wine from a gentile who owed him money. The agent took the wine, loaded it on a wagon, and when he reached town transferred it to Shimon, to whom the agent himself owed money. Rabbeinu Yerucham ruled that Shimon acquired it. At first this is difficult. If the gentile himself gave the wine in payment of Reuven's debt, Reuven should already have acquired it, and the agent should have had no power to transfer it to Shimon. The answer is that a gentile cannot appoint a Jewish agent in the ordinary Torah sense, nor can acquisition for another operate on the gentile's behalf in the same way. The collector therefore acts only as Reuven's agent in taking the property. Until Reuven himself acquires, the situation resembles seizure for a creditor where other creditors can be harmed. A second point concerns the wagon. If the wagon belonged to Reuven, or Reuven had rented it, his courtyard would have acquired the wine for him as soon as it was placed there. A person's courtyard can acquire even where the acquisition disadvantages others, just as it acquires a found object. The rule against seizure for one creditor at another's expense is a limitation of voluntary agency. A courtyard acquires as a person's hand, or because the object is placed there involuntarily, and therefore that limitation does not apply. Thus Rabbeinu Yerucham's case must be one where the wagon belonged to the agent, or where the wagon was not a protected courtyard of Reuven. The agent's own presence beside the wagon cannot substitute for Reuven's presence, because the agent would then again be acting only as a person seizing for one creditor where others are harmed. Tumim cites Rabbeinu Yerucham with two additional reasons: there is no agency for a gentile, and perhaps the halachah is not that a gentile acquires through the same mode as a Jew. He then asks that once the gentile has relinquished the wine, it should be ownerless, and the agent who lifts ownerless property for Reuven should acquire it for him. This is not difficult. A creditor can collect even from property his debtor declared ownerless. Maggid Mishneh writes that a debtor's abandonment does not place the property beyond creditors, and I explained in siman 245 that even movables abandoned by the debtor can still be collected. Therefore, once the gentile debtor has relinquished the property, Shimon, as an existing creditor, can still acquire it against Reuven through the applicable creditor rules. Tumim finally says the Shulchan Aruch properly omitted Rabbeinu Yerucham because our law supposedly concludes that a gentile acquires through meshichah. I do not know of any such decisive ruling. The great early and later authorities remain divided on the relevant mode of acquisition from a gentile. Therefore, where Shimon has already taken possession, one cannot simply remove the property from him. Rabbeinu Yerucham's ruling remains practically significant, subject to the distinction above concerning whether Reuven's own wagon or courtyard had already acquired it.
