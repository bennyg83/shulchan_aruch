If the debtor owes him, etc. The Sema holds that a creditor cannot seize for another creditor more than the amount of his own claim. Thus, if the debtor owes him one hundred, he may seize only one hundred on behalf of his fellow creditor, but not more. The Shach in seif katan 2 agrees with the Beit Yosef that once the person has a legal hand to acquire some of the property for himself, he may use that same hand to acquire a greater amount for his fellow creditor. He brings proof from two people who lift a garment together, where the Gemara says that because each can acquire for himself he can also acquire for the other. There is no proof from there. Each lifter had the legal ability to acquire the entire garment had he lifted it alone. Since his hand could extend to the whole garment, his acquisition can operate for the other as well. With a debt, by contrast, where one may seize only one hundred for himself, how could that limited right enable him to seize two hundred for another creditor? Perhaps this is what the Shach means when he says that the proof can be rejected with some difficulty, but in truth the distinction is broad and involves no difficulty at all. There is affirmative proof for the Sema from the case of the cisterns of the Babylonian exiles. Rabbenu Tam explains that although we hold that one who lifts ownerless property for another acquires it for him, the basis is that since the lifter could acquire it for himself, he may acquire it for the other. Consequently, the recipient cannot have a wider Shabbat boundary than the person who drew the water. If the drawer has only one thousand cubits remaining from his city while his fellow has two thousand, the fellow cannot carry the water beyond the drawer's thousand. We do not say that because the drawer has some proprietary hand in the water he can acquire for the other a greater right than he himself possesses. Rabbenu Tam's view is accepted in practice, as appears in Orach Chaim siman 392 in the Rama. The law is therefore clear. The same is evident from Bava Kamma 70. According to one version, the Gemara says the parties are partners and asks what practical consequence follows, answering that the representative may seize his half because he owns that part. If the Shach's principle were correct, he should be able to seize the whole. Once he has a right in part, his hand would supposedly extend to the remainder for the other party. The Gemara's limitation to his own half proves the opposite. A person cannot seize more for another than he himself could acquire. This is clear and correct.
