By virtue of a migo that, had he wished, he could have acquired it for himself. In the Perishah I proved that this applies specifically where the amount seized does not exceed the seizer's own debt. For example, Reuven owes Shimon, Levi, and Yehudah one hundred gold coins each, and Shimon seizes property from Reuven worth one hundred. If Shimon seized it for himself and Levi, each may take fifty, because since the seizure would be effective for Shimon himself it is also effective for Levi. Even if Shimon seized the entire hundred solely for Levi, it is effective, because he could have seized the entire hundred for himself. But if Shimon seizes property worth one hundred fifty and seeks to acquire for Levi the fifty beyond his own debt, it is ineffective as to the surplus, because he himself could not have acquired the entire amount, his own debt being only one hundred. One proof is that this rule is derived from the law that one who lifts a lost object for another acquires it for the other because, had he wished, he could have acquired it for himself. The cases must be comparable, meaning he could have acquired the entire object for himself. This is unlike the Beit Yosef, who writes that the seizure for another is effective even beyond the seizer's own debt because there exists at least some possibility of acquisition for himself. The Ir Shushan follows that view. In my opinion, those words are rejected.
