And another person preceded him. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 133, considers two creditors where a courtyard owned by one creditor had been rented to the debtor. The debtor died leaving movable property in that courtyard. Rabbi Akiva Eiger questions whether the landlord is deemed to have acquired the property first through his courtyard. Even according to the Rambam, cited below in siman 313, seif 3, that a landlord's courtyard can acquire found property for him, and according to the Shach there that this is so even when the landlord does not reside there, there remains a difficulty. A man's courtyard acquires by the law of agency, as Bava Metzia explains, and an act that cannot be accomplished through an agent generally cannot be accomplished through a courtyard. Since a creditor could not appoint an agent to seize property for him in a way that prejudices other creditors and thereby obtain the status of one who collected first, perhaps his courtyard cannot accomplish that result either. Rabbi Akiva Eiger leaves the practical law unresolved. Shaar Mishpat, above siman 104, seif 4, also analyzes this issue and concludes that a courtyard is stronger than an agent and may acquire even where an agent could not because the acquisition prejudices others, deriving this from the law that one's courtyard acquires found property. Rabbi Akiva Eiger rejects that proof. It works only according to Tosafot, who explain that one who lifts a found object for another acquires it for him because he could have acquired it for himself, from which one might infer that a courtyard is superior. According to Ramban, however, found property is not considered a prejudice to others at all, and even according to Tosafot the proof is not conclusive. See also the discussion cited in Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer siman 102, seif katan 4.
