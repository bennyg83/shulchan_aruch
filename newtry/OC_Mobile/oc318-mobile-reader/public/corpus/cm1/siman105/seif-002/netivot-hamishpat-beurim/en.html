Even if he appointed him as an agent. See Shach, seif katan 1, who writes at length to reconcile Rashi, who states that when the creditor appointed him as an agent, seizure is effective even where it prejudices others. The Shach explains that Rashi means he hired him as an agent, and therefore it is effective even where it prejudices others because he is like a worker, whose hand is considered the employer's hand. See there. He overlooked the Mordechai in the first chapter of Chullin, who is uncertain whether a worker may seize a debt belonging to his employer, as Tumim cites. In my opinion, a worker cannot seize where this prejudices others. At first glance, if a worker's ability to acquire is not based on agency, since agency is ineffective where it prejudices others, but instead on the principle that the worker's hand is like the employer's hand, then even those who are legally incapable of agency, such as a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, should, when working for another, acquire a found object for the employer. Yet no authority states this. The principal view is therefore like Tumim: the worker's hand is considered the employer's hand only where the worker himself could acquire the object, as Maharsha writes in Ketubot, Perek HaKotev. The reason is that because the worker can acquire for himself, the employer can acquire from the worker's hand. Therefore, where a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor cannot acquire for himself, the employer cannot acquire through him either. According to this, a non-Jewish worker might be effective. However, according to what I wrote elsewhere, that acquisition through despair in the case of a lost object is derived from the verse concerning an ox, a garment, and every lost object, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 66, and the object must come into the hand of one capable of acquiring it because the verse says, and you find it, which implies that it comes into his hand, it appears that since the verse says, every lost object of your brother, a non-Jew does not acquire a lost object through the owner's despair. Accordingly, even as a worker he would not acquire it. On this reasoning, in the law of one who seizes for his employer, where the worker has no independent hand with which to acquire it for himself, the principle that his hand is the employer's hand does not apply. Tumim likewise concludes this. As to the principal reconciliation of Rashi, it appears as follows. Nimukei Yosef in Bava Metzia, on the Mishnah concerning one who saw people running, writes that a protected courtyard, for which the owner need not be standing beside it, requires explanation. Its efficacy is not because it is considered a hand, since regarding a get even a courtyard requires proximity, nor is it because of agency, because in the name of the Ran he writes that one cannot acquire for another by agency unless he was appointed by the owner of the property, while with a found object there is no owner of the property to appoint him. Nor can one say of a courtyard, as with one who lifts a found object for another, that since he could acquire for himself he may acquire for his fellow. At first glance, why did Nimukei Yosef not simply use the Gemara's language that this is one who seizes for a creditor where others are prejudiced, since the Ran holds that even express agency does not work in such a case? The answer appears to be that Pnei Yehoshua explains that agency is ineffective where others are prejudiced because it is like agency for a transgression. But Bava Metzia 11 concludes that a courtyard can function through agency even for an actual transgression, such as theft. Therefore the Ran needed another reason, namely that monetary agency works only when the agent is appointed by the owner of the money. The Gemara did not give that reason regarding one who lifts a found object for another because the Ran may hold that acquisition for another is not based on agency and can work even where agency does not. Therefore the Gemara needed the rule that acquisition for another is ineffective where it prejudices others. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 72, however, write that even though one can acquire a found object for another, receiving money from a non-Jew does not cause the Jewish recipient to acquire it for another Jew. If the non-Jew had abandoned the money, one could acquire it for another, but where the non-Jew is transferring his own money to satisfy his debt to the lender through the borrower, the Jewish recipient would have to act as the non-Jew's agent, and there is no agency for a non-Jew. At first glance this implies that Tosafot disagree with the Ran, because they allow agency in ownerless property even without appointment by an owner. Upon examination, however, there is no dispute. I have explained elsewhere the distinction between ownerless property and an object after the owner's despair. Ownerless property has already left the owner's possession even before another acquires it, and the former owner himself must reacquire it. A lost object after despair, however, does not leave the owner's possession until it reaches the hand of one who acquires it. Tosafot therefore correctly write that agency works for ownerless property because there is no owner of the money, whereas for a found object, before it reaches the acquirer it remains the owner's, and agency without the owner is ineffective. According to this, Rashi is also understandable. One might ask the Ran: if monetary agency is ineffective without the owner of the money, why does seizure for a creditor work where it prejudices no one? The answer must be that the borrower is not regarded as the owner of the money in this respect because it is already subject to the creditor, there is no other source from which the creditor can be paid, and even by seizure the property does not entirely leave the borrower's possession, since the borrower may still discharge the debt with other money. Appointment by the owner is required only where the act removes property from the owner's possession. Therefore Rashi correctly writes that if the creditor appointed the seizer as his agent, the seizure is effective. Rashi holds that agency is effective even where it prejudices others, and the Ran's requirement of appointment by the owner of the money does not apply to seizure for a creditor because the borrower is not treated as the owner for this purpose, as explained regarding seizure that does not prejudice others, and the other creditor is certainly not the owner of the money. By contrast, Rashi agrees that agency does not work for a found object, since there is an owner of the property and agency without that owner is ineffective. This answers the Rosh's objection from the Mishnah of the rider, which concerns an appointed agent, and the commentators' objection from the baraita concerning a worker's found object, which also involves agency, because in a found object Rashi agrees that agency is ineffective as explained. Tumim's objection from Yemar bar Chashu in Ketubot is also answered, because there the case concerns heirs, and the movable property of heirs was not subject to their father's creditor. Through seizure one seeks to bring it under the creditor's lien and remove it from the possession of its owners, so the heirs are owners of the property and agency without them is ineffective. Likewise Tosafot's objection in Bava Metzia 10 from Beitzah is answered. There Rashi explains that the person appointed another as an agent to fill the vessel, and because the well is ownerless property and has no owner, agency works as explained. All this is written only to reconcile Rashi. The principal ruling, however, follows Tosafot and those who agree with them, that agency is ineffective even if he hired him as an agent, as Tumim concludes, contrary to the Shach. In the Shach there is also a scribal error. The text should read: Since here the debtor is not making a claim against him, the objection, You are not my beit din, does not apply. Later in this seif katan it should read: A worker is different, because his hand is like the employer's hand.
