Since he could acquire for himself. Beit Yosef, Sema, and Shach discuss whether one who has his own claim may seize more than the amount owed to him and thereby benefit another creditor. In my view Beit Yosef is correct that the principle "since he can acquire for himself, he can acquire for another" can extend even beyond the amount of his personal claim. We see this with partners who commit theft. Ordinarily there is no agency for a transgression, yet where one thief takes for himself and his fellow, his acquisition can work for both through the principle that because he acquires his own share he can acquire the other's share. The mechanism therefore has force even where ordinary agency is unavailable. Accordingly, if a creditor could have seized a portion for himself and simultaneously intended to seize the rest for another creditor, the same principle can support the entire act even though his own claim is smaller. The remaining question is where he seized the entire asset only for the other creditor and not at all for himself. One might try to use two successive extensions: since he could have acquired part for himself, he could have acquired part for the other, and because he could thereby have acquired for the other, perhaps the whole follows. That is doubtful. The law of peah suggests we do not stack the principle twice. The Sages say that one who gathers peah for a particular poor person may have to give it to the first poor person encountered, even though a poor person can sometimes acquire for another poor person through the fact that he could acquire for himself. This indicates that two levels of "since he could acquire" are not automatically combined. Beit Yosef's rule is strongest where the seizer actually intends some acquisition for himself together with the acquisition for the other creditor.
