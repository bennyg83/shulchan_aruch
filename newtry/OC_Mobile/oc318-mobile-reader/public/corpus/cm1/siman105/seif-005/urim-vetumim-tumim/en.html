And some say that he is not liable, etc. The Bach writes that the primary view is the first opinion, that of the Baal HaIttur, who holds that the person who seizes property for another creditor becomes responsible for it. I am astonished, because the Sefer HaTerumot, shaar 66, part 2, cites the Ramban concerning a person who holds a deposit and wants to retain it because the depositor owes money to another creditor. The Ramban says he may not retain it, and gives the reason that no one has undertaken responsibility for the deposit if an accident occurs before the creditor comes to collect it. This plainly supports the Rosh, who holds that one who seizes for another creditor does not become responsible for the seized property. If he automatically bore responsibility, the Ramban's reason would have no force. It makes no difference whether the deposit has been in his hands since yesterday and he now decides to retain it for the creditor, or he takes it now from the debtor. At the moment he holds it for the creditor he is a person who has seized property, and according to the Baal HaIttur he should be liable for it. The Ramban and the Sefer HaTerumot therefore clearly follow the Rosh. The Baal HaIttur is consequently a lone view. Moreover, the Bach contradicts himself, because later, regarding a deposit, he agrees with the Ramban's ruling, which cannot stand together with his present preference for the Baal HaIttur. It appears, however, that the Rosh's rule that the seizing party need not accept responsibility applies where his seizure does not harm other creditors. Where his seizure does harm others, and his only legal basis for acquiring for his fellow creditor is that he could have acquired the property for himself, his right cannot exceed the right he would have had if he actually acquired it for himself. Had he acquired it for himself, the debtor would have been completely discharged to the extent of the property taken, because the seizing creditor would have accepted it as his own payment. Accordingly, when he acquires for his fellow creditor through that same legal mechanism, he can do so only on the same terms, namely by accepting responsibility for the property. This is why the authorities cite the Rosh's exemption from responsibility only where the seizure does not harm other creditors. Where it does harm others, all should agree that the seizing party must accept responsibility.
