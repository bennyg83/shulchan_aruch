The purchaser and recipient receive nothing. Choshen Mishpat 66:16 rules that one who buys from a lender a debt document against a borrower, and the borrower later dies, may swear that the seller never informed him that the debt was paid and that he himself does not know it to be paid, and then collect. Even if the seller has died, this can work because after the sale the seller is no longer the principal party obligated to swear. The seller is sworn if alive only because he could say the debt was paid, by a migo that he could have waived it. This makes the Tur's ruling here difficult. If a woman sold her ketubah during her husband's lifetime, the husband then died, and the woman herself died before taking the oath, why should the purchaser receive nothing? Once she had already sold the ketubah, her case should not resemble a lender who dies before the borrower. The purchaser should be able to swear that the seller gave him no instruction that it had been paid, and collect. One can distinguish between a ketubah and an ordinary debt document. Sale of an ordinary debt is a firm sale once the collection date arrives, and the need for an oath is not certain, since if the borrower does not plead payment there may be no oath. Sale of a ketubah is inherently weak. She may never collect at all because she may die before her husband. Even if it later becomes collectible, an widow ordinarily collects only by oath, since a woman does not ordinarily stand for divorce. Therefore, the original sale is a weaker transfer and the purchaser cannot collect if the widow never swore before death. Rambam, Hilchot Ishut 17:17, writes similarly that a woman may sell her ketubah, but if she dies during her husband's lifetime or before swearing, she has nothing. Nevertheless, proof is needed for the distinction. Rambam may also hold that even an ordinary purchaser of a debt document cannot swear after the seller dies. But Rosh and Ran expressly hold that with an ordinary debt document the purchaser may swear even if the seller died. What proof is there that they disagree regarding a ketubah? Indeed, at the end of tractate Shevuot the Rosh compares the two laws and brings proof concerning a sold debt document from Rambam's rule about the ketubah, showing that he understood them to be one legal category.
