Seif 1. (Where this causes loss to other creditors.) The rule that one who seizes property for a creditor does not acquire it when doing so harms other creditors may perhaps be explained through agency. Acquisition on behalf of another is ordinarily based on agency, and one cannot become an agent to perform a wrongful act against others. It also accords with the ethical principle that what one would resent if done to oneself should not be done to another. Yet that explanation would not fit well where the creditor pays the person to seize on his behalf. A stronger explanation is that the agency here arises only through the law of beneficial acquisition. Since the act benefits one person while simultaneously harming another, the automatic law of beneficial acquisition does not operate, and therefore no agency arises. Still, the Gemara indicates that even where the creditor expressly appointed the person as an agent to seize, the seizure can fail if it harms other creditors. Perhaps the reason is that seizure is only the beginning of collection, and an ordinary agent cannot complete collection without formal authorization. Where no other creditor is harmed, however, the seizure can still become the beginning of collection because the seizer can deliver the movable property directly to the creditor. A specific lien then attaches to that property during the debtor's lifetime, similar to the concept that a debtor's land serves as guarantor for the debt. By refraining from immediate collection because the property has been placed in the seizer's hands, the creditor in effect relies upon that property, creating an analogous security interest. Where other creditors are harmed, the seizer had no right to appropriate the debtor's movable property for one creditor alone, because the property should be distributed among creditors according to the applicable order. No special guaranty can therefore arise through his seizure to give that one creditor priority. This analysis does not fully explain the application of the same rule to a found object, and requires further study. It also remains to be examined whether "harm to others" means only direct financial harm, or can include a prohibited indirect loss, such as causing another person to lose the use of his money. If the rule rests on the wrongful nature of the harm rather than on the specific creditor distribution structure, such indirect loss might also be included.
