Even if he appointed him as an agent. The Shach inclines to accept Rashi's explanation that agency is effective. He understands this to mean that if the principal pays him and hires him for this agency, his status becomes that of a worker, whose hand is like the hand of his employer, and the Gemara states explicitly that he can acquire for the employer. In truth, Rashi's language, "since the creditor did not appoint him as an agent to acquire for him," sounds unlike the Shach, for Rashi should then have said, "since he did not hire him." Still, the Shach spoke correctly on this point. Rashi, who distinguishes between one whom he appointed as an agent and one whom he did not, derived this from the rule that a worker can acquire for his employer. Rashi understood that there is no relevant difference toward third parties between one who works for wages and one who acts gratuitously. What legal force is added by wages? He can say, "I regard myself as though I had already received my wages," and in any event the benefit of the principal's gratitude is itself worth a perutah. Rashi therefore held that the same rule applies to an agent. This also answers the question raised against Rashi: how could the Gemara challenge from the law of a worker if, according to Rashi, an agent can seize? According to what I have written, Rashi said this only after the Gemara answered, "a worker is different." From that answer Rashi learned that the same applies to an agent, since there is no substantive distinction between them. At the initial stage, however, even an agent would not have acquired. The Maharsha asks in Ketubot, in the chapter HaKotev, why Tosafot challenge Rashi from the incident of Yemar bar Chashu, who appointed an agent to seize and nevertheless was treated as one who seizes for a creditor where others are thereby harmed. Rashi may have said that agency helps only where the agent himself could acquire the property, as with a found object, whereas in the case of a debt, when the debtor owes nothing to the agent, Rashi never said this. The Maharsha writes that Tosafot did not accept such a distinction. It is true that Tosafot did not accept it, because Rashi's language, "the creditor did not appoint him as an agent," is stated without qualification and appears to include even an agent who has no personal claim against the debtor. Nevertheless, in my opinion this was unquestionably Rashi's intent. When we say that a worker's hand is like the employer's hand, that is in a found object, where the worker has a legal hand in the object himself. We can then say that his hand is treated as the employer's, so that his own acquisition becomes the employer's acquisition. But where the worker has no legal hand in the property at all, as with a debt that is not owed to him, how can we say that his hand is like the employer's when he has no hand in the property to begin with? This explains why Rashi wrote this only in Bava Metzia at the initial stage, when the discussion assumed that one who lifts a found object for another does not acquire it for the other. In such a setting Rashi writes that an agent is effective because he could acquire the object for himself. Once the Gemara concludes that with a found object the other person acquires in any event, Rashi nowhere distinguishes an agent in the law of creditors. How could this distinction be rejected entirely? The Mordechai in the first chapter of Chullin itself is uncertain whether a worker of the creditor may seize the creditor's debt. At first glance this is difficult, for the Gemara expressly says, "a worker is different, because his hand is like the hand of the employer." The Mordechai's doubt must therefore be precisely whether that rule applies only to a found object, in which the worker has an independent hand, or also to a creditor's property, in which he has none. The silence of the other authorities likewise indicates that an employee or hired worker of the creditor cannot seize the debtor's property merely because his hand is treated as the employer's. The Rif and the Rosh omit the Gemara's question and answer concerning a worker, apparently because they understood that this answer was not stated as practical law for a creditor's case. Accordingly, even a worker does not help with a debt, for the reason stated. To clarify further where Rashi's view tends, consider Rashi's explanation in Beitzah 39 concerning a public well. One drew water and gave it to another, and Rav Sheshet and Rav Nachman dispute whether the water follows the feet of the one who drew it or the one for whom it was drawn. The Gemara initially suggests that they disagree whether the well is ownerless or jointly owned. Rashi explains that if it is ownerless, we hold that one who lifts a found object for another does not acquire it for the other because he is seizing for one creditor to the detriment of others, while the one who lifts it acquires it himself. Rav Nachman, however, holds that the well is jointly owned by all Israel. When the other person told him to draw from his share and he went on his agency, the drawer became his agent, and this is not a case of lifting a found object for another. This seems to show that according to Rav Sheshet, who treats the well as ownerless, even though the drawer was acting as an agent, the intended recipient does not acquire. That would contradict what I have attributed to Rashi, namely that agency itself suffices. At first I considered answering by asking why Rashi had to say that the other person appointed him as an agent to draw at all. Perhaps he simply drew for his fellow without any appointment. Rashi may have been troubled that, even at the conclusion, the entire sugya would then be contrary to the halakhah. We rule that one who lifts a found object for another acquires it for the other. On Rashi's approach, everyone should therefore agree that the water follows the feet of the person for whom it was drawn, eliminating the dispute. It would also nearly produce a contradiction in practical rulings, since we rule like Rabbi Yochanan that one who lifts a found object for another acquires it for him, yet in matters of prohibition we rule like Rav Sheshet, who says that the water follows the feet of the drawer, implying that the recipient did not acquire it. Rashi therefore explains that the drawer was indeed made an agent, but the instruction was "draw for me," not "acquire for me." Even according to Rabbi Yochanan this is inferior, and the recipient does not acquire, as is clear from the Mishnah in Bava Metzia where one says, "give it to me," rather than "acquire it for me." In such a case his words make matters worse, and silence would have been better. The present sugya can therefore concern that same case. All agree that the recipient does not acquire, and the dispute is whether the drawer acquires it. This is how Rashi explains both the initial stage and the conclusion. There is therefore no difficulty, and Rashi can concede that an agent who was told merely "give it to me" does not acquire for the principal. This also helps explain Tosafot's question on Rashi in the passage beginning HaBa. According to Rashi, at the conclusion Rav Nachman and Rav Sheshet agree that the person for whom the water was drawn does not acquire it, and they dispute only whether the drawer acquires it for himself. Tosafot object that it is unreasonable for the drawer to acquire for himself without intending to do so. Rabbenu Tam therefore explains that Rav Sheshet holds that the recipient acquires through the migo that the drawer could have acquired for himself. Consequently, the recipient cannot have a broader techum right in the water than the drawer himself could have had. Rav Nachman holds that the recipient does not acquire it and it remains ownerless. Tosafot also cite Rashbam's explanation that Rav Nachman holds that the recipient does acquire because, with water from a well, there is no detriment to others, so Rav Nachman does not contradict his own general rule. See there. Yet according to Rashbam, Tosafot's original objection would still remain under Rashi's conclusion. If the recipient does not acquire, why should the water follow the drawer because the drawer acquired it for himself when he never intended to do so? One could answer that Rashbam too accepts Rabbenu Tam's principle that where the recipient acquires only through the migo that the drawer could acquire for himself, the recipient cannot acquire rights broader than those of the drawer. Rashbam merely considered it strained to explain "like the feet of the person for whom it was drawn" on the ground that the water remained ownerless, because the language implies that the recipient acquired immediately upon the drawing, even if another person sought to acquire it before it reached him. Rashbam therefore explains that Rav Nachman holds that in water, where there is no detriment to others, one who lifts for another acquires for him directly, without the migo of his own ability to acquire. The water is accordingly immediately like the feet of the recipient. Rav Sheshet, however, agrees in practical law with Rabbi Yochanan only through the migo, and therefore the water remains like the feet of the drawer, as Rabbenu Tam explains. Tosafot in Bava Metzia bring these two explanations in Rabbenu Tam's name, which supports this reading. Nevertheless, whether according to Rashbam or Rabbenu Tam, a difficulty remains at the initial stage. There the Gemara says that Rav Nachman views the well as jointly owned and Rav Sheshet views it as ownerless, and for that reason Rav Sheshet says that the water follows the drawer. Tosafot's question returns: how does the drawer acquire it without intending to acquire? At the initial stage we cannot say that the recipient acquires only through the migo described above. According to Rabbenu Tam that explanation arises only at the conclusion. At the initial stage Rav Nachman holds that one who lifts a found object for another does not acquire for the other even where he has the migo. Rav Sheshet at that stage must therefore agree on that general rule, unless we make them disagree about two separate matters, both whether lifting a found object for another works and whether the well is jointly owned or ownerless, which is implausible. It would then follow that the drawer himself acquires, reviving Tosafot's objection. If there is a satisfactory answer at the initial stage, the same answer should seemingly remain available at the conclusion according to Rashi, for the Gemara never retracted that underlying reasoning. This requires study. According to what I have written, however, the matter is resolved. One must first understand the force of Tosafot's question, "how can he acquire for himself without intent?" It is reasonable that when a person takes ownerless property intending to acquire it for another, if the other cannot acquire through him, he should acquire it himself. Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia indeed cites this in the name of the Rosh. Tosafot's difficulty comes from the Mishnah: one saw a found object, lifted it, and said, "I acquired it," he acquires it. The implication is that he acquires only because he expressly says "I acquired it," but not automatically. According to the view that one who lifts a found object for another does not acquire for the other, and if the Mishnah is not limited to a case where he had earlier announced a contrary intent, why should the Mishnah require him to say "I acquired it"? It should simply say that the lifter acquires. The Mishnah thus implies that one who lifts it for another does not ordinarily acquire it for himself merely because the other failed to acquire. The distinction is that if he independently lifts ownerless property, we may presume that he lifted it in order that someone acquire it. His immediate intention was to benefit his fellow, but if the fellow cannot acquire, he acquires it himself. By contrast, if he lifted it because his fellow commanded him and appointed him as an agent, he never lifted it for his own purposes at all. He lifted it solely for the fellow and pursuant to the fellow's instruction. Even if the principal fails to acquire, the agent has nevertheless performed the agency he accepted. Why should the agent now acquire for himself when that was not the purpose for which he lifted it? This fits well. According to Rabbenu Tam and Rashbam, the case of drawing water for another is not one where the recipient instructed the drawer and appointed him as agent. Their conclusion can therefore remain consistent with halakhah, because the recipient does acquire. At the initial stage, if the recipient does not acquire, we may still say that the drawer acquires for himself because he did not draw in vain. The Mishnah concerning a found object does not contradict this, because there the person lifted it pursuant to his fellow's instruction, as it says, "he said to him, give it to me." In such a case the lifter does not automatically acquire it for himself until he says, "I acquired it." But according to Rashi, who must understand the water case as one of agency in order to keep the sugya consistent with practical halakhah, Tosafot properly object that the drawer cannot acquire for himself without intent because he acted pursuant to the principal's instruction. This also explains the Mishnah. However, all this remains strained within Rashi. If so, at the conclusion Rashi should have expressly explained that the case involved agency and clarified carefully that the principal said only "draw for me" and not "acquire for me." This distinction could also answer Tosafot's challenge to Rashi from the incident of Yemar bar Chashu regarding the ketubah, because there the creditor told the agent to go seize and effectively said "bring it to me," not "acquire it for me." All of Tosafot's objections would then be resolved. Therefore the true explanation appears different. Rashi was compelled to explain at the initial stage that the person appointed the drawer as an agent because he was troubled by the question raised by the authorities: if one lifts a found object for another and the other does not acquire, while the lifter himself does acquire, how can the Mishnah concerning one who gathers peah for a poor person say that he must give it to the first poor person he encounters? The gatherer himself should acquire the peah. If so, he should be obligated to give it specifically to the poor person whom he originally named, because his verbal commitment is charity and is binding upon him like a vow. Or Zarua answers that the gatherer was wealthy. See there. Accordingly, Rav Nachman, who says that the dispute in the peah Mishnah applies even from one poor person on behalf of another and who holds that one who lifts a found object for another does not acquire for him, must also hold that the lifter himself does not acquire automatically. This produces a difficulty at the conclusion. Rav Nachman and Rav Sheshet truly disagree, and Rav Sheshet too understands the peah Mishnah as applying from one poor person for another. Yet according to Rashi both agree that the intended recipient does not acquire. How then can Rav Sheshet say that the lifter does acquire? The problem is even stronger at the initial stage, where they are said to disagree whether the well is jointly owned or ownerless. We cannot reasonably say that they also disagree about a second issue concerning whether the lifter himself acquires. If they agree with Rav Nachman that he does not, then even if the well is ownerless, how can the drawer acquire the water? Rashi therefore understood that Rabbenu Tam's underlying principle is certainly correct: when one who lifts a found object for another acquires for the other only through the migo that he could acquire it for himself, the recipient cannot receive broader techum rights than the lifter. Rashi further understood that Rav Sheshet's statement here, that the recipient does not acquire and the water follows the drawer, does not mean that Rav Sheshet rejects Rabbi Yochanan's rule that one who lifts a found object for another acquires for him. If that were so, practical rulings would contradict one another. Rather, the ordinary rule is based on the principle that one may acquire for a person in his absence. That principle works where the acquisition benefits the recipient. Here, however, if the drawer did not acquire the water for him until it reached his possession, the water would follow the recipient's feet. By acquiring it for him earlier, the drawer causes him a disadvantage because it follows the drawer's feet as a stringency, while certainly also being restricted by the recipient's own feet as a stringency. Thus the earlier acquisition is a detriment to him, and the principle that one may acquire for another in his absence does not apply. Rashi therefore holds that the drawer himself acquires the water. The peah Mishnah is no difficulty, because it can concern a wealthy gatherer, while in ordinary cases Rav Sheshet holds like Rabbi Yochanan that one who lifts a found object for another acquires for him. We cannot say that the water case involved an appointed agent, because then the rule against causing detriment to another would not apply, since the acquisition was made with the recipient's consent. Moreover, Rav Nachman's reason that the water follows the recipient would become impossible. Rashi holds that an appointed agent acquires for the principal where the agent himself has a legal hand with which to acquire, as the Maharsha understood Rashi. Rabbenu Tam's principle would then return: the principal cannot receive more extensive rights than the agent could acquire for himself. How could the water have a broader techum than the agent's own techum? The case therefore cannot be one of agency at the conclusion. The drawer acted on his own, and for this reason Rashi does not read the sugya as saying that the recipient acquired directly. The dispute is then coherent. At the initial stage, however, there remains a difficulty. If the well is ownerless, Rav Nachman would agree with Rav Sheshet, yet Rav Nachman holds that the peah dispute applies even between poor people, which indicates that the lifter himself does not acquire when the intended recipient fails to acquire. Rav Sheshet must then likewise hold that absent acquisition by the recipient, the drawer would not acquire. For this reason, at the initial stage Rashi had to explain the case as one in which the recipient told him to draw. In that circumstance everyone agrees that the recipient acquires. Rav Sheshet nevertheless says that the water follows the drawer, based on Rabbenu Tam's principle that the recipient cannot have greater rights than the agent through whom he acquires. Rav Nachman would agree to this rule in a genuine agency case, but at the initial stage he holds that the well is jointly owned and therefore reaches a different result. Rashi's words are thus precise and thoroughly coherent. There are not two independent disputes either at the initial stage or at the conclusion. This also explains why Tosafot did not challenge Rashi at the initial stage on the ground that the drawer could not acquire without intent. At that stage, Rashi understood the case as one of actual agency, and on this point his approach is the same as Rabbenu Tam's. It therefore appears that there is substantial basis for the explanation of Rashi given above, namely that Rashi did not state the agency rule as the final law concerning a creditor. I can add another support. Rashi's view that an agent can certainly acquire at the initial stage follows from the rule of a protected courtyard where the owner is not standing beside it. Such a courtyard acquires through the law of agency. If an agent could not acquire a found object for the principal, how could a courtyard acquire a found object for its owner through agency? Rashi therefore held that a true agent can acquire, at least at that stage of the sugya. Once we rule, however, that with a found object the recipient acquires through the migo that the lifter could acquire for himself, the courtyard acquires independently in any event. Although a courtyard cannot literally have a migo of acquiring for itself, the Torah equated it to an agent. At the initial stage, by contrast, if an actual agent could never acquire a found object for the principal, it would be impossible for a courtyard to acquire a found object by virtue of agency when the very agent to whom it is compared could not do so. See Shitah Mekubetzet in the sugya discussing whether courtyard is derived from the law of one's hand and is no worse than agency. It cites authorities who reason that one cannot invoke agency for a found object because even a full agent cannot acquire such an object. Ritva, however, concludes in accordance with Rashi that a protected courtyard functions like an agent and does acquire a found object. This supports Rashi's approach. See also below, siman 248, seif 3 and Sema seif katan 6, that a courtyard acquires a found object through agency. I found a further explicit indication in Bava Kamma 70, in the dispute concerning a power of attorney and whether the representative is treated as an agent. Rashi writes according to the version that if the representative seized property of the defendant, it is removed from him because he is one who seizes for a creditor in a manner that harms others. Rashi thus states clearly that even an agent cannot seize for a creditor. It is therefore certain that Rashi did not state his Bava Metzia explanation as the final law for creditors. Mahara Sasson writes that Ritva in Gittin accepted Rashi's explanation, but that text is not before us. Ritva to Bava Metzia expressly rules unlike that broad reading of Rashi and holds that even an agent cannot seize. Who knows which formulation represents his later view? In any event, we should not depart from Tosafot, the other authorities, and the ruling of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. As for the Mordechai's uncertainty regarding a worker in a creditor's case, this too requires study, as stated. The other decisors omit it, for the reason I explained: the rule applies specifically where the worker himself has a legal hand in the property. It is strained to say that now that we rule that one who lifts a found object for another acquires for him, we no longer need Rav Nachman's answer that "a worker is different." Why invent a new dispute against an explicit statement of Rav Nachman when no contrary opinion is found? At first glance one might instead argue that a worker hired specifically to collect found objects cannot acquire them for himself at all and has no independent hand of acquisition, because his hand is entirely directed toward his employer. If he truly had the power to acquire each found object for himself, and merely had a contractual duty to hand it over to the employer because that was the purpose of his employment, what was the Gemara's question from the teaching that a worker's found object belongs to his employer? One could say that the worker first acquired it personally and the teaching merely tells us that he must afterward transfer it to the employer pursuant to their agreement. The Gemara's question therefore implies that one hired to gather found objects has no independent hand to acquire them for himself at all, only for the employer. If so, he has no migo of personal acquisition. This also explains another point. Rav Nachman and Rav Chisda both say that one who lifts a found object for another does not acquire it for the other, yet Rabbah challenges Rav Nachman specifically from the baraita concerning a worker's found objects. Why does he not challenge Rav Chisda as well, since both stated the same rule? According to the foregoing, Rav Chisda can answer that the baraita follows Rabbi Eliezer, who holds in the peah case that the gatherer acquires for the poor person. One might object that there the gatherer has a migo because he could acquire for himself, whereas a worker lacks any such migo. But Rav Chisda himself holds in the first chapter of Gittin that according to Rabbi Eliezer one may acquire for a poor person even without any migo. Rav Nachman, however, says, "let the master say that the dispute is from one poor person for another," rather than saying "even from one poor person for another." Since Rabbi Eliezer clearly permits even a wealthy person to gather for a poor person, this wording suggests that Rav Nachman understands Rabbi Eliezer as requiring a migo. Rabbah can therefore challenge Rav Nachman from the worker baraita, and the Gemara answers that a worker is different. Tosafot, however, in the passage beginning VeLeima, conclude that according to Rabbi Eliezer even Rav Nachman does not require a migo, because Rav Chisda, who agrees with Rav Nachman's general rule, says in the first chapter of Gittin that Rabbi Eliezer does not require one. Their intent is not merely that two amoraim who agree on one point must agree on every detail. Rather, Tosafot regard it as an unnecessary dispute to make Rav Nachman and Rav Chisda disagree about Rabbi Eliezer when no evidence requires such a disagreement. Since Rav Chisda says that Rabbi Eliezer does not require a migo, the same should be presumed for Rav Nachman unless compelled otherwise. One should not say that Rabbi Eliezer's position would then become too extreme, since the Sages deny acquisition even from one poor person for another where a migo exists, whereas Rabbi Eliezer would allow a wealthy person with no migo to acquire for a poor person. Rav Chisda himself accepts precisely that broad dispute, so there is no reason Rav Nachman could not as well. But why did Tosafot need to establish whether, according to Rav Nachman, Rabbi Eliezer requires a migo? We do not rule like Rav Nachman on the general issue, nor do we rule like Rabbi Eliezer. The point must be to exclude the proposed answer that the worker baraita follows Rabbi Eliezer. Had that answer been available, the fact that the Gemara did not use it would prove that a worker lacks any migo, and then one could argue that his hand is nevertheless like his employer's even in a creditor's case. Tosafot show, however, that Rabbi Eliezer does not require a migo even according to Rav Nachman. The question would therefore remain: why not establish the baraita like Rabbi Eliezer? We must say that the Gemara knew the baraita was stated in accordance with the practical halakhah. It is then still possible that a worker does have a hand with which to acquire a found object, and therefore has a migo; it is only in that setting that we say his hand is like the employer's. In a creditor's case, where the worker has no personal right at all because the debtor owes him nothing, the rule does not apply. Tosafot thereby teach us a substantial point. This also appears to exclude the broad reading of Rashi. Once Tosafot establish that the baraita cannot simply be assigned to Rabbi Eliezer, the original question remains: why was Rav Nachman challenged and not Rav Chisda? According to Rav Chisda one can invoke Rashi's distinction that an agent is different, and a worker is certainly no worse than an agent. The Mishnah that says "I acquired it" can be understood as meaning that he said, "I had acquired it for myself from the outset." Rav Nachman, however, says that the peah dispute extends from one poor person for another and brings proof from the Mishnah. He therefore cannot interpret "I acquired it" as meaning "I acquired it from the outset," and it follows that even an agent does not acquire automatically. He can thus be challenged from the worker baraita, and the Gemara answers that a worker is different and has a stronger status than an ordinary agent. This is unlike the Shach, who equates the two. Tosafot therefore also undermine the claim that Rashi equates an agent with a worker as final law. I have expanded somewhat in order to clarify the sugya. For practical halakhah, since the decisors omit any special power of a worker in the creditor context, the implication is as I have written: only with a found object, where the worker himself has a legal hand, can his hand be treated as the employer's. With a creditor, however, even a worker may not seize to the detriment of other creditors.
And he gave him a power of attorney. The Bach writes that an authorized representative may seize property, and the Shach disagrees. The matter does require study, because I found in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia, quoting the Tosafot HaChitzonim, precisely the Bach's view that one who holds a formal power of attorney may seize. The incident of Yemar bar Chashu involving a ketubah concerned a person who had no such authorization. Likewise, in Bava Kamma 70 the Gemara discusses a representative who seized property. Rashi explains one version that if he seized property belonging to the defendant, even though the authorization did not contain the words "go litigate and recover for yourself," the property is not removed from him. Rav Ashi disagrees and says that the principal merely appointed him as an agent. Rashi explains on that version that if he seized property belonging to the defendant it is removed from him because he is one who seizes for a creditor where that seizure harms others. This indicates that had the authorization expressly said "go litigate and recover for yourself," he would indeed have been able to seize, and would not be considered merely one seizing for another creditor. Nimukei Yosef also records this version, so the point is explicit that a fully authorized representative can seize. It therefore appears necessary to distinguish between a debt that, under the law of the Gemara, is itself transferable through a power of attorney, as explained below in siman 122, and a claim for which authorization works only by later enactment. In the first case the representative acquires a direct litigating right and is effectively a party himself, so the rule against seizing for a creditor where others are harmed does not apply. In the second case the legal ownership does not truly pass to him, and the authorization works only because he is treated as the principal's agent, as Tosafot, the Rosh, and many other authorities explain. He then has no greater power than any other agent and may not seize to the detriment of other creditors. This distinction is clear and correct for halakhah.
