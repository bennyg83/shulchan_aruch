Rashi explains that one who seizes for a creditor at the expense of other creditors fails specifically when he acts on his own and was not appointed by the creditor. Tosafot and most authorities reject that distinction and hold that even an appointed agent cannot seize where the seizure harms other creditors. Tosafot's position follows their theory that acquisition for another works through agency. They say that even without explicit appointment, a person who acquires something beneficial for another is treated as his agent. If that were the whole matter, there would indeed be no difference between express and implied agency. I have long found difficulty with saying ordinary acquisition for another is simply inferred agency. Bava Metzia teaches that where a person acts without the owner's knowledge, later approval does not necessarily create prior agency. Abaye rejects retroactive consent. If acquisition for another depended only on the assumption that the recipient would have appointed him had he known, the same problem should arise. It therefore appears that the Torah created an independent legal institution of acquisition for another. Some authorities describe it as functioning like agency for purposes such as excluding a gentile, but it is not literally based on the factual assumption that the recipient already appointed the person. Thus where one acts without knowledge, the acquisition can work by the special law of zekhiyah. Where it harms others, however, it is no longer a pure benefit and the special law does not operate. Under this conception, Rashi's distinction becomes plausible. If the creditor expressly appointed the person as agent, ordinary agency applies even though others are harmed. The agent is legally the creditor himself. If there was no appointment, the seizer relies only on the special institution of zekhiyah, and that fails because the act benefits one person at others' expense. Pnei Yehoshua suggests that an appointed agent fails because this is agency for a transgression. I do not accept that. The transgression, if any, is primarily the agent's harm to others, not a prohibition resting upon the principal. The rule that there is no agency for a transgression normally applies where the principal himself is commanded not to perform the act, such as a kohen appointing an Israel to betroth a divorcee. Tosafot concerning collection of peah likewise shows that agency can exist where the violation is only upon the agent and not upon the principal. Thus there is conceptual room for Rashi's position even though Tosafot and the accepted authorities rule otherwise.
