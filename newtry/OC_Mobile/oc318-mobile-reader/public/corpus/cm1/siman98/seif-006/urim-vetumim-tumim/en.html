Regarding a deposit, etc. The Sema, seif katan 15, understands the word "immediately" used concerning a deposit differently from the identical word used earlier. In his view, even with a deposit the court waits thirty days to allow the defendant to prove that the deposit document is forged. This is difficult. Neither the Tur nor the Mechaber distinguishes between the two uses of "immediately," and the Gemara uses one term for both matters. It is strained to assign two different meanings to the same word and likewise to say that the Tur's word "similarly" is not exact. This also conflicts with Nimukei Yosef. He writes that the rule that an adrachta is written immediately for a deposit applies where the defendant denies having the deposit. But if he admits that he received it and only claims that he was negligent with it, so that he is now monetarily liable like an ordinary debtor, his case follows the law of a loan. Now, where the defendant admits the debt and merely asks for time or refuses to pay, he receives only the ordinary thirty days, as explained in siman 100. If the Sema were correct that even a claim of forgery concerning a deposit receives thirty days, there would be no practical difference between denial and admission. Both would receive the same thirty days. Nimukei Yosef clearly distinguishes them. The same reasoning disproves the Sema from the law of a loan. The Gemara gives the borrower ninety days. Why not one hundred twenty? The first thirty days could be used to find witnesses proving that the document is forged, the next thirty to find money after he failed to disqualify the document, the third period to sell land, and the fourth because a buyer may need time to obtain money. The answer is necessarily that if beit din sees substance in the claim of forgery, whether the matter concerns a loan or a deposit, it grants whatever period seems necessary. If the claim has no apparent substance, we do not presume a valid document forged and grant no special time at all for that allegation. Therefore, in the ordinary loan the only delay is the ninety-day collection schedule. In the case of a denied deposit, where the defendant has effectively admitted that he did not negligently lose an item and therefore his denial is equivalent to a complete denial of the obligation, there is no reason for any collection delay. "Immediately" means literally immediately. Nimukei Yosef's rule itself, however, may not accord with the Maggid Mishneh. The Maggid Mishneh explains the distinction between a loan and a deposit by saying that a loan is intended for expenditure, and the lender understood from the outset that his money might not be immediately recoverable whenever he demanded it. At first glance this rationale is unnecessary. The simple reason for the ordinary delay is that the debtor needs time to obtain money, whereas a deposit is supposed to remain intact. If the bailee admits that he was not negligent, as described above, no search for replacement money should be necessary. It therefore seems that the Maggid Mishneh understands the deposit case more broadly, including one where the bailee admits that the deposit was lost through his negligence and thus must obtain money to pay. Even then, the depositor did not enter the transaction with an expectation of delayed repayment. Theft and loss, and certainly negligence by an unpaid guardian, are not ordinary anticipated outcomes. The court therefore need not grant the same extensive collection period that it grants to an ordinary borrower. If so, Nimukei Yosef's limitation requires study.
