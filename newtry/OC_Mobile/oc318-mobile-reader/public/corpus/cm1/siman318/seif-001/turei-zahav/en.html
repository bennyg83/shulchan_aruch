(Seif 1: One who rents a mill) To clarify this rule, the Gemara's discussion must be set out. A person rented a mill on condition that, instead of paying money, he grind the landlord's household grain. The landlord became wealthy and bought another mill and donkey for his own grain, and demanded money rent. The tenant answered that he would continue grinding. The Gemara concludes that the tenant may say: grind with the mill you bought and sell that flour, and use my mill to grind your household grain. This applies where the tenant cannot obtain other grinding work and would otherwise sit idle. If he can obtain paid grinding work, compelling him to grind for the landlord without payment would be conduct like Sodom, and he must instead grind for customers and pay the landlord money. Tosafot ask why the Gemara conditions the tenant's defense on the landlord's ability to sell the output of the new mill. Even if the landlord cannot sell it, why should the tenant lose money from his own pocket? They answer that, although the tenant need not pay money for the past, the landlord could cancel the lease prospectively even during the term because he too suffers a loss; therefore the lease continues only when the landlord can sell the other output. The Rosh disagrees, reasoning that rental for the term is a sale and the landlord chose to purchase another mill; when both suffer equal loss, the landlord should bear it and the lease should continue. The Gemara's mention of sale is only an added circumstance. Tosafot's view nevertheless appears correct. The Gemara stresses that the landlord became wealthy and bought an animal-powered mill. A wealthy household may no longer be satisfied with hand-ground flour, either in quantity or quality. His new wealth therefore created a genuine necessity for a different mill, comparable to a sale made for a specific need where the need later disappears and the sale is rescinded. Since the original arrangement was expressly for the landlord's household food, his necessary change could cancel it unless he can sell the flour produced elsewhere. The Rosh understood that the second mill was purchased without necessity, and on that premise correctly held that the lease remains. There may therefore be no practical dispute: the Rosh and Tur would agree where wealth genuinely made the original service unsuitable. This also explains the Rambam's words, “if you do not need it, sell to others.” He speaks of a landlord who became wealthy and consequently no longer needed the original grinding, without fault on his part. Such a lease could be cancelled according to Tosafot unless he can sell the substitute output. Although the Rambam does not expressly mention cancellation, this is implicit in his formulation. The Shulchan Aruch therefore need not present the Rambam and Rosh as disputants. This appears to be the correct practical rule.
