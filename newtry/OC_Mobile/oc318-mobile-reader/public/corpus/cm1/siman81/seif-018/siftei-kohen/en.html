He admitted, even though he told them to write. In my humble opinion the Mechaber did not rule correctly here. He based himself upon what he wrote in Beit Yosef above, siman 32, new matter 8: “It is written in a responsum of the Rosh, collection 65, that one who admitted before witnesses without a kinyan, even if he told them to write a document, has made no valid admission unless he said, ‘You are my witnesses.’” In truth, the Rosh does not say this. Rather, he writes in collection 65, siman 3: “There are differing laws regarding an admission. One who admits voluntarily before witnesses may claim that he did so not to appear wealthy. If another demanded payment and he admitted, he may claim, ‘I was jesting with you.’ Therefore he must say, ‘You are my witnesses.’ The statement that an admission before two requires the instruction, ‘Write,’ means that he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ for without that there is no admission at all. Even when he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ they may not write without a kinyan, because he may not wish to become a debtor by written instrument.” The Mechaber understood the Rosh to mean that one must say, “You are my witnesses,” and that saying, “Write,” is insufficient. He further understood the phrase, “An admission before two requires the instruction, ‘Write,’” which implies that saying “Write” creates an admission, as referring to a case where he also said, “You are my witnesses.” Thus, according to the Mechaber, saying “Write” without “You are my witnesses” is ineffective. This interpretation is untenable for several reasons. First, the Rosh had not previously mentioned that the instruction “Write” is ineffective. Second, how could one suppose that saying “Write” is ineffective? Even saying merely, “You are my witnesses,” without instructing them to write a note, creates a valid admission. All the more so where he tells them to write a note. There could be no clearer designation of them as witnesses. Third, it is obvious that the witnesses may carry out his instruction and prepare the note, through which the creditor could even collect from encumbered property. How could the underlying admission itself be ineffective? Nor do we find anywhere in the Gemara or authorities that one who tells witnesses to write a note must also say, “You are my witnesses.” Everywhere the unqualified rule is that the instruction “Write” suffices. Rather, the Rosh means as follows. Since admissions are subject to differing laws, the debtor must say, “You are my witnesses,” and a bare admission is insufficient. He may have intended to exclude the position of Rabbeinu Baruch, cited by Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri at the beginning of the chapter HaNoseh, that when the witnesses are standing before us one need not say, “You are my witnesses.” Alternatively, he may have intended to exclude Rambam’s view that a complete admission is equivalent to “You are my witnesses.” According to the Rosh, the express declaration is required. The statement that an admission before two requires the instruction “Write” means that an admission before two constitutes an oral debt, while the instruction “Write” is required to make it a debt by written instrument. The implication that an admission before two is itself valid refers to one who said, “You are my witnesses.” Even according to the Rosh’s explanation in the chapter Chazakat HaBatim, that the statement “An admission before two requires the instruction ‘Write’” teaches that even before two witnesses they may not write unless instructed, the reason the Gemara speaks of two witnesses is not to imply that an admission before one witness is ineffective. Rather, it teaches that even two witnesses may not write until he says, “Write.” Still, an admission before two alone is effective as an oral debt. The Rosh therefore explains in his responsum that the phrase refers to a case where he said, “You are my witnesses,” yet the witnesses may not write until he tells them to do so. But if he expressly tells them to write, that alone suffices even without the additional words, “You are my witnesses.” I found a further clear proof in Rambam, at the end of chapter 17 of Hilchot Edut: “Whether one admitted before witnesses and used language of admission, saying, ‘This person has such-and-such in my possession,’ or said, ‘Be witnesses against me,’ they are witnesses. It is unnecessary to say that if they made a kinyan from him, or he said, ‘Write a note against me stating that this person has such-and-such in my possession,’ or used similar language, these are admissions and the witnesses may testify on their basis.” I likewise found in the rulings of Maharam of Recanati, siman 309: “The rule that an admission before two requires the words, ‘You are my witnesses,’ means that if he said only, ‘You are my witnesses,’ they may not write a note unless he also says, ‘Write.’” This is explicit as I have written, contrary to the Mechaber.
