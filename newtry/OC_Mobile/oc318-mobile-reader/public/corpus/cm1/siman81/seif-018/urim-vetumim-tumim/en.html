Even though he told them, "Write," etc. The Shach in seif katan 48 challenges this rule. Why should this not be equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses"? Moreover, the witnesses can do exactly what he instructed and write a document by which the creditor can collect from encumbered property. How can the defendant then claim that he was joking or merely seeking not to appear wealthy? This difficulty can in truth be answered by saying that the Rosh follows the Rambam's view above in siman 39 that even if a person tells witnesses to write, they must consult him again before actually writing. He could therefore say, "I was joking," or, "I was merely trying not to appear wealthy," intending to object when the witnesses returned to consult him. Yet we have found no proof for such an extension, and why should we create a new rule without clear support? One cannot prove it from the rule that the words of a dying person are considered as though written and delivered, which Tosafot in Bava Batra 175a, s.v. v'divrei shechiv mera, explain as having extraordinary force, so that it is as though he said, "Write," and Chazal greatly strengthened his words. One might ask why a dying person can nevertheless claim that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, and why, if a person could joke at the time of death, he could claim joking, which would seem to prove that even saying "write" leaves those defenses available. This can be rejected. Chazal instituted that a dying person's words have the force of writing only where an actual disposition is intended. In a context where a defense such as not wishing to appear wealthy applies, they never enacted that his words be treated as written. Thus there is no constructive "writing" in such a case. But when the person himself explicitly tells witnesses to write, from where do we derive that we should reinterpret his instruction as a joke or as an attempt not to appear wealthy? Furthermore, even if this could be said according to the Rosh, it would be because the Rosh follows Rabbenu Tam's interpretation of "I owe you one hundred by document" in the chapter HaNose, namely that the debtor actually writes a document. But the Mechaber ruled above in siman 40 like the Rambam that even an oral statement, "I owe you one hundred by document," creates liability because the very mention of a document is equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses." All the more so where he expressly says, "Write a document," since he has at minimum mentioned the concept of a document explicitly. It is therefore clear that his instruction is effective, as the Shach likewise proves from the Rambam. The matter requires study with respect to the Mechaber's contrary ruling.
