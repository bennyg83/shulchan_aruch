["There is no distinction in this regard between a poor person, etc. Darkei Moshe writes this in the name of Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Mi Shemet. In truth, nothing explicit is proven there. ["Whether he was a shechiv mera. With respect to speaking so as not to appear wealthy, the same principle applies to a shechiv mera. ["And if, when he admitted before them, the claimant was with him, he is not believed, etc. This is the Rambam's ruling. Because later authorities did not, in my view, fully understand his intent, I must explain at length why his words are clear and compelled, and I will also answer the Rosh, Mordechai, Hagahos Maimoniyos, and those who follow them. The Tur, seif 18, clearly understands the Rambam to mean that whenever the claimant was present, the debtor cannot say that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, even if he did not make the admission in a formally declarative manner. Kesef Mishnah in chapter 7 of Hilchos To'en, the Sema in seif katan 33 and 40, and other later authorities likewise understand the Rambam this way. Mahari ibn Lev, however, challenges the Tur and argues that perhaps the Rambam's later clause, 'if the claimant was with him,' refers only to the earlier case in which he admitted before two people in the formal manner of an admission rather than casual conversation. Baal HaTerumot is cited as support for that reading, and Gidulei Terumah likewise assumes it. In my view no proof follows from Baal HaTerumot, and the Tur's understanding is primary. The Rambam begins chapter 7 of Hilchos To'en by ruling: one who admits before two people that he owes another one hundred, and speaks to them by way of formal admission rather than conversation, creates testimony even if he did not say, 'You are my witnesses,' and even if the claimant was absent. If later sued and he denies the matter entirely, the witnesses establish the debt; if there is one witness, he must swear. If he then says that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, he is believed upon a heset oath. If, however, the claimant was present when he made the admission, he may not claim that he admitted only to make himself appear poor. If he claims that he later paid, he is believed with a heset oath. Several questions arise. What is the source that a statement made 'by way of admission' constitutes testimony? Why does the Rambam stress that the claimant need not be present? Why does he distinguish between a claim of jesting and a claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy, if formal admission is itself testimony? The difficulty increases because in chapter 10 of Hilchos Zechiyah the Rambam rules that a shechiv mera who says, 'I owe so and so one hundred,' does not cause payment unless he says, 'Give it to him,' because perhaps he spoke merely so that people would not say that his heirs are wealthy. Yet if he speaks in a formal manner of admission and there is no concern of evasion, payment is made even without 'Give it to him.' The Maggid Mishneh understood the Rambam's rule here as derived from the Rif at the end of Get Pashut, but that very source appears to make formal admission effective even against the claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy. The Kesef Mishnah attempts to reconcile the texts by saying that in chapter 10 the phrase 'no concern of evasion' means, for example, that the admission was made before the claimant. This is difficult, because the decisive condition is then omitted from the Rambam's formulation, and the Kesef Mishnah himself understands the Rambam here to say that the claimant's presence is effective even without formal admission. The Raavad's criticism is that the Rambam's initial distinction is weak and that the end of his ruling does not conform to the Rif. I understand the Raavad as objecting that if a formal admission is truly complete, why should it defeat a claim of jesting but not the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy? He further objects that the Rif never expressly distinguishes between an admission before the claimant and one made in his absence. But this objection can be answered from the Mishnah and Gemara in Zeh Borer. The Mishnah there, Sanhedrin 29, teaches how witnesses are examined. They are asked, 'How do you know that this person owes that person?' If a witness says, 'He told me that he owes him,' that is insufficient. Rashi explains that people sometimes say that so and so is their creditor merely so that they will not be regarded as wealthy. The testimony is sufficient only when the witness says, 'He admitted before us that he owes him.' Rashi explains that both parties were present and the debtor intended the declaration as an admission so that the listeners would be witnesses. The Gemara says that this supports Rav Yehudah in the name of Rav, who requires the debtor to say, 'You are my witnesses.' It then cites Rabbi Yochanan's ruling that when one says, 'You owe me one hundred,' and the other answers yes, but the next day says, 'I was jesting with you,' he is exempt unless he had made the witnesses formal witnesses. This structure is decisive. If the Mishnah's opening case were rejected merely because of a claim of jesting, then the Mishnah would already state exactly Rav Yehudah's law and there would be no reason to say that it merely 'supports' him, nor to add Rabbi Yochanan and a baraita as further support. Rather, the Mishnah's first clause concerns the separate claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy, because the debtor's statement was made in the claimant's absence. The later clause concerns a statement made to the claimant in the witnesses' presence. The claimant's presence removes the claim that he spoke merely so as not to appear wealthy. The Mishnah then uses the word 'admitted' to teach an additional point: although the claimant's presence defeats that claim, a mere conversational acknowledgment can still be dismissed as jesting unless it is made in a formal manner of admission or with 'You are my witnesses.' This also explains Rashi's precise language. In the opening clause he says that a person may say, 'So and so is my creditor,' in the third person, so as not to be considered wealthy. In the latter clause he says that both parties were before the witnesses and the debtor intended to admit to the claimant. Thus, one feature, the claimant's presence, removes the claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy, while the other feature, formal admission, removes the claim of jesting. The Rosh and Mordechai understand the Mishnah differently. They infer that even in the claimant's presence one may still say that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, and that the words 'he admitted before us' mean specifically that he said, 'You are my witnesses.' In my view this creates serious difficulties. The Gemara's statement that the Mishnah 'supports' Rav Yehudah then becomes circular, since on their reading the Mishnah simply states Rav Yehudah's rule. More importantly, Rav Yehudah's rule is brought in the context of jesting, while the Rosh's reading uses the Mishnah's words to defeat the separate claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy. Hagahos Maimoniyos itself recognizes the difficulty and answers that the Mishnah means that no admission is effective in any situation until 'You are my witnesses' is said. But the Gemara never establishes such a broad proposition, and the answer leaves unexplained why the Mishnah contrasts its clauses in the language that it does. The Rambam's reading is therefore clearer. The Mishnah has two independent axes. A statement in the claimant's absence is vulnerable to the claim that it was made only so that the debtor not appear wealthy. A statement in the claimant's presence is not vulnerable to that claim, even if the claimant did not first sue him and even if the statement was not made in a formal manner of admission. Separately, a casual statement can be dismissed as jesting. A formal admission, or 'You are my witnesses,' defeats the claim of jesting even if the claimant was absent. The very wording of the Mishnah supports this. Had a formal admission before witnesses in the claimant's absence been entirely ineffective, the Mishnah could have taught the stronger case directly. Instead it says, 'He told me that he owes him,' using both casual speech and the claimant's absence. In the later clause it says, 'He admitted before us that he owes him,' using both formal admission and the claimant's presence. This indicates that each element independently has legal force against one of the two defenses. We can now explain every phrase of the Rambam. His rule that formal admission before two people constitutes testimony is derived from the Mishnah's word 'admitted' and the Gemara's use of it to support Rav Yehudah. His statement that this is true even when the claimant is absent follows from the fact that the opening clause did not say that a formal admission in the claimant's absence is worthless. Thus such an admission defeats denial and jesting, though it remains open to the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy. His later rule that if the claimant was present he cannot make that latter claim is directly the distinction between the Mishnah's first and second clauses. Rashi and Bartenura both explain the Mishnah in this manner. The Rambam's placement of the clause 'if the claimant was with him' after the discussion of formal admission does not mean that the claimant's presence is effective only together with formal admission. He places it there because he is completing the Mishnah's case of a fully effective admission. The claimant's presence alone defeats the claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy, but without formal admission the debtor might still claim that he was jesting. Once both elements are present, the only remaining defense is that he later paid, and that is why the Rambam concludes, 'But if he claims that he paid, he is believed with a heset oath.' This also answers the Raavad's criticism. The Rambam's distinctions are not speculative but emerge from the Mishnah and Gemara. Indeed, the Rif at the end of Get Pashut can also be reconciled with the Rambam. The Rif's failure to restate the distinction based on the claimant's presence is unsurprising because he had already cited the Mishnah in Zeh Borer. When the Rif addresses Rava's case of a shechiv mera who said, 'I owe so and so one hundred,' the language itself indicates a statement in the claimant's absence, so the Rif necessarily explains the case through formal admission. Immediately afterward the Rif cites Rava's question whether a shechiv mera must say, 'You are my witnesses,' and concludes that a person does not jest at the time of death. That later case may readily be understood as an admission made before the claimant, or as a case where the claimant sued him, as Tosafos and other authorities explain. The Ramban in Milchamos likewise understands the Rif in that general direction. In my view, however, it is simpler to read the Rif consistently with the Mishnah: the first case is an admission in the claimant's absence, while the later question concerns an admission before the claimant. A further distinction is necessary between a healthy person and a shechiv mera. From Zeh Borer we have shown that even a formal admission by a healthy person may still be explained as having been made so as not to appear wealthy. Yet the Rif in Get Pashut and the Rambam in chapter 10 of Hilchos Zechiyah treat a formal admission by a shechiv mera as effective against that claim. The Lechem Mishneh explains that because a shechiv mera cannot plausibly be said to jest, a formal mode of admission can operate against the remaining concern of speaking so as not to appear wealthy. Alternatively, the claim of speaking so as not to enrich one's sons is intrinsically weaker than the claim that a healthy person sought not to enrich himself, and therefore formal admission is sufficient to defeat it. I would add more simply that a shechiv mera is not presumed to engage in so elaborate a formal admission merely to manipulate others' perception of his wealth, just as a person does not ordinarily jest at the time of death. Or Zarua, cited in Hagahos Ashri and Mordechai in Mi Shemet, likewise distinguishes between a shechiv mera and a healthy person regarding formal admission of a deposit. The Rivash, siman 392, writes without qualification that one who speaks by way of formal admission cannot afterward claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, citing the Rif's rule concerning a shechiv mera and arguing that a healthy person should be the same. It appears that he overlooked the Rambam and Maggid Mishneh, and also did not analyze the Mishnah and Gemara in Zeh Borer, which show that a healthy person who formally admits in the claimant's absence may still raise that defense. Nor do I accept the Maggid Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh's suggestion that the Rambam derived the entire rule of formal admission here from the Rif's discussion of a shechiv mera. The two cases are not identical: in the shechiv mera case formal admission also defeats the claim of speaking so as not to enrich the heirs, whereas for a healthy person it defeats only the claim of jesting. It is far more natural to derive the Rambam's present rule directly from the explicit Mishnah and Gemara in Zeh Borer, as explained above. Mahari ibn Lev also seeks support for his reading from Baal HaTerumot, Gate 42. In my view there is no proof. Baal HaTerumot mentions formal admission there in order to teach the greater novelty of the later clause: even if the debtor spoke in a formal manner, when the claimant was absent he may still say that he spoke only so as not to appear wealthy. Thus formal admission is not equivalent to 'You are my witnesses' for that defense. The practical conclusions are therefore as follows. One who admits a debt without 'You are my witnesses' may claim that he was jesting, whether the admission followed a demand or was voluntary. If he made the statement in a formal mode of admission, such as saying to the witnesses, 'I admit before you,' then even if the claimant was absent he cannot claim that he was jesting, but he may still claim that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. If he admitted in the claimant's presence, he cannot claim that he spoke only so as not to appear wealthy, even if the claimant had not sued him and even if he did not use a formal mode of admission. If he said, 'You are my witnesses,' he can claim neither jesting nor speaking so as not to appear wealthy. A shechiv mera who makes a formal admission cannot be said to have admitted merely so as not to enrich his heirs, even when the claimant was absent. Likewise, if a shechiv mera admitted before the claimant, even without formal language, we do not say that he was jesting, because a person does not ordinarily jest at the time of death, and his heirs must pay."]
["He is not believed, etc., rather, that he said it only so as not to appear wealthy, etc. The Sema's note printed here belongs later, on the clause that one is believed to say that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. Nimukei Yosef in Zeh Borer writes that even where the debtor's language appears designed to make himself seem poor, such as saying, 'Who has a claim against me other than so and so?' it is still not a binding admission. The printed text in the Sema should therefore read that even so it is not called an admission. Know that according to the Rashbam's explanation in Mi Shemet, Bava Batra 149a, Rava's question concerning a shechiv mera who admitted is resolved from the incident of Issur Giyora: his admission is effective, and with respect to a deposit we do not say that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. Tosafos disagree and hold that even with a deposit that concern may apply; the case of Issur Giyora is different because we know that his intent was to transfer the money to his son. Although Tosafos' reading of the Gemara appears primary and is accepted by the authorities, the Rashbam's substantive rule may nevertheless be correct regarding a deposit. Hagahos Ashri cites Rabbeinu Yitzchak as rejecting the Rashbam, but then cites Or Zarua that where the actual money is intact in another person's hands and the shechiv mera says, 'This money that I deposited with so and so belongs to Reuven,' Reuven acquires it. Or Zarua limits this to a shechiv mera. Nimukei Yosef appears to agree with the Rashbam, distinguishing between money that is intact and an ordinary debt admission. Mordechai similarly records the issue. The Bach below, siman 255, seif 5, writes that Maharam in Mordechai and Rav Hai Gaon agree with Tosafos. That is not precise, because the passages he cites address whether admission can transfer a loan or deposit, not whether the concern of speaking so as not to appear wealthy applies. It remains possible that the Rashbam and Nimukei Yosef speak only of a shechiv mera: when he admits that an intact object is a deposit belonging to another, we do not say that he merely sought not to enrich his heirs. This may be the intent of Or Zarua's statement that such an admission works specifically for a shechiv mera and not for a healthy person. Mordechai similarly writes that a healthy person who says outside the depositor's presence, 'I hold a deposit belonging to so and so,' may claim that he said so merely so as not to appear wealthy, or may claim that he was jesting, whereas a shechiv mera who dies without retracting may be treated as having admitted genuinely, especially where the deposit is intact. Nevertheless, the Rambam in chapter 9 of Hilchos Zechiyah, halacha 9, appears to follow Tosafos even regarding a shechiv mera. He writes that if a shechiv mera admits that he holds a sum belonging to another and says, 'Give it to him,' or says that a specific vessel is another's deposit and says, 'Give it to him,' the admission is effective. His repeated requirement of 'Give it to him' implies that without those words we remain concerned that he spoke only so as not to enrich his heirs, as the Rambam expressly rules in chapter 10. The Maggid Mishneh and Kesef Mishnah state this explicitly. Semag writes in accordance with the Rambam, and the same is implied by the Rambam in Hilchos Arachin, by the Mechaber below, siman 295, seif 3, by the Sema there, seif katan 9, and by the Chiddushei Ramban at the end of Bava Batra that I cite below, siman 255, seif 3. See there and note carefully. See also the responsum of Maharshadam, siman 142, where it appears that these sources were overlooked."]
["He must take a heset oath. The Sema, seif katan 36, explains that he swears simply that he owes nothing, and need not include in his oath that his earlier intention was only not to appear wealthy, because we advance that defense for him. He finds some support in the Rosh's language that, disregarding the witnesses, the only basis for an oath is the claimant's certain demand. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 3, Part 6, however, writes that although we advance for him the claim that a person may speak so as not to appear wealthy, he must swear both that he holds nothing and that he said what he said only for that purpose. This disagrees with the Sema. I found the same in Maharam of Recanati, ruling 308, who writes that whenever one claims, 'I was jesting with you,' he must swear, and similarly one who claims that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy must swear that this was his intention. If he does not himself claim jesting, that defense is not advanced for him, according to Or Zarua. The Sema's proof from the Rosh is not compelling. It is true that the original basis for imposing an oath is the claimant's certain demand, but once he must swear he may be required through gilgul shevuah to swear even concerning a doubtful matter, including whether his earlier statement was truly made for the asserted purpose. We also find several places where the Sages required a litigant to clarify language used in jesting because of concern for deception. See below, siman 138, seif 2. It appears that even the Sema's ruling follows only his own view that the defense of speaking so as not to appear wealthy is advanced for the defendant. According to what I write below, seif katan 50, that this defense is parallel to the claim of jesting in this respect, the same law applies here."]

The same applies if he says, 'I was merely jesting with you,' or even if he says nothing at all, because where the claimant himself does not assert with certainty that the defendant owes him, we advance the claim that the defendant intended only to jest, and we do not impose an oath on a mere doubt. ["For there was no demand there from the claimant himself. According to this reason, and according to what I wrote above, seif 5, seif katan 12, that the primary view is that even one who makes a voluntary admission may later claim that he was jesting, it would appear that here too he could claim jesting. Nevertheless, the ruling of the Rivash and Rama appears correct to me, though not for the reason they give. Rather, because the admission was made in response to an announcement of beit din, a claim of jesting does not apply. Ir Shushan likewise writes this. The Sema's objection in seif katan 39 is directed only at the fact that Ir Shushan first gives the reason that there was no demand from the claimant himself and then also says that an announcement of beit din is not jesting. But the substantive reason is sound. Indeed, even regarding the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, the Rivash and Rama did not need to rely on there having been some form of demand. Once the admission was made through an announcement of beit din, neither jesting nor speaking so as not to appear wealthy applies, just as those defenses do not operate against an admission before beit din, as explained above, seif 6, and below, seif 22. It further appears that even without a public announcement, if a person makes the admission to beit din through an authorized agent, such as by sending an agent to tell beit din that he owes so and so one hundred, he cannot later claim either jesting or that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy. Since a person's agent is legally equivalent to the principal, it is as though he personally admitted before beit din. The Rivash's case is different because the debtor merely instructed an agent to make the statement before witnesses, not before beit din. The Rivash proves that such an admission is ineffective absent the special reason discussed there. But if he sends an agent to beit din to make the admission, the Rivash's own reasoning establishes that it is equivalent to the principal's admission before beit din. The Rivash writes that an admission made by an appointed representative is an admission where the representative was appointed before witnesses. If the principal himself admits that he appointed the agent, the agency and resulting admission are likewise established. Accordingly, the Rama's case too must be understood as one in which the agent was sent to convey the statement before witnesses, not to beit din. If he was sent to beit din to admit that the principal owes one hundred, the Rama's additional reason is unnecessary, because the agent's admission before beit din is legally the principal's own admission and creates liability. See the Rivash there and note carefully."]
