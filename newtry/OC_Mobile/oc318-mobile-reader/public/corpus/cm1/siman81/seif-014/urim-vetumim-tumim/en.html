Whether he was a dying person. The Sema asks that below in siman 255, seif 2, the Mechaber copies the Rambam's rule: if a dying person says, "I have one hundred belonging to so and so," then if he spoke by way of formal admission and there is no concern of trickery, the money is given, and we do not there suspect that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. The Sema answers that the phrase "there is no concern of trickery" means that the admission was made in the claimant's presence. The Shach correctly objects that it is clear that according to the Rambam, when an admission is made in the claimant's presence, the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy is unavailable even if the statement was not phrased as a formal admission, as is implied by the Tur and as I wrote above. Why then would the Rambam also require the words to be spoken by way of formal admission? The Shach discusses this at length. The essence of his answer, also stated by the Lechem Mishneh, is to distinguish between a healthy person and a dying person. Therefore, in Hilchot To'en, chapter 7, the Rambam writes concerning a healthy person that even if the statement was phrased as an admission, he can say that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. But in Hilchot Zechiyah, chapter 10, the Rambam writes concerning a dying person that when he spoke by way of formal admission he cannot claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. On this basis the Shach objects to the Shulchan Aruch, which here groups together the healthy and the dying person, contradicting its own ruling below in siman 255. My understanding does not incline to creating a distinction between otherwise equal cases. In brief, the reasons suggested for distinguishing a healthy person from a dying person are independently strained, as one can see upon examination. There is also another difficulty in the Rambam. In Hilchot To'en 6:7 he records the incident involving a certain measure of saffron, where the named person came and sued the defendant, who said, "I owe him nothing." The Sages ruled that he takes a shevuat heset and is exempt. This proves that even though the defendant did not claim, "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy," we advance that defense for him. Yet at the beginning of chapter 7, where a person admitted by way of formal admission, the Rambam writes that if, when witnesses came, he claimed, "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy," he is believed. This wording requires him to make the claim himself. We do not advance it for him, and the Rambam explicitly rules that if he does not raise the claim, even if there was only one witness he must pay. Likewise, at the end of chapter 6 he writes that although concealed witnesses do not constitute testimony in the ordinary way, and similarly where a person admits on his own while witnesses overhear, or one says to his fellow before witnesses, "You have one hundred of mine," and the other answers, "Yes," in all these and similar cases, when the parties come to beit din we ask the defendant why he will not pay, etc. If he does not raise a defense, we do not advance one for him. But if he says, "I was joking," or, "It never happened," or, "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy," he is exempt upon taking a shevuat heset. This too shows that even for the latter defense he must himself raise it. Later authorities already note these apparent contradictions. See the Shach, seif katan 50. It therefore appears that the Rambam's system is as follows. The Rambam holds that speaking by way of formal admission defeats the joking defense but not necessarily the defense that one admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, while the latter defense does not apply in the claimant's presence. He derives all this from the Mishnah's language, "until the witnesses say, 'He admitted to him before us.'" The Mishnah requires the witnesses to establish two elements: first, that they heard language of admission, as indicated by "he admitted"; second, that the claimant was present, as indicated by "to him." Why are both required? They cannot both be required merely to eliminate the defense that one admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. If that were so, how could the Gemara infer from this Mishnah the separate joking defense, which is not explicitly hinted at in the Mishnah? Nor can both be required merely to eliminate joking, because joking is more plausible when the claimant is present and has made a demand than when he is altogether absent, as most authorities write. Necessarily, the two elements eliminate two separate defenses. Formal language of admission eliminates joking, while the phrase "to him" indicates the claimant's presence and eliminates the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy. The Gemara therefore correctly infers from the Mishnah that the joking defense exists. Otherwise, why would formal language of admission be necessary? This is the Rambam's clear approach, and the Shach also writes substantially the same, though at greater length and in a way that somewhat obscures the structure. See what I wrote in seif katan 24 for another proof for the Rambam from Tosafot. If so, the Rambam was troubled by the Rif's statement in the chapter Get Pashut concerning heirs who say, "Our father told us that he has one hundred belonging to so and so," that if the father did not later tell them, "I repaid," they must pay. Why do we not advance for the father the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy? The Rif answers that the father spoke by way of formal admission. But according to the Mishnah, formal admission alone does not eliminate the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy. The Rambam therefore understands the matter as follows. When a person says by way of formal admission, "I have one hundred belonging to so and so," it is highly unlikely that he was merely seeking not to appear wealthy. Such a possibility is distant and very uncommon. If the person himself raises it, however, who can extract money from him merely because his explanation is unusual? Even an unusual and remote defense can suffice if he personally asserts it. But if he does not raise it, we do not advance it for him. Indeed, even for heirs we do not advance it, although their father himself could have raised it, because it is wholly uncommon, and according to all opinions we do not advance a wholly uncommon claim for heirs. Thus the Mishnah's rule that even a formal admission can be met by the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy is correct when the defendant personally raises that defense. The Rif's rule concerning heirs is likewise correct, because if the father spoke by way of formal admission we do not advance such an unusual defense for them, and therefore they must pay. All of the Rambam's rulings now fit precisely. In the incident involving the saffron there was no formal admission at all. The defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy is therefore ordinary enough that we advance it even if the defendant does not raise it, so the Rambam rules that he is exempt without requiring him to say so. At the end of chapter 6, where the Rambam lists several distinct cases together, his concluding list of defenses cannot be understood as applying each defense to every preceding case. In the case where a person says to his fellow before witnesses, "You have one hundred of mine," and the other answers, "Yes," the defense that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy does not apply because the claimant was present. Rather, the Rambam lists the possible defenses disjunctively, each for the particular case where it is relevant, exactly as the Sema writes in seif katan 30. In the case of one who admits on his own while witnesses overhear, the wording indicates a formal admission, as the term "admits" suggests. The joking defense therefore does not apply, but the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy can apply. Since the statement was formally phrased, however, that defense is uncommon and is not advanced unless the defendant himself raises it. This is why the Rambam requires him to claim it. Likewise at the beginning of chapter 7, the Rambam explicitly states that the statement was by way of formal admission, and therefore says, "If he claimed and said that I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy," because there a personal claim is required. All is consistent. The Rambam's words are refined, rational, and founded upon the Rif, his teacher, exactly as I have written. The Mechaber's rulings are also consistent. Here the dying person himself is described as having recovered and personally raising the defense, so his unusual claim is heard. Below in siman 255 the case concerns heirs, as is clear there, and we do not advance for them a wholly uncommon defense. All is therefore well. Consider this carefully.
The claimant was with him, etc. One must examine the Gemara at the end of Bava Batra 174b. A dying person consecrated his property and then said, "I owe one hundred to so and so." He is believed because there is a chazakah that a person does not engage in collusion against consecrated property. The Gemara asks that perhaps he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. Why is this a question? Perhaps the claimant was present, in which case that defense does not apply. The same difficulty arises according to the Rosh. Perhaps the claimant actually demanded payment. It is possible to explain as follows. The rationale for saying that in the claimant's presence a person would not make a false admission merely so as not to appear wealthy is that he would fear that the claimant might later sue him. Yet perhaps the debtor trusts this particular person not to make a false claim. Ordinarily we respond with a dilemma: if he trusted the claimant, then he effectively accepted the claimant as trustworthy, and if so he must pay because he voluntarily accepted that person's reliability. Even if there was no preexisting debt, the admission itself creates an obligation once he accepts the claimant's word. That reasoning applies to the debtor's own property. But where the property has been consecrated, does he have authority to surrender the rights of consecrated property? Certainly not. The Gemara's question is therefore proper. Perhaps he did not admit as part of a collusive scheme, but only so as not to appear wealthy. Even though the claimant was present, he trusted him as an honest person who would not sue falsely. The usual response that such trust itself obligates him applies only to his own property, not to consecrated property, which he has no authority to waive. Therefore even if the claimant now demands payment, perhaps the admission was merely for appearances and the debtor trusted him. Consider this carefully. See what I write below in siman 255 at length concerning the Gemara and the poskim.
Remove the witnesses from the case, etc. The Sema infers from the Rosh's wording, "remove the witnesses from here," that the claimant's argument has no substance at all. Therefore, even through gilgul shevuah the defendant need not swear that he did not intend to make a genuine admission. The Shach cites Rabbenu Yerucham and the Piskei Recanati, who hold that such an oath can be imposed through gilgul, and he struggles to reconcile the Rosh's wording with that conclusion. None of this is necessary. I already wrote in Urim that the specific oath that the defendant intended merely not to appear wealthy is needed because otherwise, even if it is true that he did not previously owe anything, he can become obligated through his own admission. This follows the Rambam's view, as I wrote above in siman 40, that a mere oral admission can create a new obligation even where none previously existed. The Rosh, however, holds at the beginning of the chapter HaNose that a person can create a new obligation only through a written instrument, not by an oral statement, even if he said, "You are my witnesses." Therefore, once the defendant swears that he does not actually owe anything, what need is there for an additional oath whether he intended merely not to appear wealthy? He owes nothing, and his oral statement did not itself create a debt. This is simple.
For there was no demand by the litigant himself. Rivash writes this. It implies that if the claimant is an heir who says uncertainly, "Perhaps you have one hundred belonging to my father," and the defendant answers, "Yes," the defendant can likewise say, "I was joking with you." Rivash explains that the joking defense did not apply in his case because no demand was made by the actual litigant, only through a public announcement by beit din. Why did he need that reason? There the claim itself was not certain, because they did not know whether the defendant possessed any property belonging to the deceased. Even if the heir himself had made the demand, one might have thought that the joking defense would not apply. Rivash's reasoning therefore suggests that joking can apply even against an heir. This requires study as a matter of practical law, because it is difficult to understand what sort of joking is involved when the heir himself does not know the affairs of his father. Rivash may simply have stated one of two sufficient reasons. This must in any case be how his responsum, siman 392, is read. There he gives as the reason that the joking defense does not apply the fact that no demand was made by the litigant himself, only through an announcement. Yet when explaining why the defendant cannot claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, Rivash says that the statement was made by way of formal admission. The latter rationale could equally have been used to negate the joking defense, for the Rambam, who is the source of the incident, says that formal admission defeats joking, not the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy. Necessarily, Rivash simply chose one available rationale while another would also suffice. Therefore this inferred law requires study.
