So as not to make himself appear wealthy. Bava Batra asks whether a dying person who made an admission is believed. Rashbam explains the doubt as whether we suspect that he admitted only so as not to make his heirs appear wealthy. The Gemara resolves the matter from Issur the convert, whose admission was accepted. Tosafot challenges Rashbam, because in Issur's case there was no concern of impoverishing heirs, and Rabbeinu Yitzchak therefore explains the question differently: when a dying person admits that he owes a maneh, does the statement work merely as a deathbed gift, from which he may retract upon recovery, or as a complete admission like that of a healthy person? The proof from Issur is that formal admission itself can transfer ownership completely. Other authorities explain that the question is whether an admission that specific property in his possession belongs to another can transfer it merely by speech. I have written in siman 40 that acquisition through formal admission is unlike ordinary acquisitions. Witnesses are needed not merely for proof but as part of the act itself, and the formula, "You are my witnesses," is therefore required. Even if the speaker later says he genuinely intended to transfer the property, an informal statement is not yet the legal act of admission. Responsa Maharash Halevi argues from Tosafot that a dying person's ordinary admission itself creates a complete gift and therefore self impoverishment cannot be claimed. Ritva, however, explains Rabbeinu Yitzchak as speaking specifically where the dying person made a proper formal admission, with "You are my witnesses," concerning property known to be his. Then, if the factual admission cannot literally be true, the statement works as acquisition by admission. Maharash Halevi asks why Ritva requires "You are my witnesses." If the statement is really a gift, why should a donor need that formula? The answer is that acquisition by admission is not the same as an ordinary gift. Where a factual admission is possible, the litigant's own statement is accepted as one hundred witnesses. Where everyone knows the statement is factually false and it is used only as a mode of acquisition, the legal form of admission itself must be present. If the person could still say he spoke merely to impoverish himself, then there is neither true admission nor intent to transfer. This is why Beit Yosef in siman 295 says that an admission used to transfer property must be one made according to the rules of admission, so that the defense of self impoverishment and similar explanations do not remain. Issur the convert differs because the ordinary concern of not making heirs appear wealthy did not apply in the same way. His statement could therefore function as an effective admission even without the usual problem. Nevertheless, where people did not know the recipient was his son, perhaps even his act of acquisition required formal witnesses, and this remains in need of study.
