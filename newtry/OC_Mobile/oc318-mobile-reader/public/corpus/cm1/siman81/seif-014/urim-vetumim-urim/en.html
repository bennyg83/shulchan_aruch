So as not to make myself appear wealthy. Meaning that he did not want the public to regard him as wealthy, because people would then impose various burdens upon him and government authorities might also take notice.
Whether he is poor. The concern of not appearing wealthy can apply to any person, even one already regarded as poor and needy.
Whether he was a shechiv mera. The Shach, seif katan 34, argues at length with the Mechaber because below in siman 255 the Mechaber cites the Rambam that when a shechiv mera admits a debt we do not presume that he admitted merely to avoid appearing wealthy. See Tumim. I wrote that here the case is where the shechiv mera recovered from his illness and himself asserts with certainty, "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy." According to all opinions that is a valid plea. Below, by contrast, he died and we do not invent that explanation for his heirs. This resolves the entire difficulty.
Who was a shechiv mera. If he admits, "This object is a deposit," he may likewise claim that he said so merely to avoid appearing wealthy. If it is already known that a deposit came into his hands and the only possible defense would be that it was lost through unavoidable accident, and he admits that no accident occurred, whether the same explanation can apply is discussed below in siman 255 at length.
A shechiv mera, etc. If a judge wrongly removes money from the children and gives it to the claimant on the basis of such an admission, this is an error in monetary law and the judgment is reversible. So writes the Shach, and this is clear.
He is believed to claim, etc. Even if his words initially appear to indicate the opposite. For example, people told him that he was deeply in debt, and he replied, "That is false, I owe only so and so." We can still say that although he did not wish to portray himself as heavily indebted, neither did he wish to portray himself as a wealthy man with no debts at all. The Gemara says this, as cited by the Sema.
Remove this from consideration, etc. If he is independently required to swear that he owes nothing, then through gilgul he must also swear that his prior admission was made only so as not to appear wealthy. The Shach derives this from the authorities. The reason is what I wrote at the beginning of the siman: even if no true debt existed, an unqualified admission without the intent of avoiding the appearance of wealth could itself create liability.
And he claims that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, etc. This appears to prove what I wrote at the beginning of the siman, that if he claims, "I was jesting," he too may be required to swear and the matter is not treated as a purely uncertain claim because the circumstances provide grounds for suspicion. This is unlike the Shach, who equates the two defenses. If he did, the Mechaber should have stated the stronger case of jesting, where beit din does not automatically raise the defense. In the defense of avoiding wealth, by contrast, beit din generally does raise it. Where the admission was made in a formal manner of acknowledgment, concerning which I wrote that according to the Rambam beit din likewise does not automatically raise the defense, it requires study whether the claimant may impose an oath. These rules are taken from the Rosh, who does not distinguish formal admission from other admission and did not frame the case as an express formal acknowledgment. The matter requires study.
He need not. If the claimant says, "You said 'you are witnesses,'" and the witnesses are unavailable, it is obvious that he can require the defendant to swear concerning that allegation.
The plea that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, etc. See what I wrote in Tumim. Where the admission was made in a formal manner, the Rambam implies that beit din does not raise this defense automatically. This is certainly so where a shechiv mera recovered, since many authorities hold that even he personally cannot make the defense at all.
And one admitted through an agent, etc. This means before witnesses. If the admission was made before beit din, an agent is legally equivalent to the principal, so it is as though the person himself admitted in beit din, where neither the defense of jesting nor that of avoiding wealth applies, as stated in seif 22. Shach, correctly.
A claim from the opposing litigant. The Shach gives an additional reason, that the admission arose in response to a public proclamation of beit din. But if, as we explain, the agent merely admitted before witnesses, the proclamation itself is insufficient, since the court called for claims to be presented to beit din. We therefore need the reason that there was a claim originating from the opposing litigant, and the wording is precise.
Himself. See Tumim. It requires study where an heir says, "I think you owe my father a maneh," and the defendant answers, "Yes." Can he later say, "I was jesting with you," since the heir did not assert a definite claim? See there.
