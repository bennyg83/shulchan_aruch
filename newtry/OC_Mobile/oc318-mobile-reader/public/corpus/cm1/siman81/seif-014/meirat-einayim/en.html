I admitted only so that I would not appear wealthy.Meaning, I did not want to appear to be a wealthy and fully satisfied person.He is believed.
Even though his admission was made in a full form, Rambam also holds that he may explain that he admitted only so that people would not think him wealthy, as explained above in seif 8 in his name. What Rambam writes below in siman 255, seif 2, that if a person spoke by way of admission and there was no concern of trickery, the money is given accordingly, is not difficult. The phrase 'there was no concern of trickery' means that the circumstances did not allow the explanation that he admitted merely in order not to appear wealthy. Where that explanation is plausible, it remains a valid defense even against a formal admission.
There is no distinction in this regard between a poor person, etc. Meaning, even a poor person whom we would not otherwise presume to be wealthy may explain that he admitted the debt only so that people would not think him better-off than he is. By hearing that he owes others, they will regard him as even poorer. This is the intent of Nimukei Yosef, unlike the Ir Shushan, who understands the novelty to be that even a wealthy person may make this claim. That would be obvious.
Whether he was dangerously ill. Although regarding the defense of jesting it was explained above that a person does not jest at the time of death, the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy applies even to a dangerously ill person. He may have wished that people not think his sons were wealthy because their father left them a large inheritance.
If the claimant was with him, etc. Only with the defense of jesting can he say, 'Just as you came to jest with me by making your claim, I jested in my response.' But where the defense is that he admitted spontaneously only so as not to appear wealthy, he should not have made such an admission in the claimant's presence, because he had reason to fear the claimant would hear it and later sue him on the strength of witnesses. Therefore, an admission made in the claimant's presence is treated as genuine.
Only so as not to appear wealthy, etc. Nimukei Yosef writes in chapter Zeh Borer that even where his wording appears designed to make himself seem indebted, such as saying, 'Who claims against me other than so-and-so and so-and-so?' it is still not necessarily a binding admission. This follows from Sanhedrin 29b.
And regarding the oath, etc. This is an independent matter and follows all opinions. The Tur likewise presents it separately.
He must swear. Meaning, he swears that he does not owe the claimant, because the claimant now asserts with certainty that the debt exists. It appears that he need not include in the oath that his earlier admission was made only so as not to appear wealthy, since beit din itself supplies that defense for him. The wording of the Rosh also implies that his oath is required only because of the claimant's present definite claim.
We advance for him the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, etc. Although the Mechaber later records a dispute whether beit din advances this defense, the Rama rules here that it does, following the Tur's categorical ruling in siman 32 and Baal HaItur, who rules according to the opinion that the defense is advanced. The Mechaber later follows Rambam's wording and then cites the Tur's view.
And one admitted through an agent. This is the correct reading, and so it appears there in the Rivash.
A claim by the litigant himself. Specifically where the actual opposing litigant sued him can the rationale apply that he responded in jest just as the other had sued him in jest. This is also the meaning of the Tur and Mechaber below. It is not, as the Ir Shushan writes, because a proclamation of beit din cannot itself be treated as jesting. The decisive point is who made the claim, not whether beit din was involved.
