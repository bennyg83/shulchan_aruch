He did not demand from him. This is the Tur's language. Tosafos write that the claim that one admitted merely to avoid appearing wealthy applies only where no demand was made, as in the incidents in Sanhedrin. Otherwise, how could Bava Basra say that a dying person's admission does not require "you are my witnesses," etc.? Perhaps he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy.
He admitted in the manner of an admission. Rambam writes this at the beginning of chapter 4. A claim that one admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy can succeed even where he spoke in the form of a formal admission. Only the claim that he was joking is unavailable in such a case. See Rambam at the beginning and end of chapter 7. According to Rambam there is no fundamental distinction between the defense of joking and the defense of avoiding the appearance of wealth in this respect: in both cases the defendant himself must assert the defense and we do not plead it for him; and in both cases, whether he was first sued or not, he can raise the appropriate defense. The distinction is that the defense of avoiding the appearance of wealth applies only when the admission was not made in the claimant's presence, as stated above, while joking can apply whether in his presence or not. Another distinction is whether he admitted in a formal manner, as above. This is the meaning of what the Rama writes above in seif 8, "although there is no..." Meaning, although he can still claim that he admitted so as not to appear wealthy, he cannot claim that he was joking, as stated there. Even the former defense applies only if he himself asserts it. See there, and below, seif 21, and what I write in seif 15.
(Collected note) He did not demand... in the manner of an admission... As Tosafos write at the beginning of Bava Metzia: an admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. When does this apply? When he was sued and admitted. But if he admitted on his own initiative, the same mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. This must refer to a complete formal admission, because otherwise even if he was sued and admitted he could say, "I was joking with you." Rather, it concerns such a formal admission, as stated in seif 8. This implies that even if he voluntarily admitted in this manner, he can retract. From the words of Rav Hai Gaon cited by Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, who writes that if he admitted voluntarily, etc., he can say, "I was joking with you," it appears that he holds that even where no demand preceded the admission he may plead joking, and even where the admission was formally phrased, unlike a case where he was sued and then made a formal admission. (End of note.)
And there is no distinction... As stated there, where they called him "the owner of a kav of debt," and there is no greater poor person than he. See the beginning of chapter 4 of Eruvin.
Whether he was... There, regarding one who was dying, etc.; and Bava Basra 174b concerning Rav and Shmuel, etc., and a dying person who said, etc.
And if, when he admitted... This follows from the statement there that a dying person who admitted, etc., is not presumed to be joking, etc. The defense that he admitted so as not to appear wealthy nevertheless remains relevant, as above. Rather, that case must be one where the admission was made in the claimant's presence, as indicated by the expression "he admitted." This works only according to Rambam. According to Tosafos, the case is one where the claimant first demanded payment, as above.
And there is an opinion that says. This follows from the discussion in chapter HaNoseh, Kesubos 101b: "What are the circumstances? If he said..." It appears there that beit din advances the defense for him. Necessarily the defense is that he admitted so as not to appear wealthy. The admission there was made in the claimant's presence, for he said, "I owe you." The Tosefta, cited by the Rosh at the end of chapter HaManiach, states: an admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. When? When he was sued and admitted. But if he admitted on his own initiative, he may retract. Necessarily the case is where he did not say "you are my witnesses." If so, what distinguishes the first clause from the latter? According to Tosafos it is clear: the case is where the defendant himself did not make any defense. We do not plead that he was joking, but we do plead that he admitted so as not to appear wealthy. Since the Tosefta distinguishes between being sued and admitting, and voluntarily admitting, it must concern an admission in the claimant's presence. See what I write in seif 15.
