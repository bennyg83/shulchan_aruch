A person on his deathbed. Although with respect to the defense of jesting the Tur and Shulchan Aruch explain in seif 3 that a person does not jest at the time of death, with respect to the defense that he spoke so people would not think his family wealthy, we do say even of a dying person that he may have said this so that his sons would not be regarded as wealthy because their father left them a large inheritance. So writes the Sema. The Shach writes that one who ruled that the sons must pay and they did pay committed an error in a matter expressly stated in the Mishnah and the ruling is reversed. This also appears from the Rosh, and Maharshal writes it explicitly. The same is implied by all the poskim, who write simply that a person may act so his sons will not be regarded as wealthy, contrary to the opinion of one who disagrees. See below, siman 255.
To claim. The Sema writes that only regarding a claim of jesting may the debtor say, 'Just as you came to jest with me, so I jested with you.' But where he admitted without being sued and wishes to say that he did so only so people would not think him wealthy, we say that he should not have admitted in the claimant's presence, because he should have feared that the claimant would hear and sue him on the basis of the witnesses. Rather, he certainly intended a genuine admission because he truly owed him. Thus concludes the Sema. The Shach discusses this law at great length with many proofs and concludes as follows: One who admitted a debt to his fellow without saying, 'You are my witnesses,' may claim that he was jesting, whether the admission followed a demand or was volunteered. If he admitted by way of formal admission, such as saying to witnesses, 'I admit before you,' or similar language, then even if the claimant was not present he cannot claim that he was jesting, although he may claim that he spoke only so people would not think him wealthy. If he admitted in the claimant's presence, he cannot use the latter defense even if the claimant did not demand payment and even if he did not speak by way of formal admission. If he said, 'You are my witnesses,' he can claim neither jesting nor avoidance of an appearance of wealth under any circumstances. If a person on his deathbed made a formal admission, we do not say that he admitted only so people would not think him wealthy, even if he admitted outside the claimant's presence. Likewise, if he admitted in the claimant's presence, even without formal language of admission, his heirs must pay, because a person does not jest at the time of death. Thus concludes the Shach.

Although the Sema challenges him, the Sema means only that this additional reason was unnecessary, since the Ir Shushan himself also gave the reason that there had not been, and so forth. The reason itself, however, is sound. It further appears that as to the defense that he spoke only so people would not think him wealthy, we likewise do not need the reason that there was some element of demand. Once he admitted in response to a proclamation of beit din, neither the defense of jesting nor the defense of avoiding an appearance of wealth is effective, as I wrote above in seif 6 and below in seif 22. It also appears to me that even without a proclamation, if a person sent a messenger to beit din to state that he owes so and so one hundred, he cannot claim either defense, for we rule that a person's agent is like himself, and it is as though he personally admitted before beit din. The Rivash's case is different, because the debtor did not admit to beit din through a messenger. He merely told the messenger to make a statement to witnesses, and the Rivash there proves that such an admission has no force absent the other reasons he gives. According to this, the Rama's words must likewise be explained in that manner. Thus concludes the Shach. See what the Taz writes on this matter.
