He is holding money belonging to the defendant, etc. Since the person who made the admission knew this, he should not have admitted unless he truly owed him. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who writes that the case is specifically where the claimant seized the defendant’s property at the time of the demand. His reason is that once the defendant saw that the claim was not made in jest, since the claimant had taken the trouble to seize his property, he should not have admitted. See there. The Sema, seif katan 13, rejects this, and the Bach also appears to do so. At first glance, this also seems to be the implication of the Tur and the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 42. After consideration, however, the words of the Ir Shushan appear primary. What difference does it make that the defendant knew that his money was in the claimant’s possession? He can say: “I knew that you would be obligated to return what is mine when I demanded it from you. Indeed, you yourself intended to return what is mine, for otherwise why did you say to me, ‘You owe me one hundred,’ when you already hold property of mine corresponding to that amount? Rather, you knew that you would have to pay me what you owe me, and you demanded from me a different one hundred. To that demand I answered yes, but I was jesting with you. What connection does this money have with that money?” Rather, the case must be one in which the claimant seized the defendant’s property against his will on account of this debt, or where the defendant’s property was already in the claimant’s possession and, at the time of the demand, the claimant said to him, “You owe me one hundred, and I will retain what I hold of yours against this one hundred.” In that case we say that since the defendant admitted, he certainly was not jesting. This is also the meaning of the Ir Shushan, who writes: “Since he took the trouble to seize it, this proves that he was not jesting with him but was making a genuine demand. How, then, can the defendant later claim, ‘Just as you jested with me, so I jested with you’? Once he saw that the claimant exerted himself against him to seize his property against his will, he should not have admitted. On the contrary, he should have said, ‘Why are you taking what is mine? I owe you nothing.’” End of his words. This is likewise evident from the words of the Ramah and Nimukei Yosef at the end of the chapter HaManiach, which I cite below in siman 88, seif 12, seif katan 21. Necessarily, the words of the Tur and Baal HaTerumot must be interpreted this way. Although they do not mention explicitly that the claimant seized from him at the time of the demand, and the Baal HaTerumot uses the expression “if he has property of his fellow in his possession,” one can explain that the case is where the claimant said, “You owe me one hundred, and I will retain what I hold of yours against this one hundred.” So it appears to me, and examine this carefully. The Bach further writes in the name of the Baal HaTerumot that the seizure must precede the admission. This is indeed stated in the Baal HaTerumot at the beginning of Shaar 42: “If he seized, the admission is a complete admission, as we say in the case where one claimed wheat and the defendant admitted barley, etc. The seizure must precede the admission, as we wrote in Shaar 7, where we cited the view of Rabbeinu Natan bar Meir.” End of his words. This is difficult to me. There, in Shaar 7, part 2, the Baal HaTerumot holds that the reason the defendant is exempt from the barley is that since the claimant did not claim barley, it is as though he admitted that the defendant does not owe him barley. If the claimant had already seized the barley, however, he can say, “The reason I did not claim barley is that I already held it,” as the Baal HaTerumot explicitly writes there. Therefore it was appropriate for him to write there that the seizure must precede the admission, and that this was the opinion sent to him by Rabbeinu Natan bar Meir. Here, by contrast, that rationale does not apply. Rather, the reason is that because the claimant seized the defendant’s property and the defendant remained silent, the defendant was not jesting with him. If so, the same reason applies even where the seizure occurred after the admission. This is explicitly implied by the Ramah and Nimukei Yosef at the end of the chapter HaManiach, which I cite below in siman 88, seif 12, seif katan 21, and which are also cited in the Beit Yosef there. Even after the admission, if the claimant seizes while the defendant has not yet retracted his admission, the seizure is effective. This appears to me primary, and the words of the Baal HaTerumot and Bach require further study.
In Gidulei Terumah, at the beginning of Shaar 42, the author challenges the Baal HaTerumot: how can he write that seizure is effective, based on the rule concerning one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley, when the Baal HaTerumot in Shaar 7, part 2, does not accept the Raavad’s explanation that the reason the defendant is exempt from barley is because the admission was made in jest? Rather, the Baal HaTerumot explains that it is as though the claimant admitted that the defendant does not owe him barley. These are his words. But this presents no difficulty. Even though the Baal HaTerumot does not agree with the Raavad’s explanation of that passage, he nevertheless agrees with the Gemara’s answer that where the claimant seized, the seizure is effective in every respect and the opposing party cannot advance any claim against it. We therefore see that seizure removes the claim of jesting. Examine this carefully.
Nevertheless, I have already concluded below, siman 88, seif 12, that the primary view in this matter is that of the Raavad and those who agree with him. See there.
