There are those who say that if the claimant is holding the defendant's money, etc. This law requires study, as the Gidulei Terumah also writes. From where is it derived? Baal HaTerumot derives it from the rule that where one claims wheat and the defendant admits barley, the defendant is exempt from the barley, yet if the claimant seized it, he does not have to return it. This proof works according to Ramah, who holds that the reason the defendant is exempt from the value of the barley is that he can say, "I intended only to joke." It would then follow that seizure is effective even against the joking defense. But we rule like the authorities who disagree with Ramah and explain that the exemption is not based on joking, but on waiver, as I wrote above in siman 88. Seizure is effective there because the waiver does not extend to property already held by the claimant. From where, then, do we know that seizure is effective against a joking defense? The Shach seeks to answer that it is true that in the wheat and barley case the exemption is based on waiver, but once the claimant has seized, the defendant cannot force him to return it, because the claimant can say that the defendant's admission was not merely joking. This answer is not correct for two reasons. First, the case of wheat and barley concerns a claim made in beit din, as is clear below, and in beit din the joking defense does not apply, as the Mechaber writes below in seif 22. Second, this itself is the problem. Perhaps a joking defense is indeed effective. We find in the Gemara that seizure is effective only in the case of two animals pursuing one animal, where one party says that the large one caused the damage, etc., and the Gemara answers by establishing the case where the plaintiff seized. There the joking defense does not apply according to those who disagree with Ramah, because, as the Rosh explicitly writes, witnesses saw the goring but did not know which animal caused it. Otherwise there would be a difficulty according to the opinion that half damages is a fine. See there. From where, then, is this rule concerning joking derived? If it rests on logic rather than proof, Baal HaTerumot should not have cited the wheat and barley case as a proof. The matter requires study. The matter can be reconciled as follows. This law indeed does not arise from a proof, but from logic. Baal HaTerumot begins his discussion in gate 42, part 1, section 2, by writing, "It appears reasonable," etc. His principal reason is that the debtor's admission functions like a waiver, and where there is a waiver the joking defense does not apply, as explained in seif 29. The only point requiring clarification is this: because the claimant is merely holding property belonging to his fellow, perhaps waiver cannot apply. This differs from a creditor waiving a debt owed by another, where the money was originally given to the debtor to spend and therefore stands in the debtor's possession. Property merely seized by the claimant remains legally in the debtor's domain, so perhaps the debtor can still say that his admission was only a joke. Baal HaTerumot therefore brings proof from the wheat and barley case, where seizure is effective. Why is it effective if the defendant has waived the barley? We must say that property already in the claimant's hand, even only through seizure, is treated as his for purposes of this rule and is not subject to the language of waiver. Once that is established, the same logic applies to the debtor's admission here. His admission is equivalent to waiving his right to the money already held by the claimant, and the joking defense is unavailable. This seems to me the proper reconciliation. In any event, it is clear that the Tur and Mechaber did not alter Baal HaTerumot's language without reason. Baal HaTerumot writes broadly, "if he has property of his fellow in his possession," whereas the Tur and Mechaber write, "money belonging to the defendant." They hold that the rule applies specifically to money, or other property requiring no valuation. Once the defendant says, "I owe you," the claimant is thereby entitled to retain those funds, and this constitutes a complete waiver by the defendant of any right to receive that money back. But if the claimant seized objects belonging to the defendant, they still require collection procedures and appraisal and remain legally in the defendant's ownership. Even if the defendant admits owing money, the claimant has no proprietary right in the specific object, and must return it if the debt is paid in money. The language of waiver therefore does not apply to the object, and the defendant may still say, "I was joking." This appears clear to me. It is true that the wheat and barley case provides no direct proof to this distinction. According to this explanation, Baal HaTerumot and the Bach are also correct that the claimant must already be holding the money at the time of the admission. Since the entire rationale is waiver, what waiver can occur if the claimant does not yet possess anything? The Shach's objection, that seizure should be effective even if it occurs after the admission so long as the defendant has not retracted, has force only according to Ramah's view below in siman 88, which we do not follow. The correct explanation is as I have written. See below siman 88, seif 12, in the gloss. Likewise, the Sema is correct that it is enough for the claimant to be holding the money for any reason. What difference does the reason for possession make? In the end, the debtor's statement functions as a waiver. The Shach agrees with the Ir Shushan that the claimant must specifically say, "I am holding this because you owe me," but this is incorrect. The Shach himself notes that the language of Baal HaTerumot and the Tur supports the Sema. According to my explanation, since the entire rationale is waiver, it makes no difference whether the claimant seized the money for this debt or for another reason. We require only that the debtor know that the creditor is holding his money. Otherwise there is no waiver at all. Consider this carefully.
