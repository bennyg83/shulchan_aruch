Rather, only when he demanded payment from him, etc. This is what the Tur and Rosh wrote, and the Beit Yosef cited no dissenting opinion. In truth, other poskim also wrote this. Nevertheless, I am astonished that the Beit Yosef and Mechaber did not take note that the Rambam and many other major poskim disagree, and their view appears primary, as I will explain. The Rambam nowhere distinguishes by saying that the claim of jesting applies only where the claimant first demanded payment and the defendant then admitted. On the contrary, at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot Toen he writes: “Although witnesses who were concealed are not valid testimony, and likewise where a person admits on his own while witnesses hear him, and likewise where one says to his fellow in the presence of witnesses, ‘You owe me one hundred,’ and the other says, ‘Yes,’ in all these cases, etc., if he claims, ‘I was jesting with him,’ etc., and if he did not make that claim, we do not advance it for him, etc.” The Mechaber himself cites this below in seif 21. Although the Sema there, seif katan 46, strains to explain that “I was jesting” refers only to a case where the admission followed a demand, that interpretation is certainly incorrect, as I write below in seif katan 51. This is also explicitly proven by the Rambam at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Toen, where he writes: “One who admits before two people that he owes so and so one hundred, and says it to them in the manner of an admission rather than ordinary conversation, even though he did not say, ‘You are my witnesses,’ and even though the claimant is not present, this constitutes testimony, etc.” I cite this below in seif 14, seif katan 33. Likewise, the Mechaber below in seif 8 implies that if the words were spoken merely in the course of conversation, he may claim that he was jesting in every case, even where he admitted voluntarily and not in the claimant’s presence. This is also explicitly proven by the Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot Toen. Commenting on the Rambam’s ruling cited above, which the Mechaber copies below in seif 21, he writes: “Although witnesses who were concealed, etc. These words are well reasoned. Certainly, with respect to the claim ‘I was jesting with you,’ if he does not advance it himself, we do not advance it for him, and so too with other claims. This is explicit in the Gemara regarding ‘I was jesting with you.’ But the Baal HaMaor disagrees and says that if the other did not demand payment from him, but he admitted voluntarily, then even if he did not advance the claim, we advance it for him.” End of his words. We see that the Maggid Mishneh equates the claim of jesting with the claim of making the admission so as not to appear wealthy in this respect. Moreover, since he concludes, “But the Baal HaMaor, etc.,” without mentioning the claim of making oneself appear less wealthy at all, and since the Maggid Mishneh nowhere records any distinction that when one admits voluntarily he may not claim jesting but may only claim that he wished not to appear wealthy, it follows that he means that according to the Baal HaMaor we even advance the claim of jesting for him where he admitted voluntarily. This is indeed explicit in Sefer HaMaor at the end of the chapter Zeh Borer. See there. I cite it below in seif 22, seif katan 55. According to the Rambam, by contrast, we do not advance the claim for him, but if he himself claims that he was jesting, his claim is valid whether the admission came in response to a demand or was made voluntarily. This is likewise explicit in the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 42, part 1, who writes: “That which we say, that if he did not claim jesting we do not advance it for him, applies where the other demanded from him and he admitted, etc. But if he said voluntarily, ‘I owe so and so one hundred,’ whether in his presence or not in his presence, even if he did not claim, we advance it for him. Thus we find that Rav Nachman advanced for one who was called Kav Resho the claim that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, etc. The same is found in Sefer HaMaor, etc. However, regarding that which we say that we advance the claim of jesting for him if he admitted without having been asked, there are those who disagree, etc.” End of his words. This demonstrates that according to all opinions, if he himself claims that he was jesting, he is believed even where he admitted voluntarily. Gidulei Terumah, there, folio 177c, likewise writes that this is the position of the Baal HaTerumot. See also what I write below at the end of this siman, seif katan 19. This is further explicitly proven by the words of the Baal HaTerumot that I cite below in seif 15, seif katan 41, and which the Beit Yosef himself cites above at the end of siman 32. See there. The same is implied by the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 42, part 7, who writes: “One who says, ‘I owe so and so one hundred,’ and wishes to claim that it was in jest, etc.” The Beit Yosef cites this below in seif 31. The same is further implied by the Baal HaTerumot regarding one who gave profit as principal and then claims jesting, as I write below in seif 30, seif katan 76. The Baal HaItur likewise explicitly agrees with the Baal HaMaor on this point. At the beginning of folio 105 he writes: “That which we say, that if he did not say ‘You are my witnesses’ and did not say ‘I was jesting with you,’ we do not advance the claim for him and he is liable, may be said specifically where the other party demanded payment from him. But if the other did not demand payment from him, he may retract, etc.” The Raavad, too, as cited by the Baal HaTerumot in Shaar 42, part 1, in his critique of the Baal HaMaor, writes: “Avraham says: Heaven forbid that we should open such a claim for him, etc.” I cite his words below in seif 22, seif katan 25. Examine them carefully and you will find that the Raavad disagrees with the Baal HaMaor only because, Heaven forbid, we should advance the claim for him ourselves. But if the defendant himself claims it, the Raavad agrees that jesting and the claim of making oneself appear less wealthy are equal in this respect, and that even one who admitted voluntarily may claim that he was jesting. I further cite below in seif 22, seif katan 57, that this is the view of Rav Hai Gaon in Mishpetei Shevuot, as cited by the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia. This is also the view of the Ramah cited by the Rosh at the end of the chapter HaManiach. I further cite there, and in seif 15, seif katan 40, that Maharam Recanati, siman 308, explicitly writes that the Riaz ruled this way, and that Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 3, beginning of part 5, and Nimukei Yosef at the end of the chapter HaManiach likewise wrote so. See there in seif katan 55 for what I challenged in the words of Rabbeinu Yerucham and Nimukei Yosef. Below in seif 14, seif katan 34, I wrote that the same also appears from the Mordechai in the chapter Mi Shemet. The Beit Yosef also wrote in siman 32 that Rashi’s view follows the Rosh and Tur because Rashi explains “I was jesting with you” as, “I was laughing because you were asking me for something that was not so, etc.” In my humble opinion, this is not conclusive. One can say the opposite: where the claimant did not first demand payment, it is obvious that the defendant may say, “I was jesting.” Where the claimant did demand payment, however, one might think that the defendant should not have mocked him. Therefore Rashi explains that the defendant says, “Just as you jested with me, so I jested with you.” This appears to be the primary understanding of the Gemara. As I prove below in seif 14, seif katan 33, the Mishnah’s language “he admitted to him” is stated because of the claim of jesting, and there the Mishnah is speaking of one who admitted voluntarily. There is an additional proof. The Gemara in Ketubot, at the beginning of the chapter HaNose, states: “It was taught: one who says to his fellow, ‘I owe you one hundred.’ Rabbi Yochanan says he is liable; Reish Lakish says he is exempt. What is the case? If he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ what is Reish Lakish’s reason for exempting him? If he did not say, ‘You are my witnesses,’ what is Rabbi Yochanan’s reason for obligating him?” There the person states voluntarily, “I owe you one hundred,” yet if he did not say, “You are my witnesses,” he is exempt. It is forced to say that the case is one where the claimant first demanded payment and he then admitted, for the plain language does not indicate this. Moreover, if that were the case, the Gemara should not have asked so simply, “What is Rabbi Yochanan’s reason for obligating him?” Rather, because the case is one of a voluntary admission, in accordance with the plain wording, it was obvious to the Gemara that absent “You are my witnesses” there is a valid defense. The Baal HaItur at the beginning of folio 105 and the Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 42, part 1, derive an even broader rule from here, namely, that where one admits voluntarily, even if he does not himself claim that he was jesting, we advance the claim for him, because the Gemara asks, “What is Rabbi Yochanan’s reason for obligating him?” Although one could reject that broader inference by saying that the Gemara’s question means: why does Rabbi Yochanan say simply that he is liable, implying that he has no defense at all, when in truth he could claim that he was jesting? Nevertheless, their premise that the passage concerns a voluntary admission is certainly implied by the Gemara, as they write. Nor can one say that the passage there concerns the claim that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, as Tosafot there write: “If he did not say, ‘You are my witnesses,’ why does Rabbi Yochanan obligate him? He should be exempt either because he may say, ‘I was jesting with you,’ or because a person is apt to say that he owes money so as not to appear wealthy.” This cannot be the primary basis of the passage, because the fundamental rule that one must say “You are my witnesses” is stated in the chapter Zeh Borer regarding the claim of jesting. Moreover, I have already proven below in seif 14, seif katan 33, from an explicit Mishnah and from the Gemara that where the admission is made in the claimant’s presence, the claim that he wished not to appear wealthy does not apply, as the Rambam writes. Here, the statement is made directly to the claimant, “I owe you one hundred.” Necessarily, then, the Gemara asks why Rabbi Yochanan obligates him, since he can say that he was jesting.
He cannot claim that he was jesting, etc. Although he may nevertheless claim that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, as explained below in seif 21, and according to some poskim there we even advance that claim for him if he does not raise it himself, nevertheless, if he specifically claims that he was jesting, he would be liable according to this view. Alternatively, there is a practical difference according to the opinion that in the claimant’s presence one cannot claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, as explained below in seif 14, and as I conclude there in seif katan 34. Another practical difference arises where he claims, “There never was such a matter.” If he were entitled to claim that he had been jesting, he would not be established as a denier in the face of witnesses, because people do not remember idle statements, as explained above in seif 1. According to the present view, however, he is established as a denier, even though he could have claimed that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, as is explicitly proven by the Rambam at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Toen. See there. This is also proven by the Mechaber below in seif 8 and seif 14. There are many other practical differences as well. Nevertheless, I have already concluded in the preceding seif katan that even here he may claim that he was jesting.
