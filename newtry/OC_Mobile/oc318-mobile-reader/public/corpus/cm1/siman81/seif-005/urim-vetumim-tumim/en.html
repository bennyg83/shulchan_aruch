But if the claimant did not demand payment, etc. The Shach questions this rule from the chapter HaNose, where the Gemara says: If he did not say, "You are my witnesses," what is Rabbi Yochanan's reason? What is the difficulty? Perhaps the case concerns one who was not sued, and in that situation the admission would be binding. Tosafot there, s.v. i, already write that the defendant should be exempt either by the joking defense or by the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. Their intent is that whichever way the facts are understood, if he was not sued he has the latter defense, and if he was sued he has the joking defense. But all of Tosafot's discussion there follows Rashi's interpretation at the initial stage, before they adopted Rabbenu Tam's explanation, and assumes that the defendant actually acknowledges the underlying debt while the claimant says that the debt truly exists. According to Rabbenu Tam, however, one can say that the Gemara already understood that the dispute between Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish concerns a case where the claimant admits that no underlying debt existed, as Rabbenu Tam explains. In such a case, according to all opinions, as I wrote above and as the Shach writes in seif katan 6, the defendant may say, "I intended only to jest," even if no demand had been made, since everyone agrees that he never owed the money. The Gemara therefore properly asks its question, and there is no difficulty. Nevertheless, as a matter of practical law the Shach correctly notes that many authorities disagree with the Mechaber's distinction, and the defendant can say kim li in accordance with them.
