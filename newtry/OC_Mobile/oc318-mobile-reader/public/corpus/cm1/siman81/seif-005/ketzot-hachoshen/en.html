He admitted on his own. The Mechaber holds that one who volunteers an admission cannot afterward say he was joking, because the explanation, "Just as you joked with me, so I joked with you," is unavailable. This appears correct. Whenever one seeks to withdraw from earlier words, he must give a meaningful explanation. Merely saying, "I said it as a joke," is not a sufficient explanation. Rambam rules concerning a woman who tells her husband, "I am forbidden to you," and later says, "I was joking," that she is not believed. She must supply a concrete reason, such as that she said so because his sister or mother was present. Rambam similarly requires a substantial explanation when a woman retracts from saying she was betrothed. Thus, even in prohibitory law, mere joking does not undo an unequivocal statement. All the more so in monetary law. A self impoverishment explanation is different because it is a recognized and plausible motive: the person wished others not to regard him as wealthy. Joking works only where there is an explanation for why he joked, such as, "Just as you were making a baseless claim against me, I responded in kind." The Shach argues that even a volunteered admission can sometimes be explained as joking and cites the opening of Perek HaNosei. That proof depends on interpreting the sugya as an admission. Rambam and the Mechaber understand it instead as creation of a new obligation. A new obligation certainly requires a formal legal act such as "You are my witnesses," even where no joking issue exists. Therefore the sugya does not prove that a volunteered factual admission can be withdrawn by a bare claim of joking. The Mechaber's rule remains primary.
He cannot claim, "I was joking." The Shach notes that he may still claim that he spoke so as not to make himself appear wealthy, and some authorities even advance that defense for him. He says that if he instead claims joking, he is liable, and also that if he later says, "It never happened," he may become established as a false denier even though a self impoverishment defense existed. In my view a statement made merely so as not to appear wealthy is also a form of idle speech that people may forget. A person who uses such language should not have to record in a notebook that he once spoke falsely to avoid appearing rich lest he later deny the statement and thereby become liable. There is no reason to distinguish ordinary joking from self impoverishment when neither involved a formal admission. Rambam's rule concerning one established as a false denier after a complete formal admission concerns an intentional judicial act, something a person remembers. The same distinction appears in siman 70 regarding whether witnesses were specially designated for repayment. Where the circumstance was specially arranged, it is something that rests on the person's mind and he remembers it; casual conversation is not. Therefore, if there was no complete formal admission and the earlier words can be explained as self impoverishment, a later total denial does not make him a false denier. The Sema's wording supports this. Only where the admission was formal and deliberate is the person expected to remember it.
