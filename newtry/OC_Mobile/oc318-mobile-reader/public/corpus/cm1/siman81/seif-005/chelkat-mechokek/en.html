A woman who says, "My hands shall be consecrated," etc. Meaning that she will thereby become prohibited from returning to him. According to Ran, both elements are required. If she merely says, "Konam what I produce for your mouth," meaning that whatever I produce shall be prohibited to you like consecrated property, she cannot consecrate it before it exists, because the handiwork has not yet come into the world. Likewise, if she uses consecration language and says, "My hands shall be consecrated for what they produce," since the consecration cannot take effect now, it also cannot take effect automatically after divorce. This resembles one who says, "This field shall become consecrated when I acquire it," which is ineffective because acquisition is not currently in his power. Here too, divorce is not in her power. We must therefore say that she did not consecrate her hands after divorce to the entire world with full bodily consecration that has no redemption. Such a consecration would have been fit to take effect immediately, except that the Sages strengthened the husband's lien while she remains married. After divorce, the konam takes effect even though she was not yet divorced when she made the vow. See Yoreh Deah 234, where Beit Yosef writes that the case is one in which she says, "My hands shall be consecrated for what they produce for your mouth," meaning that her hands are forbidden to him like consecrated property. Rambam's view, however, is not so. In his view a konam alone can prohibit something not yet in existence because there is no redemption, and if she says, "My hands shall be consecrated for what they produce," that is bodily consecration of her hands. The consecration can therefore rest upon them at least from the time she is divorced, and then her handiwork is forbidden to the entire world. It appears that according to Rambam, just as if a husband says to his wife, "Your hands shall be consecrated for what they produce," the consecration is effective, so too if he says, "Konam your handiwork upon me," it takes effect even before she produces it, provided he supports her. For according to Rambam a konam alone can prohibit something not yet in existence.
