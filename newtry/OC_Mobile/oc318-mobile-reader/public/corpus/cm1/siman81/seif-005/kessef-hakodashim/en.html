Seif 23 in the gloss. (And I erred in my admission.) One person received from another small bank notes customarily exchanged for a single note of five hundred reinish. The small notes were counted with great care in the presence of three people. One witness testified that there were five rows, each counted as one hundred, except that in the fifth row there were thirty-seven notes of two reinish each and the remainder were one-reinish notes. A second witness likewise testified that he distinctly heard them count those thirty-seven notes and the remainder as completing one hundred reinish. The recipient remained for a considerable time in his house while obtaining a five-hundred-reinish note. He then returned and claimed that there had been an error of one hundred reinish, displaying the notes in his possession and asserting that they totaled only four hundred. The same three people had remained in the house of the giver from beginning to end. The recipient claimed that the mistake occurred in counting the thirty-seven notes and the remaining notes mentioned above as two hundred reinish. I found no liability on the giver. The witnesses established that the alleged error did not occur in the manner in which the recipient claimed. The giver stated with certainty that there had been no error. From the accounting of his own money he likewise knew that there was no error, and he presented the accounting before beit din. Moreover, according to our ruling in siman 81 and siman 126, seif 13, a person is not believed to claim an error in a prior admission except where he has a migo. Here he had no migo, because the witnesses had never left the scene until he claimed error, and it was certain that he had not already paid the missing amount, under the known presumption that payment has not been made. What I wrote earlier at the end of siman 75 concerning a case of error where one party is certain and the other uncertain is different, because there no migo is required since the certain claim is superior. I also saw in the Mordechai, chapter Chezkas HaBatim, siman 526, that one is not believed to say "I erred" even where he has a migo, because a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses and the migo stands against witnesses. That reasoning fits our present case, where there was an unequivocal admission of liability for five hundred. It does not fit the earlier case at the end of siman 75, where one could say that the party simply relied upon the purchaser's calculation and made no formal admission. Even if he spoke, his words were not phrased as an admission but in a manner substantially removed from admission, as anyone capable of analyzing speech in context understands. Here, by contrast, the exchange involved an absolute acknowledgment of a liability of five hundred. The conclusion of the Shach, however, is that a claim of error is effective where supported by a migo, and therefore in an appropriate case one may always claim "I erred" through a valid migo.
