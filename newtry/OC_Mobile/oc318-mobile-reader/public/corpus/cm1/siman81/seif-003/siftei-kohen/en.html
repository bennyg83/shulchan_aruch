But if he did not claim. Meaning, if he did not claim, “I was jesting,” then even if he says, “I owe him nothing,” he is liable once it is true that he made the admission. The Mordechai in Zeh Borer writes: “Rav Saadiah Gaon ruled that he must swear that he intended to jest. Rabbenu Yitzchak ruled that if he does not advance the claim, beit din does not advance it for him.” The Beit Yosef, above at the beginning of siman 32, cites a reading that if he does not make the claim, beit din does advance it for him, and explains that this means: if the claimant does not demand that he swear that he intended to jest, beit din itself requires the oath. It appears that the Beit Yosef was troubled by the wording in our editions of the Mordechai. Why would Rabbenu Yitzchak need to rule that if the defendant does not claim jesting, beit din does not advance it, when this is an explicit Gemara? He therefore emended the text to read that beit din does advance it, and interpreted it as referring to the oath. The Darchei Moshe above, siman 32, followed him, and the Sema here, seif katan 3, likewise writes that this is the principal reading, contrary to our editions of the Mordechai. Their words are incorrect. Why should we assume that beit din advances a demand that he swear in this case more than in every other oath? Moreover, what could the phrase “beit din advances it for him” mean in that interpretation? The Bach raises the same objection above, siman 32. The Piskei Maharam of Rothenburg, siman 937, likewise reads: “Rabbenu Saadiah ruled that he must swear that he jested with him; but if he does not claim that he was jesting, beit din does not advance it for him.” Furthermore, old manuscripts of the Mordechai read: “Rabbenu Yitzchak ruled that if he does not advance the claim, beit din does not advance it for him; but the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy is advanced for him even if he does not claim it.” The Agudah has the same reading. The Bach’s explanation above, at the end of siman 32, that the passage comes to teach that beit din advances the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, is also unconvincing. The wording does not imply this. Moreover, immediately afterward the Mordechai and the Agudah cite Maharam’s responsum concerning Reuven who said to his son-in-law, “Study with your son,” where beit din advances the claim of jesting for him, even though Rabbenu Yitzchak explained in Zeh Borer that if he does not claim it, beit din does not advance it. Maharam explains that the case is different. This passage also refutes the Beit Yosef and those who followed him, who emended the Mordechai because the Gemara expressly states that beit din does not advance the claim. How then would they explain Maharam’s words cited immediately afterward? It appears to me that there was never a difficulty. The Gemara says only that if he did not claim, beit din does not advance the matter for him. One might have understood this merely to contrast monetary law with capital law, where beit din supplies arguments even without a claim. Thus, perhaps some claim was required, but it would suffice either to say, “I was jesting,” or simply, “I do not owe him.” The baraita itself does not state that beit din does not advance the claim of jesting. The Gemara infers it from the statement that arguments are not advanced for an inciter, and asks why an inciter was mentioned. It answers that the teaching is incomplete and should read: if he made no claim, beit din does not make one for him; in capital cases, even if he made no claim, beit din makes one for him, except for an inciter. It is therefore possible that the Gemara means only that where he makes no argument at all, beit din supplies none, unlike capital cases. Rabbenu Yitzchak accordingly explains that he must specifically claim, “I was jesting”; it is not enough merely to claim that he does not owe the money. This is necessarily the case where he says, “I do not owe him,” like an ordinary borrower. This appears entirely clear.
