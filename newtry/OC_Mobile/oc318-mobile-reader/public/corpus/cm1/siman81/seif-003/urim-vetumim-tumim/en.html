But if he did not raise the claim, etc. The Mordechai cites Ri as ruling that if the defendant does not himself raise the joking defense, we do not advance it for him. The Mordechai later repeats this in the case discussed above in seif 1, where a person said to his wealthy son in law, "Study with your son," and the defendant may say that he intended only to joke. The Mordechai adds that although Ri ruled that if the person does not raise the claim we do not advance it for him, etc. The Beit Yosef asks that this is an explicit Gemara and baraita concerning the joking defense, namely that if the defendant does not raise it, we do not advance it. The Beit Yosef answers that Ri's ruling means that if the defendant does not ask, "Make him swear," we do not advance for him the demand for an oath. The Shach correctly objects that this is not the implication of the Mordechai. Moreover, in the case of "Study with your son," the Mordechai again cites Ri that we do not advance the defense, and the Beit Yosef's answer has no application there. The Shach's own answer is extremely strained and needs no refutation. It therefore appears either that Ri's statement comes to exclude the view of Baal HaMaor, who holds that where the debtor admitted without being sued, we advance the joking defense for him. Ri writes that in every situation we do not advance it, and therefore in the case of "Study with your son," where there was no demand for payment, Baal HaMaor would say that we advance the defense, whereas according to Ri we do not advance it anywhere, even absent a demand. This is satisfactory. An even better explanation is based on the wording of the Gemara's baraita: "You have one hundred of mine in your possession." The defendant says, "Yes." The next day the claimant says, "Give it to me," and the defendant answers, "I was joking with you," and is exempt. The baraita continues: "Moreover, even if he concealed witnesses behind a fence and said, 'You have one hundred of mine,' and the defendant said, 'Yes,' and then asked, 'Do you wish to admit before so and so and so and so?' and the defendant answered, 'I am afraid that you will force me into litigation,' the next day when the claimant says, 'Give it to me,' the defendant may answer, 'I was joking with you,' and is exempt. If he does not raise the defense, we do not advance it for him," etc. At first glance, why does the baraita not state the rule that we do not advance the defense immediately after the first clause, before introducing the concealed witnesses? The question of Baal HaMaor also arises, since the Mishnah simply says that the witness's statement means nothing, which could imply that in the ordinary case where joking is common we do advance the defense, just as we advance the defense that the person admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. Perhaps the answer is that in the concealed witness case, where the claimant says, "Do you wish to admit before so and so and so and so?" and the defendant gives a specific explanation that he fears being forced into court, it is especially uncommon that he would continue repeatedly to joke in this manner. Therefore only if he personally says, "I was joking," do we accept the defense, but we do not advance it for him because such conduct is very uncommon. This would resolve all of Baal HaMaor's questions and make the entire sugya clear. Ri is therefore cited to teach that he does not accept this distinction. In his view the final statement, "If he does not raise the defense, we do not advance it for him," refers to the entire baraita, both the first and second clauses. The baraita simply waited until completing the full discussion before stating the general rule. Ri therefore teaches a significant novelty, and there is no difficulty.
