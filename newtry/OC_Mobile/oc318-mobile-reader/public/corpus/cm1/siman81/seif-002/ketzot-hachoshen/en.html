For a person does not joke. Beit Yosef writes that this remains so even if the sick person later recovers and sues, because one does not joke at the time of death. The Shach disagrees and says only heirs are not advanced the joking defense; the recovered person himself may say, "I was joking." At first sight Beit Yosef seems compelled by Raavyah's ruling concerning a widow who, while dying, instructed her son to give seven zekukim to another and the son remained silent. Afterward the son claimed his mother had not sworn on her ketubah. Raavyah held that because she instructed him in his presence and he was silent, it was as though he admitted she had those seven zekukim, and he adds that just as a person does not joke at the time of death, one does not joke with a dying person. The son was alive and yet could not use the joking defense. This appears to support Beit Yosef. However, as I explain below, Raavyah's real basis may be different. Therefore this proof is not conclusive.
They remained silent at her instruction. Raavyah's case concerns a dying woman who instructed her son to give seven zekukim of her property to another. The son remained silent and, after her death, claimed she had never sworn concerning her ketubah. Raavyah says that because she gave the instruction in his presence and he remained silent, it is as though he admitted she had seven zekukim of her own in his possession, however she had acquired them. Taz asks from siman 7, where silence from beginning to end is not treated as admission. The Shach asks from Even HaEzer siman 106 concerning division of property. Mishneh LaMelech distinguishes that in the other cases the silence concerns the body of money itself, whereas here the property already stood in her possession and the only issue was a rabbinic oath. Taz in Even HaEzer makes a similar distinction. In my view this distinction cannot be the whole answer. If the son's silence were a true admission that the ketubah had not been paid, there would be no need for Raavyah's statement that one does not joke at the time of death. Where a creditor actually holds a document and the debtor admits it is unpaid, joking and self impoverishment do not apply. Tosafot at the end of Bava Batra says that one does not make such admissions when the creditor has a document because the public would naturally believe the document remains unpaid. The same is true of a ketubah, which exists by condition of beit din even if no paper is physically held. Rather, Raavyah must mean that the son admitted a separate fact, namely that his mother had seven zekukim of her own in his possession, perhaps from a find or savings. That is a distinct debt or deposit claim against the son, not a mere waiver of the ketubah oath. It therefore normally would require a formal admission. Once understood this way, the distinctions suggested by Taz disappear, because the case is no longer about silence regarding the ketubah at all. See Responsa Maharit for further discussion.
