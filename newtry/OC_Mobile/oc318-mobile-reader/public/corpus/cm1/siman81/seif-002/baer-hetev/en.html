They remained silent. Darkei Moshe cites Mordechai concerning a woman who instructed her son to give seven zekukim from her property to her relatives. The son remained silent, and after her death claimed that she had never sworn concerning her ketubah and that he remained silent only so as not to anger her. Mordechai ruled that silence at the time of death is an admission, and this applies even if there were no witnesses to the will but the son himself admitted that this is what occurred. The Taz challenges this ruling and writes that money should not be extracted on the basis of this decision. See there. The Shach writes that the reason he is not believed by virtue of a migo appears to be that this is a migo against witnesses, for we are as certain as witnesses that his silence constituted an admission. It follows that wherever there is an effective admission, such as where he said, 'You are my witnesses,' even if the witnesses are not now present and he admits that he made the admission before them but claims that he actually owes nothing, he must pay and is not believed by virtue of a migo. Since he certainly admitted, he certainly owes. This also appears to me from what the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in siman 75, seif 17, where the claimant says, 'You yourself admitted to me,' and so forth. Their language explicitly implies this. See there. Thus concludes the Shach. See also the beginning of siman 98 and Even HaEzer at the end of siman 106.
