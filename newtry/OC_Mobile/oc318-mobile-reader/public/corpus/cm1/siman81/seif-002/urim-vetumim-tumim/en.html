A dying person, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that even if the dying person recovers, he cannot claim, "I was joking," and must pay. The Shach objects that perhaps we do not advance the joking defense for heirs because it is an entirely uncommon claim, but if the person himself recovers and asserts with certainty, "I intended to joke," why should he not be believed? Otherwise, Tosafot and the poskim's proof that although we do not advance the joking defense for an ordinary litigant, we do advance it for heirs would fail. Their proof is that otherwise what practical difference would there be in the Gemara's question whether a person jokes at the time of death? According to the Beit Yosef, however, one could say that the question concerns a dying person who recovers and himself claims that he intended to joke. These are the Shach's words. I am astonished that he overlooked Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, beginning of part 2, who writes: "Where a dying person admitted a debt, he need not say, 'You are my witnesses,' as we learn in the chapter Get Pashut: Rava asked whether a dying person who admits, etc., and we conclude that a person does not joke at the time of death, and he need not say, 'You are my witnesses,' etc. This is the halachah. Likewise, a dying person who admitted cannot retract if he recovers, as stated in Get Pashut, because the statement is not treated as a gift but as an admission, and he cannot retract even though he did not say, 'You are my witnesses.'" Thus Baal HaTerumot states without qualification that the dying person need not say, "You are my witnesses," and cannot retract if he recovers. The obvious difficulty is that he could say, "I intended to joke." This proves that he cannot make such a claim. If the law were as the Shach writes, how could Baal HaTerumot state simply that he cannot retract, when he could entirely nullify his admission by saying, "I spoke in jest"? The Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Mi Shemet likewise explains the question concerning a dying person who admitted as whether he can claim that he intended merely not to appear wealthy, and the Gemara resolves that if he recovers he cannot retract. This likewise proves that we do not entertain a joking defense. The Ramban similarly writes in Milchamot at the end of Bava Batra that if they claim that he was joking, we do not listen to them. This necessarily refers to a certain claim, because if they merely asserted uncertainty, the Ramban should have said that we do not advance the claim for them, rather than "if they claim." His intent is that even if they assert it with certainty, they are not believed. The same applies to the dying person himself if he recovers. This resolves a difficulty raised by later authorities, which the Shach records in several places, according to the opinions that even where the creditor demanded payment the debtor can claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. See the Shach, seif katan 41. If so, what is the practical difference in the Gemara's question whether a dying person jokes at the time of death? We would in any event advance for the heirs the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, and if he said, "You are my witnesses," then the joking defense is likewise unavailable. According to what I wrote, the difference is clear. The question concerns a dying person who later recovers and says, "I intended to joke." If a person does not joke at the time of death, he is not believed even though he has a migo that he could have claimed that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, because that would be a migo against a chazakah. If a person does joke at the time of death, he is believed. Another difference arises where he denies the admission entirely. With respect to joking, such words are treated as idle remarks that people do not remember, whereas with respect to the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, he would be established as a denier, as I wrote above. All is therefore correct. The Hagahot Maimoniyot already preceded the Shach in raising his question. See there for the answer. One can also answer as follows. This person is a dying person and at every moment is prepared for death. If we were to assume that if he dies his heirs would have to pay and we would not advance for them the joking defense, how could one possibly think that he intended to make such a joke? A person would not engage in such foolishness when he might die suddenly and the claimant would then take money from his heirs solely on the strength of his statement, leaving them to suffer without any legal justification. How could he joke in that manner? Accordingly, even if he recovers and says that he intended to joke, he is not believed at all. Why would he joke while standing at the threshold of the grave, knowing that his sons might have to pay? Necessarily, we do advance the joking defense for his children. Once that is so, it becomes plausible that he himself may have joked, because even if he died his children would not pay. This also follows from Raavyah, cited in the Hagahot, who writes that if other people admitted to the dying person, the same rule applies and we say that a person does not joke in such circumstances, deriving it from the case of a dying person who admits. If the rule were merely that joking is an uncommon claim which we do not advance for heirs, as the Shach maintains, this comparison would be difficult. Those other people are alive and can explicitly say, "We intended to joke." Why should their position be weaker than that of the dying person himself who recovered? Necessarily, the admission is treated like saying, "You are my witnesses." Raavyah indeed states explicitly that it is equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses," after which there is no room for a joking defense. This is clear.
And the same applies if others admitted to him, etc. The Mordechai there makes clear that this applies even if no witnesses were present at the time of the admission, provided that the person later admitted that this is what occurred. The Shach writes: It appears to me that he has no migo to say, "I do not owe him," because that would be a migo against witnesses. We can therefore learn from here that if one is sued, "You admitted before witnesses and said, 'You are my witnesses,'" and he acknowledges that this occurred, he is not believed by virtue of a migo to say that he does not owe anything, because that is a migo against witnesses. I do not understand the Shach's words. We rule that if a person says, "I owe you one hundred," with the formula, "You are my witnesses," then even if both parties agree that he never previously owed anything, he nevertheless becomes obligated, as explained above in siman 40. Likewise, a dying person becomes obligated even without saying, "You are my witnesses," because a person does not joke at the time of death. Raavyah writes that a dying person's statement is equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses." What claim, then, is there in saying that he owes nothing? Even if that were true, the admission itself creates the obligation. If the Shach means that the defendant could say, "It is true that I said, 'You are my witnesses,' but I nevertheless intended only to joke," that requires no discussion at all, because once he said, "You are my witnesses," the joking defense is unavailable. This does not fit the Shach's wording or apparent intent, and his words require study.
Or they remained silent at his instructions. The Shach asks that we rule that if a person writes all his property to others while his creditor hears and remains silent, the creditor does not lose his debt, and that only with respect to a ketubah did Chazal apply such a leniency, as stated in Even HaEzer siman 106. The Shach answers that there the existence of the debt is certain, and the only possibility is that the creditor waived it. We do not treat silence as an admission so as to create a waiver. Here, however, the doubt is whether the debt existed in the first place, and therefore silence is treated as an admission that the debt certainly exists. The Beit Shmuel there, seif katan 8, cites an answer in the name of the Taz that the Mordechai's ruling, treating silence as an admission, concerned a case in which the son's entire claim against his mother's instruction to give seven zekukim was that she had not sworn concerning her ketubah. We therefore say that she presumably waived the oath, since she never required one. These words are strained in the Mordechai and contradict the Rama's ruling here, which does not present the issue as one concerning an oath at all. Rather, the plain meaning is that the son becomes obligated through his silence just as through an actual admission.
According to the Shach's answer, if a person says in his creditor's presence, "All notes against me are presumed paid," and the creditor remains silent, the creditor loses his right, because we say that his debt too was certainly paid. However, another distinction can be drawn. If a person is merely disposing of his own property and makes no demand upon his fellow, the fellow's silence means nothing. He can say, "What do I care about his statement? Let him say whatever he wishes. I pay no attention to it." But if he says to his fellow, "You have one hundred of mine in your possession. Give it to me, or give it to so and so on my behalf," and the person remains silent, his silence is an admission, because he should have responded, "Why should I pay? I have nothing of yours." In the Mordechai's case, the mother instructed her son to give seven zekukim from property of hers that was in his hands. The son remained silent and later claimed that she had nothing in his hands because she had not sworn concerning her ketubah. The Mordechai writes that because he remained silent, this is an admission that he indeed possessed seven zekukim belonging to his mother, regardless of the precise legal basis. See there. It is clear that she was demanding payment from the son out of property of hers in his hands. In such a case he should not have remained silent, because a demand was directed to him. But if she had not instructed him to give from her property in his hands and had merely made a general statement, his silence would mean nothing. All is therefore well. According to what I wrote, the question of Maharam Alashkar in responsum 152 from Bava Metzia 6a, Tosafot s.v. hikdisha, is resolved. There the bathhouse is disputed, each party claiming ownership, and one party consecrates it while the other remains silent. The conclusion is that the consecration is ineffective, because silence is not treated as an admission. In that case the first party was not demanding anything from his fellow. He merely consecrated property that he claimed was his own. The other's silence means nothing, because he can say, "What do I care about his words? They are empty speech." Here, by contrast, the claimant is making an express demand, "You have one hundred of mine. Pay it to me, or to so and so on my behalf." Silence in response is an admission. This point is supported by Bava Metzia 37a, where five people demand payment and each says, "You robbed me." The Gemara treats silence as an admission. There too a direct claim is being made, unless one distinguishes that the latter case occurs in beit din, where silence is a stronger admission. See what I wrote above at the end of siman 80. Even without this distinction, Maharam Alashkar's question can be rejected for another reason. In the bathhouse case the parties had already disputed, one saying, "It is mine," and the other likewise saying, "It is mine." The silent party can therefore say that he relied upon his previously stated position. Must he repeat his claim endlessly like a peddler? Here, however, the defendant never previously indicated that the claimant's statement was false, so his silence can properly be treated as an admission. There is, however, still a difficulty. The Mordechai's entire basis is that a dying person's statement is equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses," because a person does not joke at the time of death. In the chapter Zeh Borer the Gemara says regarding the formula, "You are my witnesses," that there is no difference whether the lender says it and the borrower remains silent, or the borrower says it and the lender remains silent, the borrower becomes obligated. Apparently, the Mordechai understands that if the lender says, "You are my witnesses that so and so owes me one hundred," and the borrower remains silent from beginning to end, his silence is considered an admission. If so, the same should apply to a dying person, whose words are equivalent to that formula. But according to the Tur and Mechaber, following Baal HaTerumot in the name of the earlier authorities, the rule is different. Only where the borrower first says "yes," and then remains silent when the lender says, "You are my witnesses," is the silence treated as an admission. If he remains silent from beginning to end, his silence is not an admission even when the lender expressly says, "You are my witnesses." If so, a dying person's statement, where he does not literally use that formula, should certainly be no stronger. There is even some reason to say that the listener remains silent simply so as not to anger the dying person. Why then should the son or another person become obligated through silence? Although one can distinguish the cases, what is the source for doing so when the Mordechai himself derived the rule from the borrower's silence, and there an initial "yes" is required? This therefore requires study as a matter of practical law. In any event, Responsa Mahari Ashkenazi, cited above, distinguishes between a dying person who speaks directly to his son and the son remains silent, which is treated as an admission, and a dying person who says to witnesses, "My son owes me one hundred," while the son remains silent, which is not an admission. Although the proof offered there can be rejected, as I wrote above, and there is little logical basis for the distinction, as appears from the Shulchan Aruch, the entire law in any event requires study. I remember that when my wife's grandfather, the distinguished and exceptional Rav Michel Shapira, of blessed memory, died, he instructed witnesses before his death, in the presence of his heirs, that he owed no person even one perutah. The heirs remained silent. After his death some of the heirs produced a promissory note against my father in law, the above Rav. Expert rabbis of that time sat in judgment over this matter, and I discussed with them whether the heirs' silence at the dying man's instruction should be treated as an admission that the debt represented by the note had already been paid or was merely a trust document. After considerable deliberation, legal analysis, and examination of the books, the conclusion was that the promissory note could not be invalidated on that basis. The heirs remained believed upon taking the oath required of one who seeks payment from the property of heirs.
