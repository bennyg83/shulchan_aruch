(Shach, seif katan 60) The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe abbreviated here, etc. Note: According to Darkei Moshe's understanding of the Mordechai, ruling 524, the reason one is not believed to say "I erred" by a migo that he could have denied everything is not that a claim of error is inherently weak and we are certain he lies. Rather, every admission of a litigant has the force of witnesses, and just as a litigant cannot say that witnesses who testified against him erred, even where he has a migo, so he cannot say that his own admission was mistaken. A migo against witnesses is ineffective. This, however, rests on the ordinary presumption that people do not make mistaken admissions. Where there is reason to say that people commonly err in such a matter, the migo would not be defeated as a migo against witnesses, for the witnesses themselves would also be believed to say they erred in a matter in which error is common, even without a migo, as explained in the laws of testimony and in the Ran at the beginning of Rosh HaShanah. According to this, the Rif's proof is that "I erred" is not an accepted claim, for otherwise every document could be defeated by saying "I erred." Since it is not an accepted claim, an ordinary admission stands like one hundred witnesses in a matter where people are not accustomed to err, and even a migo cannot overturn it. This requires study regarding the Rama. Here his wording follows Maharam's principle that a person's oral admission has the force of witnesses. Yet in siman 126:13 he appears not to rely on that rationale, but rather on the presumption that one does not err. See also above, siman 79:3, where the Shulchan Aruch rules like the Rambam that one who admitted in beit din that he owed money and later says that he remembered having paid, and produces witnesses of payment, is believed. Baal HaTerumot, gate 11, cites Ri Migash as holding that he is not believed to say "I remembered," but after citing the Rambam he writes that the Rambam's view is more reasonable and then attempts to reconcile Ri Migash. He distinguishes the case in Gittin from one where a person is sued in beit din for one hundred, answers "yes," and afterward produces witnesses that he paid. Such a person is liable because there are no witnesses showing that his present admission of error is true, and once he paid he would ordinarily remember the payment. Thus Baal HaTerumot treats payment as a matter that rests upon a person's awareness and is not ordinarily forgotten. According to Maharam's general rule that every oral admission is like testimony that cannot later be retracted even through an excuse concerning a matter not ordinarily forgotten, the borrower should not be able to nullify his admission by saying "I now remember that I paid," even where witnesses prove payment. Why then do we rule like the Rambam and not say that just as witnesses cannot retract by saying "we now remembered what we had forgotten at the time of testimony," so too a litigant cannot retract an admission that, according to Maharam, has the force of witness testimony?
