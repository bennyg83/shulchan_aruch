But a person on his deathbed, etc. The Beit Yosef writes in siman 32: “It appears to me that every sick person confined to his bed is considered to be at the time of death. This rule was stated regarding a shechiv mera, and anyone who is sick and confined to bed is called a shechiv mera. The same applies to one in mortal danger even though he is walking on his feet.” The Darchei Moshe cites this there. The Beit Yosef further writes there: “It also appears to me that if one admitted at the time of death, then even if he recovered, rose to his feet, and the creditor sued him, he must pay, because a person does not jest at the time of death. This is obvious, for if he could have made such a claim, we would advance it for his heirs.” In my humble opinion, this is incorrect. It is obvious that the recovered person can claim that he was jesting. The Gemara’s question concerns an unexplained admission. The Gemara was uncertain and concluded that a person ordinarily does not jest at the time of death. Thus, absent another explanation, we do not assume jesting. But if he expressly says, “I was jesting,” he removes the uncertainty and is believed. We do not advance that claim for his heirs because the ordinary presumption is that a person does not jest at the time of death. The possibility of jesting is therefore even less common than an ordinary uncommon event, and beit din does not advance such an uncommon claim for orphans. This is also proved by Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Mordechai in Zeh Borer, and by the Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 7 of Hilchot To’en. They prove that beit din advances for heirs the claim that perhaps their father intended to jest from the fact that the Gemara at the end of Get Pashut asks whether a person jests at the time of death. This indicates that when it was not the time of death, we do advance the claim for his children. According to the Beit Yosef, what is their proof? Perhaps the Gemara’s question concerns the shechiv mera himself if he recovered, and not his heirs. Or perhaps it concerns his heirs where he recovered, said, “I was jesting,” and subsequently died. This proof can be reconciled with difficulty, but the principal ruling is as I have written. Consider it carefully.
The same applies to others, etc. See the Mordechai, who writes that this is so even where there were no witnesses to the will, but the son himself admits that the deceased gave this instruction. The Darchei Moshe and the Sema, seif katan 8, cite this. Why is the son not believed through a migo? It appears to me that this is a migo against witnesses, for we are all witnesses that his silence was certainly an admission. Once it is certain that he admitted, he no longer has a migo. We apply the same principle above, siman 70, seif katan 11; see there. This implies that whenever a statement constitutes an admission, such as where he said, “You are my witnesses,” then even if the witnesses are not presently available and he himself admits that he made the admission before them, but claims that he in fact owes nothing, he must pay. He is not believed through a migo, because once it is certain that he admitted, it is certain that he owes the money. This may also be proved from Baal HaTerumot, Gate 36, part 3, and from the Tur above, siman 75, seif 17. They write that if one claims, “You yourself admitted to me that you owe me such and such, and I said to the witnesses, ‘You are my witnesses,’ or you said, ‘You are my witnesses,’” but the witnesses are not present, this is a definite claim, and the defendant may be required to take a heset oath that he did not say to the witnesses, “You are my witnesses.” This clearly implies that if he admits that he did say to the witnesses, “You are my witnesses,” he must pay, even though the witnesses are absent. We do not say that he should be believed to deny the debt through a migo that he could have denied making the admission. Rather, the rule is as stated.
Or they remained silent when he gave his instructions, their silence constitutes an admission. This appears to apply specifically where there is reason to say that the shechiv mera was speaking truthfully. Such was the case discussed by Raavyah in the Mordechai, Zeh Borer, where a widow instructed that seven zekukim be given to her relatives. We presume that she possessed seven zekukim in which the son had no share. Since the son remained silent, it is as though he admitted that she owned those seven zekukim. In an ordinary case, however, a creditor or heir does not lose his rights merely by remaining silent before a shechiv mera. We rule that even where a husband, while on his deathbed, distributes his property and his wife remains silent, she does not lose her ketubah through silence, except where he distributed a share to his sons, and even then the rule is limited to a wife because of the special leniencies applicable to a ketubah. It does not apply to other creditors. This appears in all the authorities cited by the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 106. Baal HaTerumot, Gate 59, the Tur, and the Rama below at the beginning of siman 98 rule likewise. The necessary distinction is that there, the property certainly belongs to the creditor, and we therefore do not treat silence as a waiver. Here, by contrast, the shechiv mera’s words may be true, and we may therefore properly treat silence before him as an admission. This also resolves the difficulty from the Rashbam in Yesh Nochalin, Bava Batra 126b: “But here, even if he waived, what benefit does the son receive from waiving? Although the son remained silent, this is not a waiver, because he does not wish to anger his father.” There too, since he is certainly an heir, his silence is not treated as a waiver. This appears correct.
