But a person on his deathbed. See Shach, seif katan 7, who proves that only the heirs are denied the defense that the decedent was joking, but if the person himself recovers he may claim that he was joking. Tumim proves, however, that even the person himself cannot later claim joking. He derives this from the rule concerning others who admitted to the dying person, who cannot claim joking even during the person's lifetime, and that rule itself is learned from the law of a dying person's admission. This shows that the dying person himself likewise cannot later claim joking. Tumim further brings proof from Baal HaTerumot and other authorities who explicitly write that even the person himself cannot make that claim, and this is the primary ruling. Regarding the Shach's proof, see Tumim's rejection. The Gemara there asks whether one must say, "Write it," and concludes that there is no need to say so because the words of a dying person are treated as written and delivered. It is obvious, however, that the words of a dying person are not treated as delivered in every respect during his lifetime. Their special force creates, after death, the status of a documented loan for collection from the heirs, but while the person remains alive they are not treated as a written and delivered instrument against purchasers. Therefore the Gemara's question concerns collection against the heirs. Tosafot's proof is consequently valid.
And the same applies to others who admitted. See Taz, who questions this ruling, and Tumim, who agrees with the Taz. It appears that Rama's position can nevertheless be defended. Maharit, responsa 106 and 114, proves from the case where one forcibly seized an object in our presence and the other remained silent that silence can be treated as admission. He uses this principle in several responsa. It appears, however, that silence is treated as admission specifically where one claimant says that the object is his and forcibly takes possession of it as his own, and specifically where the other party remains silent from beginning to end, meaning until the claimant leaves with it, as explained in the Gemara and commentators. The Gemara and Tosafot further explain that the same applies if one consecrates the object in the other's presence and the other remains silent from beginning to end. Such silence is an admission because a declaration to the Temple treasury is equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person. According to this, the ruling of Raavyah is clear. He discusses a woman on her deathbed who instructed her son to give property to relatives. Since the words of a dying person are treated as written and delivered, this resembles consecration, which itself resembles forcible taking because the declaration operates as delivery. If the person remains silent from beginning to end, that silence is an admission, just as with consecration. This is also reflected in Rama's language in the gloss, that they "remained silent regarding her testament." This implies that she instructed that property be given to others. In such a case, silence certainly constitutes admission because the instruction is equivalent to delivery to third parties, like forcible taking followed by silence. But if a dying person merely states that a certain person owes him money, or that a certain person's note is already paid, and that person remains silent, the silence does not constitute admission, just as the Taz writes. This is clear.
