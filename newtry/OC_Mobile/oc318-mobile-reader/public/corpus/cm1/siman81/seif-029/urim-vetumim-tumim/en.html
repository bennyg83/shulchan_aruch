And the same applies to one who waives, etc. One should not ask that according to the Mechaber's ruling above in seif 4, where a claimant is already holding his fellow's property the defendant cannot raise any of these defenses, then all the more so a waiver should be effective, making this ruling unnecessary. At first glance, this might provide some support for the Ir Shushan's interpretation there that the claimant must expressly say, "I am holding this property on account of the one hundred that you owe me." If so, waiver would differ because no such declaration is needed, and the two rules would not be identical. In truth there is no proof. First, according to what I wrote above, the reason possession is effective is precisely that the debtor's statement functions as a waiver. The Shulchan Aruch therefore needed to state the primary rule and underlying principle upon which the law of possession rests. Moreover, our case can concern one who owes another one hundred secured by a pledge. The pledge is in the lender's possession, but the lender then waives the debt. There is no relevant possession on the borrower's side, because the lender still holds the pledge. Nevertheless, because a joking defense and similar claims cannot undo an actual waiver, the waiver takes effect, the lien disappears, and the lender must return the pledge. Where there is no debt, there is no pledge. This ruling therefore teaches a significant novelty.
And he need not say, "Write." See what I wrote above in siman 65, Tumim seif katan 29. From there one can clarify the Shach's statement here in seif katan 74 challenging the Beit Yosef. The Beit Yosef writes that from the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia, concerning one who finds a receipt, we learn that with respect to waiver the creditor need not say, "Write," because the waiver is effective even before the receipt reaches the debtor. The Shach writes that there is no proof, because perhaps the creditor did in fact instruct the witnesses to write. According to what I wrote above, however, it is proven that waiver does not depend upon the written receipt at all. If it did, how could beit din return a found receipt? Perhaps in the meantime the woman sold her ketubah, which would invalidate the receipt and render it a worthless shard. What difference would it make whether she had originally said "Write" if the receipt later became void? In the end there is no effective writing, even though the underlying waiver had taken effect. Necessarily, the written receipt is irrelevant to the waiver itself. Once the waiver takes effect, it stands independently. Beit din therefore returns the receipt because the writing serves only as evidence. The Beit Yosef's proof is thus sound. This also resolves a possible difficulty. The Rosh's discussion there is stated only according to Abaye, who holds that a document's witnesses acquire on behalf of the recipient, while we do not rule like Abaye. How then could the Shulchan Aruch rely on that discussion? According to my explanation, the Beit Yosef derives only the general rule that once a waiver has taken effect, the receipt can be returned even if the written instrument itself adds nothing. The same therefore applies according to our law where the creditor explicitly waives the debt: witnesses may write a receipt even if he did not specifically instruct them to write, because the receipt neither creates nor changes the waiver. There remains, however, a difficulty. How may witnesses write a receipt without the creditor's instruction? Would this not be written testimony rather than oral testimony? Tosafot already raise this issue in Bava Batra 39b, s.v. mecha'ah, concerning how a protest may be written without an express instruction to write, when testimony ordinarily must come from the witnesses' mouths rather than from their writing. Tosafot answer that Chazal specifically instituted written protest, etc. No comparable enactment exists for a receipt. It appears that the Rosh and Baal HaTerumot follow their own view that for purposes of testimony itself the witnesses do not need an instruction to write a document, as explained above in siman 39, seif 3, in the gloss, and as I discussed there at length. According to the Rosh, the requirement to say "Write" applies only if the parties seek to create the legal status of a documented loan. For ordinary evidence concerning an oral loan, the witnesses need no instruction to write, and their writing constitutes valid testimony. But according to the authorities who disagree and hold that even regarding an oral loan a written statement of witnesses is invalid absent authorization because it is testimony from writing rather than from their mouths, as I explained above and as Tosafot write concerning protest, the present ruling requires study. Why should the creditor not have to say, "Write"? Otherwise the receipt is written testimony. See below at the end of siman 50.
