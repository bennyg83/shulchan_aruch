One who admits to his fellow. At the end of Bava Batra, where the borrower admits that a document in the lender's hand remains unpaid, the lender collects without oath and the borrower cannot explain the admission as joking or self impoverishment. Tosafot says that once the lender holds a document, the borrower is afraid to make such a false admission because people will naturally say that if the creditor still holds the document, the debt probably remains unpaid. The same should apply where the claimant holds the borrower's own handwritten obligation. Although the borrower may ordinarily claim payment against his handwriting, he is still reluctant publicly to admit that it remains unpaid when the paper is visibly in the creditor's hand. It is no worse than a deposit document, against which the custodian can claim return through a migo of unavoidable accident, yet an admission while the creditor holds the document cannot later be explained as joking or self impoverishment. However, if the borrower says, "I forgot that my handwriting remained in his hands, and therefore I made the statement only as a joke or to avoid appearing wealthy," he should be believed. People do sometimes forget that a handwritten note remains outstanding, which is itself one reason many authorities allow a payment claim against such a note. With a formal debt instrument or deposit document, by contrast, a person ordinarily does not forget that the document remains in the other party's possession. Thus the existence of personal handwriting strengthens an admission, but does not create the same absolute presumption as a full formal document.
He need not say, "Write." The Shach asks from the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding a receipt: perhaps it was written in Nisan but not delivered until Tishrei, even though a receipt is valid from the time of actual waiver or admission and need not necessarily depend on delayed delivery. Shitah Mekubetzet resolves the matter expressly. The sugya there speaks of a receipt functioning as an acquisition document, for example one that says, "The particular debt is transferred or released to you by this document." Such an instrument must actually reach the recipient, and therefore delayed delivery can matter. Ordinary receipts of our type merely record a waiver or admission. The legal effect occurs immediately when the creditor waives or admits payment, and the writing is only evidence. Rava would agree that such a receipt takes effect from the time of the underlying waiver or admission. This also explains why the Gemara does not answer that it is speaking of our ordinary receipts. If the creditor does not admit the receipt, and the receipt merely evidences an earlier completed waiver, then once the waiver itself is established there is no problem of delayed delivery. The entire sugya's concern requires an instrument whose legal operation depends upon delivery. Our ordinary receipts do not.
Write it. Tumim leaves this ruling requiring study. He asks how the witnesses may write a receipt without the creditor's instruction, since this would appear to be testimony through writing, just as Tosafot asks in Perek Chazakat HaBatim concerning a protest. According to the view that written testimony is invalid, how can the witnesses write without being told to do so? The question is not difficult. An admission concerning land may be written even without the admitting party's express instruction, as explained in Perek Zeh Borer. Ran there asks the same question of testimony through writing and answers that where writing causes no prejudice and no disadvantage to the person who made the admission, we presume he is content that the witnesses write a document, as though he had expressly authorized it. The same applies here. A receipt that merely records an admitted payment causes the creditor no legitimate harm, so we presume he consents to the writing. However, as I wrote in siman 39, seif katan 4, if the party expressly protests against writing an admission concerning land, the witnesses may not write. The same applies here. If the creditor expressly objects to their writing the receipt, they may not do so. In the absence of objection, they may write, just as with an admission concerning land.
