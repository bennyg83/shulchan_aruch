One who admits to his fellow, etc. He need not say, “You are my witnesses,” etc. The Bach writes that Baal HaTerumot states an even broader rule: where the claimant already holds property of the defendant, the defendant cannot plead jesting, as the Tur cites above in seif 7. The novelty here is that the same applies where the claimant merely holds a written instrument, and “You are my witnesses” is unnecessary. It appears to me that this applies only to an admission of receipt, where we presume that he truly acknowledged having received payment. Concerning forgiveness, however, the Tur writes above in siman 12 in the name of Rabbeinu Yeshayah that where the creditor retains the instrument, forgiveness requires a kinyan. I also cite below in siman 241, seif 2, Baal HaItur’s view that this is a matter of unresolved law.
He need not say, “You are my witnesses.” Only an admission, “I owe you,” is subject to the defenses of jesting or avoidance of appearing wealthy. An acknowledgment that the debt has been paid is not. Some authorities phrase the rule by saying that these defenses belong only to a defendant, not to a claimant. Beit Yosef explains above at the beginning of siman 32: if a lender lent two hundred and later said, “I have only one hundred in so-and-so’s possession,” or spontaneously said, “What do I have other than the one hundred held by so-and-so?” and then sued for two hundred, witnesses to his admission limit his recovery to one hundred, while the borrower takes the oath of one who admits part of the claim concerning the balance. Darkei Moshe cites this. The statement that the borrower must take a partial-admission oath is difficult. If so, what did the lender’s admission accomplish? Beit Yosef’s example does not involve a written instrument. The correct result should be that the debtor is entirely released from the additional hundred without an oath, because the creditor’s admission has the force of witnesses. Gidulei Terumah raises the same objection. Possibly Beit Yosef meant that the creditor held the debtor’s handwritten obligation for two hundred containing a clause of credibility, and when asked, “Do you have two hundred from so-and-so, as indicated by the writing?” he answered, “I have only one hundred against him under this writing.” His admission shows that one hundred of the handwritten debt was paid. He may still bring an oral claim for two hundred independent of the writing, because he admitted only that the written obligation had been reduced. The debtor would then take a partial-admission oath. Beit Yosef did not explain this because his immediate subject was only the defenses of jesting and avoidance of appearing wealthy. The case cannot be explained as an ordinary witnessed instrument, for denial of a lien upon land does not generate a partial-admission oath, as explained below in siman 88, seif 28. Yet according to what I write there in seif 29, seif katan 49, even a handwritten obligation containing credibility does not generate such an oath. Therefore Beit Yosef’s oath ruling remains difficult. He should instead have stated the simple case: the creditor admitted that he received one hundred against the instrument, or that the entire instrument was paid. He is bound by that admission even without “You are my witnesses,” because the defenses of jesting and avoidance of appearing wealthy do not apply to an acknowledgment of payment. See Responsa Maharshal, part 3, siman 98.
Likewise, he need not say, “Write it.” Beit Yosef says this may be learned from Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia. In my opinion nothing can be learned from there. Rosh says only that even though the receipt never reached the debtor, the creditor’s act constituted forgiveness. The case may still have involved an instruction to write. The Tur’s rule is instead taken from Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, at the end of part 5. At first glance, the passage at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia appears to contradict Baal HaTerumot. Why does the Gemara ask concerning a receipt that perhaps it was written in Nisan to be delivered later? Even if it was delivered much later, it should remain valid, since a receipt may be written without an instruction to write. The answer is that Baal HaTerumot and the Tur speak only where the creditor simply admits payment. If she says, “Write a receipt and place it in my possession,” beit din may not write and deliver it to the debtor without her further authorization. The Gemara can therefore properly suspect that she wrote the receipt to remain in her possession and become effective only upon delivery, intending to deliver it in Nisan but not actually delivering it until Tishrei.
