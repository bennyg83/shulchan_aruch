And not. The Shach writes that this law requires study. Even according to the poskim who hold that where a claimant demanded payment and the defendant admitted, the defense of avoiding an appearance of wealth does not apply, it appears that this is specifically where the creditor himself made the demand. And even according to what I concluded in seif 14, following Maimonides, that an admission in the creditor's presence cannot be explained as an attempt to avoid an appearance of wealth, this is specifically where the creditor himself was present. The reason, as I wrote there, is that how could a person try to prevent others from considering him wealthy by admitting before the very person who knows the truth, namely that no debt is owed? What has he accomplished by that admission? Here, however, where he admitted to another person, the opposite is more reasonable. If that person asked him, 'Do you owe Levi?' he may have wished not to be regarded by the questioner as wealthy and therefore answered, 'Yes.' To make the Rama's ruling more understandable, one must say that Levi was present when Reuven asked him. This accords with the 'there are those who say' opinion in seif 14 that even an admission in the creditor's presence can be explained as an attempt to avoid appearing wealthy so long as the creditor did not make the demand. Here, because Reuven demanded in Levi's presence, it is treated as though Levi himself demanded. According to what I concluded there, however, following Maimonides, that an admission in the creditor's presence cannot be explained this way, the ruling here is obvious without this additional reasoning. Thus concludes the Shach.
