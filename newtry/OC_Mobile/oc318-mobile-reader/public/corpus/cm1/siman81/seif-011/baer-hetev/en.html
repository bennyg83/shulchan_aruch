Admit. The Tur writes at the end of siman 32: If, after one says to him, 'Do you wish to admit to me before witnesses?' he answers, 'Yes,' Ramah holds that this is an admission, while my father the Rosh holds that it is not an admission. The Bach there writes that it requires study how the Tur knew that the Rosh held this view, since the Rosh's rulings contain no explicit indication. In my humble opinion, however, it is explicit from the Rosh's rulings. Since he holds that even a complete admission is ineffective until the debtor specifically says, 'You are my witnesses,' what can be gained by his answering merely, 'Yes,' when he never said, 'You are my witnesses'? Ramah follows his own view, like Maimonides, that a complete admission is effective for this purpose without the words 'You are my witnesses.' Nevertheless, as a matter of practical law, even according to what I wrote that Maimonides' view is primary on this point, Ramah's ruling here requires study. It appears that the admission is effective only where the debtor knew that witnesses were present and spoke in the manner of a formal admission, unlike the present case. This is also demonstrated by the poskim I cited above in seif 8. See there. Thus concludes the Shach.
Exempt. Since the defendant had no reason to realize that witnesses were present, he may say, 'I said this only in jest.' Thus concludes the Sema. The Tur likewise writes this at the end of siman 32. It implies that if he knew witnesses were present and said, 'I would admit to you, but I am afraid,' and the like, he is liable. Ordinarily, of course, a person would not say such a thing in front of witnesses, for we are not dealing with fools. But if he did say it, he is liable. Shach.
