He answered him, “I would admit to you,” etc. The Tur writes above, at the end of siman 32: if, after the claimant said, “Do you wish to admit to me before witnesses?” the defendant answered, “Yes,” the Ramah rules that this is an admission, whereas my father the Rosh rules that it is not. The Bach asks how the Tur knew that the Rosh held this, since the Rosh’s rulings contain no explicit statement. In my humble opinion, it follows clearly. The Rosh holds that even a complete admission is ineffective until the defendant specifically says, “You are my witnesses,” as the Tur writes here in his name and above in siman 32, and as is evident from the Rosh in Zeh Borer and Chezkat HaBatim. What then is added by the defendant’s answer “Yes,” when he still did not say, “You are my witnesses”? The Ramah follows his own view, like the Rambam, that a complete admission is effective without that declaration. For practical law, although I write below, seif 14, seif katan 33, that the Rambam’s position is primary and a complete admission defeats the claim of jesting as effectively as “You are my witnesses,” the Ramah’s ruling here still requires study. A complete admission is effective specifically where the speaker knows witnesses are present. Here that may not be so. This distinction is also proved by the authorities cited above, seif 8, seif katan 18; see there.
The witnesses hear all this, he is exempt. The Tur’s wording above, at the end of siman 32, is that he may say, “I was jesting with you.” This implies that even here, if he does not make that claim, beit din does not advance it for him. Indeed, this case is somewhat worse for the defendant. Although he did not see the witnesses, he said, “I am afraid that you may compel me,” which gives his words a more serious character. He must therefore expressly claim jesting; if he does not, beit din does not do so for him. This is also proved by the Gemara on page 29a; see there.
