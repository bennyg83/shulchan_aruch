["Rather, they belong to so and so, etc., this is not an admission, etc. Since he could simply have said, 'They are not yours,' and there was no need for him to add, 'Rather, they belong to so and so,' those additional words are regarded as mere conversation and do not prejudice him."]
["And there are those who disagree, etc. Darkei Moshe writes this here and in siman 79, stating that the responsum of Maharam cited by Mordechai at the beginning of the chapter Chazakat HaBatim, concerning a belt and an ornament called bat nefesh, disagrees with the responsum of Rav Hai Gaon. In my view this is surprising, because there is no dispute at all. Rav Hai Gaon and the Tur discuss a case in which the person in whose favor the admission was made was not present before beit din at the time of the admission, as is also explicit in Baal HaTerumot, Gate 42. In Maharam's responsum, by contrast, that person was explicitly present. Moreover, in Maharam's case both that person and another person were suing the holder before beit din. He first said that the property belonged to one of them, and later said that it belonged to the other. That first statement therefore constituted an admission, because he was not claiming, 'I was merely jesting with you,' but rather that it belonged to the other claimant. Nor does a claim of jesting apply in such a case where he does not claim that the property was his own. There is also another distinction. There it was known that the belt and ornament had come from Reuven's household, so Shimon could not exempt himself by saying, 'They are mine.' He was compelled to say that Reuven's wife had deposited them with him for delivery to the heirs. Therefore, had he not claimed that he had erred, he would not have been believed to retract his first statement, since both claimants were present together when he said that the property belonged to one and not the other. Here, however, he claims, 'I was merely jesting with you, and I said that they belonged to so and so only to put you off, but in truth they are mine.' It is obvious that this is not an admission, because he was under no necessity to say that they belonged to the third party. According to this, the Rama's further statement that he cannot say, 'I erred in my admission and they are mine,' is also imprecise. Here, where that third party was not present, he certainly may say that he erred. Maharam's case concerns one who was initially sued before beit din and admitted the claim to the plaintiff. He cannot afterward say that the property is his own. See there. Thus the Rama's formulation requires further study. See the responsum of Maharit, siman 109."]
["I erred, etc., through a migo that he could have said, 'I returned it,' etc. Although had he made that alternative claim he would have been brazen, whereas his present claim is not brazen, we nevertheless apply the migo. Rabbeinu Yitzchak proved this from the second chapter of Kesubos concerning one who says, 'This field belonged to your father, but I purchased it from him.' Where there are no witnesses that the field belonged to the father, he is believed to say that he purchased it through a migo that he could have said, 'It never belonged to your father,' even though on the claim that he purchased it the opposing party does not know that he is lying, whereas had he claimed that it never belonged to the father the opposing party would know that he was lying. Even so, the migo applies. This is stated in Maharam's responsum cited by Mordechai at the beginning of Chazakat HaBatim, and likewise in Tosafos there, page 52b. See what I wrote above, siman 75, seif 7, seif katan 23, and siman 53, seif 2, seif katan 4."]
["But he cannot say, 'I erred in my admission and they are mine,' etc. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe were brief here, and this ruling requires examination, as I shall explain. From what I write it appears that this applies specifically where he admitted before beit din. Had the matter not been clear to him, he would not ordinarily admit and obligate himself before beit din. Therefore, even if he has a migo, such as where he later left beit din and could make another claim, he is no longer believed to say that he erred, because there is a presumption that he did not err. Mahari ibn Lev likewise writes that even Baal HaTerumot, who below in siman 126, seif 13, holds that one may say, 'I erred,' through a migo, agrees where the admission was made before beit din. But where one admitted before witnesses, he is believed to say, 'I erred,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I paid.' Maharik, shoresh 65, explicitly rules that only before beit din, or before witnesses together with a kinyan by which he obligated himself, is he unable to retract; where he did not obligate himself before witnesses, even if there was a formal admission before witnesses without a binding kinyan, he may claim that he erred. Maharik further writes that in such a case he may claim error even without a migo. That does not appear correct to me, nor is it implied by Maharam and the Ran whom he cites. Indeed, even regarding an admission before witnesses without a kinyan there is difficulty, because the words of the Ran and Maharam cited there imply that he is not believed to say, 'I erred,' even through a migo that he could have said, 'I paid.' They prove this from the passage in Shevuos concerning the litigants' oaths, where the case itself involves an admission before witnesses. Tosafos at the beginning of Gittin, the Rosh, Mordechai, Agudah, and Semag at the beginning of positive commandment 95 all write that the passage in Shevuos proves that one is not believed to say, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment. Yet there the admission was merely before witnesses. It is entirely unreasonable to say that the passage in Shevuos concerns an admission before witnesses accompanied by a kinyan, because the Gemara does not imply that. Moreover, that would require saying that these authorities hold that after a kinyan one may still claim payment, which is not implied by their words. Mordechai at the end of the first chapter of Kesubos cites the Rif that with testimony and a kinyan one cannot claim payment, and the Tur at the beginning of siman 39, the beginning of siman 70, and the ends of simanim 87 and 88 plainly imply that where there is testimony together with a kinyan he cannot claim payment. According to Maharik, the Tur would therefore stand against the Rosh, whose words imply that even testimony without a kinyan prevents a claim of error. Nevertheless, regarding practical law, it appears to me that one may claim, 'I erred,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I paid,' even where he admitted before witnesses. Although one might say that Tosafos, the Rosh, Ran, and those who follow them disagree, their proofs are not compelling and there is no firm source for their position. Their principal source is the interpretation of Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rif at the beginning of Gittin concerning the incident of Ginai, where witnesses established that he had made an error. They infer that otherwise he could not have claimed error through a migo of payment. In my view this proves nothing, because Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rif there are discussing an incident involving a kinyan, in which he could not claim payment. It is possible that Tosafos and those who follow them hold that even after a kinyan one can claim payment, as the Rivash, siman 478, writes in the name of the French Sages, yet even according to them one cannot say, 'I erred,' through that migo because one would not obligate himself by kinyan unless he had carefully checked the account. But in my view, where he could in fact claim payment, he may likewise claim error through that migo. This is also implied by the Rif's statement that otherwise every document in the world could be challenged as having been created through error and the debtor would be exempt. If the Rif were speaking of an ordinary oral admission, how could he compare it to an ordinary document? Rather, he refers to a kinyan, which is treated like a document, as the authorities and the Tur and Mechaber explain below at the end of siman 88 and elsewhere. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 15, likewise understands the Rif and Rabbeinu Chananel to mean that if there was no kinyan, even where the transfer was made in the presence of all three parties, the debtor is believed to say that he erred through a migo of payment. The proof brought by Tosafos and those who follow them from Shevuos is likewise no proof in my view. It may well be that if he actually claimed error he would be believed there; the passage concerns one who does not claim that, but instead expressly says that he never owed the money at all, as is stated in the Rosh there. Tosafos in the chapter Get Pashut, page 175a, in the name of Rabbi, also appears to hold that one is believed to say, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment, and explains that the case of Rabbeinu Chananel differs because there was a kinyan. See there. I later found the same in the Chiddushei Rashba at the beginning of Gittin. After citing the Rif and Rabbeinu Chananel, he writes that some ask why, absent witnesses proving the error, the person should not be believed through a migo that he could say, 'I paid.' Some answer that because a kinyan had been made from him, and an ordinary kinyan is intended for a written document, he cannot claim payment. The Rashba says that this is correct and clear. The Ramban in Sefer HaZechut and Baal HaTerumot, Gate 28, Part 50, likewise hold that one may say, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment, and the Tur cites this below in siman 126. The Mechaber there, seif 13, brings both views without deciding between them. Although Beit Yosef there writes that the law does not follow Baal HaTerumot because he is a lone authority, it appears that in Shulchan Aruch he retracted, perhaps because he later saw all these authorities whom I have cited. Even according to the Rama there, who adds the latter view that where one assigned the debt before witnesses he cannot say that he erred through a migo of payment, it appears that he ruled that way specifically there because the transfer was made in maamad sheloshtan, the presence of all three parties, thereby creating a special personal obligation comparable to obligating oneself before beit din, unlike an ordinary admission. I find proof for this in Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 15, who concludes that the Raavad writes that in maamad sheloshtan, even without a kinyan, one is not believed to say, 'I erred,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I paid,' whereas one who merely admitted a debt to another is believed to say, 'I erred,' through that migo. This does not contradict what the Ran writes at the beginning of Gittin in the name of the Raavad, because there too he speaks of one who specially obligated himself through maamad sheloshtan. Maharik, shoresh 65, also understands the Ran in the name of the Raavad as referring specifically to one who obligated himself before witnesses, but he explains that this means a kinyan was made. That interpretation does not appear correct to me. I later found Mahari ibn Lev, volume 1, end of siman 91, who challenges Maharik on this point. However, what Mahari ibn Lev appears to say there, that according to the Raavad even a bare declaration of 'You are my witnesses' prevents one from saying, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment, does not appear correct to me. It also seems that he overlooked the words of Rabbeinu Yerucham in the name of the Raavad just cited. Rather, the Ran's statement in the name of the Raavad applies specifically there, because he admitted before witnesses in maamad sheloshtan and thereby obligated himself through that special mechanism. Chiddushei Rashba there likewise cites a view explaining that he cannot claim error because it is a migo against witnesses: we are witnesses, as it were, that a person does not obligate himself in maamad sheloshtan unless he first checks the account carefully. This is specifically where he obligates himself in maamad sheloshtan before witnesses. If the three parties were together privately without witnesses, he is believed, because outside the presence of witnesses one does not check so carefully. Thus both elements are required: witnesses and maamad sheloshtan. Baal HaIttur, entry on assignment, page 105d, similarly writes that if there are witnesses to the assignment though not to the underlying debt, he cannot say that he erred, because once he accepted the obligation upon himself in maamad sheloshtan it is like writing a document to another on the assumption that he owes him. This too implies that the decisive factor is his acceptance in maamad sheloshtan before witnesses. Consequently, even if we were to say that the Rosh, Ran, and those who follow them hold that even an admission before witnesses without maamad sheloshtan prevents a claim of error through a migo of payment, we do not rule like them on this point, because their proofs are not compelling and the majority of the authorities cited above support the contrary position. I later found in the responsum of Maharshach, volume 2, siman 148, that he writes that the authorities did not agree with what the Ran wrote in the name of the Raavad, and therefore even one who accepted the obligation not before witnesses cannot later say that he erred. His words are astonishing. On the contrary, some authorities go even farther than the Ran's statement in the name of the Raavad and hold that even one who accepted the obligation in maamad sheloshtan may say that he erred. All the other authorities who hold that he cannot say that he erred state this only regarding an assignment before witnesses. Maharshach undoubtedly overlooked Maharik and all the authorities cited above. Where, however, he lacks a migo of payment, such as where he admitted before witnesses while the debt was still within its fixed term, or the witnesses never left his presence so that payment could not have occurred, and similar cases, it is obvious that he cannot say that he erred, contrary to Maharik, shoresh 65. This is explicit in the Ran, Rosh, Baal HaTerumot, and other authorities. Although above, seif katan 55, I concluded that one who voluntarily admitted before beit din may retract and say that he erred even before beit din although he has no migo, that case differs because he admitted on his own initiative and is like one returning a lost object. We therefore apply the principle that the same mouth that created the prohibition or liability may explain it away. If one admitted before witnesses and has no migo, however, even if the admission was voluntary he cannot retract once the creditor said, 'You are my witnesses,' and the debtor remained silent. As I wrote above, seif 15, seif katan 41, regarding 'You are my witnesses,' there is no distinction between the debtor himself saying it and the creditor saying it while the debtor remains silent, even where the admission was voluntary, with respect to claims of jesting or speaking so as not to appear wealthy. The same therefore applies to a claim of error. Indeed this is stronger than a voluntary admission before beit din, because once 'You are my witnesses' was said, we presume that he carefully considered his words. It may also be said that when the creditor says, 'You are my witnesses,' this itself constitutes a demand upon him, as Mordechai writes in the chapter Zeh Borer."]
["However, regarding an account that he wrote in his ledger, etc. This ruling requires further study. Maharik there follows his own approach, writing that the Ran at the beginning of Gittin in the name of the Raavad implies that even after an admission before witnesses one may say, 'I erred,' without a migo, and therefore all the more so regarding an account written in one's private ledger. In my view no such implication follows from the Raavad. The Raavad says only that where a person did not create a binding personal obligation he is believed only where he has a migo, whereas where he did create such an obligation he is not believed even with a migo. Indeed, one who studies the Ran and Raavad carefully will see the opposite, as I wrote just above, namely that according to the Raavad and the other authorities, one who admitted before witnesses and lacks a migo of payment cannot say that he erred. The same should therefore apply to an account entered in a ledger where he lacks a migo. This is also indicated by what was explained above, siman 69, seif katan 27, and below at the end of siman 107, that we may collect even from heirs on the basis of an entry found in the decedent's ledger where it is known that he did not pay, such as when the debt is still within its fixed term, as I wrote above in this siman, seif 17, seif katan 45. If the father himself could have claimed error, how could we collect from the heirs? Any defense their father could have raised is raised on behalf of the heirs. It may nevertheless be that the special ruling concerning a private ledger is correct for the reason Maharik gives: accounts that a person writes privately in his own ledger are not checked with such precision, because he assumes that if he errs he can later recalculate them. This is common practice, that a person quickly writes and records a provisional account and later reconsiders it and discovers an error. According to this, it is understandable why we do not advance a claim of error for heirs in the cases cited above, because those cases concern a simple statement found written in the ledger, not a running calculation. Independently, one may also say that an error is an uncommon occurrence and we do not advance such an uncommon claim for heirs. The matter still requires further study. In any event, according to this reasoning, the rule applies specifically where the ledger was originally in his own possession. If the ledger was held by a trusted third party or in another arrangement where he could not later correct an error, he is not believed to say that he erred when he lacks a migo. This appears correct to me. Note carefully. See the responsum of Maharit, siman 57, and Mabit, volume 2, siman 154."]
