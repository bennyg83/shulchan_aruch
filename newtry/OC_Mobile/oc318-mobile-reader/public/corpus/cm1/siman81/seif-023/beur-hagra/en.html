He sued him for an object... The admission concerning the other matter is treated as meaningless words. This is the explanation of the Tosefta cited above: an admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses, even though he did not say "you are my witnesses," because he admitted in beit din. This is also the interpretation of Ramah, cited by the Rosh at the end of chapter HaManiach. The latter clause, "but if he admitted voluntarily he may retract," likewise concerns an admission before beit din. Before beit din, the distinction has relevance only in a case of this type. Therefore Ramah writes there that if one sued him for wheat and he admitted barley, he is exempt because the admission concerning barley is meaningless. He brings proof from the Tosefta cited above. Nevertheless, afterward he can... Although this is a weak claim, he is believed through a migo, as stated in Sanhedrin 31a: "I know concerning this document..." because, had he wished, etc. There are many examples of weak claims supported by a migo. See the Shach, seif katan 59, and although if he had claimed so, etc. See Mordechai there.
But he cannot... As Tosafos write at the end of the first chapter of Gittin 14a, s.v. "and not," based on Shevuos concerning "you have a maneh of mine..." and Bava Basra 175a: "rather, if it was stated..." "they are not believed..." even though they are believed to say, "we paid," as stated immediately before there: "Which direction does reason point? The opposite..." See Tosafos there, s.v. "he retracted," where some explain that this applies specifically, etc. So too Mordechai there. The reason is that this would be a migo against witnesses, because a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. This applies specifically to a matter he admitted concerning himself. It does not apply to a matter that does not pertain to him. This is the meaning of the statement that the admission of his mouth is like witnesses specifically against himself, unlike the first clause. This applies specifically where he admitted before witnesses, because then he should have been precise. If there were no witnesses and he had no reason to be precise, he can say he was mistaken, as Ran writes at the end of the first chapter of Gittin there. So too Bava Basra there regarding a dying person who said, "Give a maneh to so and so..." asks why, and answers that a person is likely to remember. Even according to the alternate explanation in Tosafos cited above, this applies specifically where he admitted before witnesses. The same law applies where he makes a definite admission even without witnesses, because there too he had reason to be precise. This is why the Rama writes, "Nevertheless, regarding an accounting..."
