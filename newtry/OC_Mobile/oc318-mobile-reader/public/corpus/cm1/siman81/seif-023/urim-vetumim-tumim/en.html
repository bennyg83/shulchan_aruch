He demanded the object, etc. Responsa Penei Moshe, part 2, siman 26, cites Responsa Mahari Ben Lev, part 2, siman 51, concerning one who admits that property he has deposited with another actually belongs to a certain person. If there is reason to fear that the custodian will hear the admission and hand the property over to the person named, then the statement constitutes a binding admission and the defendant cannot claim that he spoke merely so as not to appear wealthy. How could he make such an admission for appearances when the custodian might believe him sincerely and deliver the property to the person he named? Mahari Ben Lev proves this from the rule that if a person says, "I saw your father conceal money and say, 'It belongs to so and so,'" then if the money was concealed in a field where the witness himself could take it, he is believed. The difficulty is that perhaps the father said this merely so as not to appear wealthy. Necessarily, because the witness could have taken the money and delivered it to the named person, we do not entertain that defense. Penei Moshe agrees. In truth, the Nimukei Yosef already asks this question and answers that because the witness could take the money, this proves that the deceased trusted him, and therefore the deceased would not have been making sport of him. According to that reasoning, a custodian who has no ability to deny the deposit is different, because the depositor's trust in him is not demonstrated. All the more so according to what the Perishah writes below in siman 255, that the Tur holds even in the concealed money case that the witness must testify that the deceased spoke as one who truly transferred the property, not as one acting deceptively. This shows that the above rule cannot be derived from that case. The opposite is also proven from the Gemara's case of a dying person who consecrated his assets and said, "I owe one hundred to so and so." He is believed because a person does not collude against consecrated property. The Gemara asks that even without this chazakah he has a migo that he could have annulled the consecration. The Rashba answers, as cited in the responsum of Rav Betzalel Ashkenazi and as the Shach writes below in siman 255, that the assets had already been delivered to the Temple treasurer, so the owner could no longer annul the vow. If so, why does the Gemara ask that perhaps he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy? According to Mahari Ben Lev, such a defense should be impossible, because the treasurer might hear the admission and, knowing that the owner is presumed not to collude against consecrated property, might hand the money to the person named. What difference is there between an ordinary custodian and the Temple treasurer? Likewise, according to the Rashbam, who explains the question whether a dying person who admits may claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy as concerning a deposit, and the Gemara resolves it from the admission of Isur the convert, one could ask why that incident proves anything. The deposit there was in Rava's hands. How could Isur make a false admission when Rava might hear of it? Even if Rava was not physically present at the admission, witnesses heard it and could report it, and he would then deliver the deposit to Rav Mari. According to Mahari Ben Lev, the defense should therefore be unavailable there, while the question would remain unresolved where the object or money is in the debtor's own possession. These proofs leave no basis for their ruling. I remain uncertain, however, about a case where the debtor actually tells the custodian, "Give it to so and so." Perhaps he then cannot say that he spoke merely so as not to appear wealthy. It is true that when a dying person tells his sons, "I owe one hundred to so and so," we are concerned that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, while if he says, "Give one hundred to so and so," they give it, which indicates that once he says, "Give," we do not advance that defense. Still, I am uncertain because Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, part 5, ruling 3, says that in a three party transfer the debtor cannot claim joking because the arrangement is equivalent to a kinyan. Why is that reason needed? A three party transfer is itself an instruction, "The one hundred in your possession, give to so and so." It would seem enough that the debtor said, "Give." Apparently, one can distinguish. If the debtor said "yes" and then, while everyone remained present, even after more than the minimal time for retraction, immediately clarified, "I was joking," he may still claim joking. We do not say that the instruction exposed him to the risk that the custodian would actually transfer the money, because the debtor can answer, "That is precisely why I did not leave the gathering before telling him not to give it." Baal HaTerumot therefore needs the reason that a three party transfer functions like a kinyan. Once the kinyan occurs, he cannot retract even in that same gathering after the time for immediate retraction has passed. But if he did not retract during a period in which the custodian realistically could have delivered the money, then because he said, "Give," it appears that he can no longer raise any defense, just as in the case of the dying person and his heirs. Nevertheless, Tosafot in Gittin 13a, s.v. ita, create difficulty. According to versions that establish the case as an admission, Tosafot ask why it could not concern a healthy person and money not physically gathered together. If the property was not a deposit in another person's hands and the person was healthy, what is gained by saying, "Give"? He could still say that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy, and if the other person came to collect he would object and explain his admission. Necessarily, Tosafot assume that saying, "Give," even where the property remains in his own possession and has not yet reached another person's hand, prevents the defense that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. The matter therefore requires study, but the broad rule stated by those authorities does not appear to stand as practical law.
And there are those who disagree, etc. The source of the dissenting view is Maharam's responsum cited by the Mordechai in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim. A person was sued to return a deposited belt and certain household items. He answered, "Your wife gave them to me before she married you, on condition that after her death they be given to her heirs." Beit din ruled that he should swear that his account was true and then give the property to the heirs. The heirs were present when the ruling was issued. The next day he regretted his statement and said that he now remembered that he had received the property from the husband himself. Maharam ruled that he was believed in this second claim by virtue of a migo that he could have said that he had already returned the property to the heirs, or that it had been lost through an unavoidable accident between the ruling and the next day. From this the Rama inferred that Maharam does not accept the rule at issue here. Otherwise, why would the defendant need those migo claims, which, as the responsum itself indicates, involve brazenness? He should have had the migo that he could have said, "It never happened," and that his original statement was only intended to put off the husband. Necessarily, Maharam does not accept such a defense. The Shach objects and writes that in that case it was known that the defendant had received the property, and the only question was whether he received it from the husband or from the wife. These words have no basis in Maharam's responsum, which never states that receipt was known. Moreover, even if the property had clearly been received, the defendant would still have a migo that he returned it to the wife, because there were no witnesses who had seen the property in his possession. Why then does Maharam rely on the claim that the property was lost through an unavoidable accident after the judgment, a weak claim over such a short interval when no report of theft had emerged? He could have claimed that an unavoidable accident had occurred the previous day or earlier, and that he had only been delaying the husband with his original story. Necessarily, the Shach's distinction is not correct. The Shach also writes that the wife's heirs initially joined in the claim against the defendant, but this too is not stated in the responsum. Why would they have sued when they did not even know that the wife had allegedly deposited the property on such a condition? Their entire supposed right arose only from the defendant's own statement. The Shach further distinguishes that the heirs were present when the defendant made the admission. This too is insufficient to defeat the Rama's inference. Either the statement was a legally effective admission, in which case its effect in beit din does not depend upon the other party's presence, or it was not an admission, as the Tur understands, in which case their presence should not change its character.
However, there is a genuine difficulty. Rav Hai Gaon and the Tur say only that if beit din had not yet ruled that the object be given to the other party and the defendant remained silent, the defendant may still explain his original statement. But if beit din actually ruled that he must give the object to the other party and he stood silent, and only later sought to retract, it is obvious that neither joking nor the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy should remain available. How could he hear a formal ruling of beit din and fail to protest, then later say that his original words were merely a joke? One does not make sport in the face of an operative beit din judgment. In Maharam's case, beit din ruled that the defendant must swear and give the property to the heirs. He should therefore have protested immediately. How can he later say that the entire matter was joking? For this reason, the Rama's inference from that responsum requires study.
But he cannot say, "I was mistaken," etc. The Shach in seif katan 60 discusses this at length and disputes the ruling. The principal proof of Tosafot and the Rosh is the incident of the gardeners in Gittin 14a. Two gardeners made an accounting, and it emerged that one owed the other five zuz. The debtor then instructed, through a three party transfer, that the amount be given to the landowner, and they made a kinyan from him. Later he claimed that there had been an error in the accounting and that he owed nothing. Rava exempted him. The Rif and Rabbenu Chananel explain that witnesses established the mistake, because otherwise the debtor could not simply claim that he had erred. Tosafot ask that even without witnesses he should have a migo that he could have claimed he had paid. They derive from this that even where a person has a migo, he is not believed to claim that he made an error, because such a claim runs against the chazakah that a person accurately remembers an accounting made before witnesses. The Shach objects that the parties made a kinyan, and one cannot ordinarily say, "I paid," against a kinyan. Tosafot may hold that even after a kinyan one can claim payment, but according to our law, where such a claim is unavailable, there is no proof from the incident. These words are already addressed in the Rashba's novellae in every relevant respect, and there is no difficulty. The question remains why the Rif and Rabbenu Chananel were forced to say that witnesses established the error, which is not the natural implication of the Gemara and is itself strained. If witnesses plainly showed the mistake, how could Rav Nachman have erred and thought that the debtor was not mistaken but merely sought to retract, as Tosafot explain there, s.v. chada? If witnesses stood before him demonstrating the error, Rav Nachman could hardly miss it. It would have been simpler to say that the debtor had a migo that the property was lost through an unavoidable accident and establish the money as a deposit. The Rashba explicitly writes in his novellae that the ordinary assumption is that the gardeners were holding the money as a deposit. If so, the debtor had a migo to claim unavoidable accident or theft, as in Maharam's case discussed above. If an oath was required, Rav Nachman may indeed have required one. The same must be said according to Tosafot themselves, who ask that the debtor had a migo to say, "I paid," even though Rav Nachman had instituted the shevuat heset. Necessarily, their question assumes that an oath could still be imposed, while the legal issue is whether the substantive migo exists. The same therefore applies to a migo of unavoidable accident. We must conclude that there is no migo against an admission made before witnesses, because a person is precise and remembers such an accounting. All is well. There is, however, room for examination according to the Ran, who holds that when we say a person is not believed to claim, "I was mistaken," this applies specifically where he admitted before witnesses. Without witnesses, he is less precise because he thinks that if he later discovers an error he can retract. The Rama rules this way below in siman 126, seif 13. If so, why were the Rif and Rabbenu Chananel forced to say that witnesses proved the error? They could have said more simply that no witnesses were present at the transfer, and the phrase, "they made a kinyan from him," means that the parties themselves made the kinyan, since a kinyan does not require witnesses according to the Rif in the chapter HaIsh Mekadesh. Indeed, the wording, "they made a kinyan from him before the landowner," suggests that it was not made before witnesses at all. Perhaps we can infer that Rav Nachman holds that "before witnesses" is not literal and that even one witness is enough to require exactness. How could a person fail to make an accurate statement merely because he could later retract, when he would at least have to take an oath against that witness? A migo to deny the event is unavailable because we do not apply a migo that contradicts a witness. According to the Rosh, who holds that one can retract even in a three party transfer, the debtor likewise had no migo to say, "It never happened," in the gardeners' incident. He would fear making that denial, because if he denied the transfer, the landowner would turn back against the gardener who originally owed him and collect from him. That gardener could then sue the debtor, with the landowner serving as a witness that the debtor admitted the obligation before him. The landowner would no longer be interested in the outcome because he had already received payment from the gardener. The debtor therefore prefers to claim that there was an accounting error, which does not directly contradict anyone. Thus there was effectively one witness. This works according to the Rosh. According to the Rif, however, who holds that once the transfer is made the original debtor cannot retract, the landowner is already the defendant's direct claimant. Who then can testify? The landowner is a litigant, while the gardener is also interested in the testimony, because if he denies that the debtor admitted the obligation then the landowner could turn back against him if the three party transfer were denied. Thus all of them would be interested parties, and the debtor could seemingly deny the matter entirely without fear. It is strained to say that he would nevertheless fear that the landowner, who according to the Rif cannot in any event return against the gardener, might voluntarily release the gardener from all liability whether or not the transfer was valid, thereby making the gardener a disinterested witness to the debtor's admission. Whether such a release would indeed remove the gardener's interest requires study. See below in siman 121, seif 9, and what I write there. This difficulty therefore remains according to the Ran's understanding of the Rif. We must say that the Rif and Rabbenu Chananel did not explain the case as one without witnesses because, if so, why was a kinyan made at all? A three party transfer is itself effective like a kinyan. What did the additional kinyan accomplish? Necessarily, the three party transfer concerned an oral loan, and they made a kinyan because an ordinary kinyan is presumed intended for writing, thereby converting the obligation into one evidenced by a document. If so, witnesses were necessarily present, because without witnesses there could be no collection from encumbered property and the transaction would have no public notice. It follows that witnesses were indeed present, and therefore the Rif and Rabbenu Chananel needed to say that the mistake itself was established by witnesses. All is clear. Tosafot's law is likewise still proven according to their view, because they hold that one can claim payment against a kinyan so long as no document has actually been written, even though the kinyan was intended for writing. According to our law, the migo can instead be based on unavoidable accident, as I wrote above. Thus every point is compelled, the Rif's and Tosafot's rulings are correct, and the Shach's objection was unnecessary. Consider this carefully.
