They divide. The Shach finds the Rama's presentation puzzling and says there may be no dispute at all. The Mechaber, following Rav Hai Gaon and the Tur, deals with a case where the person named by the defendant was not present in beit din at the time of the admission. Responsa Maharam, the source for the Rama, concerns a case where two claimants were both before beit din and the defendant first said the property belonged to one and then said it belonged to the other. That is a binding admission, because he did not claim jest, and such a jest defense is not naturally applicable when he is not claiming ownership for himself. Accordingly, the Rama's further statement that he cannot say "I was mistaken" is also imprecise. In the Mechaber's case, where the named person was not present, the defendant may be able to say he was mistaken. Maharam's case involved a claimant who was already suing him in beit din and to whom he admitted ownership; only there can he not later say it was his own. The Rama's wording therefore requires study. See Responsa Maharit 109.
Through a migo. Although the alternative claim would have required greater brazenness, a migo is still recognized in such a case. Ri proves this from Ketubot concerning one who says, "This field belonged to your father," and the same principle appears in Tosafot, Bava Batra. See the Shach to siman 75, seif 7 and siman 62.
And mine. The Shach writes that this rule applies specifically where the admission was made before beit din. A person would not ordinarily subject himself in beit din unless he was certain, so even if he later acquires a migo, such as after leaving beit din, he is no longer believed to say he was mistaken, because there is a presumption that he was not mistaken. Responsa Mahari ibn Lev states that even Baal HaTerumot, who later allows a claim of mistake through a migo, agrees regarding an admission before beit din. By contrast, where the admission was before witnesses, he may say "I was mistaken" through a migo of payment, as Maharik rules. If he lacks a migo of payment, such as while still within the due date or where the witnesses remained with him continuously, he plainly cannot claim mistake. Although the Shach earlier concluded that one who voluntarily admits in beit din can retract and say he was mistaken even without a migo, that is because the admission was volunteered and he is like one returning a lost item, so the mouth that created the obligation may explain it. But where he admits before witnesses and the creditor says "you are my witnesses" and the debtor remains silent, even a volunteered admission becomes binding enough to bar a claim of mistake without a migo. The formula "you are my witnesses" is effective against jest or concealment of wealth whether spoken by debtor or creditor with the debtor's silence, and the Shach says it should likewise matter for mistake because it shows the debtor spoke carefully.
In the accounting. The Shach writes that this law requires study. At minimum, he concludes that the ability to claim an accounting error applies only where the ledger was originally in the defendant's possession. If it was held by a trusted third party or in another manner preventing later alteration, he is not believed to say the account was mistaken when he lacks a migo. See Responsa Maharit 57 and Mabit II, 154.
