It appears that even according to the Shach, he must have orally admitted the debt before witnesses, as the Sema writes. Otherwise, if he merely writes before witnesses that he owes a certain person a certain amount, the writing certainly does not create liability, because he can say that he prepared the document in anticipation of giving it to that person if and when the person later lends him the money. The Sema and Shach disagree only about the reason. According to the Sema, where there is no oral admission before the witnesses, the writing is ineffective because it may have been written so that people should not think him wealthy. According to the Shach, that rationale does not fit a debt acknowledgment of this kind, but he is exempt because he can say that he was merely preparing a document to deliver if a loan would later be made. The Shach further writes that if a person writes on an object that it belongs to another, the concern that he wished not to appear wealthy does apply. Tumim questions this from Mordechai, who derives the rule from a person who hid property and said, "This belongs to so-and-so, it is maaser," where if it is evident that he is merely scheming, his statement has no effect. If so, how can the Mishnah in Maaser Sheni rule that where one finds the letter mem written upon a utensil, we presume it is maaser? Perhaps the owner wrote it merely so as not to appear wealthy, just as the laws of Maaser Sheni recognize that rationale. Hagahot Mordechai in Bava Kamma similarly derives from this that if a purse of money is found bearing the word "tzedakah," it is treated as tzedakah, and Rama rules accordingly in Yoreh Deah 259. Why can we not say that the owner wrote it merely so as not to appear wealthy, when the same source explicitly states that if a person tells his children, "These funds belong to tzedakah," and it is apparent that he is scheming, the statement may have been made merely so as not to appear wealthy? In my view the distinction is this. Maaser carries a prohibition against eating it outside Yerushalayim, and terumah carries a prohibition against a non-kohen eating it. When a person states that an item is terumah or maaser, he renders it prohibited upon himself, analogous to the principle that a person can render something forbidden to himself, as where a woman says, "I am tamei," in Ketubot 22. An explanation can later be effective, just as an explanation can sometimes undo the statement "I am tamei." But when the status was established through an act, such as where a woman conducted herself publicly as a niddah among her neighbors, a later explanation is ineffective. A person can thereby create a prohibited status even as against the entire world. Even according to the opinion in Yoreh Deah 1 that a butcher who marked the head of a sheep as treifah may later give an explanation, he must at least state that explanation expressly. Until he does so, one who eats the meat is subject to the consequences of eating treifah, just as where a woman established herself publicly as a niddah, her husband is liable for relations with her as a niddah. Rama explains in responsum 2 that the husband does not know of any private explanation. Accordingly, when a utensil is found marked "maaser," and no one knows of any explanation, it is prohibited because the owner rendered it forbidden. This is all the more so according to the authorities who hold that once one renders something forbidden upon himself, an explanation is ineffective if he performed an act, for Rama explains that once a status carrying lashes has been established, the person's later statement cannot cancel the consequence created by his first declaration. Monetary law differs. Even where a person performs an act, beit din may advance on his behalf the explanation that he merely did not wish to appear wealthy. Indeed, it appears that regarding maaser or tzedakah, even if he merely says verbally that the property is maaser or tzedakah, we require that it be apparent that he is scheming before we disregard his statement. If he wrote it down, beit din does not automatically advance the defense that he did so merely not to appear wealthy, because in matters where he renders something prohibited upon himself an explanation must be stated expressly, as in the case of "I am tamei." In monetary matters, by contrast, beit din does advance the defense that he did not wish to appear wealthy. There is another distinction. In monetary matters we can say that a person does not fear that the people whom he names will later sue him, because he may regard them as trustworthy not to misuse the statement. With tzedakah, however, why would he falsely designate money as tzedakah merely to avoid appearing wealthy and not fear beit din, who do not know his private intent and may collect it from him? Therefore we require visible evidence that he was scheming. Mordechai nevertheless derives the monetary rule by comparing a monetary statement that does not visibly appear to be a scheme with the maaser case where the scheme is visible. Regarding the Shach's question against Rama, who writes that the document is effective when it is in another person's possession, even though the Mishnah itself already states the rule that one who produces another's handwritten acknowledgment may collect upon it, it can be answered that Rama teaches an additional point. Even where the debtor has a migo that he could claim repayment, and is ordinarily believed to claim that the document was merely an accommodation through a migo of repayment, as explained above in siman 69, he is not believed to say that he wrote it merely to avoid appearing wealthy, even though he has such a migo.
