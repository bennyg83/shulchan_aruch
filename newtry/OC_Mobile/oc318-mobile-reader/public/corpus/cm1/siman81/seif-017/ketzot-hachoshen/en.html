His handwriting in another's hand. Rama uses this distinction to reconcile two responsa of the Rosh. The Sema reads the first as where the person orally admitted and also wrote. Knesset HaGedolah cites several distinctions from Divrei Rivot. The clearest is this: if a person could have achieved the self impoverishment purpose simply by speaking, yet went to the trouble of making a written act, people do not normally do so merely for appearance. But where he could not accomplish the purpose without writing on the very object or instrument, the writing can still be explained as self impoverishment. This principle is analogous to the laws of excuses in Yoreh Deah. A woman who said she was impure may later give a valid explanation, but if she actually put on the garments associated with impurity, Rashba says an ordinary verbal excuse no longer suffices because she need not have gone so far. Yet where the explanation itself required an act, such as a slaughterer who marked an animal as nonkosher so that others would not buy it, the act can be explained by the necessity of the purpose. The Drishah makes this distinction. So too here. If a person could have said orally, "I owe so and so," in front of many people and thereby avoid the appearance of wealth, but instead wrote a debt in his own handwriting, the excessive act undermines the self impoverishment explanation. If, however, he wrote on a particular object, "This belongs to so and so," because only writing on the object would cause viewers to think it was not his, the explanation remains plausible. Likewise, if a note states that half a particular document belongs to another, the writing may have been necessary for the intended appearance. This creates a difficulty from the Mishnah concerning a vessel marked with letters indicating consecrated property, second tithe, terumah, and so forth. If self impoverishment can explain written labels on one's property, perhaps a mark indicating consecration should also be disregarded. The answer may depend on whether the defense applies at all to consecrated property, which I discuss below.
