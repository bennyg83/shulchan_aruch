In writing. The Rama concludes that this means where the handwritten admission is in the possession of the person to whom he admitted. One cannot object that this is obvious, since the Gemara explicitly states that a creditor may collect from unencumbered property on the basis of a debtor's handwriting, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in siman 69. One might otherwise have thought that this applies only where the debtor wrote the document in the formal wording of a note, or wrote, 'My signature shall testify against me like one hundred witnesses,' in which case it is equivalent to saying, 'You are my witnesses.' The same explanation must be given to the Tur and Shulchan Aruch's references to an admission made with a kinyan or in the presence of three, namely, that they teach that it is unnecessary also to say, 'You are my witnesses,' or to make a separately formal admission. Thus concludes the Sema. The Shach writes that one may claim, 'I erred,' against one's own handwriting where one has a migo that he could have said, 'I paid,' as the Rama writes in siman 126, seif 13. See there.
Specifically. The Sema writes that it appears the Rama made this distinction so that the responsum of the Rosh cited here would not contradict what the Tur writes in his name at the end of siman 65, which the Mechaber also cites there and here in seif 18. It appears to me, however, from the wording of the Tur and the Rosh's responsum that even if the handwriting is still in the possession of the person who made the admission, he cannot claim that he wrote it only so people would not think him wealthy. The case is where he admitted before witnesses and said, 'I owe so and so one hundred, and I am also writing before you in my own hand that I owe him.' A person would not do so much merely to avoid being regarded as wealthy. There is therefore no difficulty from the ruling at the end of siman 65, because there the case is where he made no oral admission. This can also be somewhat proven from the Mechaber's returning to write in seif 19, 'One who delivers to his fellow,' and so forth. There too the case is where he made no oral admission, and therefore the document must be delivered into the creditor's hand. I later found that the responsum of Drashot HaRashba, siman 113, likewise explains that the case is where the handwriting did not reach the person to whom he admitted. Thus concludes the Sema. The Shach writes that the Sema is certainly correct that even if the handwriting remains with the writer, he cannot claim that it was written only to avoid being regarded as wealthy, and he brings proofs. See there. However, the Sema's statement that the present case involves an additional oral admission before witnesses does not appear correct. First, the Rosh's responsum never mentions an oral admission, and neither the Tur nor Shulchan Aruch mentions it. Moreover, the Rosh's responsum actually implies the opposite: he needed the reason that a person would not go to such lengths only where he both admitted and wrote before witnesses. But if a notation is merely found in his own ledger in his handwriting, the defense of avoiding an appearance of wealth does not apply in any event, because he wrote it privately and no one knew about it. For another reason, however, a writing kept in his own possession does not obligate him unless he also admitted before witnesses or wrote it in his ledger in a manner showing an existing obligation. Otherwise, we are concerned that he prepared it for use when he might later borrow, as written at the end of siman 250. As to seif 18, where it is written on a document that half of this note belongs to so and so, there is no difficulty. Because he wrote it on that very document, we say that if it were a genuine admission he should have written it separately in his ledger. Rather, he wrote it there so that one who saw that note would not regard him as wealthy. The same applies to the Mordechai's case in Zeh Borer where he wrote on the money or on the object itself. Accordingly, if one's own handwriting is found in his possession stating that he owes so and so one hundred, in circumstances where we cannot say that he merely prepared it for a future loan, and where there is likewise no concern that he later paid, the money is collected from the heirs and we do not say that he wrote it only to avoid being regarded as wealthy. The same applies if it is written in his ledger, 'This object belongs to so and so,' where the heirs cannot be pleaded for that perhaps their father later bought it, such as where the object is now seen in their possession, as explained in siman 297 and at the end of siman 107. Thus concludes the Shach.
