And specifically handwriting that is in another's possession, etc. The Rama writes this to reconcile apparently conflicting responsa of the Rosh. Here, a handwritten acknowledgment is effective, while in the following seif the Rosh and Maharam allow the writer to claim that the writing was only joking or intended to avoid appearing wealthy. The Sema asks why the Rosh labored to say that although oral words can be explained as joking or self impoverishment, a person does not perform an act for such a purpose. Is this not already the Mishnah that one who produces another's handwriting may collect from unencumbered property? The Sema and Bach offer difficult distinctions. The responsum itself appears to concern handwriting found with relatives or other people rather than in the claimant's own possession. The heirs argued that perhaps the deceased had previously given his signature blank to relatives who handled matters for him, and they later wrote whatever they wished above it. The Rosh rejects such remote suspicion. This suggests one possible meaning of the Rama: "in another's possession" may mean in the claimant's possession rather than left in the writer's own papers. A signed acknowledgment in the claimant's hand carries the presumption that it was delivered for use. A writing merely found elsewhere may not. There is a second and more substantial distinction. The Mordechai at the beginning of Perek HaNoseh challenges Rashi's interpretation of "I owe you one hundred by document" where the writing is not signed: by what act does the obligor become bound, and why can he not say he was joking? This indicates that the familiar Mishnah of handwriting collecting from unencumbered property concerns the obligor's signature. An unsigned handwritten sentence, even if in his script, is weaker. The Rosh's responsum appears deliberately to extend the rule further. He says that joking and self impoverishment are normally found only with bare speech, but "one does not perform an act" for that purpose. He adds, "whether he wrote it in his own hand or another wrote it and he signed." The phrase "he wrote it in his own hand" naturally means the body of the writing is his handwriting even without a separate signature. Otherwise he could simply have said, "his signature." Thus the Rosh's labor was needed precisely because he wanted to validate even an unsigned handwritten act, relying in part on Rashi's broader conception even though he did not adopt Rashi's entire interpretation of the Gemara. This explains why the ordinary Mishnah did not suffice. Accordingly, the Rama's distinction can be understood: an unsigned handwriting found only among the writer's own papers may have been drafted without dispositive intent and can be explained as joking or self impoverishment. If it was delivered outward as an operative act, however, the act of writing and delivery shows seriousness. This resolves the apparent contradiction without the forced readings of the later commentators.
But. The Shulchan Aruch, citing Mordechai, says that if a person writes another person's name on an object, that too may have been done to avoid appearing wealthy. This requires study. Mordechai derives the idea from the teaching that one who conceals property and says, "this belongs to so and so," may be acting deceptively. Yet the Mishnah in Maaser Sheni says that if the letter mem is written on a vessel, we presume that what is inside is maaser. Likewise, Hagahot Mordechai derives from that Mishnah that if one finds a purse marked as charity money, it is treated as charity, and the Rama rules accordingly in Yoreh Deah. Why do we not say the inscription was written merely to avoid appearing wealthy? One could answer that in the maaser case the concern is different. When a person says orally, "this is maaser," he may be saying so merely to discourage others from using it. But an inscription on the actual vessel is an act, and people do not ordinarily perform such an act merely to avoid attention. Rambam, however, rules both that an oral declaration may be deceptive and that a written mem on the vessel establishes maaser, confirming that distinction. The same should then apply to a charity inscription. Therefore the broad rule that one can explain an inscription on the object itself as merely self impoverishment requires study, especially since it appears to conflict with the Rama's own ruling elsewhere.
It is possible to say that he wrote it so as not to appear wealthy. The Shach writes that if the notation was merely written privately among one's own papers, rather than on the instrument itself, self impoverishment is more plausible because no one knows what a person writes privately at home. Rosh says this explicitly. Even so, if the writer is alive and himself insists, "I wrote it only to avoid appearing wealthy," I am uncertain whether we can extract money from him merely by rejecting that explanation. The Rosh's discussion concerns what beit din should claim for heirs on uncertain grounds, and an unusual claim is not ordinarily made for them. A living person who expressly makes the claim may be different. This may be why the Mechaber and Rama did not codify the distinction in the unqualified form stated by the Shach. The matter requires study.
