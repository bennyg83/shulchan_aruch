If he admitted in his own handwriting, etc. Even though he has a migo that he could have said, 'I paid,' he is not believed to claim that the writing was merely joking or made so that people would not think him wealthy. We effectively witness that one does not write such an admission for those purposes. He is, however, believed to say, 'I made a mistake,' through the migo that he could have claimed payment.
That is in his fellow's possession. Rama writes this to reconcile the rule with the following seif. Sema asks: if the writing is in the other party's possession, this is obvious from the Mishnah, which says that one who produces another person's handwriting may collect from unencumbered property. He gives a strained answer. According to what I wrote above there is no difficulty: the novelty is that the writer lacks even the migo of saying, 'I paid,' against the nature of this admission. In Rosh's case, however, this cannot be the entire answer, because the writing was an admission that all the property belonged to the writer's father, where a payment claim is irrelevant. In Tumim I gave two explanations. Either the handwriting was not in the claimant's possession but in a third party's hands, and it was not known whether it had ever been delivered to the third party in the claimant's presence; or the novelty is that the writer did not sign his name at all, but merely wrote the statement in his own handwriting, and nevertheless once it is in another person's possession it has force. See what I wrote above in siman 69. Sema's answer is strained and is not sufficient reason to reject Rama. Knesset HaGedolah likewise writes that many authorities explain like Rama, and this is correct.
It is possible to say, etc. The Shach writes that if a person wrote the admission privately in his ledger, he cannot say it was written so that people would not think him wealthy. Who would see what is written inside his private ledger? See Tumim, where I suggested a distinction between heirs and the litigant himself.
