But only when. The Rama writes this in order to reconcile two responsa of the Rosh with one another, and also so that the Mechaber’s words below in seif 18 should not contradict this seif. It also resolves the statement of Mordechai in the chapter Zeh Borer that when one writes another person’s name upon a particular object or upon particular money, we say that he did so merely in order not to appear wealthy. The Rama therefore explains that here the handwritten acknowledgment is in the other person’s possession. The Sema disagrees with the Rama, while Bach disagrees with the Sema and concurs with the Rama. In my humble opinion, neither position is correct in its entirety. The Rama and Bach’s assumption that the present case concerns a handwritten acknowledgment held by the other person does not appear correct. Rather, on this point the Sema’s view is primary. First, the Rosh bases his reasoning upon the following statement at the beginning of responsum collection 65: “It appears that since his handwriting exists, we do not say that a person commonly acts so as not to make his sons appear wealthy. We find such a principle only regarding a mere oral statement, such as, ‘Who has claims against me other than so-and-so and so-and-so?’ or a dangerously ill person who says, ‘I owe one hundred to so-and-so.’ But when it is in his own handwriting, or another wrote it and he affixed a recognizable and verifiable signature, a person does not go to such lengths merely so as not to make his sons appear wealthy.” This implies that because the claim of not wishing to appear wealthy is not found with respect to handwriting, we say that a person does not go to such lengths even when the writing remains in his own possession. Second, if the writing was in the other person’s possession, why did the Rosh need to say that we do not find this principle regarding writing? The entire Talmud is filled with the rule that one who produces the debtor’s handwriting may collect from unencumbered property. Although the Sema and Bach attempt to answer this, their explanations are strained. Bach also writes that it is clear from the Rosh’s responsum that the handwriting was produced from the other person’s possession, because at the end of the responsum the Rosh rejects the heirs’ claim that perhaps the deceased’s signature had been in the claimant’s possession and the claimant wrote whatever he wished above it. The Rosh responds that if this were a valid claim, the mishna’s rule that one who produces the debtor’s handwriting may collect from unencumbered property could never apply. Bach adds that the case cannot be understood as one in which the deceased wrote and signed the document but did not deliver it, because even a deed of gift made with a kinyan and witnesses but found tied to the donor’s thigh is ineffective, as taught in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, and the same is true even if he deposited the deed with another, as stated above in siman 55, seif 10. All the more so, mere handwriting would be ineffective. Bach therefore regards the matter as obvious and says that the Rama likewise ruled this way, unlike Maharu. These words are unconvincing. Even if the actual case there involved a document in the claimant’s possession, the Rosh’s reason that this principle is found only with oral statements, whereas one does not make such an elaborate written acknowledgment merely to avoid appearing wealthy, implies that the same rule applies even to handwriting retained by the writer. Nor is Bach’s comparison to a deed of gift found tied to the donor’s thigh valid. It is true that there, even a promissory note is ineffective in such circumstances, as I write below at the end of siman 250. That is because it is not known that the document was written as an admission. Perhaps he merely prepared a witnessed loan document intending to deliver it and later reconsidered. The present case is different. See above, siman 65, seif katan 53. A proof appears from the Rosh’s responsum, collection 86, siman 1, concerning a ledger in which the deceased had written in his own hand: “A record of the pledges I received from Reuven’s one thousand gold coins for a profit-sharing arrangement.” The Rosh ruled that the money could be recovered from the heirs. He added that the principle that a person may make an admission merely so as not to appear wealthy does not apply, because that principle applies only where a person says before others, “I owe so-and-so and so-and-so,” intending that people should not regard him as wealthy. But here he wrote the entry privately in his ledger, and no one knew or heard of it. Thus, even handwriting retained by the writer is not treated as an admission made merely to avoid appearing wealthy. The Sema writes that the Rosh’s responsum in collection 65 and the Mechaber here concern a case where the debtor orally admitted before witnesses that he owed one hundred and then said, “I am also writing before you in my own hand that I owe him,” for going to such lengths is not done merely to avoid appearing wealthy. The Sema says this removes the difficulty from the end of siman 65 and from seif 18 below, where there was no oral admission. In my opinion this explanation is incorrect. First, the Rosh’s responsum never mentions an oral admission. Neither the Tur nor the Mechaber mentions it. Second, the responsum in collection 86 concerning the entry in the debtor’s ledger implies the opposite, namely, that making such a statement before witnesses would weaken the inference, because it could be intended to publicize an apparent lack of wealth. The correct explanation is therefore the reverse. The Rosh at the beginning of collection 65 and the Tur here needed the reason that a person does not go to such lengths merely to avoid appearing wealthy only where he both admitted and wrote before witnesses. But where the entry is found privately in his ledger, the claim of not wishing to appear wealthy does not apply at all, because he wrote it privately and no one knew of it. There is, however, a different reason why handwriting retained by the writer would ordinarily not create liability unless he also admitted before witnesses that he owed the money or wrote it in a ledger. Otherwise, we may suspect that he prepared the writing for delivery if a future loan were made, as stated below at the end of siman 250. As for the Rosh’s statement in collection 68, siman 15, and the Tur and Mechaber at the end of siman 65 and below in seif 18, that where one wrote upon a document, “Half of this belongs to so-and-so,” we say that he wrote it merely not to appear wealthy, there is no initial difficulty. There he wrote the notation upon the document itself. We reason that if it were a genuine admission, why did he write it upon the document rather than separately in his ledger? He evidently wrote it there so that anyone who saw the document would not think the entire debt belonged to him and regard him as wealthy. The same applies to Mordechai’s case in Zeh Borer, where one wrote another person’s name upon the money or object itself. We say that he wrote it there so that anyone seeing the object in his possession would not assume it belonged entirely to him. This appears clear. Accordingly, where a person’s own handwriting is found in his possession stating that he owes another one hundred, in circumstances where one cannot say that he merely prepared the document for delivery upon a future loan, such as where he admitted before witnesses that the other had lent him the money and that he was recording it in his own handwriting, or where an entry is found in his ledger stating that he owes a specified amount to another payable on a specified date and he dies before that date, so that there is no concern that he repaid it, the debt is collected from the heirs. We do not say that he wrote it merely not to appear wealthy. This is also evident from Rashba’s responsum and the Mechaber below at the end of siman 107, and from the words of Tashbetz that I cite above in siman 69, seif 5, seif katan 27. The same follows where his ledger states, “This object belongs to so-and-so.” It may be recovered from the heirs where no claim can be advanced for them that their father subsequently purchased it, such as where the object is now seen in their possession, as discussed below in siman 297. I later found that Maharam Alshich, responsum 137, reconciles the Rosh’s responsa by distinguishing the case where one writes upon the note, “Half belongs to so-and-so,” because that notation contradicts the note’s own testimony, whereas the present case contains no contrary indication. This explanation also appears strained to me. What I have written appears clear. The Sema further records that Divrei Rivot, responsum 113, offers strained answers to this issue.
