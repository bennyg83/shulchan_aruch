If he admitted in his own handwriting, etc. Rama concludes that this refers to a handwritten acknowledgment that is in the possession of the person to whom he admitted. One might ask that this is obvious, because the Mishnah and Gemara already establish that a person's handwritten obligation may be collected from unencumbered property, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 69. The novelty is that one might otherwise have thought that this applies only when he writes the standard language of a handwritten promissory note, or expressly states that his signature shall testify against him like one hundred witnesses, which is equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses." The same explanation is needed for the Tur and Mechaber's concluding cases of an admission accompanied by a kinyan or made through ma'amad sheloshtan. They teach that no additional designation of witnesses or formal language of admission is required. See what I write further nearby.
And as for what Rama writes, "But if it was found written among his own papers, etc." It appears that Rama draws this distinction so that the present responsum of the Rosh not contradict what the Tur cites in the name of the Rosh at the end of siman 65, which the Mechaber also states there and again in the next seif here: if it is found written among a person's papers that a certain note belongs half to another person, he may say that he wrote this only so as not to make himself appear wealthy. See there. In the Perishah and Derishah I wrote that the Tur's language and the Rosh's responsum indicate that even if the handwritten acknowledgment remains in the possession of the person who made the admission, he cannot make the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy where he verbally admitted before witnesses and said, "I owe so and so one hundred, and I am also writing before you in my own hand that I owe him." A person does not ordinarily go to such lengths merely to avoid appearing wealthy. This does not conflict with the case at the end of siman 65, because there he did not separately admit orally before witnesses. This interpretation is also somewhat supported by the Mechaber's repetition below in seif 19: "One who delivers to his fellow a document stating that he owes him one hundred has made an admission." Why repeat the law already stated here in seif 17? One might answer that seif 19 concerns a document neither written nor signed by the debtor himself and without witness signatures, but written by another and delivered before witnesses. Yet a document delivered before witnesses may in any event be collectible even from encumbered property, unlike an ordinary handwritten obligation not delivered before witnesses, as explained above in siman 40, so that would contain little novelty. According to my explanation, however, it is clear. Here the defendant made an oral admission before witnesses and also wrote it, so the writing is effective even while still in his own possession. In seif 19 he did not orally admit before witnesses, and therefore the document must actually be delivered to the person in whose favor the admission was made. See the Derishah for further discussion. I later found responsum 113 of Divrei Rivot, which also interprets the case as one where the handwritten acknowledgment did not come into the possession of the person to whom he admitted, though the responsum gives strained resolutions to the apparent contradiction between the two responsa of the Rosh.
