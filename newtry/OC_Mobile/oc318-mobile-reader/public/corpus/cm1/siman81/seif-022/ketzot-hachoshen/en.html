He admitted on his own. I already proved above that, according to Tosafot in Bava Kamma, even a volunteered admission before an expert ordained beit din does not bind where the true claimant did not demand it. Shiltei Gibborim cites Rav Hai Gaon differently. Rav Hai says an admission after demand is binding, while a volunteered admission can be withdrawn because "the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted." Shiltei Gibborim tries to reconcile this with the sugya in Perek Zeh Borer, where an admission after demand may still be explained as joking, by reading Rav Hai's "volunteered admission" as a particular configuration involving "You are my witnesses." This reconciliation is not convincing. The Gemara says expressly that whether the borrower himself says "You are my witnesses" or the lender says it and the borrower remains silent, the formal admission can bind. Once the borrower himself says the formula in response to the claim, he cannot later withdraw. There is no general power of "the mouth that prohibited" after such a formal admission. The dispute among authorities is real. Several hold that a volunteered admission can still be explained as joking, and some even advance that explanation automatically. Tosafot is especially stringent about the need that the admission be to the proper litigant, deriving the requirement from the verse underlying partial admission, which itself follows a claim. Since the Torah's special credibility of a litigant's admission is learned from "this is it," where the admission comes in response to a claim, there is strong reason to require an actual claimant's demand for the strongest form of monetary admission. This supports the authorities who say a volunteered statement alone is not fully binding unless it is formalized through "You are my witnesses" and made in the proper litigational setting.
