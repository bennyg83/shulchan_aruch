A beit din of three. The Tur writes that the same applies before a single qualified expert judge. Agudat Ezov infers from the Mechaber’s phrase “a beit din of three” that three are specifically required. Although in siman 3 Beit Yosef rules with the Tur that an expert may judge alone, Agudat Ezov suggests that an admission is different, as Rambam distinguishes in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sanhedrin. This suggestion was unnecessary, for the Mechaber himself expressly makes that distinction above in siman 3, seif 2, following Rambam. See my comments there in seif katan 8.
Whether he admitted of his own accord, etc., he may retract only within the time required for an utterance. This ruling is not clear to me. The Tur states it without qualification in the name of a responsum of a Gaon, taken from Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, and Beit Yosef and the later authorities cite no dissent. Since several novel rules depend upon the matter, it requires extended analysis. The Tosefta at the beginning of Bava Metzia teaches: “The admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. When is this so? When he was sued and admitted. But if he admitted of his own accord, he may retract, for the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted.” This plainly concerns retraction after the brief interval for immediate retraction, since within that interval even one who was sued and admitted may retract. We cannot dismiss an explicit Tosefta merely because it is not quoted in the Babylonian Gemara, when the Gemara states nothing contrary. Rif and Rosh cite it at the beginning of Bava Metzia, as do Halachot Gedolot, many other authorities, Maharam of Rothenburg, and Mordechai. Rav Hai Gaon likewise cites it in Mishpetei Shevuot. Baal HaTerumot does not actually quote a responsum of a Gaon for the Tur’s rule. He writes that some early authorities held that an admission before beit din may not be retracted after the immediate interval even if volunteered, provided it was made before three judges, whereas an admission before a single expert is ineffective. Baal HaItur adopts that position. He reads the Tosefta as concerning an admission outside beit din, before witnesses without the formula “You are my witnesses.” That reading is difficult. The phrase “the admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses” naturally refers to an admission before beit din, where the parties are litigants and testimony has legal force. The continuation concerning a third-party depositary, quoted in Gittin, likewise describes parties standing before beit din. Rav Hai Gaon and Rabbeinu Yerucham expressly understand the Tosefta as speaking of admission before beit din. Many authorities therefore rule that a volunteered admission before beit din may be retracted. These include the Tosefta, Halachot Gedolot, Rav Hai Gaon, Rif, Rosh, Mordechai, Maharam, Baal HaMaor, Ramah, Nimukei Yosef, Maharam of Recanati in the name of Or Zarua, Responsa Ramban, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. Baal HaMaor distinguishes between an admission made in response to a demand, for which beit din does not supply the defense of jesting, and a volunteered admission, for which it does. Raavad objects that the Tosefta itself says only that he may retract, implying that retraction is required. In my view, however, the Tosefta speaks of an admission before beit din, while Raavad understood it as an admission before witnesses. There is therefore no proof from Raavad against Baal HaMaor’s reading of the Tosefta. Nevertheless, the Tosefta and Rav Hai Gaon should not be understood as permitting a person who made a formal, deliberate admission before beit din to say that he had been jesting. If he said expressly, “I hereby admit before you, as a binding admission, that I owe so-and-so,” it is unreasonable to treat the statement as jest. A person who makes a complete formal admission before beit din is presumed serious. I explained above in seif 8, seif katan 18, what constitutes a formal admission. This reading also preserves as much as possible of the position of Baal HaItur, Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber. Indeed, in one respect the position of Baal HaItur, Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber appears correct: even where an admission before beit din was volunteered and expressed in ordinary conversational form, one should not ordinarily permit the defense, “I was jesting.” A person who speaks before beit din is presumed not to jest. The Tosefta’s phrase “he may retract,” together with “the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted,” does not naturally describe jesting, for if he was merely jesting he never truly imposed a prohibition upon himself. Rather, it means that he may retract by saying, “When I admitted, I believed the facts to be so, but I have now remembered and discovered that I was mistaken.” His original admission was genuine, and his present retraction is a claim of error. This is why the phrase “the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted” fits. As explained below in siman 126, seif 13, a claim of error is stronger than a claim of jesting. This understanding is also supported by the many authorities who hold that the defense of jesting applies only where the claimant first demanded payment, whereas the Tosefta permits retraction after a volunteered admission before beit din. They must understand the Tosefta as allowing a claim of remembered error, not a claim of jesting. Even though I ruled above in seif 5, seif katan 12, that jesting may be claimed after a volunteered admission before witnesses, the authorities’ treatment of the Tosefta still proves that an admission before beit din is different. Accordingly, Rif, Rosh, and Maharam may all accept the Tosefta as permitting a volunteered admission before beit din to be retracted on the ground, “I remembered that I was mistaken.” Rosh must be understood this way, for in Zeh Borer he holds that a volunteered admission before witnesses does not permit the defense of jesting. There is also a special case in which all should agree that jesting may be claimed: the claimant demanded wheat and the defendant admitted barley, or the claimant asserted one matter and the defendant responded with a different matter. The very fact that he ignored the claim and answered about something else supports the inference that he was mocking or deflecting the claimant. Ramah, Raavad, Mordechai, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and others support this distinction. The disagreement of Rosh with Ramah in the case of wheat and barley concerns whether the admission to barley is independently binding when the defendant stands by it, not whether a defendant who expressly says, “I was jesting when I spoke of barley,” may rely upon that defense. The result is as follows. If one is sued before beit din and admits the claim, he may not retract after the immediate interval under any theory. If he volunteers an admission before beit din, he may retract by saying, “I was mistaken in my admission,” but he may not ordinarily say, “I was jesting.” If he was sued concerning one matter and admitted an entirely different matter, as where wheat was claimed and barley admitted, he may say, “I was jesting.” This is the principal ruling, notwithstanding the unqualified language of Baal HaItur, Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber. It is possible that even they intended only that he may not retract by claiming jesting, since their discussion concerns the rules of jesting, while they would agree that he may plead a genuine computational or factual error.
