Whether he admitted on his own, etc. The Shach in seif katan 56 extensively challenges Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber, and brings a large number of authorities who, in his view, disagree and hold that one who makes an unsolicited admission may retract even in beit din. He counts authorities, but there is no actual refutation, because all of them merely cite the Tosefta: "An admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. When? When the other party demanded payment and he admitted. But if he admitted on his own, he may retract." Certainly the halachah follows the Tosefta, but it does not follow that the Tosefta concerns an admission in beit din. This is especially so according to the Shulchan Aruch, which rules above in seif 15 like the Raavad that if the claimant remains silent when the defendant says, "You are my witnesses," the formula is ineffective. The Raavad derives that very rule from this Tosefta, so the Tosefta already has a clear interpretation outside beit din. Although I wrote above in seif katan 23 that the Raavad may establish the Tosefta in beit din and derive from it by analogy the same rule outside beit din where the defendant says, "You are my witnesses," there is no necessity to say this. It can simply concern a case outside beit din, in accordance with the Raavad's explanation, as I discussed at length above. Even according to those who disagree with the Raavad, I clarified above in seif katan 24 that according to the Rambam the Tosefta can concern a formal admission made without saying, "You are my witnesses," while according to the Rosh it can concern saying, "You are my witnesses," outside the claimant's presence. In either event, the entire case can be outside beit din. Even if one were to understand it as occurring in beit din, it would have to be outside the claimant's presence, because according to the Rambam the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy does not apply in the claimant's presence. Once we are already interpreting "he admitted on his own" as meaning outside the claimant's presence, why force the case into beit din at all? The Shach himself writes that it is strained to say that such a defense applies in beit din, and therefore suggests instead that the defendant can retract by saying, "I was mistaken." This is even more strained. Why should we accept a claim of mistake when he has no migo? It is much smoother to understand the Tosefta as referring to a case outside beit din, as Tosafot explain and as I wrote above in seifim 23 and 24. There is therefore no proof from all the authorities who cite the Tosefta that they hold one may retract from an unsolicited admission made in beit din. The Shach follows his own view below in siman 88, where he rules like Ramah in the case of one who was sued for wheat and admitted barley, explaining the exemption by joking. For that reason he was driven to extend the possibility of retraction even to beit din. But since we do not rule like Ramah, as I explain below in siman 88, there is no need for this strained conclusion. We can simply say that in beit din a litigant cannot retract at all, and everything is consistent. According to the Shach's attempt to attribute to Rav Hai Gaon the view that one who makes an unsolicited admission can retract even in beit din, there is an additional difficulty. Why does Rav Hai Gaon need to say in the next seif that the statement is merely superfluous conversation? He could have said more simply that the admission was made to the other party without any prior demand, and even if it were a genuine admission he could retract. Necessarily, if it were a true admission he could not retract.
