But if he denies. It appears that he must take an oath that he did not give the formal handshake. Siman 87 says that one who is accused of having given a handshake to make a gift is exempt from an oath, because the only obligation to give arose through the oath like handshake itself, and we do not impose one oath concerning whether another oath was taken. Here, however, the underlying debt existed independently. The handshake serves only to remove the defenses of joking and self impoverishment, much like "You are my witnesses." Therefore if the claimant says, "You formally admitted with a handshake," and the defendant denies it, an ordinary oath is required just as where one says, "You admitted before me with You are my witnesses." The same applies if the person who made the admission dies. If beit din knows that he admitted the debt under oath or formal handshake, the heirs must pay because the form of the admission proves it was serious, just like "You are my witnesses." This differs from one who merely promised a future gift by handshake. There the heirs do not inherit the personal oath obligation. The reason for the rule in siman 87 also does not apply. There we say that one suspected of violating the first oath cannot sensibly be subjected to another oath about it. Here the defendant did not swear that he would repay. He swore only that the debt existed. Failure to pay does not itself establish that he violated that oath. He therefore remains fit to take the judicial oath denying that the handshake occurred.
