Specifically where he admits concerning the matter, etc. This implies that even if he took the oath outside the presence of witnesses and later says, "I swore, but I was mistaken," then although he has a migo that he could have denied ever taking the oath, he is not believed to claim mistake. This is contrary to Maharshal's view that where a migo exists he is believed, as the Shach cites in seif katan 68. I myself remain uncertain. Certainly, with a migo that he could have claimed payment, he is not believed. But where he admitted only outside witnesses' presence, the matter requires study. The source is Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, part 7, section 2, and there it explicitly says that he swore in the presence of witnesses. The Rif likewise writes that an oath taken before witnesses is powerful and must be fulfilled without any need for a kinyan. Baal HaTerumot later similarly writes that once witnesses came and established that he had sworn falsely, he is not believed to say that he had forgotten and later remembered, etc. Their wording carefully emphasizes that witnesses were present at the oath. The Rif also says that an oath is equivalent to the witnesses having made a kinyan from him. The Ran holds that one who admits privately can claim mistake even after a kinyan, as in the incident of the gardeners, because a person is not as precise when he speaks outside witnesses' presence, as explained below in siman 126 and as I wrote above in seif katan 33. If the oath by itself were so powerful that witnesses were unnecessary, the Rif should not have compared it specifically to witnesses taking a kinyan. He should simply have said that an oath is stronger than an ordinary admission. His comparison indicates that the oath must be taken before witnesses. Therefore this ruling requires study.
