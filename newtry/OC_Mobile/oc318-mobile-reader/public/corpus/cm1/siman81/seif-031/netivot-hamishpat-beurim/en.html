The law is with Reuven. See Sema, seif katan 14, who quotes the responsum of the Rosh through the statement that Levi's claim is no claim because he did not deliver a protest before witnesses. At first glance this is difficult, because the Sema's wording implies that this applies specifically where Reuven could collect from whichever partner he wished, and that is likewise implied by the Mechaber and other authorities who quote the responsum. Yet if the rationale is merely that Levi failed to issue a formal protest, there should be no such distinction. It therefore appears that there is a textual omission and the passage should read: "Furthermore, Levi's claim that they were compelled because of the document is no claim, since he did not issue a protest," and so forth. There are thus two independent reasons. First, Reuven had the right to demand the entire debt from whichever partner he chose. Second, Levi did not issue a protest, and by later paying without qualification he demonstrated consent, so his later claim is ineffective. According to the first reason, Levi's argument would fail even if he had issued a protest. Nevertheless, the Sema's statement that this applies specifically when Levi does not have sufficient funds to pay his share remains difficult. Even if Levi does have funds, once the partner denies liability he is treated as though he has no available funds, as explained above in siman 77, seif 5, Shach seif katan 12. The stated rationale is also not entirely clear. It therefore appears that because Shimon initially paid the one hundred himself, according to the Rosh, who treats the partners as primary obligors for one another, it is as though Shimon himself had borrowed. According to Reuven's claim, Shimon had previously admitted that Reuven was entitled to twenty-five gold coins as profit on his share. Reuven could therefore seize twenty-five from the hundred that Shimon paid and apply it to the profit, as explained in siman 83, leaving the promissory note in force for the remaining twenty-five. Reuven would then be believed even though Shimon denies having made such an admission, because the note remains in Reuven's possession. According to our ruling, however, that the partners are not primary obligors for one another but only guarantors, when Shimon himself paid the one hundred he could not have all twenty-five applied to the profits before Levi was first pursued. Perhaps Levi would admit and pay his own portion. Even beit din that collected from a guarantor before first pursuing the borrower must reverse the collection, and one may not seize even the borrower's property before pursuing the borrower personally, because a person's property is only a guarantor for him, as explained in Bava Batra and Bekhorot. Thus Reuven could apply only twelve and a half gold coins to the profits attributable to Shimon's share. The rest must be treated as principal. Consequently the promissory note's lien was discharged to the extent of eighty-seven and a half gold coins. Reuven can therefore claim from Shimon only Shimon's own half, namely twelve and a half. He cannot claim the other twelve and a half attributable to Levi, because once the note's lien has been discharged as to that share, possession of the document does not make him believed through a migo in order to extract money, as explained in siman 57, seif 2, and siman 83, seif 4. This is specifically where the profits have not yet been paid, for then reliance on a migo would be to extract money. If, however, they already paid him the profits, even under compulsion, they cannot later recover them if they failed to issue a formal protest, which is the Rosh's second rationale. The Sema's objection is therefore only that the Shulchan Aruch did not state the Rosh's second rationale, which implies that even if a protest was made, or even if the payment had not yet been made, Reuven could still collect by virtue of the note. That is true only if the partners are primary obligors. When the Sema writes that this applies specifically when Levi has no money, his intent is that Levi was already pursued and claimed that he had nothing. When he writes that Reuven could collect from partnership funds found in Shimon's possession, the intent is that Reuven could do so if additional partnership funds beyond the one hundred had already been given to Shimon, even if Levi had issued a protest. Although all of this is somewhat strained in the Sema's wording, the halachic conclusion appears clear. Ketzot HaChoshen objects further that no guaranty for the profits can arise without a kinyan, as the Shach writes in Yoreh Deah 170, seif katan 5. This is not difficult. The Shach's rule applies to profits received from a non-Jew. In an iska arrangement, by contrast, the case resembles two people who jointly received a deposit. According to the Rosh they are primary obligors for one another, and certainly each is a primary obligor even for the increase produced by the deposited capital, because they jointly accepted responsibility to safeguard both the deposited principal and its increase. The increase comes into their hands and they conduct business with it for their own benefit. According to those who hold that two joint recipients of a deposit are only guarantors, they are likewise guarantors for the increase, because if one withdrew from the business they would have to return the funds to the investor. By continuing to deal with the funds, each causes money to remain outstanding on the other's reliance, and each therefore becomes guarantor for his fellow with respect to later losses of the increase. This differs from siman 129, where Shimon alone received money as an iska and Levi separately became guarantor for the future profit. That resembles a guarantor who undertakes liability after the money has already been given. When Shimon later receives profits, those profits do not come into his hands because of Levi, while when Levi became guarantor the profits had not yet come into Shimon's hands. This is straightforward. Nor should one object to the Rosh's second rationale that because they failed to issue a protest their payment is effective. One might argue that this payment resembles a gift, and with a gift coercion is sufficient to invalidate the transfer even without a formal protest, unlike a sale. Since the responsum of the Rosh speaks of compulsion, why should a protest be required? This is not difficult. An admission is different. Only where one gives a gift without any demand does coercion alone suffice. But where the claimant demanded payment and the other paid voluntarily without a judicial ruling, even if he did so because of pressure, the payment operates as an admission unless he issued a protest.
