The law is in Reuven's favor. The Tur cites the Rosh: Since Reuven lent jointly to Shimon and Levi, they became jointly responsible and guarantors for one another, and Reuven had the power to demand the entire debt from either one. He demanded it from Shimon, and Reuven swore that Shimon had told him that the profit was as stated, and they gave him from it his portion. Reuven was thereby discharged from any further claim by Shimon and Levi, since they voluntarily paid their debt. Levi's claim that they acted under duress because of the document is no claim, since he did not make a prior declaration before witnesses. This reasoning fits the Rosh, who holds that the creditor may collect the entire debt from either joint borrower. According to the Rambam, however, whom the Mechaber and Rama follow at the end of siman 77, he may not collect the entire debt from one while the other can pay his share. The Mechaber therefore should seemingly have limited this ruling to a case where Levi lacked funds for his share. Nor can it be answered that Reuven could collect from partnership funds in Shimon's possession, because the responsum and the Mechaber imply that the only partnership money was the maneh Reuven had given them, which they had already returned. The Rosh also specifically says that Reuven swore that Shimon told him so, while the Mechaber omits this. This latter point can be resolved: to prevent Levi from recovering from Reuven, Reuven need not already have sworn, since Levi cannot assert with certainty that Shimon never made that statement. The Rosh mentions the oath only for a case where Shimon himself asserts with certainty that Reuven is lying. Then Reuven must swear against Shimon's definite claim, and once he has sworn they have no further claim against him.
