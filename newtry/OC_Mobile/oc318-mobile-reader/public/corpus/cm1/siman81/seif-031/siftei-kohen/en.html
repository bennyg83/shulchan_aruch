The law is with Reuven. The Sema quotes Rosh’s responsum: because Reuven lent jointly to Shimon and Levi, they became mutually responsible and guarantors, so Reuven could collect the entire debt from either. Reuven demanded payment from Shimon, swore that Shimon had told him the money represented Reuven’s share, and received it; Reuven thereby withdrew from both Shimon and Levi because they paid him knowingly. The Sema asks that this reasoning fits Rosh, who permits collection of the entire debt from either joint borrower, but not Rambam, whose view the Mechaber and Rama follow in siman 77, under which one may not collect the other borrower’s share while that borrower can pay. He therefore says the present ruling should be limited to a case in which Levi lacks property. This is mistaken. Rosh did not make the validity of Shimon’s payment depend upon the rule of joint guaranty. Shimon plainly had authority, even initially, to hand Reuven all the money in his possession while asserting that it belonged to Reuven. The same principle appears below in siman 93, seif 13: where Shimon says the partnership owes a debt and he holds enough partnership money, he may pay that debt before the partners settle accounts. Here payment was already made, and Reuven says Shimon identified it as Reuven’s profit. Shimon was believed because he held the money. Rosh mentioned mutual responsibility only to explain that Reuven could have sued either partner, not as the legal basis of the completed payment. The Mechaber’s ruling is therefore correct according to all views. In any event, there is no distinction between a Levi who owns property and one who does not, because Levi denies the debt. A denying debtor is treated, for this purpose, like one without collectible property, as Baal HaItur and Hagahot Mordechai expressly state. See my comments above in siman 77, seif 5, seif katan 12.
The Sema further asks that Rosh’s responsum states that Reuven swore Shimon had made the statement, whereas the Mechaber does not mention the oath. The Sema answers correctly that Levi cannot recover from Reuven even before Reuven swears, since Levi cannot state with certainty that Shimon never made the statement. Rosh mentioned the oath only because Shimon himself denied having spoken, in which case Reuven had to swear against Shimon. The Bach writes that Rosh’s ruling rests upon the fact that Shimon gave the money as profit; Levi can no longer recover it from Reuven, and Reuven’s oath is not the legal foundation of the result. He criticizes the Sema for making the ruling depend upon the oath. This criticism is misplaced, because the Sema himself says that the oath is unnecessary as against Levi and was required only against Shimon, who denied the statement.
