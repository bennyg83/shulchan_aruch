For even according to Reuven's words, etc. This implies that if the recipient voluntarily wishes to give the profits, even though no condition was made when the money was originally advanced, it is permissible for the lender to accept them; the lender simply cannot compel payment through beit din. This requires explanation. The lender's entitlement to profit is based on treating half the funds as a deposit. If their later conversation can convert half the loan into a deposit, why is the recipient not legally obligated to remit the profit earned on the lender's deposit? And if their conversation does not convert it, how may the lender accept profit without creating later interest? It appears that the lender can, by verbal declaration, change part of the money in the borrower's possession from loan to deposit by relinquishing the borrower's ordinary loan liability for that half. He can say: "From now on this half is not at your risk as a loan. It is a deposit, and you may use it like loose money deposited with a moneychanger." But that does not automatically create an obligation upon the recipient to surrender the profits. If one deposits money with another and permits its use, why should that permission alone obligate payment of profit? Still, a difficulty remains. If the deposit half is lost through unavoidable accident and the recipient has not undertaken to give profits, his use of the deposited money ordinarily turns it into a loan for which he bears full responsibility. If he does give the profits in the proper iska form, he may bear only the liability of a paid custodian on the deposit half. How, then, can we say it remains entirely optional whether he gives profits? If he keeps all profits, he appears to enjoy full benefit and should bear the stronger liability, which in turn makes the money fully his and raises the interest problem again. I discussed this at length in Yoreh Deah, where the laws of interest are the proper place for the issue.
