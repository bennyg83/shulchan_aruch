One who delivers a note to another. The Mechaber abbreviated this law. Know that where one delivers to another, before witnesses, a note stating, “So-and-so owes so-and-so one hundred,” or, “I owe you one hundred,” even though the note is not in the debtor’s handwriting, it constitutes an admission. He need not say, “You are my witnesses,” and he cannot claim jesting or that he merely wished not to appear wealthy. This is Rashi’s position at the beginning of the chapter HaNoseh in Ketubot, cited by the Tur above at the beginning of siman 40. Although Rabbeinu Tam and the other authorities interpret the passage differently, namely, as discussing one who creates a new personal obligation, they do not appear to disagree with Rashi’s practical ruling. Ritva writes there that Rabbeinu Tam’s interpretation is correct, but Rashi’s legal conclusion is nevertheless true. Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri ask that since the note is not in his handwriting, he should be able to claim, “I was jesting with you.” I do not understand their difficulty. Rashi himself guarded against it by explaining that because he delivered the note before witnesses, it is as though he said, “You are my witnesses.” They also challenge Rashi from the Gemara’s question concerning one who writes an obligation to a priest, arguing that there the person signed his name. Rashi likewise anticipated this and answered it in the language cited above: if it were a signed note already in the creditor’s possession, what novelty would the law contain? Tosafot write this explicitly. See also Ritva’s defense of Rashi. Bach, at the beginning of siman 40, deletes words from the Tur’s text, challenges Beit Yosef, and raises a difficulty from siman 50. None of his comments there is correct. Examine the matter carefully.
The same rule applies if he said in this formulation, “I owe you one hundred by note.” It appears that since, according to Rambam and the Mechaber above in siman 40, a person may create a new obligation upon himself through such language, it certainly suffices as an admission. Bach likewise explains the words of Ramah cited by the Tur in siman 40 this way. Rav Hai Gaon also writes this in Mishpetei Shevuot, page 8, end of the first side. It appears that even Ramban and Rashba, who disagree with Rambam concerning creation of a new obligation, concede that this language is effective as an admission. Examine the matter carefully.
