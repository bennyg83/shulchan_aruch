An admission before one witness. The Shach discusses this at length and concludes that the party in possession can say kim li in accordance with Baal HaMaor and those who support him, who hold that an admission before one witness is entirely ineffective even if the defendant says, "You are my witnesses." The Shach nevertheless explains, according to their view, the accepted rule that one admission can combine with another admission differently from the Ramban's explanation of their opinion. The Ramban says that each admission was actually made before two witnesses, except that one witness later departed. The Shach instead explains that each admission was made ordinarily before only one witness, yet because the two witnesses ultimately combine there are now two witnesses. The words of Maharit in Choshen Mishpat siman 18 also incline somewhat toward this distinction. In my opinion this is incorrect. Baal HaMaor's entire inference is from the Mishnah's wording: "If he says, 'He told me,' he has said nothing, until he says, 'He admitted to him before us.'" The first clause uses the singular and the latter the plural, which, according to Baal HaMaor, shows that an admission before one witness is ineffective. Yet it is certain that the Mishnah itself is not speaking of a case with only one witness. There are two witnesses, but the court examines each one separately, as the Mishnah explicitly states, and afterward brings in the second witness. If their accounts are found consistent, etc. Thus there are two witnesses. The difficulty therefore remains: if the first witness, examined alone, says, "He told me," why does the Mishnah state categorically that this is nothing? If the second witness later gives the same account, the combined testimony would be complete testimony sufficient to extract money. Necessarily, once Rav Huna's explanation is rejected, which had established the Mishnah as one admission after another where the debtor said, "The same one hundred that I admitted before you I also admitted before your fellow," we must explain, as the Ramban does even according to Baal HaMaor, that each admission was made before two people, except that one of those witnesses is no longer available. Baal HaMaor's difficulty arises specifically with Rav Huna's explanation, because when Rav Huna says, "The one hundred I admitted before you I also admitted before your fellow," it indeed sounds as though each admission was made before only one person, as the Shach himself writes. Baal HaMaor therefore answers that this case is different: once the defendant tells this witness that he also admitted before the other witness, his present statement is no longer equivalent to an ordinary admission before only one witness. The Ramban therefore correctly writes that Rav Huna's explanation was rejected, and Baal HaMaor has no other answer except to establish the case as an admission before two witnesses where one is no longer available. The Shach writes, with respect for the Ramban, that he did not fully grasp Baal HaMaor's intention. In truth, the Ramban descended precisely to the depth of Baal HaMaor's reasoning and reached its true foundation.
After we have clarified this, the Shach himself admits that it is strained to explain the Gemara's rule of one admission after another as involving this unusual circumstance, where a second witness had been present but was no longer available. Therefore the Shach's own ruling agreeing with Baal HaMaor is likewise untenable. I am surprised that the Shach changed his approach. In seif katan 19 he asks: if saying, "You are my witnesses," outside the claimant's presence does not prevent the defense that the admission was made merely so as not to appear wealthy, why does the Mishnah say only, "He told me, and this means nothing"? It should teach the greater novelty that even if he expressly said, "You are my witnesses," it means nothing. This question is even stronger according to Baal HaMaor, who establishes the first clause as an admission before one witness because the Mishnah uses the singular. Since that wording is the entire root of Baal HaMaor's rule, why does the Mishnah merely say, "He told me"? It should teach that even if the defendant said, "You are my witnesses," the admission is ineffective. If one answers that the Mishnah itself is not the proof, and the proof instead comes from the statement in Chezkas HaBatim that an admission is made before two witnesses and they need not be told to write, that too does not establish the rule. It would do so only if we assumed that the parallel statement, "A kinyan is before two and they need not be told to write," means that a kinyan is valid only before two. But we rule, as the Shach himself writes, that a kinyan made in the presence of only one person is effective. Necessarily, two are mentioned there because the statement "they need not be told to write" naturally concerns two witnesses, and the clauses are phrased in parallel. The same can be true of an admission: two witnesses are mentioned because of the rule that they need not be told to write, with the clauses connected in wording and subject. Therefore we should not depart from the Shulchan Aruch's ruling. As the distinguished Maharit also writes in the cited responsum, this is the settled and widely accepted halachah in Israel.
