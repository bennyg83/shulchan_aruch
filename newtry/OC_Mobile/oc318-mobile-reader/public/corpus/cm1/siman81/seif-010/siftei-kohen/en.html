An admission before one witness, etc. Baal HaMaor disagrees and holds that an admission before one witness is no admission at all, even where the person admits the witness’s account that he admitted before him and said, “You are a witness.” The Tur cites this view without attribution above, siman 32. The Raavad and Rabbenu Ephraim agree, and report that a Gaon held likewise and that one who rules otherwise is mistaken. Baal HaTerumot, Gate 42, part 1, cites them and objects from the general rule that wherever two witnesses impose monetary liability, one witness imposes an oath. He also objects from the Gemara in Zeh Borer that successive admissions combine. I do not understand his objection, for Baal HaMaor himself noticed both points and answered them clearly. He writes that the Mishnah changes from singular language, “He told me,” to plural language, “until he says, He admitted before us.” This indicates that an admission before one witness alone is ineffective even if he said, “You are a witness.” If he denies the admission, he does not swear on that witness’s word; and if he admits making it but says, “I was jesting,” he is not liable. Idle words do not become an admission until he says to two, “You are my witnesses.” This is why Bava Batra says, “An admission before two; validation of documents before three.” Just as validation before fewer than three is nothing, an admission before fewer than two is nothing. The general rule concerning one witness does not apply, because the principal liability here arises from the defendant’s own admission, not from the witnesses. Therefore his words must be examined to ensure that they were not jest. Until he admits before two, they retain the status of jest. The joining of successive admissions is different because, in the end, there are two witnesses. Their combined testimony removes the matter from the category of jesting. The same applies where a later loan joins an earlier admission. Thus Baal HaMaor fully answered Baal HaTerumot’s objections. Many authorities disagree with Baal HaMaor, beginning with the Ramban in Milchamot, and later authorities followed him. Since Baal HaMaor’s words appear correct to me, I shall summarize the Ramban’s objections and answer them. The Ramban argues that the Gemara’s proposed explanation, that the debtor told each witness that the same hundred admitted before one was also admitted before the other, was rejected. He further argues that the Gemara joins admission after admission, and loan after admission, even though each admission had only one witness, proving that an admission before one witness is an admission. He says that those who disagree must force the case to be one where each witness says another person was with him, though there is no basis for that. He also rejects Baal HaMaor’s analogy to validation of documents, noting that the Gemara paired admission with kinyan, and a kinyan is valid even before one witness or without witnesses. He cites the Yerushalmi that if one intended to transmit testimony, such as by saying, “You are a witness,” the testimony is valid even though only one witness heard it and the claimant was absent. Finally, he argues from the Mishnah’s wording that if Baal HaMaor were correct, it should have distinguished between “he admitted before me” and “he admitted before us.” In my humble opinion, these objections do not overturn Baal HaMaor. Although the proposed explanation concerning the same hundred was rejected for another reason, Baal HaMaor’s answer remains. Once there is a second witness, the first statement is shown not to have been jest. The two witnesses ultimately combine, whether the second event was another admission or a loan. It is therefore unnecessary to say that another witness was present at each admission. Indeed, the Gemara’s proposed wording indicates that only one was present at each, yet the combination is effective precisely because there are ultimately two witnesses. The proof from kinyan is also not decisive. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 6b concludes that a kinyan requires two witnesses, and records the answer that an ordinary threshing floor has people present. The Mordechai in Chezkat HaBatim reaches the same conclusion. The Raavad in his glosses to chapter 5 of Hilchot Mechirah writes that not all agree that a kinyan is valid without two witnesses. Rabbenu Ephraim also holds that one who says a kinyan or admission is effective without two is mistaken, as cited by Baal HaIttur and Baal HaTerumot. The plain Gemara in Bava Batra 40a supports this: “A protest before two, and they need not be told to write; a notice before two, and they need not be told to write; an admission before two, and they must be told to write; a kinyan before two, and they need not be told to write; validation of documents before three.” The phrases appear parallel. Just as protest and notice require two, so do admission and kinyan. If the purpose of saying “before two” were only to teach that two may write without three, the Gemara could simply have said, “A kinyan need not be told to write.” It likewise could have said merely, “An admission must be told to write.” The repeated phrase “before two” indicates a separate requirement of two witnesses. Rava’s question there also supports this. He asks whether a kinyan is like an act of beit din and therefore should require three; if not, why may the witnesses write without instruction? The Gemara answers that it is not an act of beit din, but an ordinary kinyan is intended for writing. This suggests that without Rav Nachman’s statement, we might have compared it fully to validation of documents and required three for the kinyan itself. The conclusion excludes three, but not two. The statement in Kiddushin that witnesses were created only to prevent falsehood applies to matters such as division between partners, not necessarily to a formal sudar kinyan. A kinyan establishes the transaction, and an admission creates and confirms the obligation through the speaker’s mouth. The Sages may therefore require two. There is no difficulty in saying that an admission before one remains jest even if he says, “You are a witness,” just as an admission before two without “You are my witnesses” or without complete formal language is ineffective, while an admission before three may have stronger force. These are formal rules of the Sages. Nor does the rule that one witness imposes an oath apply. That rule concerns a witness whose testimony is legally effective for an oath as two are for money. Here we accept that the witness speaks truthfully, yet the event itself is legally ineffective because an admission before one is treated as jest. Many passages also use the plural formulation “they made a kinyan from him,” including Bava Metzia 58a, Bava Metzia 94a, Bava Batra 3a and 43a, and Ketubot 83a. This implies that the witnesses made the kinyan. Even if one holds that a kinyan can be effective without two, a kinyan may still differ from an admission. A kinyan is an act and may be effective between the parties even without any witness. An admission is certainly ineffective when spoken privately to the claimant alone. Thus one may require two for an admission even if one does not require them for a kinyan. The Raavad’s wording also suggests that he was uncertain only concerning kinyan, while he was certain that an admission before one is ineffective. The Maggid Mishneh may likewise hold that a private kinyan is effective but an admission before one is not, for at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot To’en he cites, without rejecting, the authorities who say that an admission before one is nothing. It is therefore possible that Tosafot and other authorities who hold that kinyan is valid without witnesses still agree with Baal HaMaor concerning an admission. The phrase “admission before two” would then be exact as to legal validity, while “kinyan before two” would address its monetary effect and the authority to write. Baal HaMaor himself may also agree that a private kinyan is valid, since he did not challenge the Rif in Kiddushin. The Ramban’s proof from the Yerushalmi is also inconclusive. The Yerushalmi may concern two witnesses, as its plural wording “their testimony is established” suggests. Alternatively, it may concern the kind of case discussed in our Gemara, Zeh Borer 30a, where one saw a person conceal money and say that it belonged to another. If the circumstances show that he was transmitting instructions, the words are effective because the speaker could control the money. That is not the ordinary law of admission. The Mishnah’s language also favors Baal HaMaor. The opening singular phrase, “He told me,” can mean that each witness, examined separately, says this, while both were present. The Mishnah uses singular language because witnesses are examined one after another. The conclusion deliberately changes to “He admitted before us.” The Yerushalmi likewise has the plural “their testimony.” If one witness sufficed, why would the Mishnah change its wording? The change indicates that two are required. Accordingly, Baal HaMaor’s reasoning appears primary. Nevertheless, since many authorities followed the Ramban, I am not worthy to rule categorically against them. It is clear, however, that the party in possession may plead kim li in accordance with Baal HaMaor, especially since the Raavad, Rabbenu Ephraim, and a Gaon agree; the Rashbam’s commentary to Bava Batra 40a also appears to support them; this was Rivam’s original view; Tosafot in Sanhedrin 6b and the Mordechai in Chezkat HaBatim conclude similarly; and the Maggid Mishneh cites both positions without decision, ending with the view that an admission before one is ineffective, though a heset oath remains. Mahari ibn Lev, Book III, siman 38, likewise rules that because the Raavad in the name of a Gaon and Baal HaMaor hold that an admission before one is not an admission unless the witness says that the debtor admitted both before him and another, the claimant cannot extract money from the party in possession where the second alleged witness is unavailable or irrelevant. Maharshadam, siman 37, also rules that the defendant may plead kim li like Baal HaMaor and the Raavad. This is all the stronger in light of the additional authorities cited above. The Raavad’s further view, however, that two successive admissions before separate witnesses are ineffective unless each says that another witness was present, is a solitary opinion. Even Baal HaMaor holds that one admission can join another although no second witness was present at either event. The plain Gemara says, without qualification, that one admission joins another, and all the authorities rule so, as explained above, siman 30, seif 6. The Gemara’s wording that the debtor told each witness that the same hundred admitted before him was also admitted before the other proves this. The reason is that once he repeated the admission before another witness, it becomes evident that the first admission was not jest. This is the principal ruling. See Responsa Maharit, simanim 72 and 73.
It constitutes an admission, etc. Giddulei Terumah, Gate 42, part 1, page 4a, writes that there is room to question the Rambam’s ruling where the defendant says, “It is true that I admitted, but I was jesting.” He suggests that the defendant should be believed through a migo, because the reasons given by the commentators to defeat a migo in the case of Rabbi Abba’s piece of metal do not apply here. His words are incorrect. It appears to me that according to the Rambam, if the defendant admits the one witness’s account, he must pay. Baal HaTerumot there expressly implies this in explaining the Rambam, and so does the Tur’s formulation: it constitutes an admission, either to require an oath if he denies the witness’s account, or to require payment if he admits that he made the admission before him. There is further proof. Wherever a person could claim jesting, if he instead says, “The matter never occurred,” that denial is treated as the equivalent of a claim of jesting, because people do not remember idle words, as I wrote above, seif 1, seif katan 3. Giddulei Terumah himself states this on page 178b according to the Rambam. If the defendant could claim jesting here, why, when he denies the admission, must he swear against the witness? We should say that because the statement was idle, he simply does not remember it. The fact that he must swear proves that jesting is unavailable here. Consider this carefully.
