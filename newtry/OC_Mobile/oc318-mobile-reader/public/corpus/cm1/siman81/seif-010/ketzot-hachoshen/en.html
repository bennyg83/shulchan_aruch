An admission before one witness. Baal HaMaor and Raavad hold that an admission before a single witness is ineffective. Raavad further holds that even two admissions before two witnesses one after the other do not combine unless each witness says, "He admitted before me and before another witness." The Shach understands Baal HaMaor differently: although one witness alone is insufficient, two separate admissions before two separate witnesses can combine because the defendant repeated the admission. I find this difficult. If an admission before one witness is nothing more than idle words, why should its repetition in front of a second person turn either event into a binding admission? How do we know there were in fact two admissions? Perhaps only the first occurred, and the second witness is mistaken or lying. A single witness cannot establish money. In ordinary special testimony two witnesses can combine because each establishes an independent fact that, under both accounts, creates a monetary result. Here, however, if one witness is false there is no valid admission at all. This resembles half testimony concerning half a fact. Therefore the principal reading of Baal HaMaor is like Raavad: separate admissions combine only where the testimony itself shows that each admission took place in the presence of another witness. Then each event was independently a valid admission before two. Ramban's objection to Baal HaMaor is accordingly strong. Maharit likewise adopts this reading.
It is an admission. Gidulei Terumah questions what happens according to Rambam if the defendant says, "It is true that I admitted before the one witness, but I was joking." He suggests perhaps the defendant is believed through migo, because the ordinary reasons for rejecting the migo in Rabbi Abba's piece of metal do not apply. I do not understand why they do not apply. Tosafot gives two reasons why the migo fails there. First, the defendant does not wish to contradict the witness and swear directly against him. That applies here as well. If he denied the admission entirely, Rambam requires him to swear against the witness. Second, Rabbeinu Yitzchak explains that Torah law says one who faces one witness must swear or pay; until he swears, an ordinary migo does not help. That applies here too. There is also a broader point. Even in matters such as land, slaves, or documents, where the rule of inability to swear does not operate in the same way, once the defendant admits that he made a formal admission to the witness, he cannot say he intended joking merely through a migo. A formal admission with "You are my witnesses" creates a strong presumption that he did not intend joking. If two witnesses prove such a statement, he cannot say, "Yes, I admitted, but I was joking," through a migo of payment. The migo stands against a strong presumption created by the very form of admission. The same is true where one witness establishes the admission and the defendant acknowledges that formal wording. Therefore the joking claim fails even beyond the specific rule of Rabbi Abba.
