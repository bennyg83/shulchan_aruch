A person on his deathbed. The Sema writes: Perhaps the reason is that if his intent was merely to avoid appearing wealthy, why did he not make such an admission to one of the healthy people? Presumably, the person to whom he admitted the debt would not sue him, since that person knows the truth that he does not owe him. But when the recipient is on his deathbed and dies, his heirs will not know that the admission was made merely to avoid appearing wealthy. They will sue him, he will have to take an oath, and the matter will also become known so that people will think him wealthy. The Shach writes that this ruling requires study. The Mechaber derived it from the Mordechai, but there the issue of an admission made merely to avoid appearing wealthy is not mentioned at all, only the claim that a statement by a person on his deathbed was made in jest. Accordingly, just as one may claim that a person on his deathbed made an admission merely to avoid appearing wealthy, perhaps the same applies to an admission made to a person on his deathbed. However, all this follows the opinion cited in seif 14. I already concluded there, in accordance with the Rambam, that when one admits before the claimant, even a healthy person cannot say that he spoke merely to avoid appearing wealthy. But where the admission was not made in the claimant's presence, it appears that even an admission that one owes a person on his deathbed may be explained as having been made merely to avoid appearing wealthy.
