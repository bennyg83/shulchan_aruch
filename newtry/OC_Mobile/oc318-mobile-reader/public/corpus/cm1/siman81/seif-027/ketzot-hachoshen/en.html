Before a dying person. The Shach says Beit Yosef understood Mordechai to mean that if a person voluntarily admits before a dying person, he cannot claim self impoverishment, and similarly if the dying person sues and he admits he cannot say, "I was joking," because people do not ordinarily joke with one who is near death. The Shach reads Mordechai differently and says the source speaks only of joking, not self impoverishment. Therefore, just as a dying person himself can sometimes say he spoke to impoverish his heirs, perhaps one who admits to a dying person can likewise use that defense. The words of Raavyah in Mordechai reveal a different principle. In the case of the dying widow who instructed her son to give seven zekukim, Raavyah says that a dying person's demand is itself like saying, "You are my witnesses." His exact reasoning is that just as a dying person does not joke, one does not joke in response to the dying person's demand, and he compares the two formulations in the Gemara where either lender or borrower says, "You are my witnesses," and the other remains silent. Thus a dying person's words can carry the formal force of "You are my witnesses." This is consistent with many laws where a dying person's speech is interpreted more strongly than that of a healthy person. For a get, a healthy person must say both "write" and "give," whereas a dying person's command is understood more fully. Similar rules appear regarding acquisition and transfer. Accordingly, if a dying person sues another, the demand itself can function like "You are my witnesses." If the defendant admits in his presence, the combination has the status of a formal admission and can remove both joking and, where ordinarily removed by "You are my witnesses," self impoverishment. The episode of Kav Reshu, where a dying person named his creditors yet the self impoverishment defense remained, was outside the creditors' presence. "You are my witnesses" said outside the claimant's presence can still leave a self impoverishment defense according to the Tur. That distinction resolves the sources. This also explains the Gemara's phrase, "A dying person who admits need not say, You are my witnesses." The novelty cannot be merely that joking is absent, because the Gemara already states that a person does not joke at the time of death. Rather, the dying person's words themselves are treated like a formal "You are my witnesses," which also affects the self impoverishment defense in cases where that formalization would ordinarily remove it. Therefore, where the dying person himself is present with the claimant or defendant, his admission or demand has enhanced formal force. Where he merely speaks about an absent person, the ordinary rules concerning self impoverishment can still apply.
