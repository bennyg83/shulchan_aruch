He said, “I hereby admit before you,” etc. The Mechaber continues, “He said this by way of a complete admission.” His wording is not precise in my eyes. Once he said, “I hereby admit before you,” that itself is a complete admission. The Tur, seif 12, writes in the name of the Ramah: “The same applies if he says, ‘I hereby admit before you’; he need not also say, ‘You are my witnesses.’” When the Tur later writes, “The Rambam likewise states that if one admitted before two by way of a complete admission and not merely casual conversation,” it refers to the same type of statement, namely, “I admit before you.” The Rambam’s wording is: “One who admits before two that he owes so-and-so one hundred, and says it to them by way of admission rather than casual conversation.” His meaning is that he gathered them for the purpose of testimony, or said, “I admit before you.” This constitutes a complete admission, as I write in the following seif katan.
By way of a complete admission. The Maggid Mishneh writes that the Rambam derived this from the Rif at the end of Get Pashut. The Beit Yosef writes that he learned it from the Yerushalmi, which says that if the speaker intended to transmit testimony, the testimony is established. In my humble opinion, neither explanation is correct. Rather, the source is as I explain at length below, seif 14, seif katan 33; see there. I saw in the Semag, page 182, end of column 3: “We learned there, in the examination of witnesses, that if a witness says, ‘So-and-so told me that he owes him,’ he has said nothing unless the speaker intended to transmit testimony, as the Yerushalmi says: ‘Until he says, He admitted before us.’ This wording implies that they assembled for the purpose of testimony and that he spoke by way of admission. If he did not speak in that manner, it is ineffective until he says, ‘You are my witnesses.’ This supports Rav, who says that he must say, ‘You are my witnesses.’ Meaning, where he did not assemble them for testimony, it must be recognizable that he spoke by way of admission.” Rabbi Mattityah of Kerbresh wrote this in a responsum. The Hagahot Maimoniyot at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot To’en cites it. I have copied their words to clarify what is called a complete admission. Baal HaTerumot, Gate 42, part 1, likewise writes: “Know and understand that one who admits before two without saying, ‘You are my witnesses,’ but admits before them as one who summons them to testify against him and speaks wholeheartedly by way of a complete admission, is considered as though he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ and cannot say, ‘I was jesting with you.’ Rabbenu Moshe likewise wrote.” From all of these authorities we learn that where he did not speak by way of a complete admission, his words are ineffective even if he knew that witnesses were standing there. This is also implied by all the authorities, contrary to the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri at the beginning of HaNose. Their expression of astonishment against Rabbenu Baruch is, in my humble opinion, unwarranted; see there. Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 7, writes that the same applies if he says, “I admit,” for that is language of admission. The Nimukei Yosef at the end of Get Pashut says similarly regarding the Rif’s ruling that words stated in the language of admission are binding, such as, “I admit that I owe so-and-so one hundred.” Maharam Alshich implies that even if he did not assemble the witnesses, the words “I admit” constitute an admission. Responsa Mabit, part I, second collection, siman 136, likewise implies this. Maharam Alshich adds, however, that if he says, “Know that I owe so-and-so one hundred,” this is not an admission. See there. See also Responsa Mabit, part I, siman 54; part II, simanim 167 and 292; and Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 24 and the end of siman 134.
Even though he did not say, “You are my witnesses,” and even though the claimant was not with him, etc. This implies that all the more so, if he did say, “You are my witnesses,” the declaration is effective even when the claimant was absent, and he cannot advance any defense. Nothing remains after the declaration “You are my witnesses.” Even according to the Rambam and the Mechaber below, at the beginning of seif 14, that one who made a complete admission outside the claimant’s presence may say, “I admitted so as not to appear wealthy,” he cannot say so after declaring, “You are my witnesses.” This is explicit in the Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot To’en: “It appears to me that Rabbenu interprets this admission as a case where he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ or where the claimant was with him.” The Tur, however, writes in seif 9 that if, outside the claimant’s presence, the borrower said to witnesses, “I owe so-and-so one hundred, and be witnesses against me,” this is ineffective because the admission was not made before the lender. The Tur writes the same above, siman 32, seif 16. The Mordechai in Zeh Borer likewise rules so, and the Beit Yosef cites him there. It appears to me that the Rambam and Maggid Mishneh are correct. The plain implication of the Gemara everywhere is that nothing remains after “You are my witnesses.” Mahari ibn Lev, Book I, beginning of rule 14, siman 81, and Book II, siman 51, writes that according to the Rambam, even after saying “You are my witnesses,” one may still claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy if the admission was made outside the claimant’s presence. His words are not compelling, and the Lechem Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot To’en already challenges him. I later saw that Mahari ibn Lev himself, Book III, siman 38, remained uncertain about the Rambam’s intention, though he did not cite the Maggid Mishneh. In my humble opinion, there is no uncertainty. First, the Rambam’s words, “Even though he did not say, You are my witnesses,” refer back to the end of chapter 6, where “You are my witnesses” creates valid testimony. Here he teaches that even without that declaration, a complete admission defeats the claim of jesting. But in chapter 6, and likewise at the end of chapter 17 of Hilchot Edut, the Rambam states without qualification that “You are my witnesses” creates testimony, implying complete testimony for every purpose. Second, at the beginning of chapter 7 he writes, “When these witnesses came, he claimed, I admitted so as not to appear wealthy.” Why does he say “these witnesses”? He could have written simply, “He claimed that he admitted so as not to appear wealthy.” His wording implies that only concerning an admission established by these witnesses, without the declaration “You are my witnesses,” may he make that claim. After “You are my witnesses,” he cannot. Third, otherwise the Rambam’s lengthy formulation is unnecessary. He should simply have stated regarding “You are my witnesses” itself: one who says “You are my witnesses,” or who makes a complete admission, cannot claim jesting but may claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy. Since a complete admission would then be identical to “You are my witnesses,” he should have combined them. His failure to do so shows that they differ in this respect. The Rambam’s words at the end of chapter 17 of Hilchot Edut also imply this. He writes: “Whether one admitted before witnesses and said by way of admission, ‘This person has such and such in my possession,’ or said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ they are witnesses.” In the first clause he specifies, “This person has such and such in my possession,” meaning in the claimant’s presence. In the second clause he says simply, “You are my witnesses,” without requiring that he say, “This person has it in my possession,” indicating that even outside the claimant’s presence the declaration suffices. The Bach, seif 9, writes that the Tur derived his ruling from Baal HaTerumot, Gate 42. His words are astonishing. Baal HaTerumot appears to hold the opposite. He writes: “We must still consider one who admitted to his fellow one hundred before witnesses by way of admission rather than conversation, without saying, ‘You are my witnesses,’ and did not claim jesting, but claimed, ‘I intended not to appear wealthy.’ Some say that if the litigant was with him and he admitted in his presence, we do not listen to that claim. But if the claimant was absent and he admitted voluntarily, although he spoke by way of admission but did not say, ‘You are my witnesses,’ we listen to his claim that he intended not to appear wealthy. If he claims jesting or denies the admission, however, he is not believed. Rabbenu Moshe ruled so.” By saying “although he spoke by way of admission but did not say, You are my witnesses,” Baal HaTerumot implies that if he did say “You are my witnesses,” we do not listen even to the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy. Mahari ibn Lev, Book III, siman 38, also understands Baal HaTerumot this way. The Bach also cites Baal HaTerumot’s discussion that the Tur brings below, seif 29, but that passage proves nothing. There, Baal HaTerumot gives the true reason that the claimant was present, and the same rule would apply even without “You are my witnesses.” The Tur’s wording there also implies this. Moreover, all of Baal HaTerumot’s discussion there is a responsum of earlier authorities. Baal HaTerumot accepts the basic ruling but not necessarily every statement in the responsum, as I explain below, seif 24, seif katan 62. This resolves why that responsum says that when a claim preceded the admission, the claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy does not apply, and that when one admitted voluntarily, the claim of jesting does not apply, even though Baal HaTerumot himself holds the opposite on both points, as cited below, seif 16, seif katan 41, and above, seif katan 12. The Giddulei Terumah, page 177c, raises this difficulty, and according to what I have written it is resolved. The Nimukei Yosef in Zeh Borer also proves the Rambam’s position. He writes in the name of the commentators: “These words apply when he admitted in response to a demand. If he admitted voluntarily, or spoke to witnesses outside the claimant’s presence, there is no reason to claim jesting, for why would he jest when no one demanded anything? The Mishnah’s statement, ‘He told me that he owes him,’ is ineffective not because of jesting, but because a person may speak so as not to appear wealthy. But if he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ he is always liable, whether the admission followed a demand or not. Once he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ it was not jesting; and there is no claim of avoiding the appearance of wealth, because a person does not ordinarily summon witnesses and say, ‘Be witnesses against me,’ concerning a statement made only for that purpose. The Yerushalmi likewise says that if he intended to transmit testimony, he is liable.” The Nimukei Yosef says “he is always liable” and does not distinguish between the claimant’s presence and absence. His reason, that a person does not summon witnesses concerning a statement made merely not to appear wealthy, applies equally outside the lender’s presence. Moreover, he states that the Mishnah’s case of “he told me” is outside the claimant’s presence, yet the Mishnah concludes that liability exists when the witness says, “He admitted before us,” meaning after “You are my witnesses.” His proof from the Yerushalmi likewise concerns the Mishnah’s case, which is outside the claimant’s presence. The Rosh also implies this. In Zeh Borer he writes that even in the claimant’s presence, if one says, “I owe you one hundred” without a prior demand, he may claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy. He derives this from the Mishnah’s case, “He told me that he owes him one hundred,” which implies that the statement was outside the claimant’s presence, and the Mishnah nevertheless says that it has no effect until the witness says, “He admitted before us,” meaning “You are my witnesses.” This implies that even in the claimant’s presence a voluntary admission is ineffective without “You are my witnesses.” The Mishnah therefore makes everything depend upon “You are my witnesses,” not upon the claimant’s presence. One might force the Rosh to mean that the Mishnah requires two elements, “before us,” meaning “You are my witnesses,” and “he admitted to him,” meaning specifically in the claimant’s presence. The Giddulei Terumah appears to understand him that way. This is unconvincing. If that were so, the opening clause should have taught the greater novelty that even if he said “You are my witnesses,” it is ineffective outside the claimant’s presence. Instead it merely says, “He told me.” The clear intention is as stated. Mahari ibn Lev, Book II, siman 51, expressly understands the Rosh in this manner, though he mistakenly writes that this is contrary to the Rambam. In my opinion, as explained, the Rambam agrees. I am astonished that Mahari ibn Lev did not challenge the Tur, especially since he himself writes elsewhere that the Tur holds that “You are my witnesses” outside the lender’s presence is ineffective. That is contrary to the Rosh’s own reasoning. The same conclusion follows from the Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot, since they copy the Rosh’s words. The earlier contrary statement in the Mordechai must be from another authority, whereas the later passage is from Rabbenu Yitzchak. The Krakow edition indicates this, and the Giddulei Terumah also cites it in Rabbenu Yitzchak’s name. Since the Rosh and Hagahot Maimoniyot likewise state it, it is reasonable that all three record Rabbenu Yitzchak’s ruling. There is further proof from the Rosh in Chezkat HaBatim. He concludes that an admission before one witness can be effective and cites the Ramban’s proof from the Yerushalmi concerning the Mishnah, “He told me.” If the person intended to transmit testimony, meaning he said to the witness, “You are a witness,” it is effective. Since the Rosh himself explains in Zeh Borer that “He told me” concerns a statement outside the claimant’s presence, this proves that saying “You are a witness” is effective even then. The Ramban in Milchamot states this explicitly. Accordingly, the principal ruling is that “You are my witnesses” is effective in every circumstance, even outside the claimant’s presence. This is the plain meaning of the Rambam, the Maggid Mishneh, Baal HaTerumot, the Nimukei Yosef, the Rosh, the Mordechai, the Hagahot Maimoniyot, and the Yerushalmi.
Even though the claimant was not with him, etc. This is difficult. It implies that if he did not speak by way of a complete admission, the testimony is ineffective and he may claim that he was jesting. Yet the Mechaber wrote above, seif 5, that the claim of jesting applies only where the claimant demanded and the defendant admitted. The Rambam himself is consistent, for he holds that even one who admits voluntarily may claim jesting, as I wrote above, seif 5, seif katan 12. The difficulty is against the Mechaber. Necessarily, this passage concerns the claim of jesting, for regarding the claim that he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, even one who made a complete admission may advance that claim according to the Rambam and the Mechaber below, at the beginning of seif 14. The Rambam states this expressly there, and in seif katan 33 I prove that this is the principal ruling. One is forced to explain that the Mechaber understands the words “even though the claimant was not with him” as follows: since the claimant’s absence makes a difference regarding the claim of speaking so as not to appear wealthy, as stated below in seif 14, the Rambam teaches that it makes no difference here regarding jesting. Alternatively, perhaps the claimant had first demanded payment, and the defendant later admitted outside his presence. Both explanations are strained. The truth indicates that the Mechaber did not fully penetrate the Rambam’s intention and that of the other authorities on this point, and therefore wrote nothing about it in the Beit Yosef, as I explained above, seif 5, seif katan 12. According to what I established there, that the Rambam and many authorities allow even one who admitted voluntarily to claim jesting, and that this is the primary reading of the Gemara, the words here are straightforward.
