A complete admission. Semag writes: We learned concerning examination of witnesses, and so forth, until they say, 'He admitted to him in our presence.' This wording implies that the witnesses assembled for the purpose of testimony and that he made the statement as an admission. Otherwise it is ineffective unless he says, 'You are my witnesses.' Meaning, where he did not gather them for testimony, it must otherwise be evident that he spoke by way of formal admission. This is cited in Hagahot Maimoniyot at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Toen. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes this. I have copied their words so that one may know what qualifies as a complete admission. From all of them we learn that where he did not speak by way of a complete admission, even if he knew that witnesses were standing there, it has no effect. This is also implied by all the poskim. Maharam Alshich writes in responsum 6 that the same applies if he says, 'I admit,' for that is language of formal admission. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes at the end of the chapter Get Pashut, and that responsum implies that the phrase 'I admit' is effective even if he did not gather the witnesses. The same appears from Mabit, volume 1, second set of questions, siman 136. However, Maharam Alshich writes there that if he merely says, 'Know that I owe so and so one hundred,' this is not a formal admission. See there. See also Mabit, volume 1, siman 54, and volume 2, simanim 167 and 292, and Maharam Alshich, siman 24 and the end of siman 134. Shach.
With him. The Shach writes that it follows all the more so that if he said, 'You are my witnesses,' it is effective even if the claimant is not with him, and he cannot advance any defense against the admission. This appears to me to be the principal view, for the plain sense of the Gemara in many places is that nothing can be said after an effective declaration of 'You are my witnesses.' See there, where the Shach brings many proofs. He concludes, however, that there is a difficulty: the text implies that if he did not make a complete formal admission, it is not testimony and he may claim that he was jesting. Yet the Mechaber writes in seif 5 that a claim of jesting applies only where the claimant demanded payment and he then admitted. Maimonides himself presents no difficulty, because he holds that even one who admits on his own initiative may claim that he was jesting. The difficulty is only with the Mechaber. Necessarily, the present discussion concerns a claim of jesting, because as to the claim that he spoke only so people would not think him wealthy, even a complete formal admission can be explained that way, as written in seif 14, and Maimonides says so explicitly. One must therefore say that the Mechaber explains the phrase 'even though the claimant is not with him' as teaching that although, for the separate defense of avoiding an appearance of wealth, there is a distinction whether the claimant was present, for the present law there is no such distinction. Alternatively, the case is where the claimant first demanded payment and the debtor later admitted outside his presence. All this is strained. The truth indicates that the Mechaber did not fully penetrate the intent of Maimonides and the other poskim on this matter, and for that reason wrote nothing about it in Beit Yosef. According to what I wrote in siman 65, that Maimonides and many poskim hold that even one who admits on his own initiative may claim that he was jesting, the words here are understood in their straightforward sense. Thus concludes the Shach.
On their word. The Sema asks why the Mechaber wrote without qualification that payment is made on their testimony, since the debtor can claim that he admitted only so people would not think him wealthy, and even if he does not raise that defense, beit din pleads it for him. The Sema answers that until this point the discussion concerned an admission following a demand, where the defense of avoiding an appearance of wealth does not apply, as the Rama writes in seif 21. Although the Mechaber began, 'If he said before two,' without mentioning a prior demand, and concluded, 'even though the claimant is not with him,' in which case no one was there to demand payment, nevertheless the phrase 'even though the claimant is not with him' implies that the main case is where the claimant is with him. Incidentally, the Mechaber teaches the greater novelty that even when the claimant is absent, we treat the statement not as casual conversation but as a complete admission. Thus concludes the Sema. The Taz writes that this explanation is strained and resolves the matter differently. See there.
