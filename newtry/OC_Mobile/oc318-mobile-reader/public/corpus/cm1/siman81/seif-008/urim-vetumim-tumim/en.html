Even though the claimant is not with him, etc. The Shach asks that the Mechaber copied the Rambam's language, and the Rambam follows his own view that even if the claimant did not demand payment, the defendant may still say, "I was joking with you." But the Mechaber ruled above in seif 5 that without a demand the defendant cannot raise the joking defense. If so, when the claimant is not present, why is there any need for the admission to be made in a formal manner? If the concern is that the defendant may say that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, that defense is available even when the admission was made formally. The Shach therefore writes that the Shulchan Aruch copied this language imprecisely. In my opinion, however, the Mechaber distinguishes between two situations. When the person to whom he claims to owe money is present and has made no demand, it is implausible that he intended to make sport of him when the other person did nothing to provoke such conduct. Is this how one ordinarily treats a person standing with him? But when the supposed creditor is absent, people certainly do sometimes say jokingly or mockingly, "I owe so and so such and such." The Mechaber's language is therefore entirely correct. This distinction also resolves most of the proofs cited by the Shach above in seif katan 12 that the joking defense applies even without a demand. The cases can be distinguished as I have written. It also explains why the Mechaber specifically says, "even though the claimant is not with him," rather than merely, "even though the claimant did not demand payment." Consider this carefully. This appears correct as a matter of law, especially because most authorities do not distinguish between a case with a demand and one without it.
On this basis we can also resolve what the Gidulei Terumah, page 177b, leaves requiring study regarding Baal HaTerumot. Baal HaTerumot's words plainly imply that the joking defense can apply even when the claimant is not present, yet later in part 5 of the same gate he writes that the defendant cannot claim he was joking because Levi did not demand payment from him. We can say that in the latter case Levi was actually present but made no demand, whereas when he is absent the defendant can still say that he was joking. All is therefore well.
This is complete testimony. This applies with respect to the joking defense. The defendant may still claim that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. If he said, "You are my witnesses," outside the claimant's presence, many authorities hold that he cannot raise the latter defense either. The Tur, however, holds that he can. The Shach asks that this contradicts the Tur's father, the Rosh. The Rosh writes that even in the claimant's presence, without a demand, the defense that the person admitted only so as not to appear wealthy can apply. The Mishnah says: "If a witness says, 'He told me that he owes him,' the witness has said nothing," which implies that the statement was made outside the claimant's presence and therefore no demand was possible. Yet the Mishnah says that the testimony means nothing unless the witness says, "He admitted to him before us." This shows that even in the claimant's presence the formula, "You are my witnesses," is required because a person may admit merely so as not to appear wealthy even in the claimant's presence. The Shach infers that if so, why does the first clause not say, "He admitted to me," meaning with the formula, "You are my witnesses," and nevertheless the testimony means nothing? Necessarily, when the formula is used outside the claimant's presence it is effective, contrary to the Tur. The Mishnah therefore says only, "He told me," which is ineffective, while an actual admission would be effective. The later clause, "until he says, 'He admitted to him before us,'" meaning in the claimant's presence, teaches that without the formula even a statement made in the claimant's presence means nothing. This is not such a strong inference. One can say that the latter clause itself reveals the meaning: "until he says, 'He admitted to him before us,'" requires two elements, both the formula, "You are my witnesses," and the claimant's presence. This would show that the formula is ineffective outside the claimant's presence. The first clause, "He told me," can then itself mean that he used the formula, and the Mishnah's structure fits even better. The phrase "he admitted to him" means that he said, "You are my witnesses," while "to him" indicates that the claimant was present. Both are necessary. We can further learn this from Responsa Rosh, rule 65, responsum 1, where the Rosh was uncertain whether one who made an admission in his own handwriting could claim that he did so merely so as not to appear wealthy. The Rosh inclined to say that this defense applies only to mere speech, but that a person would not go so far as to write such a statement. See there. Why was this discussion necessary? The Rosh writes at the beginning of the chapter HaNose that a handwritten admission is equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses," and after that formula the defense that one admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy is unavailable. Necessarily, in the responsum the person had written, "These assets belong to so and so," outside that person's presence. Thus even if a handwritten statement is exactly equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses," it was still made outside the claimant's presence and the defense could remain. The Rosh therefore needed the separate distinction between speech and writing. The Tur's interpretation of the Rosh is therefore compelled. See what I write in seif katan 24, where the Rosh's treatment of the Tosefta must likewise be understood this way.
