Even if he said, "You are my witnesses," and remained silent. The Sema explains that the borrower said, "I owe you one hundred," and, "You are my witnesses," while the lender remained silent. Nevertheless, the borrower can claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. The Shach objects and explains instead that the borrower said, "I owe you one hundred," the lender responded, "You are my witnesses," and the borrower then remained silent. He brings proof from the Piskei Recanati, which explicitly cites the Or Zarua as saying that if the borrower makes an unsolicited admission and the claimant responds, "You are my witnesses," while the borrower remains silent, the borrower may say, "I was joking with you."
Now, this interpretation indeed makes the straightforward reading of the sugya in Ketubot, chapter HaNose, easier. There, concerning one who says, "I owe you one hundred," the Gemara says: if he said, "You are my witnesses," what is Reish Lakish's reason? According to the Sema, an essential fact is missing from the Gemara's formulation. It should have said: if the claimant demanded payment, the debtor admitted, and then said, "You are my witnesses," what is Reish Lakish's reason? For if there had been no prior demand, as the Gemara's plain wording, "I owe you one hundred," suggests, what does saying, "You are my witnesses," accomplish? The debtor could still say that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. Why, then, is there any difficulty according to Reish Lakish? According to the Shach's interpretation, however, this problem is avoided. It also helps us understand the Tur's objection to the Raavad from the Gemara's question whether a dying person who admitted must say, "You are my witnesses." The Tur understands that if the dying person did say the formula, he could no longer raise any defense. The Beit Yosef asks that the Gemara there cannot concern an unsolicited admission, because in such a case it is obvious that saying, "You are my witnesses," would be needed to defeat the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. Therefore, the case must concern one who was sued and then admitted, as Tosafot and all the commentators explain, and in that situation there is no difficulty at all with the Raavad. If the debtor was sued and admitted, certainly saying, "You are my witnesses," is effective. According to the above interpretation, however, the Tur can be understood. The Mordechai questions the explanation that after the borrower said, "I owe you one hundred," the claimant said, "You are my witnesses," and the borrower remained silent. The Mordechai says that this itself is a demand for payment. We must understand the force of that objection. What does it matter that the claimant is making a demand when the borrower has not answered anything? What admission has the borrower made at that point? We must say that the Mordechai holds that there is no meaningful difference between the following two cases. In the first, the claimant says, "You owe me one hundred," the defendant says, "Yes," then the claimant says, "You are my witnesses," and the defendant remains silent. In the second, the defendant first says on his own, "I owe you one hundred," the claimant says, "You are my witnesses," and the defendant remains silent. Why should the defendant's silence not function as an admission in the second case just as in the first? One can answer that because the defendant did not personally and expressly say, "You are my witnesses," even if silence is treated as assent, it should be no stronger than an express answer, "Yes," to a demand, where the defendant can still say, "I was joking." All the more so can he say this where he only remained silent in response to the claimant's declaration, "You are my witnesses." The reason the first case is different is that we do not attribute joking to the defendant twice in succession. First he answered "yes" to the claimant's demand, and then he remained silent when the claimant said, "You are my witnesses." One does not ordinarily continue joking to that extent, and therefore he must pay. But in the second case, where he initially volunteered the statement, "I owe you one hundred," which may have been intended merely not to appear wealthy because no demand had been made, when the claimant then says, "You are my witnesses," thereby turning the matter into a demand, the claimant is now the one who begins the joking interaction. The defendant's silence, even if treated as an admission, is no stronger than saying "yes," and he can still say that he intended only to joke. This may be the intent of the Or Zarua as cited by Recanati, who writes specifically that the defendant can say, "I was joking with you," rather than that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. Once the claimant responded, "You are my witnesses," he was effectively making a demand, as the Mordechai asks, so the latter defense is no longer appropriate. Only the joking defense remains. If so, the Tur's objection is sound. The Gemara asks whether a dying person who admitted must say, "You are my witnesses," or not, and whether a person jokes at the time of death. This implies that if he did say, "You are my witnesses," there would no longer be any concern of joking. Otherwise the Gemara could have asked more simply whether a person jokes at the time of death, since there exists a form of joking even after the claimant says, "You are my witnesses," namely where the debtor first volunteered an admission and then remained silent when the claimant used the formula. For a healthy person he could say he was joking, while for a dying person that would be the subject of the Gemara's doubt. If so, the Gemara should simply have asked whether a dying person jokes, rather than framing the question in terms of whether he must say, "You are my witnesses," which implies that everything depends upon that formula. Necessarily, saying, "You are my witnesses," eliminates the joking defense entirely. Nevertheless, the Sema's interpretation appears more correct to me. First, the Mordechai itself explicitly explains the matter as the Sema does. It did not accept the analysis I just suggested, and instead writes that the claimant's declaration, "You are my witnesses," itself constitutes a demand. Second, the Shach's explanation of the Tur's question is still not completely satisfactory. The Gemara could be precise in asking whether the dying person himself must say, "You are my witnesses," because according to the Shach's own view, once the debtor himself says the formula there is no concern of anything further. The main point, however, is the Raavad's source for this rule, namely the Tosefta cited by the Rif at the beginning of Bava Metzia: "An admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. When? When the other party demanded and he admitted. But if he admitted on his own, the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted." This troubled the Raavad. If the defendant did not say, "You are my witnesses," then even where he was sued and admitted he could still say, "I was joking." If he did say, "You are my witnesses," then an unsolicited admission should likewise be effective. From this the Raavad derives his rule that even if he says, "You are my witnesses," but the creditor remains silent and had not made a demand, the debtor may retract. According to the Shach's interpretation, however, what does the Tosefta's phrase "he admitted on his own" mean? We are supposedly saying that the defendant's silence after the claimant says, "You are my witnesses," is not considered an admission at all, for if it were an admission why could he retract? It is strained to say that the phrase "he admitted on his own" refers only to the debtor's initial statement, "I owe you one hundred," when that initial statement is not the operative admission and the later silence is what is in question. The essential circumstance, that the claimant rather than the debtor said, "You are my witnesses," is entirely absent from the Tosefta. By contrast, the Mordechai's and Sema's interpretation fits smoothly. "An admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses when the claimant demanded payment and the defendant admitted," meaning that the claimant said, "You owe me one hundred," the defendant answered "yes," and the defendant himself said, "You are my witnesses." But if the defendant admitted on his own, saying, "I owe you one hundred," and, "You are my witnesses," while the claimant neither demanded payment initially nor afterward, he may say that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. This is also how the Raavad must derive the law from the Tosefta. One may ask that perhaps the Tosefta concerns an admission made in beit din. In that setting, when the claimant makes a demand the defendant cannot retract, whereas when he admits on his own he can retract, as some authorities hold. Nor can we say that the Raavad believes an admission in beit din is always irrevocable, because Baal HaTerumot, gate 7, records the Raavad's view that where one is sued for wheat and admits barley he is exempt from the barley because he can say, "I was joking," showing that according to the Raavad even a statement in beit din can be retracted. See the Shach below, seif katan 56, who addresses this. We must therefore say that the Raavad equates the two settings. What difference is there between saying, "You are my witnesses," and admitting in beit din? An ordinary admission in beit din is itself equivalent to saying, "You are my witnesses." Just as one who admits on his own in beit din can retract, so one who admits on his own before witnesses and says, "You are my witnesses," can retract. This necessarily supports the Sema: the debtor himself made the unsolicited admission and himself said, "You are my witnesses," exactly as an unsolicited admission in beit din is the debtor's own statement. The addition that the creditor remained silent serves only to exclude a case where, after the debtor's unsolicited admission, the creditor responds, "You are my witnesses," and the debtor remains silent. To understand the Tur, we must say that the Tur interpreted the Gemara's sequence concerning a dying person carefully. The Gemara asks whether a dying person who admits must say, "You are my witnesses," or need not do so; then whether his words must be written, or need not be written; and then whether a person jokes at the time of death. The Tur understood that if the final question, whether a person jokes at the time of death, were merely an explanation of the first question, whether he must say, "You are my witnesses," the Gemara should have placed it immediately after that first question rather than interrupting with the separate question whether writing is required. Moreover, there would have been no need for two formulations. The Gemara could simply have asked whether a person jokes at the time of death. And once the Gemara resolved the first question, if its essence was whether one jokes, it should have concluded, "He need not say, 'You are my witnesses,'" rather than, "A person does not joke at the time of death." The Tur therefore understood that these are distinct questions. First, when a dying person makes a formal admission, is the formal manner itself sufficient so that he need not say, "You are my witnesses," as some authorities hold, or is the formula still required? If the formula is required to eliminate the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy, there remains a separate question concerning joking: does a person joke at the time of death or not? Under the general principle that where a first question depends on an "if you say" premise we resolve the dependent question, the Gemara resolves only the latter uncertainty, that a person does not joke at the time of death. But with respect to the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy, the dying person still must say, "You are my witnesses." If so, the first question, whether he must say the formula, concerns the latter defense and necessarily concerns a case where no demand was made. The Tur can therefore argue against the Raavad: how can the Gemara frame the issue merely as whether the dying person must say, "You are my witnesses," implying that this alone would suffice, when according to the Raavad an unsolicited admission is ineffective even with that formula? The Gemara should instead have asked whether a prior demand is necessary. The Tur's reasoning is simple and correct according to his approach.
On this basis another difficulty in the Tur is resolved. The Tur objects to the Raavad that the Gemara implies that once the dying person said, "You are my witnesses," no defense remains. The Shach likewise understands the Tur to mean that nothing can follow after that formula. One could ask the Tur himself: the Rosh holds that if an admission is made outside the claimant's presence, even saying, "You are my witnesses," is ineffective. Necessarily, then, the Gemara in Bava Batra cannot concern every possible case, but only one in which the claimant was present. Why not say the same in defense of the Raavad, namely that the Gemara does not concern the particular kind of unsolicited admission in which the formula remains ineffective? According to the explanation just given, however, all is clear. The Gemara's first question necessarily concerns the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy. If the claimant were absent, then saying, "You are my witnesses," certainly would not defeat that defense according to the Rosh. If the claimant had actually demanded payment, then the defense would be unavailable even without the formula. The only remaining possibility is that the claimant was present but made no demand, and the defendant himself said, "You are my witnesses." It cannot mean that the claimant said the formula, because the Gemara asks whether the dying person "must say" it, which grammatically refers to the dying person who made the admission. The Tur's question against the Raavad is therefore fully justified. Without this explanation, the Tur's words indeed require study.
And there are those who disagree, etc. Certainly even according to the Mechaber there are those who disagree, because his own language is, "According to the opinion that even..., he may claim...," which implies that according to the Rambam cited above this rule does not apply. The Beit Yosef, however, follows his interpretation corresponding to the Shach's explanation. On that reading, the claimant is present and is the one who says, "You are my witnesses." According to the Rambam, who holds that in the claimant's presence the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy is unavailable, this rule does not apply. The Rama, however, appears to interpret the case like the Sema, as the Sema infers from the Rama's wording, "since he said, 'You are my witnesses,'" etc. On that interpretation, the defendant himself said, "I owe so and so one hundred," and, "You are my witnesses," while the claimant was not present. Such a case is possible even according to the Rambam, and therefore the Rama adds, "There are those who disagree." I do not understand, however, who the dissenting authority is supposed to be. The Tur and Rosh explicitly hold above in siman 32 that saying, "You are my witnesses," outside the claimant's presence does not prevent the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy. They infer this from the Mishnah's phrase, "until he admits to him," meaning both the formula, "You are my witnesses," and an admission made to the claimant, as I discussed above at length. Consistently with this approach, they explain the Tosefta's rule that one who "admits on his own may retract" as necessarily referring to one who said, "You are my witnesses." Otherwise, even if the claimant had demanded payment, he could still say, "I was joking," as the Raavad writes. They therefore understand "on his own" to mean outside the claimant's presence, where the formula is ineffective. The Tosefta uses the ordinary factual pattern: when the claimant is present, he generally makes a demand. We have not found that the Rambam disagrees with this rule when the claimant was entirely absent at the time of the admission, such that saying, "You are my witnesses," would bar the defense. It is true that the Shach argues above in seif katan 20 that the Rambam does hold it effective even in that case. If so, the Rama's language can be understood broadly. "There are those who disagree" refers to the Rambam as disagreeing where the claimant was absent, while in a case where the claimant was present and the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy would otherwise be available, the Rosh and Tur agree that saying, "You are my witnesses," prevents it. Thus in every factual arrangement there is some dissenting view. According to the Rambam, the Tosefta's phrase "he admitted by way of admission" does not mean that he explicitly said, "You are my witnesses." Therefore, when the claimant demanded payment, so that the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy is unavailable and only joking remains, formal language of admission is effective, because it was spoken as a true litigant's admission. But when he admitted on his own, meaning that the claimant was not present, which is the ordinary meaning as I wrote, he can retract because formal admission does not eliminate the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy. All is well. The Rambam's distinction between formal admission as defeating joking but not the defense of not wishing to appear wealthy is compelled by the Tosefta. The Rosh, according to his own view that formal admission without the formula, "You are my witnesses," is ineffective, must explain the Tosefta as concerning an express declaration, "You are my witnesses," and thereby derives that if it was made outside the claimant's presence it is ineffective. Each authority therefore follows his own consistent approach. Consider this carefully.
