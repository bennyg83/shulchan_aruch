Even if he said, "You are my witnesses," etc. In the Perishah and Derishah I demonstrated that this means the defendant himself said to the witnesses, "You are my witnesses," while the claimant remained silent. The Mordechai states this explicitly. If, however, the claimant says, "You are witnesses," and the defendant remains silent, no authority holds that the defendant can later claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. Once the defendant heard the claimant formally designate the witnesses, it was clear that the claimant intended to pursue the debt, and the defendant should not have remained silent. The Tur's explanation of the Raavad also proves this. Rama's language in his gloss likewise indicates that the one who said, "You are my witnesses," is the one who later seeks to claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, for Rama writes, "Since he said, 'You are my witnesses,' he can no longer say that he intended only, etc." This is unlike the Beit Yosef, who explained the Raavad as referring to the claimant saying, "You are my witnesses," while the defendant remained silent, and therefore challenged the Tur. The Ir Shushan followed the Beit Yosef's explanation and did not analyze the matter carefully.
