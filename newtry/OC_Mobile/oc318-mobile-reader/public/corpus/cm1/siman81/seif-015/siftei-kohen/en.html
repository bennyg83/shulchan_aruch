Even if he said, “You are my witnesses,” and the other remained silent. The Sema explains this as referring to the defendant saying, “You are my witnesses,” while the claimant remained silent. He criticizes Beit Yosef and the Levush, who explain that the claimant said, “You are my witnesses,” while the defendant remained silent. His words are incorrect. The term “remained silent” plainly applies throughout the law to the defendant, as above in seif 6 and in many other places, because the defendant’s silence and failure to protest are treated as assent to the claimant’s assertion of liability. The expression is not ordinarily used of the claimant. Although Mordechai in the chapter Zeh Borer distinguishes between the two cases, he does so because, when the claimant says, “You are my witnesses,” that itself constitutes a demand. Raavad and the Tur, however, do not state this distinction, nor is it implied by their words. The explanation of Beit Yosef and the Levush is therefore primary, and Bach understood it likewise. Beit Yosef asks against the Tur that the case of a dangerously ill person who admitted a debt concerns one who was first sued and then admitted, as Tosafot state. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The Tur holds that the Gemara implies that the declaration “You are my witnesses” is effective in every circumstance and leaves no room for a later retraction. A prior demand and responsive admission are required only where no such declaration was made. This is also evident from Tosafot and the Rosh themselves in the chapter Zeh Borer, for their difficulty arises only from the Gemara’s conclusion that one need not say, “You are my witnesses.” Where that declaration was made, they found no difficulty. I have discussed this at greater length in my commentary to the Tur, resolving the Tur and refuting several points made by Bach. I abbreviate here because I have already concluded above, seif 14, seif katan 33, that regarding the claim of not wishing to appear wealthy, whenever the claimant is present the defendant cannot make that claim, even without the declaration “You are my witnesses.” I likewise concluded above, seif 8, seif katan 19, that once “You are my witnesses” was said, even outside the claimant’s presence, the defendant may make neither the claim of jesting nor the claim of not wishing to appear wealthy. The practical difference concerns the claim of jesting, according to what I wrote above in seif 5, seif katan 12, that such a claim may apply even to a voluntary admission. It appears that Raavad himself was precise in writing, “If he said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ and the other remained silent,” because when the defendant himself says, “You are my witnesses,” Raavad concedes that it is effective even for a voluntary admission. The case of the dangerously ill person who admitted was one in which that person himself said, “You are my witnesses,” as Beit Yosef explains at the end of his discussion. Raavad understood that the Gemara in Zeh Borer implies that where the lender said it and the borrower remained silent, the result is weaker. Although the Gemara states that there is no difference between the borrower saying it and the lender saying it while the borrower remains silent, that discussion concerns an admission made in response to a demand. For a voluntary admission, Raavad holds that even if the claimant says, “You are my witnesses,” the defendant may still claim that he wished not to appear wealthy or that he was jesting. This is explicit in Raavad’s words cited by Baal HaTerumot, Gate 42, part 1, which I cite briefly below in seif 22, seif katan 55. The Tur mentions only the claim of not wishing to appear wealthy because he follows his own view that jesting does not apply to a voluntary admission. I also found in the rulings of Maharam of Recanati, siman 308: “If one demanded payment outside beit din and the person admitting said, ‘You are my witnesses,’ or the opposing party said it and he remained silent, he cannot claim, ‘I was jesting with you.’ If, however, he voluntarily admitted outside beit din and the person admitting did not say, ‘You are my witnesses,’ but the opposing party said it, he may claim, ‘I was jesting with you.’ So ruled Rabbi Yitzchak Or Zarua.” This is unquestionably also Raavad’s position upon careful examination of his words, and it is the reverse of Mordechai’s distinction in Zeh Borer. The Tur, however, makes no distinction, and this appears primary. The end of the chapter Get Pashut implies that after the declaration “You are my witnesses” there is no basis for any retraction in any circumstance. This is especially so according to what I wrote above in seif 14, seif katan 33, that the principal interpretation is that the passage at the end of Get Pashut concerns a voluntary admission. Furthermore, the Gemara in Zeh Borer states without qualification that there is no difference between the borrower saying, “You are my witnesses,” and the lender saying it while the borrower remains silent. What basis is there to distinguish between them for a voluntary admission? The Gemara also states there that Rabbi Yehudah’s requirement that he say, “You are my witnesses,” applies equally in both forms. Rabbi Yehudah evidently addresses a voluntary admission as well, since the Gemara earlier cites in his support the mishna, “He admitted before us,” and that mishna concerns a voluntary admission. Rabbi Yehudah therefore presumably also addresses such a case, and the Gemara’s statement that there is no difference applies to it. The same is implied by Rambam at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot To’en and at the end of chapter 17 of Hilchot Edut, where “You are my witnesses” is effective in every circumstance, and by the other authorities, who make no such distinction. Although below, seif 22, seif katan 55, I write that many authorities hold that one who voluntarily admits, even before a beit din, may retract and claim that he was jesting, I explain there that this is only where the admission was made in casual conversation. Here, because one of the parties expressly said, “You are my witnesses,” he should have considered his words carefully. He must therefore have spoken deliberately and intended a complete admission. It may also be said that when the lender says, “You are my witnesses,” this itself constitutes a demand, as Mordechai writes. In any event, I conclude there as a matter of law that he cannot claim that he was jesting.
