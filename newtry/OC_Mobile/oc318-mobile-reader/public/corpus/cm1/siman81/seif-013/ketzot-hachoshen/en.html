He hid witnesses from him. I was uncertain whether the hidden witnesses must actually see the speaker well enough for visual recognition, or whether voice recognition is sufficient. Chullin says that visual recognition is stronger than identifying marks and proves that voice recognition has force in prohibitory law, since a blind man may live with his wife and people may be with their wives at night through recognition of voice. One might therefore think voice recognition is sufficient even in monetary testimony. Sanhedrin, however, says concerning the special concealment of witnesses for an enticer that the witnesses are placed in an outer room where they can see him and hear his voice while he cannot see them. Rashi explains that they must see him by the light of the lamp, because if they do not see him they cannot testify to impose capital punishment. Even if they recognize the voice, he could say, "It was not I." This indicates that voice recognition is insufficient for capital cases and, by analogy, for extracting money. Pri Chadash suggests perhaps an enticer ordinarily speaks in a whisper, changing his voice, but his first explanation is stronger: voice recognition is accepted for prohibitory matters but not as full evidentiary identification for monetary or capital adjudication. Shiltei Gibborim cites Ramah that witnesses can testify to a person's words only if they saw him and heard him. That source confirms the rule. This is comparable to identifying marks. Signs can suffice in many prohibitory matters yet two witnesses who identify a murderer only by signs cannot establish capital liability. So too voice recognition alone does not suffice to extract money. The defendant can say, "It was not I." This is unrelated to Raavad's ruling concerning kiddushin before hidden witnesses. There his concern is not that the witnesses failed to identify the man and woman. Rather, because the woman did not know witnesses were present, she may have accepted the kiddushin only as a joke, assuming that without witnesses no legal kiddushin could occur. Even if the witnesses visually saw both parties through a hole, that separate issue would remain.
