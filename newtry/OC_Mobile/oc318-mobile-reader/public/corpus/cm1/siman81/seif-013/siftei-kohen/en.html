“Let the waking and the sleeping be witnesses against you,” etc. The Beit Yosef writes above, at the end of siman 32: “It appears to me that the words, ‘Let the waking and the sleeping be witnesses against you,’ were stated affirmatively. If they were phrased as a question, then even if the borrower remained silent, this would not be an admission, for the claimant asked him a question and he gave no answer.” This is astonishing. Rashi in Zeh Borer and the Nimukei Yosef expressly explain the opposite: “Do you wish all who hear, whether awake or asleep, to testify against you?” The claimant knew that the debtor would not admit before witnesses, so he thought that perhaps the debtor would assume everyone was asleep and answer yes. Giddulei Terumah, page 171d, likewise challenges the Beit Yosef. In my humble opinion, the plain Gemara also supports Rashi and the Nimukei Yosef. Had the claimant made a direct declaration, he presumably would not have feared that the debtor would respond “No”; he could simply have said, “Let all who hear be witnesses.” The Gemara’s wording, “He said to him, No,” likewise implies that the debtor answered a question. The Tur also writes first, “He said to him, Let the waking and the sleeping be witnesses against you,” which sounds interrogative. Otherwise the Tur should have written simply, “He said: Let the waking and the sleeping be witnesses.” The Beit Yosef’s concern that one person asked and the other remained silent presents no difficulty. Certainly, if one asks a question and the other remains silent, silence is not an admission. Here, however, the debtor had already answered “Yes” to the initial demand. His later silence may therefore be understood as relying on his previous affirmative answer. This is clear.
It is not an admission. See the Bach above, at the end of siman 32, who asks why this is not obvious and offers a strained answer. He cites Maharshal’s explanation that one might otherwise think that because the debtor noticed the witnesses and did not deny the debt, but merely objected that they not serve as witnesses, he effectively admitted. Chochmat Shlomo in Zeh Borer offers the same explanation. In my humble opinion, this strain is unnecessary. Here, because the debtor expressly said, “No,” there was no admission at all, and he need not claim, “I was jesting with you.” This differs from the earlier case, where beit din does not advance that claim for him, as the Gemara and the Tur state. The Gemara’s words, “Rav Kahana said: But he said no,” prove this, as does the Tur’s formulation above, at the end of siman 32: “But if he said no, he expressly protested.” This appears entirely clear.
