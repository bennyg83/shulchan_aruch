It is not an admission. Maharshal and Bach ask what novelty there is, since he expressly said "no." The Shach correctly answers that the word "no" itself shows the entire exchange was not a binding admission, so even if the defendant does not later plead, "I was joking," beit din makes that argument for him. One may nevertheless ask from the Gemara's inference that silence is admission because the baraita emphasizes, "but he said no." Perhaps silence is not itself admission. Rather, when a person remains silent he must personally plead that he was joking, while when he said "no" he already revealed the joking character of the exchange and beit din can make the argument for him. If so, the phrase "but he said no" would not prove that silence is admission. This point can be reconciled, but the Shach's practical explanation of the present ruling remains sound.
