["Alternatively, he admits that when he gave, etc. The Sema writes that one should not ask why he is not believed through a migo that he could have said, 'I told you only that it was toward principal,' because that would be a migo involving brazenness. This follows his approach below, siman 82, seif katan 43, in the name of Nimukei Yosef. I have already challenged Nimukei Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and the Sema on this at length there. In truth, this is not the reason, as I wrote above, seif 23, seif katan 59, siman 75, seif 7, seif katan 53, siman 93, seif 2, seif katan 4, and many other places, that even where the alternative claim would be brazen we apply a migo in a case of this sort. Rather, the reason is that this is like a migo against witnesses. Gidulei Terumah, page 183, beginning of column 2, likewise writes that although there is a migo here, it is like a migo against witnesses, because we are effectively witnesses that this was certainly the meaning once he performed the act of payment, and after an act there is no room to reinterpret it. I wrote similarly above, seif 2, seif katan 8. See there."]
["He admits to him, etc. This implies that if he denies it and says, 'I did not give it to you as profit,' then even though the other party contradicts him, he swears and is exempt. It therefore follows that the case concerns one who has no note against the manager for the principal, because if he did have a note he could not say, 'I did not give it to you as profit,' as explained below, seif 31. We must therefore say that the Raavad, who is the 'there are those who say' cited afterward by the Mechaber, disagrees with Baal HaIttur only in a case like this, where the principal is owed orally. If, however, there is a note for the principal or another arrangement in which the manager must pay the principal without any possible defense, the Raavad agrees that no claim of jesting applies and that he cannot say, 'I still have your money in my possession.' The creditor can compel payment of the principal through the note, and if the holder of the note dies or sells the note, the manager must pay the person who produces it. He therefore has no defense based on what he already paid unless we treat that payment as a genuine payment. This appears to me the primary law. It also follows here that although he could have claimed that he paid the principal, according to the first view he is not believed to say, 'I erred and thought there was a profit, but after recalculating I see that there was none.' It is difficult to say that the case speaks only where he claims he was jesting but that a claim of error would be believed, for I wrote above, seif 22, seif katan 56, that a claim of error is stronger than a claim of jesting. Moreover, the wording, 'He has no standing to reckon it now as principal,' implies that he has no such defense at all. This is also explicit from Baal HaTerumot's statement that if he can prove that he erred, etc., which implies that if he cannot prove it, even if he says that he erred, he is not believed. This is also explicit from the responsum of Mahari ibn Lev that I cite in the next seif katan, where in this case he is not believed to say that he erred even though he has a migo that he paid the principal. See what I write there."]
["He swears. Although the claimant does not assert with certainty that there was a profit, the manager nevertheless swears in accordance with the law of one who manages another's funds, as stated below, siman 93, seif 4. See what I write there."]
["And there are those who say, etc., he shall now swear, etc. This is because the case resembles one who admitted before witnesses, 'I owe you one hundred,' and immediately gave him fifty of it. If he did not say, 'You are my witnesses,' the admission is ineffective and he may say, 'I was merely jesting with you.' These are the words of the Raavad in Baal HaTerumot there. His meaning is that just as in that case a claim of jesting allows him to avoid paying the remaining fifty because he has not yet paid them, here too it allows him to retain what remains in his possession. This also proves that Baal HaIttur, the Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot hold that a claim of jesting applies even to a voluntary admission, as I cited in their names above, seif 5, seif katan 112. See there. It is strained to say that here the case is one in which he was sued and then admitted. Moreover, according to the Rosh, Tur, and those who follow them, the reason a claim of jesting applies specifically where he was sued and admitted is that he may say, 'Just as you were jesting with me, I was jesting with you.' That rationale does not fit here, because the investor did not conduct the business with the money. Even if he asked, 'Perhaps there is profit in your hands,' the manager should have no reason to jest with him, because there may indeed be profit. It is therefore difficult that the Tur and Mechaber, after ruling above in seif 5 that a claim of jesting applies only where the claimant sued him and he admitted, should cite here the dispute between Baal HaIttur and the Raavad, which under that approach would not apply at all. Perhaps they hold that only where one says, 'I owe you one hundred,' a voluntary admission cannot later be dismissed as jesting, whereas when he says, 'I have profit belonging to you,' a claim of jesting applies even to a voluntary admission, because he may have said it in order to induce the investor to leave the money with him for a longer period. All this is strained. The truth is that Baal HaIttur, the Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot follow their own positions, as I have explained. Mahari ibn Lev, volume 1, klal 16, siman 91, discusses a case in which Reuven gave money to Shimon to trade jointly with Shimon's own money. When they later dissolved the partnership, Shimon gave Reuven an accounting, said that the total came to a specified amount, paid him all the principal and part of the profit, and promised to pay the balance. To make the matter clear, Shimon gave Reuven a handwritten statement setting out the entire account, what had already been paid and what remained due from the profit. Shimon later claimed that some debts owing to the partnership had become uncollectible. At the time of the accounting he had assumed those debtors would pay, but once they failed to do so it emerged that the profit had been smaller and that he owed Reuven nothing. Mahari ibn Lev answers that at first glance the matter appears to depend on the dispute between the Raavad and Baal HaIttur cited by the Tur. On closer examination, however, he concludes that all agree in this case that Shimon cannot make any claim that contradicts his earlier statement. Even the Raavad agrees that where one says, 'You are my witnesses,' he cannot later nullify his words through any claim. In the Raavad's own case the debtor could have raised claims regarding the money similar to those Shimon now raises. Here, since Shimon gave Reuven a handwritten statement, it is equivalent to 'You are my witnesses,' as the Rosh writes in a responsum that just as 'You are my witnesses' defeats a claim that one spoke so as not to appear wealthy, so too does one's handwriting. The same should apply here. Mahari ibn Lev adds that one cannot say Shimon should be believed through a migo that he could claim payment, even according to the majority of authorities who allow a claim of payment against a handwritten obligation, because this would be a migo against witnesses. As the Rosh writes at the end of the first chapter of Gittin in the incident of Ginai, people do not ordinarily make an admission until they have carefully checked the account. Mahari ibn Lev therefore concludes that Shimon's claim has no force and he must pay the profit for which he obligated himself in writing. This ruling does not appear correct to me. On the contrary, even Baal HaIttur should exempt Shimon here. Baal HaIttur speaks only where the debtor actually transferred money, because we say that a person would not go so far as to pay money as profit unless he knew with certainty that profit existed. Giving only a handwritten statement is different. This is clear from Mahari ibn Lev's own comparison of handwriting to an admission before witnesses accompanied by 'You are my witnesses.' Baal HaTerumot, Gate 28, Part 5, plainly holds that even after 'You are my witnesses,' one is believed to say, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment, as I wrote above, seif 23, seif katan 60, in his name. Yet here Baal HaTerumot cites Baal HaIttur's view that the payer is not believed, and his language indicates that he agrees with Baal HaIttur. I also wrote above, seif katan 60, that Baal HaIttur himself appears to hold that after 'You are my witnesses' one is believed to say, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment. The distinction is therefore exactly as I wrote: where he actually paid money, a person would not ordinarily perform such an act of payment unless the matter were clear, and after an actual transfer of money there is no room to reinterpret the act, as I wrote at the end of seif katan 76. A mere handwritten statement of profit is different. Since I concluded above, seif 23, seif katan 60, that one is believed to say, 'I erred in my accounting,' after an admission with 'You are my witnesses' where he has a migo of payment, and since a claim of error is stronger than a claim of jesting, the same law applies to a handwritten obligation. Maharik and the Rama below, siman 126, seif 13, explicitly rule that one may say of his own handwritten statement, 'I erred,' through a migo of payment. See what I write there in seif katan 21. All the more so here, where Shimon's claim is even stronger than an ordinary claim of error. With ordinary error one may say that he should have checked the account more carefully. Here Shimon says that he did check the account carefully, but assumed that the partnership's debtors would pay, and he issued the handwritten statement based on that assumption. Once they did not pay, there was no profit. Mahari ibn Lev himself writes at the end of his responsum that according to Baal HaTerumot, Shimon wins, but says that Shimon cannot claim to follow Baal HaTerumot because Maharik wrote that one cannot say, 'I follow so and so,' where all the Sages of Israel disagree with that authority. Those words are certainly inapplicable, because, as I demonstrated above, seif katan 60, many authorities agree with Baal HaTerumot, and I concluded that the law follows him. Therefore in this case Shimon is exempt, and the matter is straightforward, contrary to Mahari ibn Lev's ruling. This appears clear to me. See also the responsum of Maharam of Lublin, siman 135."]
