Admits. The Sema says the defendant should not be believed through a migo that he could have said, "I told you that payment was principal, not profit," because that would be a brazen claim. The Shach rejects the Sema's broader theory that brazenness automatically defeats such a migo. Here the true reason is that the migo stands against witnesses in substance: the objective act of payment demonstrates the meaning of the transaction, and after an accomplished act the contrary claim has no force. Gidulei Terumah writes similarly.
Who says. Even according to this opinion, the case concerns principal owed orally. If the claimant possesses a document for the principal, all agree that a jest defense concerning alleged profit is unavailable, because the document empowers the claimant to recover the principal involuntarily and the defendant gains nothing from pretending to acknowledge profit. The Shach regards this as the principal law. The passage also shows that according to the first view, even where the defendant could claim that he paid the principal, he is not believed to say, "I was mistaken; I thought there was profit, but after recalculation there was none." It would be strained to say the text concerns only a jest defense while a claim of mistake, which is stronger, remains available. Baal HaTerumot and Responsa Mahariv explicitly indicate that here he cannot claim mistake even with a migo of payment of the principal.
Shall swear. The Raavad explains that the case resembles one who admitted before witnesses, "I owe you one maneh, and here are fifty of it." Unless he said "you are my witnesses," the admission is not fully binding and he may say he was jesting. This shows that the Raavad recognizes a jest defense even for a volunteered admission. The Shach therefore questions how Tur and Shulchan Aruch, who earlier limit jest to a demanded admission, can present this dispute as relevant. He suggests, with difficulty, that perhaps a volunteered statement "I have profit belonging to you" is different from a simple volunteered admission "I owe you one maneh," because he may have said it merely to induce the other party to leave funds with him longer. The better explanation is that the cited authority follows his own broader view regarding volunteered admissions.
Principal. The Shach cites Responsa Mahari ibn Lev concerning partners where one paid his fellow all the principal plus part of the profit and gave him a signed accounting showing what had been paid and what still remained. He later claimed that debts counted as collectible at the time of the accounting became uncollectible and reduced the profit. Mahari ibn Lev ruled that once he gave a signed writing, it was equivalent to "you are my witnesses," so all agree he cannot retract. The Shach challenges aspects of that ruling in his full discussion.
