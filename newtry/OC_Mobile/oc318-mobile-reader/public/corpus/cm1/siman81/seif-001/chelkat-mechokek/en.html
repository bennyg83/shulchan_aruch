She works and eats. Even if he provides her food and also gives her the ma'ah of silver, he still cannot consecrate her future handiwork before she actually produces it, because one cannot consecrate something that has not yet come into existence. So writes the Ran. Ketubot 58b discusses the dispute of Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yochanan HaSandlar regarding a husband who consecrates his wife's handiwork. The mishnah states that she works and eats, and with respect to the surplus Rabbi Meir holds that it is consecrated while Rabbi Yochanan HaSandlar holds that it remains non-sacred. The Gemara asks when, according to Rabbi Meir, the consecration takes effect. Rav and Shmuel say the surplus becomes consecrated after her death, while Rav Ada bar Ahavah says it becomes consecrated during her lifetime. Rav Pappa asks: if the case is one where he provides her food and gives her the silver ma'ah, why should anyone say it takes effect only after death? It should take effect even in her lifetime. This implication that the husband can consecrate his wife's handiwork applies only according to Rabbi Meir, who holds that a person can transfer something not yet in existence. Likewise, Tosafot there, passage beginning "what, is it not where she is supported," speaks only according to Rabbi Meir. I am astonished at the Bach, who writes that here we rule like Tosafot and Rashi that if he supports her, her handiwork becomes consecrated. How did he overlook that Tosafot and Rashi did not state this as practical halachah? Even a woman cannot consecrate her own handiwork before she produces it, as Rashi writes on 59a, passage beginning "a thing not yet in existence." It is possible that the Bach meant only that if the husband says, "Your hands are consecrated for what they produce," the consecration takes effect when he supports her, in accordance with Rambam.
