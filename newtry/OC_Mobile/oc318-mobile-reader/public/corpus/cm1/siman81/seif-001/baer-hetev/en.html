As he said. The Sema writes that he must also swear that he owes the claimant nothing, and the two matters can be included in a single oath. Even if he swears generally that he owes nothing, his earlier admission may have referred to a genuine loan or binding obligation, so he must also swear that his earlier admission was only jest. Beit din does not automatically supply the defense of jest for him, nor is his bare assertion believed without an oath. Tur and Shulchan Aruch omit the separate formulation that he owes nothing because it is obvious from siman 75 and many other places that one who denies everything must take a shevuat heset. Mordechai in the name of Ri says the oath is required even if the claimant did not specifically demand it, just as heset itself applies without the claimant formally saying, "Swear to me."
Ever. The Shach adds that he may likewise claim, "I was mistaken in my admission." Baal HaTerumot and Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 28 imply this, and such a claim is even stronger than "I was jesting," as discussed earlier. When the Shach elsewhere said that one cannot claim mistake except through a migo of payment, that concerned a binding admission with "you are my witnesses," where jest is also unavailable. Here, because he is believed through a migo of jest, mistake is likewise available. See the cited responsa of Mahari ibn Lev, Mabit, and Maharit.
People. Even according to the witnesses, there is still no certainty that the defendant owes money solely because of his prior admission, since that admission may have been in jest, and people commonly forget such idle exchanges. This differs from siman 79, where witnesses prove that an actual loan occurred and was later repaid. Since the loan definitely happened, one should not forget it and say, "There was never any transaction," and such a denial establishes him as a false denier. Sema. The Shach infers that even if after the witnesses appear the defendant does not expressly renew the claim "I was jesting," he remains exempt. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 21 appear to support this. Although beit din does not ordinarily supply a jest defense when the defendant admits making the admission, where he now says the entire event never occurred, beit din may explain that he does not remember because it was merely idle jest. The Shach thinks all authorities agree.
With a community. The Shach says this law requires study. Rivash used the public character of the transaction only as one supporting consideration alongside other reasons showing that the admission was not jest. Indeed, Rivash had earlier said that a community could in principle raise a jest defense. One cannot prove otherwise from Responsa Rosh cited by the Rama at the end of siman 163, which says communal action does not require a kinyan, because there the community was not claiming jest but only trying to retract for lack of kinyan.
The wealthy. This is not literal. It means any person who is not poor and therefore had an obligation to teach his son or hire a teacher. That is why the Rama at the beginning of siman 336 does not use the phrase "his wealthy son-in-law." The Sema reads differently.
In other cases. See the Shach's distinction between this case and seif 5, where a jest defense is ordinarily recognized only in response to an actual demand. The Shach concludes that here and in comparable cases, even if the defendant said "you are my witnesses," he may still be exempt. See his full discussion.
