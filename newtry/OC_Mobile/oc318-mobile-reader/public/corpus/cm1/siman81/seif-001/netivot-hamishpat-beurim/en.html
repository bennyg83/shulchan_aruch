I was joking with you. See Shach, seif katan 1, who writes that the claim, "I made a mistake," is stronger than the claim, "I was joking." This means only in a situation that does not constitute a complete admission, where the person could otherwise claim that he was joking. There, saying that he did not calculate carefully is a stronger explanation than saying he was merely joking. But where there was a complete and binding admission, such as an admission by a person on his deathbed or a similar case in which he cannot claim joking, he likewise cannot claim that he made a mistake. When a person makes a formal and complete admission, we presume that he checked his calculation carefully, as is evident in several places. This is straightforward, and Tumim writes likewise.
The wealthy person. See Shach, through his statement that the rule applies to anyone who is not poor. Tumim cites the original responsum, which explains that the principal obligation arises from the laws of vows and tzedakah. The measure of poverty is one who lacks two hundred zuz and is therefore eligible to receive tzedakah. Although elsewhere a ferry payment or a mistaken chalitzah can create exceptional circumstances, it appears that no distinction should be made here between rich and poor, because the authorities state their rule without qualification. There, the person was compelled to speak as he did, so the commitment resembles a vow made under duress. Here, by contrast, he made the statement without any compulsion. It is reasonable to assume that he intentionally promised the payment and became obligated through a vow. If he dies, however, his heirs are exempt, just as with an ordinary vow that dies with the person. According to this, if there was a kinyan and a written instrument, the obligation is enforceable. This is unlike chalitzah in siman 169, where even a kinyan and a document do not help because the coercive circumstances are known and invalidate the gift. Here there is no such coercion, so a kinyan and document are effective. Merely saying, "You are my witnesses," does not help, as the Shach and Tumim write. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that the obligation arises as wages for services, but this is incorrect. Since the other person was already obligated to perform the act, it does not create an ordinary wage obligation, just as saying, "Make a sukkah or tefillin for yourself," does not create employment. Hagahot Maimoniyot, which uses the rationale of joking, apparently holds that when a person intentionally undertakes to obligate himself monetarily in connection with performance of a mitzvah, it can resemble wages, except that he may still claim that he was joking. The Shach disagrees and holds that chalitzah is different because it is equivalent to saying, "Make a sukkah for me," since the chalitzah benefits her where the yavam otherwise wishes to perform yibbum. In his second answer the Shach writes that even according to Hagahot Maimoniyot, which requires the defense of joking, the declaration "You are my witnesses" still does not create liability.
