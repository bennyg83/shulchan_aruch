It never happened. Ramban, citing Rabbi David, writes that one who previously made an informal admission may even later say, "I paid after that admission." Although this later plea is not itself mere idle talk, the original admission was of a kind that did not create binding liability. It is therefore not sufficient to establish him as a false denier regarding that earlier statement. This is the meaning of the Gemara's general statement that he was not established as a false denier. See what I wrote in siman 79, seif katan 10.
Idle words. The Shach writes that if the defendant later says, "It never happened," he is exempt even if, after the witnesses come, he does not expressly add, "I was joking with you." He concludes that all authorities agree. Gidulei Terumah, however, says that according to Baal HaTerumot and others he must later claim expressly that he was joking. Ramban's novellae support Gidulei Terumah. Explaining Rashi's phrase that "people do not remember idle words," Ramban says that because the earlier admission was mere joking, a person may forget it and therefore the later denial does not establish him as a false denier. He can then return and say, "I was joking with you." Ramban adds that where the claimant says, "Yesterday you admitted before so and so," and the defendant remains silent without pleading joking, we tell him to pay. Thus the later total denial does not itself automatically function as the joking defense. The defendant must invoke it. Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam can likewise be understood this way. Therefore Gidulei Terumah's reading is well grounded.
To his wealthy son in law. The Sema says the word "wealthy" must also be inserted in siman 336, because Mordechai uses that term. The Shach says "wealthy" is not literal; it means merely that the son in law is not poor and therefore is already obligated to teach his own child or hire a teacher. For that reason Rama omitted the word later. Responsa Maimoniyot clarifies the matter. Reuven promised his son in law Shimon two litra if Shimon would study with his son, Reuven's grandson. Reuven later wished to retract because no kinyan had been made. The response says that if Shimon had become so poor that he possessed less than two hundred zuz and was eligible for charity, Reuven's verbal promise would have the force of a vow to charity and must be fulfilled. The fact that Shimon has a mitzvah to teach his son does not change this. If Reuven merely said, "I will give you a maneh," the vow would still bind. If Reuven denies making the promise, he takes hesset and is exempt. Thus the word "wealthy" means one who has at least two hundred zuz and is not supported by charity, not some undefined level of affluence. Even a poor father is obligated to teach his son, so the payment is not ordinary wages merely because he is poor. His claim arises through the law of a vow. Consequently, if the promisor dies, the heirs ordinarily need not fulfill a mere vow that bound only him personally. However, as I explain below, if the promise was accompanied by an oath or other circumstance conclusively showing that he was not joking, the transaction can become actual wages rather than a mere vow. In that case the heirs can be liable.
He can say, "I was joking." The Shach goes further and says that even if the promisor said, "You are my witnesses," he is exempt, because even if he intended a present new obligation, most authorities hold that a person cannot create a new monetary obligation without kinyan, and even Rambam's contrary rule concerns an unconditional undertaking. Here the obligation depends upon another's act and there is nothing upon which it can rest. In my view, however, ordinary hire does not require kinyan. Once another person performs work at one's request, the requester owes whatever compensation he promised. If the promised amount exceeds normal value, he may sometimes say, "I was joking." But if "You are my witnesses" removes the joking explanation, the obligation rests on hire, not on a bare self undertaking. The act was performed at his request. One might object that where the worker was already independently obligated to perform the act, there is no law of hire. But Responsa Maimoniyot itself proves otherwise. It discusses whether the promisor must swear that he intended joking. The very possibility of such an oath shows that if it were known he did not intend to joke, payment would be due. If there were no law of hire at all because the son in law was independently obligated to teach his child, the promisor would owe nothing even if he sincerely intended payment. Rashi in Yevamot likewise says that where a woman promised two hundred zuz for chalitzah, after the chalitzah one initially tells her to pay because she accepted the condition, like any other hire. The Gemara exempts her only because she can say she was joking in order to induce performance. Thus even where the other party has a mitzvah obligation to act, a payment promise can have the law of hire if it was genuinely intended. Accordingly, if it is independently known that the promisor did not intend joking, he must pay. If he swore or gave a formal handshake, that certainly proves seriousness and creates a full wage obligation. The same applies if "You are my witnesses" is sufficient in the circumstances to remove the joking defense. One caveat is chalitzah, where Rashba suggests that even a document and kinyan may not permit extraction because the woman acted under duress to obtain a mitzvah act owed to her. That special coercion does not apply to ordinary ferrying or teaching. Where there is no such duress, removal of the joking defense leaves a valid wage obligation, and even heirs can be bound by it.
