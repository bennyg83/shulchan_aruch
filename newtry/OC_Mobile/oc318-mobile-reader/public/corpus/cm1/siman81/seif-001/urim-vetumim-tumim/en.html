I was joking with you. The Shach writes that the same is true if he says, "I was mistaken," and that this is an even stronger claim than saying, "I was joking." On that basis he has difficulty with the Gemara in Sanhedrin, which derives from the Mishnah that one must specifically say, "You are my witnesses," because otherwise the defendant can claim that he was joking. Perhaps the reason he is exempt without such a declaration is instead that he can say, "I was mistaken." See the Shach's strained answer based on the wording, "he said nothing." In my opinion, however, it is clear that the claim, "I was mistaken," is weaker than the claim, "I was joking." Joking is common, as the Gemara explains: since the claimant made sport of me by demanding money I did not owe, I likewise made sport of him by admitting in a joking manner. But it is not common for a person simply to be mistaken, because a person is generally precise regarding his words and financial affairs. If mistake were common, what would be gained by saying, "You are my witnesses"? He may truly have thought he owed the money and therefore admitted it, only he was mistaken. We would have to answer that once he says, "You are my witnesses," he is especially precise. Moreover, the Gemara asks whether a person jokes at the time of death. Why not simply advance for him the claim that he was mistaken, especially since mistake is more common for a dying person than for one who is healthy and certain? See what I wrote above in the rules of migo. Necessarily, the claim, "I was mistaken," is believed only when supported by a migo that he was joking. Where the joking defense does not apply, he cannot claim mistake either. There is therefore no difficulty. The Gemara properly derives from the Mishnah that without the declaration, "You are my witnesses," he can say, "I was joking with you," and therefore such a declaration is required. Otherwise, even a claim of mistake would not be believed.
And he must swear, etc. The Sema writes that he must also swear that he does not actually owe the claimant anything. The fact that he intended to joke does not help if he was independently obligated, in which case he certainly must pay. Why then is it not sufficient for him simply to swear that he owes nothing? The Sema writes that since he admitted the debt, we presume that he does owe it and do not believe his general oath that he owes nothing. The Shach writes similarly. Their words are strained. What does it mean that we do not believe him? He will swear that he owes nothing at all. If we suspect that he will swear falsely on that point, he can just as easily swear falsely that he intended to joke. Why is the latter oath stronger than the former? The matter is clear according to the rule that a person can obligate himself merely by saying before witnesses, "You are my witnesses," even if he never previously owed anything, as explained above in siman 40. The same follows even without the formula, "You are my witnesses," if his actual intent was to undertake an obligation rather than to joke. The formula itself does not create the underlying obligation. It merely prevents him from later saying, "I was joking." If he genuinely intended to obligate himself and was not joking, he becomes obligated just as if he had expressly said, "You are my witnesses." For this reason a dying person can create liability even without that formula, because a person does not joke at the time of death. This demonstrates that the obligation itself does not depend upon saying, "You are my witnesses"; the formula serves only to negate the joking defense. If he did not intend to joke, he becomes obligated. It is therefore clear why he must swear specifically that he intended to joke. Even if he owed the claimant nothing beforehand, if he did not intend to joke his own present statement would create the obligation. There is no difficulty. In a responsum I discussed at length why, if a person's spoken words can create liability, it matters that privately he intended to joke, since we rule that unexpressed thoughts have no legal effect. The answer must be that because the other party prompted him into joking, this is not a case of unexpressed thoughts. Unexpressed intention is disregarded where the spoken words contradict the person's inner intent. Here, however, the two are consistent, because even his spoken statement was made only in a manner of jest and mockery. His mouth therefore does not contradict his inner intent. This supports the ruling of Responsa Tashbetz in Even HaEzer siman 72, contrary to the Beit Shmuel there, seif katan 5. I discussed this at length in my novellae to Even HaEzer. See there.
That it is as he says. The Shach in seif katan 2 writes that if the claimant does not assert with certainty that the defendant owes him, he cannot require the defendant to swear, because this is only an uncertain claim and no oath is imposed on the basis of doubt. According to the Shach, I do not understand what novelty the Rosh and Mordechai, in the name of Rav Saadiah Gaon, teach by saying that a shevuat heset is imposed. Either the claimant does not assert with certainty, "You owe me," in which case there is no oath at all according to the Shach, or he does assert with certainty that the defendant owes him, in which case the defendant must in any event take a shevuat heset that he does not owe the money, and the claimant can then impose through gilgul any additional oath he wishes, even that the defendant is not his slave. It is strained to say that the novelty is the substantive rule I wrote in the previous seif katan, that if the defendant did not intend to joke, then even though he previously owed nothing he must now pay. If that were the point, the authorities should have stated that law. Their statement that the claimant may impose an oath would be unnecessary. This is especially impossible in the Rosh's wording, where he writes that it is reasonable that the Rabbis instituted a shevuat heset for all matters. The difficulty therefore remains from either side, as I wrote. Likewise, Responsa Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 64, discusses one who vowed to his son in law that he would study with his son, etc. Maharam writes that the son in law cannot require him to swear that he intended merely to joke, citing Rav Saadiah Gaon's rule that a shevuat heset may be imposed, because there, if the defendant does not raise the joking defense, we do not advance it for him, etc. See there. The difficulty is that the claimant is uncertain whether the defendant intended to joke, and the defendant was not otherwise obligated at all. According to the Shach there should be no oath even according to Rav Saadiah Gaon. Rather, it is clear that because the defendant expressly said with his own mouth that he owed the claimant money, and his assertion that he intended to joke is merely a later excuse, there are stronger grounds to suspect liability than in the case of a coin that fell from two people. According to all opinions, the claimant may therefore require an oath on this point, and Chazal instituted a shevuat heset in such a case. For this we need the Gaon's testimony that this is the law. The Shach cites as proof siman 75, where one says, "Perhaps you admitted to me that you owed one hundred," but the alleged admission was not made with the formula, "You are my witnesses," and the claimant cannot impose an oath. It is true that the source of that rule is Baal HaTerumot, gate 56, in the name of Raavad, and he himself holds there that even in the case of a coin that fell from two people a shevuat heset cannot be imposed at all. He therefore follows his own view. But the difficulty remains for the Mechaber and Tur. We must therefore explain that the earlier case concerns a claimant who says, "You admitted to me," without the formula, "You are my witnesses," while the defendant entirely denies the event and says, "It never happened." There is no shevuat heset there because even if the defendant admitted making the statement, there still would not necessarily be a definite monetary obligation, since perhaps he intended only to joke. The claimant does not know that the defendant truly owes him, and the defendant would be exempt from payment, subject only to a possible shevuat heset. Consequently, when the defendant denies the entire event there is no shevuat heset. What would he swear? If he swears that he intended to joke, he is already saying that the event never occurred. If he swears that it never happened, even if he admitted it there would still be no certain monetary liability. This is also implied by the language there, which says that he denies everything. See what I wrote in Tumim to siman 75, seif katan 16.
Rather, "It never happened," etc. The Shach writes that in this case, even if the defendant did not himself claim, after witnesses came and testified that he had made the admission, that he intended to joke, we advance that defense for him. Such statements are treated as idle words, which people do not remember having said at all, and he therefore need not expressly make the claim. The Shach's conclusion accords with most authorities. However, the Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Zeh Borer, as the Gidulei Terumah explains him, writes that Rashi and Rabbenu Chananel hold that even where the defendant says, "It never happened," he must himself raise the joking defense. See there. I do not know where this view is indicated in Rashi, because I found no explicit statement to that effect. Perhaps the inference is from Rashi's explanation of the Mishnah, "He said that he owed him, this means nothing unless he says before us that he admitted to him." Rashi explains that the reason is that the person can say, "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy." Rashi does not explain that he can say, "I was joking with you," even though the Gemara later applies the joking defense to this very clause of the Mishnah. We must say that the phrase, "this means nothing," implies that the testimony provides no benefit whatsoever. If the only issue were the joking defense, the testimony would have some benefit, because if the defendant failed to assert, "I was joking," he would have to pay. Necessarily, Rashi invokes the defense that he admitted only to avoid appearing wealthy, which we advance for him even without his claim, so that the testimony has no practical effect. The Mishnah must therefore concern a case in which that defense is available, such as where no one demanded payment from him. Yet once we must say that the Mishnah does not speak of every possible situation, why not establish it in the ordinary case where the defendant denies everything and says, "It never happened," as one would expect when witnesses are needed? The Mishnah would then be entirely clear because of the joking defense, as appears from the Gemara, and the testimony would again have no practical effect if we advance the defense for him. Since Rashi did not explain it this way, perhaps this shows that he holds that even in such a case the defendant must personally raise the joking defense. Rashi therefore had to explain the Mishnah as concerning a situation where the defense of avoiding the appearance of wealth applies and is advanced for him. Alternatively, the inference may be from Rashi's statement that once the defendant has been established as a denier, "he is not believed with an oath." Why did Rashi mention an oath? He could simply have said that the defendant is not believed. Rashi may have intended to exclude the other opinion, which holds that the defendant is believed because people do not remember idle remarks. Even according to that opinion, however, he at least must swear that he intended to joke. Rashi therefore explains according to the opinion that he is not believed even with an oath. This would show that we do not advance the joking defense for him automatically, for if we did, what role would an oath have? Consider this carefully. As a matter of practical law, however, most poskim follow the Shach's view.
The wealthy person. The Sema writes that he too is wealthy, so that the obligation rests upon him to study with his son or hire someone to do so, etc. This implies that the Sema holds that a poor person has no obligation to study with his son. We have not found such a rule. Especially where the father himself teaches his son, a wealthy and poor father are equal. The Mishnah states without qualification that a father is obligated to teach his son Torah, with no distinction between rich and poor. Rather, the matter is explicit in Maharam's responsum, cited in Responsa Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 64. Had the person been poor, he would have been obligated by virtue of his vow, because "that which has left your lips" applies to tzedakah, and he would have had to fulfill his vow as an obligation of tzedakah. Since, however, he possessed two hundred zuz and was therefore ineligible to receive tzedakah, he was no longer obligated on that basis. Accordingly, the Shach's statement in seif katan 5 that the word "wealthy" is not literal, and that the same applies to anyone who is not poor because he is obligated to study with his son, etc., is not precise. Rather, it means anyone who is not considered poor for purposes of receiving tzedakah, namely one who possesses two hundred zuz. This is simple.
He may say, "I was joking," etc. Even if he said, "You are my witnesses," that does not help. Maharam compares this case to one who says, "Take a dinar and ferry me across," or, "Take two hundred and perform chalitzah for me," where the promisor can say, "I intended only to deceive you," because the other party was independently obligated to perform the act. There too, saying, "You are my witnesses," does not help. Therefore, although Maharam's above responsum implies that a kinyan would create liability, and the Shach likewise writes this in seif katan 6, that point too is subject to dispute. In Even HaEzer siman 169, seif 51, the Shulchan Aruch rules in accordance with the Rashba concerning mistaken chalitzah that even a written instrument or kinyan is ineffective. See there. If so, the same should apply here, since Maharam's responsum compares the two cases, unless the promisor placed money in the hands of a third party with instructions to give it if the other person studied with his son. In that case he is obligated to pay, as the Shulchan Aruch writes there in siman 169 concerning chalitzah. See below in siman 129, where I write that the Rosh appears to disagree with the Rashba and to hold that a kinyan is effective. This also appears from Maharam's above responsum. The matter is therefore a doubt in the law. However, the Shach further writes that even where the above rationale, comparing the case to chalitzah, does not apply, a person still cannot create a new obligation by saying, "You are my witnesses," if he was not already obligated. This is so, the Shach says, even though above in siman 40 we rule like the Rambam that one who says, "I owe you," and adds, "You are my witnesses," becomes obligated even if he previously owed nothing. The Shach distinguishes that the Rambam's rule concerns an unconditional obligation, whereas a conditional obligation cannot be created without a kinyan. This does not appear correct. Even Tosafot and the Rosh, who generally disagree with the Rambam and hold that a person cannot create a new obligation merely by saying, "You are my witnesses," without a kinyan or written instrument, agree that if one says to another, "Do this and I will give you such and such," and the other person acts in reliance on that instruction, the promisor becomes obligated. The worker is entitled to his wages. Tosafot in Yevamot 106a and the Rosh likewise write that where the value of the service is not fixed, the promisor must pay whatever amount he stipulated if the other person performs the requested act. The joking defense does not apply, and no kinyan or written instrument is required at all. The Rama rules similarly below at the end of siman 264. Maharam himself in this responsum gives only the reason that the case resembles, "Take a dinar and ferry me across," because the other party was independently obligated to study. Without that reason, the promise would be binding. This is simple. Indeed, even the formula, "You are my witnesses," is unnecessary, because the other party performed an act in reliance upon his words. This is clear and simple.
