I was jesting, etc. Just as the claimant intended to jest with me by demanding something I did not owe him, I too intended to jest by admitting something I did not owe.
He is believed. See Tosafot. The same person may be believed to say, "I made a mistake," where he could otherwise have claimed that he was merely jesting, because he has a migo. Where no such migo exists, he cannot simply say that he erred.
And he must swear, etc. He must swear not only that he does not owe the money, but also that when he admitted it he intended only to jest. See Tumim. I explained that if he did not intend merely to jest, his admission itself obligates him to pay even though no underlying debt ever existed.
That the truth is as he says, etc. The Shach holds that this oath applies only where the claimant says with certainty, "You owe me," so there is a definite claim. If the claimant himself is uncertain whether any debt exists and only questions whether the admission was in jest, there is no oath on an uncertain claim. See Tumim. I clarified that this is incorrect. Even on the isolated issue whether the defendant intended to jest, he may be required to swear because the surrounding circumstances provide a concrete basis for suspicion, like the case of a coin that fell from one of two people. If, however, the defendant simply denies that any admission occurred, there is no oath unless the claimant says with certainty that money is owed. The Sema cites the Mordechai that even if the claimant does not expressly demand an oath but merely demands payment, beit din itself imposes the appropriate oath. See there.
It never happened. Meaning he says, "I never said those words, even before witnesses." He is exempt. Since at the time he intended only to jest, he paid no attention to meaningless words and later forgot them, so his present statement that he never said them does not make him a false denier even if witnesses show that he did. The Shach concludes that even after the witnesses arrive he need not personally explain, "I was jesting and therefore forgot." Beit din raises that explanation for him, because had he remembered he could have made that defense. See Tumim.
But not concerning a community that admits. Meaning a public community. The Rosh already wrote in responsa, klal 8, that a communal undertaking does not require a kinyan and is treated as though a kinyan was made. How then can a plea of jesting apply, when one cannot say after a kinyan that he was merely jesting? Since communal words have the force of a kinyan, the Shach's doubts are not compelling, and the Shulchan Aruch's ruling is firmly grounded.
Since he is not poor. The Sema explains that if the father is poor he has no obligation to hire a teacher for his son. See Tumim. This is incorrect. If he is poor, meaning he lacks two hundred zuz, the support becomes a charitable obligation under the verse obligating charity according to one's verbal undertaking.
He can say, "I was jesting," etc. See Tumim. Since he was already obligated independently, this resembles one who promises a dinar for an act that another party was already halachically bound to perform. The extra promise creates no obligation. Even an express "you are witnesses" may not help, and even a written instrument or kinyan is disputed among the authorities, unless he deposited money with a third party to be paid if the service is performed. But if he said to another person, "Do this act and I will pay you such and such," I concluded in Tumim that he becomes obligated even without saying "you are witnesses," because the payment is compensation for the recipient's labor. This is unlike the Shach, who writes that even "you are witnesses" is ineffective because no preexisting debt exists.
