Seif 7, in the gloss: One who said to his wealthy son-in-law, “Study with your son,” etc. Note: See Shach, who writes that in his opinion even if the father-in-law said, “You are my witnesses,” he is exempt. Ketzot HaChoshen challenges him and maintains that he is obligated under the law of hiring, since hiring does not require a formal act of acquisition. See Ketzot HaChoshen. In my humble opinion the Shach is correct. The law of hiring applies when Reuven tells Shimon to perform work that Reuven himself needs and from which Reuven himself derives benefit. In that case Reuven owes wages. But if Reuven tells Shimon to perform work for Levi, from which Reuven himself derives no benefit, then although Reuven may still be liable to Shimon where he cannot tell him, “Take back what you did,” Reuven can generally recover from Levi the value of the benefit Levi received, as is explained in the relevant laws. The same is true here. If the son-in-law studies with his own son, Reuven, the father-in-law, receives no proprietary benefit. The benefit belongs to the son-in-law himself, for he is independently obligated to teach his son or hire a teacher, and a son’s merit benefits his father. If we were to say that Reuven is obligated to pay his son-in-law as wages, Reuven would then turn around and recover from the son-in-law the value of the benefit that the son-in-law himself received. What purpose is served by transferring the same money back and forth? Rather, one might have thought that this is not ordinary hiring but an independent personal undertaking created by the condition. The Shach therefore correctly rules that the statement does not create liability. Understand this well.
