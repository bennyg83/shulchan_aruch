I was jesting with you, etc. It appears that the same applies if he claims, “I was mistaken in my admission.” This is also implied by the words of the Baal HaTerumot and by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch below, seif 28. Indeed, it appears that this follows all the more so, as I wrote below, seif 22, seif katan 55, and siman 126, seif katan 54, that a claim of mistake is stronger than a claim of jesting. Although below, seif 29, I wrote that he is not believed to say, “I was mistaken,” except by virtue of a migo that he could have said, “I repaid,” that concerns a case where he admitted with “You are my witnesses,” in which case he likewise cannot claim that he was jesting. Here, by contrast, he is believed by virtue of the migo that he could have claimed that he was jesting. Moreover, the claim of mistake is stronger than the claim of jesting, as I wrote. One should not ask: if so, how does the Gemara at the end of the chapter Zeh Borer infer from the Mishnah that he may say that he was jesting? Perhaps the Mishnah requires “You are my witnesses” because of a possible claim of mistake, whereas a claim of jesting is unavailable in any event. One can answer that the Mishnah teaches that he has said nothing until the witnesses say, “He admitted to him in our presence,” meaning that he said, “You are my witnesses.” This implies that if he did not admit with “You are my witnesses,” he has said nothing, and his original admission is treated as nonexistent. If the issue were mistake, however, his admission was in truth an admission, only that he now remembers that the matter was not so. Rather, the Mishnah must be speaking of an admission made so as not to appear wealthy, or an admission made in jest, where his original statement was never an admission at all, but merely a jest. Other answers may also be given, and this is clear.
And he must swear that the matter is as he says, etc. Meaning, he swears that he owes him nothing and that he intended only to jest with him. See below, seif 14, seif katan 35. It is obvious that when the claimant asserts that the defendant holds money belonging to him, the defendant must swear that he owes him nothing, as stated by the Rambam, the Baal HaTerumot, and the other poskim. The Sema and Bach likewise wrote this. See below, siman 75, seif 17, seif katan 59, where I wrote that when the claimant does not assert with certainty that the defendant owes him, the defendant is not subject to any oath, and we advance for him the claim that he intended to jest. See Responsa Mahari ibn Lev, book 2, siman 51; Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 286; and Responsa Maharik, end of siman 70, siman 73, beginning of siman 101, and siman 115.
Rather, he says, “There never was such a matter.” Meaning, he says, “I never admitted,” while witnesses say that he did admit. He is exempt, because people do not remember idle statements. It appears that this is so even if, after the witnesses came, he did not return and claim, “I was jesting with you.” This is also evident from the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber below, seif 21. Although we do not ordinarily advance for him the claim “I was jesting with you,” as explained below, seif 3, that applies when he acknowledges that he made the admission. But when he says that there never was such a matter, we advance for him the claim that he certainly was jesting, and for that reason he does not now remember having admitted before them, because people do not remember idle statements. It appears to me that all the poskim agree to this, and this is also the intent of the Baal HaTerumot. This is unlike the author of Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 42, part 1, folio 178a and 178b, who writes that according to the Baal HaTerumot and the other poskim he must later expressly claim that he was jesting. See there. This is incorrect, and the law is as I have written. I subsequently found support for my words in the Baal HaItur, who writes at the beginning of the section on admission, end of folio 104: “There is no difference whether he says, ‘I was jesting,’ or whether he says, ‘There never was such a matter’; he is exempt, as we say that people do not remember idle statements.” Meaning, had he remembered that he admitted, he would himself have claimed, “I was jesting with you,” but if he advances no claim at all, we do not advance it for him. End of his words.
And we say the claim of jesting only regarding an individual, etc. This requires further study as a matter of practical law, for the Rivash did not write this reason except as an additional consideration alongside the other reasons he gave in that case. He gave several reasons there why the claim of jesting did not apply, and wrote this point only in passing. See there. Moreover, earlier there the Rivash wrote that the community could have claimed that they were jesting. See there. Nor can proof be brought from what the Rosh wrote in a responsum, cited by the Rama below at the end of siman 163, that an act of the community does not require a kinyan. There the case is one in which they do not claim that they were jesting, but merely say that they wish to retract because there was no kinyan.
The wealthy man, etc. This is not precise. Rather, it means anyone who is not poor, since he is obligated to study with his son or hire a teacher for him. For this reason, the Rama below at the beginning of siman 336 did not write “his wealthy son in law.” See there.
He may say, “I was jesting with you.” Although the Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote in seif 5 that we say the claim of jesting only when the other party first demanded payment from him, that applies specifically where he admits, without having been asked, that he already owes his fellow one hundred zuz, for there is no reason to assume he was jesting. But where there is a reason and rationale to say that he intended only to jest, such as here, where his intent concerned a mitzvah, that the other should study with his son, who is also his grandson, and he is wealthy and therefore was himself obligated to study with him or hire a teacher for him, in such a case we even advance on his behalf the claim that he intended to jest. These are the words of the Sema. This forced explanation is unnecessary, for the reason for this law is straightforward. Only where he admits that he already owes the other party do we require that he himself claim that he was jesting, and that the claimant first demanded payment and he then admitted. Otherwise we say that, presumably, it is true that he already owed him. Here, by contrast, it is known that he did not already owe him anything, and the alleged obligation would arise only from his statement, “I will pay you.” Therefore, since in any event he was obligated to study with his son, he is exempt. The same law applies wherever he did not already owe the other party and only now promises verbally to pay him: he is exempt. This is as stated below, at the end of siman 264, regarding one who escaped from prison and encountered a ferryman and said to him, “Take a dinar and ferry me across.” The ferryman receives only his ordinary wage. Here it is not relevant to say that the teacher should receive his ordinary wage, because in any event he is obligated to study with his son. The same applies to a mistaken chalitzah, where one says, “Perform chalitzah on condition that she give you two hundred zuz”; he receives nothing, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 169, seif 50. It is implied there in the Gemara that we advance for her the claim that she was merely jesting, and we do so even initially. See the Gemara in the chapter Mitzvat Chalitzah, folio 106a. The Rama likewise wrote this in Even HaEzer, siman 165, end of seif 1, and see what I wrote there. Maharam, in Mordechai, chapter Zeh Borer, similarly derived the present law from the case of the ferryman and from the case of chalitzah. This responsum of Maharam is cited more fully in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 64. See there. Accordingly, the same applies in every comparable case, namely, where it is known that he did not previously owe the other party. Even if he volunteered on his own to pay, we say that he was merely jesting with him, for by what mechanism should the obligation to pay take effect? This is clear. Moreover, it appears to me that here, and in every comparable case, even if he said, “You are my witnesses,” he is exempt. Even if you say that he presently undertakes an obligation to pay, most poskim agree that a person cannot create a new obligation through the declaration “You are my witnesses,” without a kinyan, for something that he did not previously owe, as explained above in siman 40. Even according to the Rambam, who holds that one may obligate himself, I wrote there in seif katan 4 that this applies only where he undertakes an unconditional obligation. Here, however, his obligation is conditional upon the other studying with his son, ferrying him across, performing chalitzah, or the like. One can further say that the Rambam ruled only where a person independently undertakes an obligation without any external cause. Here, however, where he undertakes it on condition that the other study, ferry him, perform chalitzah, or the like, we are witnesses that even though he said, “You are my witnesses,” he intended only to induce the other to act, because he could not accomplish the matter otherwise. There is therefore nothing upon which the obligation can take effect, since he did not bind himself through a kinyan. So it appears to me, and examine this carefully.
