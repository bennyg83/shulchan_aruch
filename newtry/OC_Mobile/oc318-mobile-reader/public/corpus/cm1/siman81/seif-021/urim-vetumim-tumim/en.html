And if he did not raise the defense, etc. The Shach in seif katan 50 discusses at length an apparent contradiction in the Rambam, because the Rambam himself records the incident of the measure of saffron, which implies that beit din advances the defense for the defendant. The Shach therefore enters into strained distinctions concerning whether the claimant asks the defendant why witnesses contradict him or not. These suggestions have no substance. I already clarified above in Tumim, seif katan 19, that there is a distinction. When the statement was made by way of formal admission, we do not advance the defense for him. The Rambam's rulings are each directed to their own case. Likewise, what the Shach writes in seif katan 51 to prove that according to the Rambam even one who makes an unsolicited admission and then says, "I intended to joke," is believed, is incorrect. The Rambam lists distinct rulings together, and the defense that the person admitted only so as not to appear wealthy applies to the case of an unsolicited admission. All is therefore well. Another distinction is possible. If the claimant asserts with certainty, "You owe me," and there are witnesses as well, we do not advance a defense for the defendant. But if the claimant himself does not know whether the defendant owes him and sues only on the basis of the witnesses' testimony, then we do advance the defense, because there is no genuine certain claim by the claimant. This is indeed suggested by the language of the Gemara and the Rambam. After the person said, "Who owes me? So and so," that person came and sued him, implying that the suit was based upon the defendant's own statement. Therefore beit din advances the defense for him. In my opinion this distinction is logically correct. It is difficult, however, to place it in the Rambam's intent, because the Rambam requires a shevuat heset. If the claimant sues only on the basis of witnesses and we ourselves advance the defense that the statement was made merely so as not to appear wealthy, what is the basis for that oath? This is especially difficult because the Rambam does not hold that an oath is imposed in the case of an open money pouch.
Therefore, the correct explanation of the Rambam is what I wrote above in seif katan 19. With respect to other authorities who hold that we generally do not advance the defense, however, the distinction just stated may be used. It also explains the Raavad's objection to the Rambam's ruling concerning the incident of the measure of saffron, where the Rambam concludes, "he takes a shevuat heset and is exempt." The Raavad writes, "This does not accord with the Gemara." The Lechem Mishneh explains that the Raavad holds that we do advance the defense, but the Shach already objects that the Raavad's view is that we do not advance it. The Shach himself does not offer a satisfactory explanation of the Raavad, and there is no need to reproduce it. According to what I wrote, the Raavad's intent is clear. Since the Raavad holds that we do not advance the defense, the incident of the saffron must concern a claimant who did not sue from certain knowledge but only on the basis of the witnesses, as I wrote. If so, what is the nature of the oath? This is why the Raavad objects specifically to the Rambam's statement, "he takes a shevuat heset," saying that this does not follow the Gemara, because there was no certain claim upon which to impose a shevuat heset. This is clear and correct. One could force an alternative answer, namely that in the saffron incident the defendant actually raised the defense that he admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. The Shach asks what novelty the Gemara would then teach. One could answer that it teaches that even where the person's original statement, "Who owes me?" seems designed to avoid appearing poor, he can nevertheless say that he then spoke merely so as not to appear wealthy. This is strained. The explanation I gave is more reasonable and clarifies the Raavad's gloss.
