If he did not raise the claim, we do not raise it for him. Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Zeh Borer likewise writes that the commentators agreed that beit din does not raise even the defense that the admission was made so that people would not think him wealthy. Beit Yosef cites this above in siman 32. This is also plainly the view of Raavad in his glosses and in the passage of Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, part 1, cited below, as well as the view of Rav Hai Gaon in Mishpetei Shevuot. This appears correct, for once the Gemara says, “If he did not claim it, we do not claim it for him,” there is no basis to distinguish between a defense of jesting and a defense that he wished not to appear wealthy. It appears to me, however, that this applies only when the claimant says, “You admitted the debt to me before witnesses.” He must then answer that particular contention. This is the case of the baraita, where the claimant says the next day, “Give it to me,” meaning, “Give me the maneh that you admitted yesterday.” Concerning that case the baraita teaches that if the defendant does not claim jesting or avoidance of appearing wealthy, beit din does not claim it for him. Where the claimant does not rely upon the admission, but merely says, “You owe me a maneh,” and the defendant answers, “I owe you nothing,” beit din tells the claimant to bring his witnesses. If the witnesses then say only that the defendant told them he owed the claimant, that evidence has no force unless the claimant himself rests his case upon the admission. The defendant need not independently say that he was jesting or that he wished not to appear wealthy, because the claimant never asked, “Did you not admit before witnesses?” Beit din need not frame a stronger claim than the claimant himself. This explains the Mishnah that says, “He has said nothing.” The Baal HaMaor was compelled to explain that because the admission was volunteered, beit din raises the defense for him. Yet, as explained above, we do not follow the Baal HaMaor in holding that a volunteered admission automatically causes beit din to raise the defense. Rather, the Mishnah asks the witness, “How do you know that this one owes that one?” This indicates that the claimant merely asserts the debt and the defendant denies it, while the witness says, “He told me that he owes him.” Such testimony proves only that the defendant spoke before the witness, but the claimant never made the admission itself the basis of his action. Beit din therefore need not ask why he made the admission and require him to answer that he was jesting or avoiding the appearance of wealth. This also resolves the incident of the man known as “the master of a kav of promissory notes,” concerning whom Rav Nachman said that a person may make such a statement so as not to appear wealthy. Tosafot and those following them inferred that beit din raises that defense on its own. That is not the principal ruling, for the defenses of jesting and avoidance of appearing wealthy are alike. Nor is it satisfactory to say that the defendant himself raised the defense, both because that is strained and because the Baal HaMaor correctly asks what Rav Nachman would then have taught beyond the Mishnah. Rather, the man had said, “Who is owed money by me other than so-and-so and so-and-so?” One of those persons later sued him simply for the alleged debt. Rav Nachman taught that although we know of the prior statement, we regard it as having been made to avoid appearing wealthy even though the defendant did not expressly say so, because the claimant did not sue upon the admission itself. Rav Nachman was thereby explaining the Mishnah. Raavad’s gloss that Rambam’s presentation is “not according to the Gemara” should be understood accordingly. Lechem Mishneh explains that Raavad believed beit din automatically raises the defense of avoiding the appearance of wealth, while Rambam believed that even this defense must be raised by the litigant and that the Gemara merely omitted mention of his claim. This is difficult. First, Rambam’s own words there imply that the defendant said only, “I owe him nothing,” and Raavad would more naturally have objected to Rambam’s immediately following rule that if he did not claim the defense, beit din does not claim it. Second, Raavad surely did not mean that no oath is required, for even if the witnesses are disregarded the defendant must take a hesset oath that he owes nothing. Third, Raavad himself holds that beit din does not raise the defense of avoiding the appearance of wealth, as Baal HaTerumot records in gate 42, part 1. Raavad therefore meant the opposite: Rambam’s wording makes it appear that the defendant merely denied the debt and was nevertheless released, whereas the Gemara does not record that he expressly said the admission was made to avoid appearing wealthy. Rambam writes in chapter 6 of Hilchot To'en that the man called “the master of a kav of promissory notes” said, “Who is owed money by me other than so-and-so?” That person sued him, and the defendant said, “I owe him nothing.” The sages ruled that he take a hesset oath and be released. This seems to contradict Rambam’s next rule that if the defendant does not claim jesting or avoidance of appearing wealthy, beit din does not claim it for him. The distinction stated above resolves the contradiction. The latter rule concerns a claimant who says, “You admitted to me before witnesses,” thereby making the admission itself the basis of the suit. The defendant must then explain the admission. In the incident, however, the claimant merely demanded payment of an alleged debt. Although beit din knew of the earlier statement, the claimant did not sue on the basis of that statement. The defendant therefore sufficed with a denial and a hesset oath, while beit din treated the prior statement as one made to avoid appearing wealthy. This appears to me the correct reconciliation of Rambam and the correct practical rule. It may be that all authorities agree that where the claimant does not say, “You admitted to me,” the defendant’s simple denial is sufficient, even though an admission before witnesses is known, and beit din may attribute it to jesting or to avoidance of appearing wealthy. The authorities did not spell out this distinction because they assumed the ordinary case in which the claimant expressly relies upon the admission.
But if he claimed, “I was jesting,” etc. This is Rambam’s wording, and it implies that the defense of jesting applies even where the admission was volunteered. Rambam first writes, “Similarly, one who admits of his own accord,” and then continues, “But if he claimed, ‘I was jesting,’” which refers back to all the cases. I demonstrated above in seif 5, seif katan 12, that Rambam and Maggid Mishneh hold that one may plead jesting even after a volunteered admission. This is unlike the Mechaber’s unqualified ruling above in seif 5 and the Rama’s gloss here, which restrict the defense to an admission made after the opposing party demanded payment and do not mention any dissent. It is also unlike Beit Yosef and the Sema in seif katan 47. I cited there many leading authorities who hold that jesting may be claimed even after a volunteered admission and showed that this is the principal reading of the Gemara. One should not object from Rambam’s alternative defense that the admission was made so as not to appear wealthy. I demonstrated above in seif 14, seif katan 34, that one cannot claim this where he admitted before the claimant himself, while Rambam’s opening case says that one told his fellow before witnesses, “I have a maneh of yours,” and the fellow answered affirmatively. Rambam’s phrase, “or that he did so in order not to appear wealthy,” refers only to his broader words, “in all these matters and similar ones,” meaning cases in which that defense is logically available, such as an admission outside the claimant’s presence. Rambam relied on his express rule in the next chapter that the defense applies only when the admission was not made before the claimant. The jesting defense cannot be restricted in that fashion, because Rambam begins with it immediately and says, “But if he claimed, ‘I was jesting,’” indicating that it applies to all the preceding cases. Nor does Rambam state anywhere else that jesting is unavailable after a volunteered admission.
