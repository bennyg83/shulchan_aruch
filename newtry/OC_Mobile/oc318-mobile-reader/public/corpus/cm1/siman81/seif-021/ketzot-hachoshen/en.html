So as not to make himself appear wealthy. Radvaz rules that Reuven, who bought a house and said he bought it for consecrated property, cannot later say, "I said so merely not to appear wealthy," because a person fears making a false claim in favor of consecrated property. Knesset HaGedolah objects from the baraita in Perek Zeh Borer: heirs saw their father hide money and heard him say it belonged to second tithe, yet the statement can be treated as a ruse. In my view Radvaz is correct. The usual self impoverishment defense does not apply toward consecrated property because any person may act on behalf of consecration and demand its property. This resembles an admission made in the claimant's presence, where the person fears immediate demand. The baraita about second tithe is no proof. Second tithe remains the owner's property for many purposes, and even according to the view that it is property of Heaven, the owner himself eats it in sanctity. More importantly, Rashi explains that the father told his sons this in order to make them reduce their expenditures. That is not the ordinary self impoverishment defense at all, but a separate concrete explanation directed specifically toward his children. This distinction is clear because Rashba holds that the self impoverishment defense does not apply to an admission that particular property is a deposit. Yet the baraita's words, "It belongs to so and so," sound precisely like a deposit, since a loan is given for expenditure and one would not ordinarily say the actual coins "belong" to the lender. The fact that the baraita nevertheless permits disregard proves that it rests on the separate explanation that the father sought to restrain his sons' spending, not on the general rule of self impoverishment. Therefore no proof can be drawn from there against Radvaz's rule concerning consecrated property.
The self impoverishment defense does not apply. I wrote in siman 80 that in monetary law ordinary explanations do not permit withdrawal from an admission. Only the two explanations specifically recognized by the Gemara, joking and speaking so as not to appear wealthy, are generally accepted. Mordechai in Perek Mi SheMet, however, appears broader. He writes that where a person says in the absence of the owner, "So and so has a deposit in my hands," and witnesses hear him, he can say he spoke so as not to appear wealthy, or can offer another explanation and be believed because the owner was not present. This sounds as though other explanations may also work. If so, why would they not also help when the claimant is present or even after a demand? One might answer that the distinction between presence and absence applies not only to self impoverishment but to every explanation. Where someone actively confronts the person or is present to demand the money, a false admission is much less plausible. Maharit makes this point concerning prohibitory law. He says explanations are ordinarily accepted only when a person volunteered the statement. If another witness confronted her or someone said, "Are you not married?" and she answered yes, an explanation is far weaker, because people do not ordinarily admit falsely when someone is actively asserting the fact against them. He compares this to money, where a volunteered admission can sometimes be explained as self impoverishment but an admission in response to a demand is much stronger. According to Mordechai, therefore, perhaps other concrete explanations can work where the claimant was absent, but only when the person himself articulates a specific, credible reason recognized by beit din. We do not advance such unknown explanations for heirs. That also answers why Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah did not advance some unspecified explanation for the heirs in the episode of Kav Reshu. Self impoverishment is a known common explanation and can be advanced; an unknown personal explanation cannot. The same is true in prohibitory law. Until the woman herself explains her earlier statement, her husband must treat it as binding unless the explanation is already known from surrounding circumstances. Thus even according to Mordechai, the broader rule is limited. Nevertheless, the plain rulings of Tur and Shulchan Aruch mention only joking and self impoverishment and do not recognize other explanations in monetary law. The primary practical rule is therefore as I wrote in siman 80: no general explanation undoes a monetary admission beyond those specifically recognized by the Gemara, unless the later words truly clarify the earlier statement rather than contradict it.
