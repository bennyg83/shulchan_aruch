He acquired his share, etc. Shach attacks this ruling. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, records a dispute. Even R. Natan bar Meir, who supports the rule that delivery to one creditor can operate for both, appears to say so only where the borrower did not expressly require the document to be placed in his own hands. If he said, "Give it specifically to me," then according to that view the second creditor should not acquire merely because the document reached the first creditor, unless the first was explicitly appointed to acquire for his colleague. Shach therefore says Mechaber's ruling is too broad. There is no difficulty against Mechaber. R. Natan bar Meir's entire discussion follows Rif's system, in which even after a kinyan the creditor's right does not fully arise until the document reaches him. If so, where the borrower says, "Give it to me," the second creditor plainly has not received what Rif requires. But we follow the later authorities who reject Rif and hold that an ordinary kinyan itself creates the creditor's substantive right without requiring physical delivery. On that view, once the kinyan was made, both creditors already acquired. The reason Mechaber speaks of the document being delivered to one of them is not because that delivery creates the second creditor's legal acquisition. It removes the borrower's evidentiary migo. So long as the document remains in the borrower's hands, he can destroy it or claim the obligation never became operative. Once the paper leaves his possession, that migo is gone. Then both creditors can enforce the rights they already acquired through the kinyan. This is why Mechaber's ruling is sound. Even if one accepts Rif's requirement of delivery, there is another analogy that supports Mechaber. A deathbed gift and a deed found tied to the deceased's body ordinarily require some act showing the donor intended the gift to take effect. Yet Mechaber rules below in siman 250, seif 25, that where one person gives gifts to many and effects acquisition for one beneficiary, that can confirm the entire testamentary arrangement. Sema explains that giving effect to one portion reveals a general intent that the whole gift stand. R. Natan bar Meir himself derives the present rule from the case of acquiring for one through another. Thus, even under a delivery-based model, delivery to one can sometimes confirm the entire multi-beneficiary obligation. Mechaber's ruling is therefore defensible from either direction. The greater difficulty is actually Tur. Tur seems, as explained above, to follow Rosh's position that in a loan kinyan physical delivery can still matter. If so, Shach's objection has more force against Tur: why should delivery to one creditor help the other? There is also a textual difficulty. Below in siman 250, Tur omits R. Natan bar Meir's explanation of the deathbed-gift rule and instead cites Rashbam and Raavad. If he did not adopt R. Natan bar Meir's mechanism there, why rely upon it here? The answer is that Tur's present case is not an ordinary future loan at all. He says the debtor "obligated himself to two people by kinyan." That language indicates a complete present obligation, like a gift, effective whether or not any later loan is advanced. The phrase "obligated himself" means a final undertaking, not merely a contemplated borrowing. Baal HaTerumot, the source of Tur's law, likewise discusses it in the framework of a gift-like absolute obligation. In such a shtar aknayata, as explained above, the borrower cannot retract the substantive obligation even before the paper reaches the creditor. Thus both creditors already acquired. Why then does Tur say, "Once the document left the obligor's possession, both acquired"? Because while the document remains in his own hands he retains an evidentiary migo, such as destroying the document or claiming the obligation was subject to a condition. Once it leaves his possession in any manner, that migo disappears. This is why Tur does not say, "Once the first creditor acquired, the second acquired through him." He says specifically, "Once the document left the obligor's possession." That wording proves the reason is not that the first creditor acts as an agent acquiring for the second. The underlying rights already existed. The only significance of the paper leaving the debtor is removal of his migo. Accordingly, Bach and Shach's explanation omits the main point. Tur's language is exact, and Mechaber's ruling is correct from every side.
