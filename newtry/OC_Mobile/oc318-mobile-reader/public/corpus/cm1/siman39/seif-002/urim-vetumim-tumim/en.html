So that they should not convert an oral loan into a written loan, etc. This is Rambam's wording. One should ask why Rambam gives this reason. If the borrower did not tell the witnesses to write, their written statement should in any event be ineffective as testimony because it is "from their writing and not from their mouths," as Rama later writes in seif 3 in the name of Rashba. If so, even a document expressly without property warranty should not be written. The problem would not be only that writing turns an oral loan into a written loan. The writing itself would be legally ineffective testimony. It is strained to say Rambam rejects Rashba and follows the view later cited by Rama that if such a document is authenticated, beit din can judge upon it. There are two possible explanations. First, Rambam follows his own theory that all documents are invalid by Torah law as written testimony, even where the borrower says "write," and Chazal validated documents as an enactment for commercial necessity, as I explained above in siman 28. If so, Rambam must explain why Chazal suspended the "not from their writing" rule where the borrower authorizes writing but not where he does not. His answer is that even independently of the written-testimony problem, witnesses are not entitled to write without authorization because doing so upgrades the debt from oral to documented status and allows collection from encumbered property. Therefore Chazal had no reason to validate that unauthorized writing. They left it under the Torah rule of written testimony and it remains invalid. But where the borrower expressly says "write," the witnesses are authorized to create the document. Chazal then gave the document legal force despite the Torah written-testimony issue. On this reading Rambam's reason is fundamental. Second, perhaps Rambam's reason refers especially to his additional rule that even after the borrower originally says "write," the witnesses must consult him again before delivering or completing the document after delay. There the ordinary "not from their writing" objection is no longer available because he did authorize writing. Why must they ask again? Because converting the debt into a collectible written obligation has serious consequences, and the borrower may have changed his mind before the document was finalized. This explanation fits the immediate language. However, it raises another question. If the witnesses must consult him again before delivery, what did the original instruction "write" accomplish? If he later says to deliver the document, that final authorization itself should suffice. Why need the earlier instruction? One answer is that the original "write" removes the document from the category of invalid written testimony. Once he authorizes writing, the witnesses' writing becomes a legitimate documentary form. Even if he later withdraws permission to give the document to the lender, the paper can still function in a limited way. For example, if the witnesses later forget the details but the paper is delivered to beit din, it may establish the underlying oral debt so that the borrower cannot deny the event entirely, even though it cannot be used to collect from encumbered property because he revoked delivery as a written loan. Thus the first instruction gives the writing testimonial status; the later consultation governs whether it becomes an enforceable creditor document. According to the first explanation, one can say instead that even after an original instruction to write, the witnesses should not physically prepare the document before a final authorization because it appears false or creates a document not yet intended to be operative. This gives some support to Maggid Mishneh's position, cited by Mechaber below in seif 13, concerning documents that appear false if prepared before the proper moment. In any event, Rambam's wording is not redundant. It either explains the rabbinic boundary between authorized and unauthorized documents, or it explains why renewed consultation is needed after an original instruction to write.
They must consult him again. This is Rambam's view. He explains the Gemara in Perek Af Al Pi, where one who says, "Write, sign, and give it to him" requires renewed consultation, as referring to loan documents. Raavad disagrees and says the Gemara concerns a ketubah or a gift document, where the giver may have changed his mind about conferring a voluntary benefit, not an ordinary loan. Shach in seif katan 4 writes that Rambam is alone in extending the rule to loan documents. Beit Yosef thought Rosh's responsum, klal 57, supports Rambam. Rosh says that even where there was a kinyan, if the obligor protests before the document is written, the witnesses may not write. Beit Yosef reasons: if Rosh understood the Gemara's renewed-consultation rule only in gifts, how could one prove anything about loans? Perhaps a gift can be withdrawn from documentary form even after kinyan, while a loan cannot. Shach answers that perhaps Rosh infers all the more so from a gift to a loan. I do not understand Shach's "all the more so." Rashba, responsum 1034, holds exactly the opposite distinction: after a kinyan a gift giver can still stop the document from being written, while in a loan the creditor's right is stronger. Mordechai in the name of Rabbenu Tam also distinguishes between gift and loan, as Shach himself cites later in seif katan 13. There is therefore no obvious kal vachomer from gift to loan. One can still defend Rosh in another way. Perhaps the Gemara concerns a gift where renewed consultation is needed because the giver may not wish to complete the gift. But after a kinyan, the gift itself can no longer be revoked. At that point the only remaining issue is the document. Regarding that documentary issue, gift and loan are similar: in both, the underlying obligation or transfer already exists, and the question is whether the party consents to a writing that strengthens collection or proof. Rosh could therefore infer from a kinyan gift to a loan. Still, Beit Yosef is entitled to read Rosh more directly. Rosh's own words in Perek Af Al Pi support Rambam's broad interpretation. Rosh asks why renewed consultation is required at all. If someone says to another, "Take this object to so-and-so," does the messenger have to ask again before delivery? Rosh answers that the circumstances reveal a reason for reconsideration: the party did not immediately complete the transfer through a kinyan sudar, even though that would have been stronger and simpler. That reasoning applies to a loan document as well. If the borrower wanted the strongest immediate documentary obligation, he could have made the kinyan in the form that automatically stands for writing and would not have needed a separate "write" instruction. His choice of a weaker route suggests he may wish to reconsider before the document is issued. Thus the same reason operates in both gift and loan. Accordingly, Rosh can reasonably be read as supporting Rambam, and Beit Yosef's inference is not refuted by Shach.
