They may not write, etc. Rashba writes there that reason itself requires this. Otherwise, if many people were present, every pair could write a separate document for the creditor, allowing him to collect repeatedly. Ritva writes similarly in chapter HaIsh Mekadesh regarding the rule that an agent may become a witness: If one makes a kinyan obligating himself monetarily to another and the beneficiary secretly stationed witnesses behind a fence, the witnesses may testify about the kinyan in beit din, because witnesses exist to prevent falsehood. But they may not write a document for him without the obligor's authorization. The rule that an ordinary kinyan stands to be written applies only when the kinyan was made before witnesses whom the obligor designated to witness it. Otherwise, a borrower could hide many groups of witnesses behind a fence, none knowing of the others, and each group would prepare a separate document. Even if a person makes a kinyan before ten people, if he expressly designated all ten as witnesses, they may jointly write and sign one document, as discussed in Gittin. If he did not designate them as witnesses, however, they may not prepare and deliver a document without his consent. The rule that three who entered to visit a sick person may, if they wish, write and, if they wish, act as judges does not refer to writing an ordinary formal document. It means a memorandum of testimony, like the testamentary record of a shechiv mera that may be written after his death, as Ritva explains there.
But if he says, "I paid," he is believed. Although a kinyan ordinarily has the force of a document, this case is different because the witnesses were not designated to witness the kinyan.
