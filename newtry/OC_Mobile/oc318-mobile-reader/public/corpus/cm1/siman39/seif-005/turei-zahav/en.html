(There: they know both parties.) According to the Sema’s first explanation, the concern is that the lender owes money to another person and changes his name, so the witnesses must recognize the lender. But when the borrower comes alone, why do we not fear that the lender conspires with him? At first I thought the concern was that the borrower might bring a stranger and say, “This is Yosef ben Shimon, from whom I borrow,” and later deliver the document to his actual creditor, also named Yosef ben Shimon. The stranger could then return to the witnesses, claim payment and loss of the document, and obtain a receipt, because they think he is the lender. Yet when the borrower comes alone, no such concern exists, since they would not issue a receipt based on some later stranger. After considering siman 238, where recognition of both seller and purchaser is likewise required, the concern appears the same in both cases. The borrower may bring a stranger and identify him as Yosef ben Shimon, then acknowledge owing him money. He later takes the written admission and gives it to the actual Yosef ben Shimon, from whom he borrows against that document. When the true Yosef seeks collection, the borrower returns to the original court and proves that his admission concerned the stranger who had presented himself under that name. The true lender is then harmed, since no admission was ever made to him. This concern exists only when the borrower appears with an unidentified person. If he appears alone, there is no such concern, and therefore a document may be written for the borrower even without the lender present. The same fraud could occur in a sale and harm the true purchaser. Therefore, when the lender is present, the witnesses must know his identity. We do not fear that another person has the same name.
