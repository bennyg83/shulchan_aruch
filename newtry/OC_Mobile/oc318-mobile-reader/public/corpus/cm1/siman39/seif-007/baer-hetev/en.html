Perhaps. This applies specifically in such a case, where he would not imagine that the three judges wrote and signed his admission and delivered it to the person to whom he admitted liability. But where the claimant sued him and beit din summoned him, he knows that they will give the claimant a written and signed record. He therefore will not pay unless he first seeks the written admission and retrieves it. Consequently, he is not later believed to say that he paid. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach writes that according to most authorities, even where he was sued and summoned, and in every act of beit din, this applies specifically when the writing is requested in the borrower's presence. If he was not summoned, they cannot write unless the borrower instructs them to do so, since it is not an act of beit din. See siman 79. The Nimukei Yosef writes that where one admits liability in response to a claim before three and says, "Act as judges over me," they constitute a full beit din and may write a document for the claimant. They recognize. The Sema writes that even according to Tosafot and the Rosh, who hold that a document's witnesses confer rights through their signatures, and therefore there is no concern that the lender is absent and the document will reach him only later, the witnesses must nevertheless recognize the lender. Perhaps the lender owes money to another person and changes his name in order to remove his assets from that creditor. Moreover, a person does not want a public report to circulate that someone owes him money when it is untrue. This applies when both parties appear before beit din or the witnesses. If the borrower alone appears and says, "Write that I owe so and so," there is no concern that he will change the lender's name, because a person does not ordinarily sin for no personal benefit. Nor does such an admission create a reliable public report that the lender owns a given sum, since the admission was made outside his presence and people may assume it was made merely to avoid appearing wealthy. They therefore may write it even without recognizing the lender. In all events they must recognize the borrower, because of concern for collusion, namely that he may give himself another person's name in order to extract from that person the amount stated in the admission. The Shach writes that he finds the later authorities' discussion unpersuasive, and that the primary law is as the Mechaber rules in siman 49, seif 2. See the Taz there.
