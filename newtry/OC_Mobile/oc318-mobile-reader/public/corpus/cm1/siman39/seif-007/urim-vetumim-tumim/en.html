They were not sitting as an established court, etc. Nimukei Yosef writes in Perek Zeh Borer that this requirement applies where the person admits voluntarily, without a claim first being brought against him. In that setting the three do not yet have the status of judges. But where he admits in response to a claim before three judges, the three constitute a full beit din. They can obligate him, and any tribunal with power to obligate is a full beit din and may write the judicial record. Darkei Moshe and Bach ask that once the defendant was summoned, there is already a plaintiff's claim, yet the Gemara still requires the additional conditions that the judges have fixed a place and the like. Shach answers that Nimukei Yosef distinguishes two cases. If there was no real dispute or contradiction between the parties and the defendant merely came to beit din after a summons and admitted, the beit din did not adjudicate anything. Therefore the special conditions are still needed before the admission acquires full judicial-document status. But if there had been genuine argument and dispute, and only through the adjudication of beit din was the defendant held liable, then the beit din truly exercised judicial power. In that case no further condition of fixed sitting is needed. Shach notes that Nimukei Yosef infers this because the Gemara speaks specifically of an "admission" rather than of a judgment generally. Likewise, the rules concerning a written judgment do not mention a need for prior summons in the same formulation. This is a good explanation of Nimukei Yosef. Shach, however, goes further and says Rambam, Mechaber, and almost all authorities agree with Nimukei Yosef. In my view that is incorrect. Rif in Bava Metzia asks: the Gemara says that where beit din tells the defendant, "Go give him," or, "You are liable to give him," they do not write a document. Yet elsewhere we rule that testimony or admission before beit din is like a written loan. Rif answers that the distinction is whether the defendant accepted the judgment willingly. If the process was forced upon him and he had to be summoned and brought before beit din, the matter acquires stronger judicial status. But if he came, accepted the ruling, left beit din intending to obey, and later says, "I paid," he is believed. Rambam understands Rif this way, and Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 12, part 2, explains expressly: "where he did not obey the judgment" means that he did not submit until beit din summoned him and brought him by compulsion. According to this approach, the Gemara's case of "go give him" must concern a defendant who came without summons. If he had been summoned, he would not later be believed to say he paid, and the matter would carry written-loan force. This directly undermines Shach's proof from the absence of a distinction between summoned and unsummoned cases in the judgment passage. Rif makes precisely that distinction there. Moreover, the Bava Metzia case itself must involve an actual dispute and adjudication. Otherwise, why would the Gemara distinguish between "go give him" and "you are liable to give him" on the theory that the latter defendant may be delaying while beit din continues examining the law? If the defendant had simply admitted with no dispute, there would be nothing for beit din to investigate and no possibility that further deliberation might free him. Thus the case necessarily involves claims and defenses and a judicial determination. Yet Rif, Rambam, and Baal HaTerumot still distinguish whether he was summoned. That is plainly unlike Nimukei Yosef's rule that once there was a real dispute and the liability arose through adjudication, no special summons or establishment conditions are needed. Rambam's phrase that "the defendant said yes" does not mean he never disputed the claim. It means that through the give-and-take his liability became established and he accepted the resulting ruling. Nimukei Yosef evidently understands Rif differently. In his view, Rif's phrase about obeying the judgment means whether the defendant remained compliant or left beit din declaring that he would not obey. This is similar to Raavad's explanation cited by Baal HaTerumot. If he accepts the ruling, payment remains plausible. If he refuses to obey, there is a strong presumption he did not pay and the creditor can collect as from a public judicial obligation. Under this reading, Rif does not distinguish between summoned and unsummoned defendants at all. Then Nimukei Yosef can maintain that in a genuine adjudication, once beit din has resolved the parties' dispute, no further establishment conditions are required. This interpretation is coherent, but it is a dispute among the Rishonim. Lechem Mishneh also interprets Rif more like Nimukei Yosef and therefore wonders why Maggid Mishneh says Rambam is close to Rif. He evidently did not take account of Baal HaTerumot's explicit interpretation of Rif in the Rambam's direction. Accordingly, Nimukei Yosef's law cannot be presented as unanimous. Rambam and Baal HaTerumot do not follow it as Shach claims, and Mechaber generally follows Rambam here. The practical matter is therefore subject to a real dispute among the authorities.
So that the two of them should not scheme to impose liability upon another person. Sema explains that even according to the view that omission of warranty is a scribal error and there is no concern of an antedated document, we still fear manipulation. He suggests two possibilities. Perhaps the debtor changes his name in order to hide assets from his own creditor. Or perhaps the alleged creditor does not want beit din to write that this person owes him when in truth no debt exists, because he does not want a false public report circulated in his name. Both explanations are strained, especially the first. If the debtor's creditor already knows who his debtor is, changing the name in this document does not stop the creditor from suing him. If the creditor does not know about the debtor's assets or obligations, the debtor can conceal matters in many easier ways. There is no need for this elaborate maneuver. Maggid Mishneh, as discussed below in siman 49, explains why without a kinyan the court must know the identity of the creditor. Since the law is that a document may not be written for the debtor alone when the creditor is absent, perhaps another person will falsely present himself as the creditor, causing the document to be prepared now but delivered to the true creditor only later, thereby creating an antedated document. If one accepted that concern here, the matter could be explained differently even according to the view that omission of warranty is a scribal error. Here the power of beit din to create the written admission depends upon a proper summons, and the summons must come from the real plaintiff. If some other person appears under the plaintiff's name, then there was never a true summons by the creditor. The resulting judicial admission would nevertheless appear to authorize collection from encumbered property. That would be improper because the condition that gives beit din that power never existed. Still, I am uneasy with this whole type of concern. If the parties wish to fabricate a collectible obligation, why use such a complicated device? The debtor can simply make a kinyan before witnesses and tell them to write. Everyone agrees that produces a valid public lien without this difficulty. Likewise, they could make the kinyan before beit din itself. Why would they need a false identity scheme? It therefore appears that the concern is not merely creation of a fictitious debt, but manipulation that harms a third person in a way a simple kinyan would not accomplish. For example: Reuven deposits property with Shimon. Shimon wrongfully entrusts it to Levi, and it is lost. If Reuven does not ordinarily entrust property to Levi, Shimon may be liable under the rule of a custodian who transfers entrusted property to another custodian, because Reuven did not deem Levi trustworthy. Levi and Shimon can then go to beit din, with Shimon falsely using Reuven's name. Beit din records that "Reuven" summoned Levi regarding money or property held by him and Levi admitted the obligation. Shimon can now argue that Reuven in fact trusts Levi, since a judicial record apparently shows Reuven himself dealt with Levi as a person to whom he entrusts or lends property. This can release Shimon from his negligence and cause Reuven loss. Levi does not care about making the admission because he has no assets anyway, and Reuven will not sue him because Reuven knows no such transaction occurred. That is a genuine scheme to harm another person. Another example concerns hazamah. Suppose Reuven is designated as a witness on a document stating that a loan occurred in Lod on a certain day. Shimon knows Reuven is expected to testify concerning that document. On that very day Shimon goes with Levi to a beit din in Jerusalem and falsely uses Reuven's name. The court writes that on that day "Reuven" appeared in Jerusalem, summoned Levi, and Levi admitted liability. Later, when Reuven produces the original document or testifies that he was in Lod, the false judicial record can be used to disqualify him by proving that he was supposedly in Jerusalem that day. This is a real scheme that creates liability or disqualification for another person and cannot be duplicated merely by the two conspirators making a private kinyan between themselves. Accordingly, the requirement that beit din know the true identities of the parties is readily understood. The concern is not merely fictitious debt. It is the special evidentiary force of a judicial record being weaponized against a third party. See further below in siman 49, where I discuss this more fully.
