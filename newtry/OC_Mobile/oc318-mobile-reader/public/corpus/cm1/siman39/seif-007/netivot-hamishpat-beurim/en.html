Shach concludes that where claimant and defendant came before judges and the judges actually adjudicated their claims, the ruling can be written without the additional formality of having first summoned the defendant. But where people merely came before them to receive testimony and no judgment was rendered, they are not yet acting as a court that may create a judicial document for the creditor. If the defendant was summoned and refused, his conduct can elevate the proceeding so that a written ruling may be issued. Testimony received outside the borrower's presence for a permitted reason can be written with an explicit notation that it was received outside his presence; such a writing is not itself treated like an ordinary debt document against him.
Lest he give it to him. This seif and seif 9 address different concerns. Here the court is considering writing a ruling at a time when it is still known that payment has not yet occurred. If the debtor is not informed, they may not write because he may later pay without taking the document and the creditor could then collect again. In seif 9, the question is the status of a judicial admission even before any writing was made. Where the defendant was formally summoned and refused, the admission itself can have the force of a written loan. By contrast, where both sides came voluntarily and no such formal procedure occurred, the debtor may still claim payment. Even if the court now informs him that it intends to write, there can remain a concern that he already paid before the writing. Shach also disputes Sema regarding whether a ruling that still permits a plea of payment can reach purchasers. The comparison to an oral-loan guarantor is imperfect. A guarantor undertakes specifically on the understanding that the borrower may later become liable through beit din even though the original loan was oral; otherwise guaranty of an oral loan would never function. Purchasers, by contrast, acquired property and their liability depends on the actual lien and its publicity. I further conclude that a court judgment or instrument dated on a particular day does not generally collect from purchasers who bought earlier that same day, just as a ketubah or deed dated that day does not establish which transaction occurred first. But heirs who inherit after the judgment or document can be liable even on that same day, because inheritance occurs after the obligor's death and the judicial or documentary act necessarily preceded it. According to Sema, a written judicial admission can be stronger than a mere judgment document regarding a plea of payment. According to Shach, the primary practical law is that an actual judgment document bars the payment plea only once the debtor knows it has been issued; before that, even after formal summons, he can still say he paid.
