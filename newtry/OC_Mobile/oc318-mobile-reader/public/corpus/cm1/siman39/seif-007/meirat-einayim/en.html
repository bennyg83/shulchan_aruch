Provided that beit din recognizes both of them, etc. Even according to Tosafot and the Rosh, who hold that the witnesses' signatures acquire for the creditor and therefore there is no concern that the creditor himself is absent and the document may not reach him until later, the court must still identify the creditor. Perhaps this supposed creditor actually owes money to someone else and changes his name in order to divert the debt from the person to whom he owes money. In addition, a person does not want a rumor to circulate that so-and-so owes him money when that is false. This requirement applies where both parties come before the court or witnesses and one says, "I admit that I owe this person," so the admission is written in direct form as an admission to the person standing there. But if the borrower comes alone and says, "Write that I owe so-and-so," there is no concern that he will alter the creditor's name in order to benefit another person, because a person does not ordinarily sin for the benefit of someone else. Nor is the admission written in direct form; rather, it records that the borrower admitted owing so-and-so, and the writing is handed to the borrower. Such an admission does not create a public presumption that the named creditor actually owns the money, because perhaps the borrower admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. Therefore the creditor need not be identified in that case, as the Tur and Mechaber write below in seif 13. The borrower, however, must always be identified, lest there be collusion to impose an obligation upon another person by assuming his name. I wrote another strong reason below in siman 49. The Ir Shushan labored at length over these points, but the approach I have written resolves the matter.
