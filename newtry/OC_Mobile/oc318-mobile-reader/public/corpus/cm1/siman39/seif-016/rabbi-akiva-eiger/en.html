Note: Urim seeks to prove from Bava Metzia 77 that if one gives another a promissory note, the recipient does not thereby acquire land by the acquisition of money, and from this he concludes that the law follows the Rashba. For according to the Ramban, just as a promissory note upon which one has a lien is viewed as transferring land, so too it should effect an acquisition like an exchange of land for land. In my humble opinion, Urim's premise that according to the Ramban one can acquire land by giving a promissory note is contradicted by the Ramban in the first chapter of Kiddushin, in the sugya of "there is no maneh here." The Ramban explains that since the principal money remains with the giver and the other party acquires only the lien, this is not considered money. See there. Necessarily, when the Ramban holds that the borrower must make the loan, the reason is that once the borrower acquired the lien he became obligated to pay its value. Conversely, Urim's assumption that as a matter of law one cannot acquire land with a promissory note is also not correct according to the other approaches in the first chapter of Kiddushin. According to those authorities, a woman who receives a promissory note in kiddushin is betrothed by it; see Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer, siman 29. It is therefore clear that, according to them, land can likewise be acquired in this manner. In my view, this particular question depends upon that dispute among the authorities. The dispute here, however, whether the lender must actually lend, does not necessarily depend upon it. Even if a promissory note cannot acquire land because it is not considered acquisition-money, one can still say that the borrower became liable for its value and therefore must lend him. Likewise, even if land can be acquired by a promissory note, that may be because in a sale the land is given in exchange for the money. In a loan, by contrast, the borrower is not giving the money in exchange for the lien, for the lender wants his money returned and does not intend to purchase anything from the borrower with it. He wants only the lien on the borrower's assets as security for repayment. Thus the borrower does not transfer anything to him as an exchange for the money. Yet the Rashba's own words in Responsa, siman 1054, which are the source of this law, are difficult to me. He first proves that the lender need not lend from the law that one may write a deed for the borrower, because we do not fear that the borrower will then compel the lender to lend. This very proof would imply that even in a sale, if the seller writes a deed of sale with the buyer's consent, the buyer has not thereby become obligated to pay. Yet regarding a mortgage the Rashba writes there that if the lender took possession he may not retract, while if he did not take possession he may retract. He concludes that a kinyan does not solve the problem, because even a deed with a kinyan does not acquire without money. This implies that the matter was doubtful to him. If with a mortgage the lender cannot retract after possession, then certainly in a sale the buyer cannot retract. The Rashba should therefore have resolved the dispute in favor of the view that a kinyan without money does not acquire, based on the rule that a deed may be written for the seller, which necessarily speaks of a case with a kinyan, since otherwise we would fear that he wrote it in order to give it only later. See there. This requires study.
