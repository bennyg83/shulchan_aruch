[Shulchan Aruch] Even if the borrower died. Note: This requires analysis. Since the writing must be with the consent of the person being obligated, because the witnesses are considered agents of the litigant, once the borrower dies their agency terminates, and the testimony should then be regarded as testimony from their writing. Urim writes that this is effective by virtue of a rabbinic enactment, as Tosafot write regarding a protest and a declaration of coercion, but I do not understand this. That answer would be satisfactory if the rule were explicit somewhere in the Gemara, in which case we would be compelled to say that the Sages enacted it, as with a declaration of coercion. But from where were the Rishonim certain enough to establish this law? Can we, by reasoning alone, compare one enactment to the law of a declaration of coercion and a declaration of trustworthiness? This is especially difficult according to the Rashba, Responsa, siman 1211, who holds that a protest and declaration of trustworthiness operate by law, because where no additional obligation is created through the writing over and above the oral statement, and the person does not obligate anything of his own to another, consent of the obligor is unnecessary. See there. Even with respect to the proposed enactment, one can distinguish. Why would the Sages need to enact such a rule? If the borrower's heirs consent, they can certainly authorize the writing; if they do not consent, it is obvious that they may object just as their father could have objected. A reason is therefore certainly required to explain the source from which the Rishonim derived this law. This requires study.
