Seif 17. A borrower who comes to beit din with a promissory note in his possession. I was asked: this necessarily concerns a document containing a kinyan, for without a kinyan witnesses may not write the document in the lender's absence. Yet above the Shach concludes that the main explanation is that the lien takes effect whether or not the lender actually advances the money. If so, how can the borrower compel the lender to make the loan, when the kinyan is effective even without the loan? I answered that there is no difficulty, for two reasons. First, the Shach does not mean that the lender need never lend at all. He means that the lien takes effect even if he does not lend at the present moment. If the obligation arose only if the lender advanced the money immediately, then the borrower would not have undertaken a new independent obligation for something he did not yet owe. His lien would instead arise only through the loan and receipt of the money. Before receiving the money there would be no lien, and the document would be improperly predated. But where the borrower undertakes the lien now even though the money will be lent only later, his present undertaking is an independent obligation concerning something he does not yet owe. The stipulation that the lender eventually lend him money is merely an ordinary condition, comparable to an undertaking made on condition that the other party later travel to a certain place. Such an undertaking is not treated as predated merely because the condition will be fulfilled later. This is also clear from the Shach's use of the phrase “from now.” If he meant that there need never be a loan at all, there would be no condition, only an unconditional obligation, and the phrase would be unnecessary. Necessarily, the parties stipulated that the lender will lend him at some time, but that if the loan is made the borrower's obligation and lien relate to the present. The essential obligation therefore begins now as an independent undertaking. Nevertheless, because the borrower conditioned his obligation and lien on the lender's eventual loan, the lender is obligated to fulfill that condition. Having acquired a lien on the borrower's property, he must at some point make the loan. Second, even if the Shach is read literally to mean that the lien is effective even if no loan is ever made, the law is still understandable. It resembles a sale in which the buyer acquires an object and becomes obligated to pay its price. Even if the buyer later fails to pay, the sale is not automatically void and the object is not treated as stolen property, unless the seller expressly conditioned the sale on payment. Chelkat Mechokek, Even HaEzer 120, discusses this question, and the primary view is that the object is not treated as stolen property. So too here. If the lien were conditioned on the lender actually making the loan, and failure to lend would retroactively cancel it, there would indeed be a concern that the document is predated. But if the lien is acquired whether the lender later advances the money or not, the arrangement is analogous to an unconditional sale of property. The lender acquires the lien regardless of payment. Since he has acquired that property right, however, he owes its value to the borrower just as a purchaser owes the price of an object. The borrower can therefore compel him to pay that value by advancing the agreed loan. The lien itself remains acquired whether or not the lender actually advances the money, because it was not made conditional on the loan. Examine this carefully.
