And some disagree and say that if time passed, etc. Shach in seif katan 6 understands this view as follows. If a long time passed after the kinyan, we fear perhaps the debtor already paid before witnesses or obtained a receipt. But if he cannot produce such proof, Shach says he should not be believed merely to say "I paid." Otherwise, when the debtor dies, why would the creditor ever collect from heirs? We would plead repayment for them. This is incorrect. According to this view, if the debtor dies after a long delay, one indeed does not collect from his heirs merely on the kinyan testimony. We plead that their father may have paid. Ran's language is explicit: if much time passed and the debtor died, the witnesses should not write. Ramban proves this from the law of a found authenticated document that is not returned because we fear payment. There too the debtor need not possess a receipt or identifiable repayment witnesses. The mere possibility of payment is enough. Rashba likewise says that if the debtor paid without taking a receipt, he caused his own loss. According to Shach's reading, the opposite should be true. We would be assuming he did take a receipt or paid before witnesses and therefore withholding the document. The correct law is therefore that under this view, so long as no document was actually written, the debtor retains the ordinary oral-loan claim of repayment after sufficient time. If he dies, we plead repayment for the heirs.
More than thirty days, etc. Shach asks a strong question. Ran explains that the authorities who permit writing within thirty days hold that an ordinary loan is treated like a debt within its term during those first thirty days. The borrower is not believed to say he repaid, so the witnesses can still write. But according to the opposing authorities, whom Mechaber follows below in siman 78, where no explicit term was fixed the borrower can say "I repaid" even within the first thirty days. If so, Mechaber should not have written here that the concern begins only after thirty days. According to his own later ruling, the witnesses should refrain from writing even earlier unless a definite term was fixed and the debt remains within that term. The question is correct, and Ran's words support it. I believe Mechaber nevertheless holds that some minimum period must pass before failure to obtain the document itself becomes suspicious. The whole concern here is not merely abstract possibility of repayment. It is that the transaction has become impaired because the creditor did not promptly ask the kinyan witnesses to write. If the kinyan occurred today and the creditor comes tomorrow, why should we suddenly fear repayment? Such a rule would nearly destroy the principle that an ordinary kinyan stands for writing. The creditor would have only moments to obtain his document. The Geonim cited by Ran say similarly: if the matter dragged on and he did not demand the document, the witnesses should no longer write. Their language implies a meaningful delay, not an immediate possibility. What period counts as meaningful delay? Mechaber uses the thirty-day period fixed by Chazal for an ordinary loan. Even according to the view that a borrower can technically repay before thirty days, the commercial expectation remains that a standard loan may run for thirty days. A creditor therefore has no reason to rush immediately to the witnesses for a document. He can reasonably think: the borrower will pay within the ordinary period, so why trouble the witnesses and create a formal instrument? Once thirty days pass and the debt remains unpaid, continued failure to demand the document becomes suspicious. If the creditor truly remained unpaid, he should now secure his written proof. Thus the thirty-day measure can govern the evidentiary defect even though it does not create an absolute presumption against repayment within the period. This is one answer. A stronger explanation follows Sema's distinction between a fixed term and an ordinary thirty-day loan. Why do we say a person does not repay within an explicitly fixed term, yet in a standard loan he may repay before thirty days? Because when he expressly fixed a term, he revealed that he needed the money until that date. Early repayment is therefore unlikely. In an ordinary loan, the thirty-day rule is imposed by Chazal; the borrower never personally revealed a need to retain the money that long. If he has funds, he may repay earlier. Tosafot and Rosh ask at the beginning of Bava Batra why a widow must swear concerning her ketubah if a person ordinarily does not repay before the time of payment, and a ketubah is not collectible during the husband's life. One might ask: the noncollectibility of a ketubah during marriage is also a legal rule, not a term chosen by the husband. Under Sema's logic, there is no personal indication he wished to retain the obligation, so early satisfaction should remain plausible. The answer is that repayment of an obligation normally evidenced by a document without taking a receipt is itself unusual. Why would a debtor pay early, before he can be pressed, and leave the written claim outstanding? Where the creditor can presently demand payment, the debtor may hurry to pay even without perfect documentation, perhaps because he has cash now and fears future pressure or spending the money. But where payment is not yet demandable, there is no such urgency. Paying early without obtaining a receipt is far less plausible. Therefore Tosafot can ask that a ketubah should carry a strong presumption against early satisfaction even though its term comes from law rather than an express agreement. Apply this here. The kinyan witnesses themselves are a form of strong proof. Why should the borrower pay without a receipt, without notifying the kinyan witnesses, and without ensuring the creditor cannot later obtain a document? Before the ordinary thirty-day period ends, there is no urgency compelling such risky payment. Although he could legally repay, it is unlikely he would do so in this careless manner. After thirty days, the debt is fully mature and pressure for payment is ordinary. The possibility that he paid informally becomes much more realistic. Thus Mechaber can use thirty days as the dividing line even while ruling elsewhere that an ordinary borrower can technically claim early repayment. This is especially strong where the kinyan itself is treated as equivalent to a document for several purposes. Paying without a receipt while the transaction is still within its normal period is less plausible than repaying a simple oral debt. Accordingly, Shach's difficulty can be resolved. The thirty days here are not an absolute rule that no repayment can occur earlier. They mark the point at which failure to obtain a document becomes a meaningful defect and the repayment concern becomes substantial enough to prevent the witnesses from writing.
Some say that if it was authenticated, we judge on its basis. Shach in seif katan 9 argues at length against this ruling. He says it is not halachah and was not truly Maharam's position. Whatever Maharam intended, the ruling has strong support from Tosafot. Ketubot 20b says a person may testify from his prior written record only if he remembers the testimony. Tosafot ask: why not leave his handwriting with beit din and treat it as a document? They answer that writing made without the knowledge of both parties does not have the status of a document. Tosafot then ask from the law that a protest can be made before two and the protester need not tell them to write. Why is the resulting written protest not invalid as "from their writing and not from their mouths"? According to Rabbenu Tam, one can answer that so long as the witnesses still remember, written testimony is valid because they are capable of oral testimony. Ri gives other answers. One answer is that Chazal were lenient concerning protest. Another answer is more important here: Ri says we do not actually require the consent of both parties for every document. In the Ketubot case, the problem is that the witness wrote only a private memorandum, not a proper document. Had it been written in normal documentary form, he could have deposited it with beit din and the court could judge from it. Tosafot in Yevamot 31b say the same. The writing there is only a memorandum and therefore is not "testimony examined in beit din." Ri again cites Rabbenu Tam's practice of sending written testimony to beit din. This establishes the rule cited by Rama: where the writing is drafted in proper documentary form and is authenticated, beit din can rely upon it even though there was no explicit instruction "write." There is no need to search for Maharam. Tosafot state the principle. Shach follows Darkei Moshe in siman 28 and argues that Ri's answer works only according to Rabbenu Tam, who allows a witness capable of speaking to send written testimony. Since our halachah follows Rashi against Rabbenu Tam, Shach says this answer cannot be practical law. I do not understand this. The order and language of Tosafot do not support it. In Yevamot, Tosafot first distinguish between a properly drafted document and a mere memorandum. Only afterward they add, "Ri also said in the name of Rabbenu Tam that today they send testimony in writing." This strongly implies the proper-document answer is independent of Rabbenu Tam's separate doctrine. Had the first answer depended entirely upon Rabbenu Tam, Tosafot should have introduced Rabbenu Tam first and then stated its consequence. The same is even clearer in Ketubot, where the proper-document distinction appears without being framed as merely Rabbenu Tam's view. More fundamentally, the conceptual attempt to make this depend upon Rashi versus Rabbenu Tam does not work. If, at the time the writing is presented to beit din, the witnesses still remember the testimony, then according to Rabbenu Tam even a memorandum should be usable because they are capable of oral testimony. If they no longer remember, Rabbenu Tam's doctrine of "fit for oral expression" does not help. They are no longer capable of giving the testimony orally. What then makes a properly drafted document different? The answer can only be that a proper document acquired documentary status at the time it was written. Once it became the equivalent of testimony examined in beit din, later forgetting does not matter. But if we have reached that conclusion, the same document should be valid according to Rashi as well. Rashi certainly agrees that a valid document is not rejected as ordinary written testimony. Whether by Torah decree, rabbinic enactment, or another mechanism, legal documents are valid. Thus the issue is whether the writing became a legal document when created, not whether Rabbenu Tam allows ordinary written testimony. If it did become a document, both Rashi and Rabbenu Tam can accept it. If it did not, then even Rabbenu Tam cannot save it once the witnesses no longer remember. This makes Shach's distinction untenable. The same point appears in the phrase "as though their testimony had been examined in beit din." This does not mean merely as though they handed a paper to the judges. It means the document itself carries the legal finality of testimony already given. A witness cannot retract a signed valid document after signing, under the rule that once testimony is given it cannot be retracted. Likewise, where the law of hazamah applies to documents, the signatories can be subjected to consequences based upon what the document asserts. These rules make sense only if the document is treated as substantive testimony, not as a paper waiting to be converted into oral testimony. Therefore, once Ri says a properly drafted writing has document status, Rashi's ordinary objection of "from their writing" no longer applies. One may ask from Tosafot in Bava Batra 40a. There Tosafot ask how a protest can be written without an instruction to write, since it appears to be written testimony. They answer, according to Rashi, that Chazal specially allowed this for protest, and according to Rabbenu Tam that witnesses capable of oral testimony can send writing. This might suggest the proper-document answer is indeed unavailable under Rashi. But the passages are consistent. In Bava Batra, Tosafot do not distinguish between formal documentary language and an ordinary written report. Their question assumes the protest writing is not inherently a formal document in the special sense discussed by Ri elsewhere. If one treats every written report of witnesses as a document merely because it uses polished language, then there is no objective boundary. Who determines which formula magically creates document status? Tosafot there therefore deal with the general case of witnesses writing their testimony without authorization. According to Rashi, such ordinary writing needs a special rabbinic exception. According to Rabbenu Tam, the capacity for oral testimony can suffice. Ri's separate answer in Ketubot and Yevamot concerns a writing that is not merely testimony recorded in writing but has the recognized legal form and function of a document. That is a distinct category. The law therefore depends upon the nature of the writing, not merely its wording. This also explains why the private memorandum in Ketubot is invalid. A person may write accurate facts for his own memory in language that resembles a document, but if it was plainly created as a personal note, it never became a legal instrument. Conversely, a paper deliberately created as a formal evidentiary instrument and retained for use as such can acquire document status even without an express "write" instruction according to Ri. One should not reduce the distinction to grammar or formula alone. There is further support from Rosh. In Perek HaSefinah and in responsum klal 57, Rosh asks that if a seller can retract only from writing the sale document but not from the completed sale itself, why should the witnesses not simply write the document against his will? This question would be impossible if every writing without renewed authorization were automatically invalid as "from their writing." Once the seller protests, their original agency to write has ended. Yet Rosh still considers that the witnesses might be able to create an effective document because the underlying transaction is already complete. This shows that for documentary validity, express present agency of the obligor is not always indispensable. Rashba disagrees and therefore does not ask Rosh's question in the same way. But this itself confirms a genuine dispute among major authorities. Rabbenu Yerucham likewise writes that where witnesses were not told to write, the resulting writing does not have full document status for a repayment claim, but he implies it still functions as oral-level evidence. The debtor may be able to say "I paid," yet cannot necessarily say "the transaction never occurred." This middle status also supports Rama's cited view. There is an apparent contradiction within Rashba's own responsa. In responsum 1209, concerning witnesses who wrote that a woman was betrothed without being instructed to write, Rashba says the paper is only a memorandum and ineffective. But in responsum 1149, concerning an acknowledgment document written without the debtor's consent, Rashba says it lacks the full status of a document so the debtor can claim repayment, implying he cannot deny the underlying acknowledgment entirely. Mahari Avuhav in Even HaEzer siman 42 notes a similar tension with another responsum in which Rashba says beit din should investigate whether the handwriting is authentic, implying that if authenticated the writing has some effect. Beit Yosef distinguishes monetary law from kiddushin. Many challenged him, asking why "from their writing" should differ between money and personal status. The distinction can be defended. According to Rabbenu Tam, monetary testimony has broader room for written evidence because Chazal relaxed formalities in civil cases and because a person capable of oral testimony can be treated as fit for written delivery. Kiddushin and other personal-status matters can remain stricter. Yevamot itself treats such testimony differently in several procedural respects. Mordechai also implies that Rabbenu Tam's written-testimony leniency does not automatically extend to kiddushin. Thus Beit Yosef's distinction is not baseless. Even more simply, in monetary law a signed paper may function as admission, proof, or quasi-document even if it lacks the full formal status needed to establish a new personal-status bond. A debtor's own acknowledgment and commercial reliance create additional legal mechanisms not available in kiddushin. Accordingly, Rashba can say: In money, the paper can at least prevent total denial, though without authorization it may not defeat a repayment claim or collect from encumbered property. In kiddushin, where the document must itself establish a severe status, unauthorized written testimony is insufficient. This resolves much of the apparent contradiction. It also shows that even Rashba does not necessarily support Shach's absolute rejection. Shach's argument rests heavily on reading Ri's formal-document answer as merely Rabbenu Tam's personal view and then ruling against Rabbenu Tam. But the sources do not bear that reading. The more natural conclusion is that a properly constituted document can have evidentiary force even without an express instruction to write, though its exact strength depends upon the category of law and whether it creates an encumbered-property lien. Rama's phrase "if it was authenticated, we judge on its basis" should therefore be understood with proper limits. It does not mean every private note written by witnesses becomes a document once the handwriting is verified. The writing must have been created as a legal document, in normal documentary form and with the intention to serve as evidence. A mere memorandum remains a memorandum. Where those conditions are met, authentication allows beit din to treat it as documentary evidence. In monetary law, at minimum, it can prevent the debtor from denying the underlying transaction. Whether it also removes a repayment claim or allows collection from encumbered property depends on the further rules of authorization, kinyan, and warranty discussed in this siman. This nuanced reading harmonizes Tosafot, Rosh, Rabbenu Yerucham, and much of Rashba. Accordingly, Shach was too forceful in rejecting Rama's ruling entirely. The law has substantial Rishon support and should not be dismissed. There is another useful distinction between two questions that Shach tends to merge. Question one is whether the writing itself is valid evidence that the transaction occurred. Question two is whether the writing has the full legal strength of a creditor's document, including defeating a repayment claim and collecting from encumbered property. A writing can answer the first question affirmatively while failing the second. This is exactly what Rabbenu Yerucham and several Rashba responsa imply. If witnesses write an acknowledgment without formal authorization, the debtor may still be unable to say, "This never happened." Their authenticated signatures prove that they recorded an actual event. Yet because the document was not authorized in the manner required for a full shtar, he may still say, "I later paid," and the creditor may be unable to collect from purchasers. This intermediate status makes sense. Ordinary oral witnesses could testify that the transaction occurred but would not thereby create a written lien. An authenticated writing can preserve their evidentiary content at least to the same oral level without necessarily becoming a full written loan. This is an important reading of Rama's words. When Rama says "if it was authenticated, we judge on its basis," he need not mean that every legal consequence of a formal document follows. He can mean beit din may use the writing as evidence of the underlying transaction. The exact consequences then depend upon the rules of repayment, property lien, and authorization. This is why the first opinion, attributed to Rashba, can call such a writing a mere memorandum in one context yet still allow it evidentiary weight in another. "Memorandum" can mean it is not a formal shtar, not that it is worthless paper. In monetary law, authenticated signatures of competent witnesses remain significant. The distinction with kiddushin becomes even sharper from this perspective. A monetary writing that does not qualify as a full document can still function like preserved testimony that a debt or acknowledgment occurred. Kiddushin, however, requires testimony that establishes a personal status. If the witnesses are not presently giving oral testimony and the writing lacks formal document status, there may be no intermediate mechanism by which the paper alone creates the marriage bond. Thus Rashba can be lenient in money and strict in kiddushin without inconsistency. The same distinction appears in the laws of protest. A protest is not a debt instrument and does not itself transfer money or property. Its function is evidentiary: to preserve notice that the possessor's holding is disputed. Chazal therefore had particular reason to allow writing without the protester's express instruction, because otherwise the very purpose of protest could fail when the witnesses forget or leave. This does not mean every unauthorized writing becomes a full shtar. It means the law recognizes several categories of documentary evidence, each with its own function. Once this is appreciated, the Tosafot passages fit together. In Bava Batra, Tosafot discuss a protest and answer according to Rashi that Chazal specially allowed the writing. In Ketubot and Yevamot, Ri distinguishes a formal document from a private memorandum. These are not mutually exclusive answers. Some writings are valid because Chazal specially recognized their evidentiary function. Some are valid because they were created as actual legal instruments. Others remain mere notes. Rabbenu Tam adds a third route: where the witness remains capable of oral testimony, sending written testimony can itself be accepted. Different mechanisms can coexist. Shach's method effectively assumes there is only one route, Rabbenu Tam's "fit for oral expression" theory, and therefore once we reject Rabbenu Tam every other writing falls. The Rishonim do not structure the law so narrowly. There is also a practical indication from commercial custom. Courts routinely distinguish between a signed formal acknowledgment, a private diary entry, an unsigned draft, and a letter intended merely as correspondence. The fact that all are "writing" does not make them equal. The intention and legal form with which the paper was created determine whether it functions as a shtar, preserved testimony, admission, or mere memorandum. Ri's distinction reflects this obvious legal reality. That is why he says the private note in Ketubot cannot simply be deposited with beit din. It was never created as a legal instrument. Had it been drawn in proper documentary form for the purpose of evidentiary use, it could have entered the court as such. Another proof comes from the rule that a valid document is treated as though its testimony has already been examined in beit din. This legal fiction has powerful consequences. The witnesses cannot retract. The document can survive their later death. The court can act upon it without re-hearing the original event. These consequences show that document status is created at writing, not at later oral recollection. If the witness later forgets, the document does not become invalid. That fact alone refutes the idea that the document's validity under Ri depends upon Rabbenu Tam's present "fit for oral expression" test. At most, present capacity matters for ordinary written testimony that never became a shtar. Once the paper became a shtar, a different legal mechanism applies. This is precisely why the form and purpose of writing matter. There is also support from the law of protest itself. If a protest were valid only while the witnesses remembered, its purpose would be badly undermined. A protest may be needed years later, after the witnesses forgot details. The whole point of allowing it to be written is to preserve evidence over time. Chazal's recognition necessarily gives the writing durability beyond present recollection. Again, documentary categories cannot all be reduced to Rabbenu Tam's rule. Rosh's question about a sale document is particularly telling. He asks why witnesses cannot write even after the seller protests, if the sale itself has already become binding. If unauthorized writing were inherently worthless under Rashi, the question would never begin. Rosh must hold that once the underlying transaction is complete, the witness writing can still have legal significance independently of a continuing agency from the seller. That does not mean every document can be written against a party's will. The writing may add new liabilities, warranty, or encumbered-property consequences that the party never accepted. But where the document merely records an already completed legal state, the absence of renewed authorization is not automatically fatal. This concept is exactly what supports Rama's cited opinion. The document must be examined to determine what it does. If it merely preserves an existing fact, authentication may suffice. If it creates a stronger lien or new obligation, further authorization can be required. Thus the law is not binary. Shach's concern remains valuable as a warning against treating every authenticated writing as a full creditor instrument. But it does not justify rejecting the evidentiary validity of a proper document altogether. Accordingly, the practical conclusion should be nuanced. An unauthorized memorandum written by witnesses has no automatic status as a shtar. A writing in proper legal form, intended at the time of creation to function as documentary evidence, can be judged upon once authenticated according to the authorities cited by Rama and the first answer of Ri. In monetary cases, such a writing can at minimum establish the underlying transaction. Whether it additionally defeats a repayment claim or permits collection from encumbered property depends upon whether the debtor authorized those stronger consequences through kinyan, express instruction, or another recognized mechanism. In kiddushin and other severe personal-status matters, one should not automatically transfer the monetary leniency. And where the witnesses merely write a private reminder for themselves, even authentication does not transform it into a legal instrument. With these distinctions, the authorities can be harmonized without forcing Ri into Rabbenu Tam's framework and without discarding Rama's ruling.
