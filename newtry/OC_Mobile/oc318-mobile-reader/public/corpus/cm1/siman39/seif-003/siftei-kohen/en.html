And they need not be concerned, etc. This passage implies that a borrower cannot plead "I paid" against witnesses to a kinyan. Even according to the latter opinion, which holds that the witnesses should not write a document, the reason is concern that perhaps the borrower paid, meaning that if he were present he might establish payment with witnesses or the lender might admit it. It is strained to reverse the rule and say that ordinarily we do not suspect payment, but if the borrower explicitly says "I paid" he is believed. Moreover, if that were true, why may the creditor collect from the heirs after the borrower dies? We hold that whenever the father could have pleaded payment, beit din advances that plea for minor heirs. It is strained to say that because a kinyan ordinarily stands to be written, repayment is uncommon and therefore we do not advance such an unusual plea for heirs, as discussed below at the end of siman 361. Rather, the primary explanation is that a kinyan has the status of a written document in every respect, both for collecting from encumbered property and for preventing the borrower from pleading payment. There is another proof from the Mechaber's ruling above, seif 1, that if there are witnesses who saw the kinyan, even though no document was written the creditor collects from encumbered property. This necessarily means that the borrower cannot plead payment, because wherever he can plead payment the creditor certainly cannot collect from encumbered property, as is clear in many places in the Gemara and poskim. See what I write below, seif katan 61, and at the beginning of siman 70, seif katan 4, where I bring additional proofs that this is the Mechaber's position. See Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 251.
There are those who disagree, etc. The primary ruling follows the first opinion, which is the view of most authorities. I likewise conclude this below at the beginning of siman 70, seif katan 4, and this is the accepted practice. See Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, part 2, siman 64.
For if the matter extended beyond thirty days, etc. This is difficult. These dissenting authorities hold that with an ordinary loan the borrower is not believed within thirty days to say he repaid. Ran in chapter 2 of Ketubot explicitly writes that according to their view an ordinary loan resembles a loan with a fixed due date. But according to our ruling, only a loan with an expressly fixed due date has that rule. With an ordinary loan the borrower may say "I paid" even within thirty days, as the Tur and Mechaber themselves rule at the end of siman 78. The Mechaber should therefore have written here that according to the dissenting opinion, with an ordinary loan the witnesses may not write even within thirty days. The passage can be reconciled with difficulty and requires study.
Nevertheless, some say that if it was authenticated, etc. The Rama's words here, in siman 46, seif 10, at the beginning of siman 61, and in Darkei Moshe do not appear correct to me. As practical law, whenever the debtor did not tell the witnesses, "Write and sign," their writing is treated as though they had not written testimony at all. They must testify orally before beit din, as I will explain. I must therefore discuss the matter at some length. Darkei Moshe cites Hagahot Mordechai at the beginning of Ketubot, in a responsum of Maharam, also printed in Hagahot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 39, concerning conditions that had been written but were challenged as not constituting a valid document because no kinyan appeared in them. Maharam gives several answers, including that a person may write his testimony in a document and later testify from it. Although the Gemara concludes that this is so only when the witness remembers the testimony independently, Maharam says that this limitation applies to one witness. When two witnesses are signed on a document and the document was written with the knowledge of both, it is a complete document for all purposes even if they no longer remember the events. Mahari Weil, siman 24, writes that from this we learn that a document not prepared according to every rabbinic formality may fail as a formal document, but still contains witness testimony. Darkei Moshe questions Maharam because the Yerushalmi cited by Rif, the Rosh, Tosafot, and Mordechai in chapter 2 of Ketubot does not appear to distinguish between one witness and two. Darkei Moshe suggests that Maharam speaks only of a document already authenticated, so that the witnesses need not testify orally. Their signed testimony therefore has effect even though they no longer remember, and even if the document lacks some formal feature, it still constitutes evidence. He then says that Rashba appears to disagree, because Rashba writes that whenever the debtor did not say, "Write and sign," the writing is merely like a private memorandum and is invalid as testimony from writing, as explained in his responsum. In my humble opinion, no authority disagrees with Rashba. His position is clear and correct in principle. Whenever testimony is deemed testimony "from writing," it is no testimony at all, because the Torah requires testimony "from their mouths and not from their writing." Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer, siman 42, writes that Rashba's responsum applies only to kiddushin, but that is entirely inaccurate, as Mahari Sasson, Responsa siman 229, already objected. See also Responsa Menachem Azariah, beginning of siman 121, and Responsa Mahari ben Lev, volume 4, page 3a. Tosafot and many authorities repeatedly prove that written testimony of this sort is invalid. Mahari Weil there discusses a different issue, as I will explain. Nor is Maharam any proof against Rashba. On the contrary, Maharam's own words support Rashba, for he says that when the document was written with the knowledge of both parties it is a complete document for every purpose, even if the witnesses no longer remember, and that Rabbi Yitzchak explains similarly in chapter 2 of Ketubot. This explicitly implies that if it was not written with the knowledge of both sides, it is invalid as testimony from writing. Tosafot, Ketubot 20a, likewise cites Rabbi Yitzchak that a writing counts as a formal document only when made with the knowledge of both parties, meaning with the debtor's knowledge and authorization that he is obligated. A witness who merely writes his recollection without the debtor's knowledge has not created a document, and while he no longer independently remembers the event, the writing is not valid testimony because it is testimony from writing. Although Tosafot later cites another explanation of Rabbi Yitzchak that does not require both parties' knowledge, Maharam plainly follows Rabbi Yitzchak's first explanation. As practical law, that first explanation is primary and accords with Rashba: whenever the writing was not prepared with both parties' authorization as a formal document, it is written testimony and is entirely invalid. This is also the view of Razah in HaMaor, chapter Arba'ah Achin, and Ramban in Milchamot there appears to agree; his words indicate that Rif also holds so. Tosafot in chapter Chezkas HaBatim, Bava Batra 40a, states explicitly that according to Rashi's interpretation of "from their mouths and not from their writing," meaning that witnesses may not send written testimony to beit din, a deed of sale written without the seller's authorization or a loan document written without the borrower's authorization is not a document at all and does not even count as testimony. Tosafot then cites another teaching from Rabbi Yitzchak in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that the practice is to send written testimony to beit din and that "from their mouths and not from their writing" excludes only a mute person who is inherently incapable of oral testimony. According to our ruling, however, we follow Rashi and not Rabbeinu Tam on this issue, as explained above in siman 28, seifim 11 and 12. It therefore follows plainly that a writing made without the borrower's authorization is neither a document nor testimony. It appears that the latter explanation of Rabbi Yitzchak in Tosafot, chapter 2 of Ketubot, follows Rabbeinu Tam's approach, just as Tosafot in Chezkas HaBatim says, "Rabbi Yitzchak further said that he heard from Rabbeinu Tam, etc." Darkei Moshe in siman 28 explicitly connects Rabbi Yitzchak's latter explanation with Rabbeinu Tam and writes that Rivash, siman 210, follows Rambam and Rashi, who disqualify written testimony because testimony must be oral, and therefore even if the text has the form of a document it is invalid unless it was actually written as a formal document. Although Darkei Moshe may have written this only to show that Rivash is not necessarily rejected by Rabbi Yitzchak, in my humble opinion Rabbi Yitzchak does not truly disagree with Rivash in any event, because Rabbi Yitzchak speaks of a text written in complete document form for delivery as such, whereas Rivash explicitly discusses a writing not prepared for delivery but merely as a model for the scribe. Still, it may be correct that Rabbi Yitzchak's latter explanation depends upon Rabbeinu Tam's general position. If so, the Rama's gloss here is astonishing. Having ruled above in siman 28, seif 11, like Rashi, how can he write here, and again in siman 46, seif 10, and at the beginning of siman 61, that some say an authenticated writing may be adjudicated upon? The same difficulty applies there. See what I write below, seif katan 31. As for Darkei Moshe's citation of Mahari Weil, I believe he misunderstood it. Mahari Weil writes that Amram's argument that the document was not written according to rabbinic enactment does not strengthen his position. Even if it is not a formal document, it still contains testimony. Mahari Weil then cites Maharam that the absence of a kinyan does not destroy the document, and that a witness may preserve his testimony in writing, with the limitation that one witness must independently remember, while two witnesses signed on a document written with the knowledge of both sides create a complete document even if they no longer remember. Mahari Weil then adds that in his own case Amram himself admits all the substantive contents of the document. Plainly, Mahari Weil's case was not a writing made without the debtor's knowledge. He says only that the document was not written in the complete rabbinic form. If it had been written without the debtor's authorization, it would be biblically invalid as testimony from writing, not merely defective under rabbinic document form. Moreover, Maharam's very words cited by Mahari Weil say that it was written with the knowledge of both parties. Rather, Mahari Weil discusses defects such as the absence of a kinyan, or perhaps the absence of a date, as below at the beginning of siman 43, or failure to repeat the substance of the document in its final line, as at the end of siman 44. In such cases Rashba also agrees that the document can be valid, as Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe write below at the beginning of siman 44 in Rashba's name, that a document not made according to every rabbinic formality can nevertheless be valid. Thus the Rama's statement below, siman 46, seif 10, that Mahari Weil supports one opinion while "others disagree, as explained above at the beginning of siman 39, seif 3," meaning Rashba, is incorrect. In the cases of siman 46, where the document is merely defective in formal rabbinic drafting, Rashba agrees, as explained. Mahari Weil's reasoning is that even if one granted Amram's mistaken premise that the writing was not a formal document, it would at least evidence what the witnesses had testified, while Maharam goes further and shows that the writing is actually a complete document. It is therefore obvious that Mahari Weil did not cite Maharam merely to prove the lesser proposition that some evidentiary value remains, when Maharam explicitly says it is a complete document. All this is clear. Ir Shushan, seif 6, writes that some say if the document was authenticated in beit din, we adjudicate on its basis because the signer certainly knew of the authentication, which is publicly known. Those words are baseless. See below, siman 69, seif katan 7. He was led to this because he did not know the source of the rule.
