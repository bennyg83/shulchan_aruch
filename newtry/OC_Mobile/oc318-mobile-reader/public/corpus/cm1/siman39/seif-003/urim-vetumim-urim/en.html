Some disagree and say, etc. According to this view, the borrower is believed to say that he repaid even if he has no witnesses or receipt at all. If the borrower died, we do not collect from his heirs because we plead on their behalf that their father may have paid. This is what I concluded in Tumim, unlike the Shach, who holds that according to all opinions the borrower is never believed merely to say he paid, and that the only reason the witnesses may not write is because he might later prove payment through witnesses or a receipt. See there.
If he fixed a term, etc. Even if the term is two years, we do not suspect payment before the due date. See Tumim regarding the Shach's difficulty from an ordinary loan, whose term is thirty days, where one is nevertheless believed to say that he paid within that period. According to this view, the statement at the end of seif 1 that where there was a kinyan one collects everything refers to collection within the fixed term. So too writes the Taz.
Merely like a notebook. It is not testimony at all, and the borrower may plead, "It never happened."
If it was authenticated, etc. Certainly authentication is required, for without authentication even an ordinary document is invalid. The distinction is that an ordinary document may be authenticated by its own witnesses testifying to their signatures. Here, however, the document serves only to collect from unencumbered property and to prevent the borrower from pleading that the event never occurred. If the document's own witnesses authenticate it, they must in any event remember the underlying testimony, as explained below in siman 46. Once they remember it, the document itself adds nothing, since their testimony can be given orally. The practical difference therefore arises only when the document is authenticated through other witnesses and its original witnesses are absent. According to the first view, such a document has no evidentiary force and the borrower may say, "It never happened." According to the second view, we adjudicate on its basis because it constitutes testimony and prevents that plea. See Tumim, where I defended this view at length, unlike the Shach who rejected it entirely.
