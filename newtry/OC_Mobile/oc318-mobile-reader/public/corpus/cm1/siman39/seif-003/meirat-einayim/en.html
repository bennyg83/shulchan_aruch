They write the date of the kinyan. The Ir Shushan says the reason is that if they write the date of writing instead, they may cause loss to the borrower, for perhaps he already paid the creditor and received a receipt, and the creditor may later use the newly dated document and claim it concerns another debt because its date is later than the receipt. This is not precise. Tosafot in Bava Batra, cited by the Tur below in siman 54, explain that for this very reason one ordinarily does not write a date in a receipt, lest the debt document have been postdated. If a date was in fact written in the receipt, the debtor caused his own loss. Rather, the reason is as the Tur writes in siman 43, which I cite nearby.
It is written that Hashem has not yet been named. The column in the afternoon and the time of the Leviathan was written and the time of the Levites was given to him, and then the land sold in the air, and then the melody was removed from the fire, and he was not allowed to take away from his time A friend of Dhamma and his wife, and even David Dasney, wrote in his book, and even to Dr. Dawkni, he was called "Amen" to write at the time that he had no responsibility, and that he had not said to him
Even if the borrower died, etc. The same applies if the lender died. In Perishah I wrote that the same rule applies if both lender and borrower died, and indeed that is an even stronger case.
If the matter continued more than thirty days, etc. The reason is that an ordinary loan is presumed to be for only thirty days, as the Tur and Mechaber rule below at the beginning of siman 73. Therefore, if they fixed a term that the loan may not be demanded for a year, the witnesses may write during the entire year because we do not suspect payment before the due date. This is why the Mechaber concludes that if a repayment date was fixed, they do not write after that date, but before it they may write even though much time has passed. According to the first opinion, however, they may write even after the due date.
