When is this said, etc. The preceding rule applies to a voluntary admission in court. Where liability was imposed after adjudication, after testimony, or after the defendant refused to comply with a summons, the ruling has stronger status. This distinction explains when the creditor may receive a written judgment and when the debtor may later plead payment. 7080
But two who came for judgment, etc. When both parties voluntarily appeared before the court and judgment was rendered, the court may record the ruling according to the governing rules. If the defendant left before a document was written, the court must consider whether payment may already have occurred. The writing should therefore be made while nonpayment is known, or with appropriate notice to the debtor, so that the document does not create an unjust presumption. 5070
If he says, 'I paid,' he is believed. This is true where the obligation remained only an oral admission or an unwritten ruling, for there is no document in the creditor's possession against which the debtor must explain why it remains outstanding. The Rif distinguishes a defendant who obeyed the court from one who refused its authority and had to be compelled. Where he refused and judgment was entered against him, the judicial act is like a written loan and he is not believed to plead payment; where he voluntarily complied, he may be believed. The Raavad similarly limits the rule that standing in judgment is like a written loan to one who left the court in defiance. This resolves the difficulty raised against Baal HaTerumot. The Rambam, however, speaks expressly of a defendant who immediately admitted, 'Yes, I owe you,' without any adjudication. Such a statement is an admission rather than a litigated judgment. Thus the Rif and Rambam need not disagree: the Rif discusses a defendant who initially resisted and was obligated through the court's ruling, while the Rambam discusses a voluntary admission. Where liability arose from the defendant's own legal argument or from evidence, it is likewise a true judgment rather than a bare admission. 70100120202
Even according to the one who says, etc. Darkei Moshe explains that the reason a court does not write and deliver the ruling is not necessarily that the debtor would thereby lose the plea of payment. Since he is presently believed to say that he paid, there is no need for the court to issue a document; writing an unnecessary ruling would demean the court. Ir Shushan gives the same reason, not that the writing appears false, as the Sema attributes to Darkei Moshe. Maharl ibn Chaviv rejects the latter explanation at length. Nevertheless, the Rama's answer does not fully resolve the Beit Yosef's difficulty, nor does it explain how the Tur and Mordechai infer their view from the Rambam and Semag. Maharl ibn Chaviv explains that their inference comes from the Rambam's precise phrase, 'Write my admission,' implying that only an admission may not be written, while an actual judgment may be written because even after writing the debtor may still plead payment according to their view. Maharl ibn Chaviv ultimately rejects that conclusion and holds that the Rambam and Semag agree that one cannot plead payment against a judgment in the creditor's possession. Their phrase concerning an admission teaches only that a true judgment, such as liability established after summons, testimony, or adjudication, may be written, because even without writing it already has the status of a written loan. I add that the same applies where the defendant refused to pay because he believed himself exempt and the court obligated him from his own arguments; liability emerging from his claim is like liability established by witnesses. 307080100
Lest he already paid him. The Sema infers that if the creditor comes while it is certainly known that payment has not yet occurred, the court may write the judgment. I agree with that conclusion, but not with his additional concern that the debtor may later pay, deny ever owing, and then be branded a liar when the judgment is produced. If that concern controlled, a court could never write a judgment. The Gemara in Bava Metzia 17a and the poskim state explicitly that when nonpayment is known the court may write, and the Tur says so expressly. Raavyah does not disagree on this point. The Sema's concern is therefore not persuasive. At most, one could distinguish a case where the debtor is not told that a judgment is being written, since notice enables him to protect himself by taking back or cancelling the document upon payment. Even so, the Sema's formulation remains difficult.
