But two people who came to litigation, etc. Sema writes that although the defendant may be believed to say, "I paid," if he admits that he did not pay, the creditor may nevertheless collect from encumbered property because a judicial proceeding has publicity. He understands Tur, Rambam, and Mechaber this way. I do not think Rambam and Mechaber agree. The entire distinction is difficult, as Shach notes. If the debtor himself is believed to say he paid, why should we not plead payment on behalf of purchasers? How can the debtor's present admission that he has not paid prejudice third-party purchasers who bought property in reliance upon the ordinary rules? Tosafot and Rosh were forced into such a distinction because of a specific contradiction in the sugyot. Bava Metzia says that after beit din tells the defendant, "Go pay him," the defendant can later say he paid. Yet elsewhere, once a person stood in judgment, the obligation is treated like a written loan for purposes of collection from encumbered property. Tosafot and Rosh reconcile the passages by saying: Against the debtor himself, repayment can be claimed. Against purchasers, however, where the judicial proceeding generated publicity, we do not suspect collusion merely because the debtor now says he paid. This is admittedly strained, but necessary within their approach. Rif and Rambam solve the contradiction differently. They distinguish between a defendant who came or complied voluntarily and one who had to be summoned and compelled. Where there was a summons, the judicial proceeding has stronger force. Where there was no such summons, the defendant can say he paid and the matter does not become the equivalent of a written loan. Once Rambam adopts that distinction, there is no need to invent the separate Tosafot rule that the debtor may claim payment while purchasers cannot. Indeed, if Rambam accepted Tosafot's distinction, Rif would not need the special and otherwise unmentioned division between summoned and unsummoned judicial proceedings. This is especially clear from Rambam's language. He writes: "When is an admission in beit din like a written loan? When..." and then gives the condition involving the defendant's failure to accept the proceeding voluntarily. If the obligation were always a written loan for purchaser collection and the distinction affected only whether the debtor could say he paid, Rambam should have written: "When is he unable to say he paid?" His formulation shows that in the weaker case it is not treated as a written loan even for encumbered-property collection. Mechaber follows Rambam and likewise does not record Sema's distinction. Rosh and Tur take the other path. Rosh normally copies Rif, yet here he omits Rif's distinction between a compliant defendant and one compelled to appear, and records only Tosafot's answer. That omission is meaningful. Tur, when citing Rambam, begins in the middle of Rambam's wording with "two who came to litigation" and omits the later qualification by which Rambam distinguishes whether the defendant was summoned. This shows Tur did not accept Rambam's procedural distinction for an ordinary judgment. Thus: Rambam and Rif distinguish summoned from unsummoned proceedings. Rosh and Tur instead distinguish the debtor's repayment claim from purchaser collection. Sema improperly combines both systems as though there were no disagreement. Tosafot in Gittin 37a further support Rosh's model. They ask concerning the view that a debt secured by property is not cancelled by shemittah: how can a fine or compensation obligation that has reached judgment be cancelled, when a judgment collects from encumbered property? The case must involve no written judgment, because a written judgment certainly has stronger collection status. Yet even without a written judgment, the defendant can ordinarily say he paid. Tosafot nevertheless assume the judgment can collect from encumbered property. This is exactly the Rosh-Tosafot distinction. Rambam need not accept it because his own framework resolves the source differently. Shach writes here and again in siman 79 that Sema misunderstood Tur and Rosh. Shach asks: if the debtor admits nonpayment, why should we accept his statement against purchasers? He cites many authorities rejecting that reasoning and therefore explains Tur as referring only to purchasers who acquired land after the debtor had already admitted in beit din that he had not paid. This does not solve the problem. Suppose the debtor admitted today that he had not paid and tomorrow sold a field. Why can the purchaser not say that perhaps the debt was paid between today's admission and tomorrow's sale? The later purchaser is still being harmed through the debtor's own statement. Unless we say, as Tosafot and Rosh do, that in this context we do not fear collusion where there is no defect in the judicial obligation, Shach's answer gains nothing. Shach says Bach gave the same explanation, but Bach in fact writes expressly that if we truly fear collusion, even purchasers who bought after the debtor's admission could claim perhaps payment occurred afterward. Thus Bach itself recognizes the weakness. One could establish Shach's case like Taz: the debtor admitted nonpayment and the creditor then said, "Do not pay me except before witnesses." Purchasers bought only afterward. Then there is objective proof that payment did not occur privately. But that is no longer a case of mere debtor admission. It is the separate case Tur lists as "it is known that he did not pay." Tur and Rosh distinguish two situations: it is independently known he did not pay, or the debtor admits he did not pay. Shach's reading collapses the two. Mahari T also struggles with the problem. He explains that the judicial admission is like a written loan only for collecting from encumbered property when either nonpayment is independently proven or the debtor admits nonpayment, because beit din can then write a judicial document. Once a document exists, the creditor collects from purchasers. I do not understand this either. If the written judgment is what creates the collection right, why call the earlier judicial admission itself a written loan? And if he follows Raavyah, cited by Tur, who says even a written judgment does not necessarily prevent a repayment claim, the original problem returns: why should purchasers not be protected by the possibility of payment? Some authorities cited by Ritva and Shitah Mekubetzet indeed establish the Bava Metzia rule as a case where an actual written judgment exists. But that is not Rosh's answer. Accordingly, we should not force Tur and Rosh into Mahari T's explanation. The better conclusion is to preserve Sema's core reading of Rosh and Tur: Where a judicial proceeding itself generated public notice, and there is no specific defect suggesting repayment, we do not suspect collusion merely because the debtor says the debt remains unpaid. Purchasers who chose to buy despite the known judicial obligation entered that risk knowingly. The debtor himself may still say he paid, because that is his personal factual defense. The distinction is unusual, but it is the only reading that fits Rosh and Tur. This also answers the difficulty from Tur in siman 79. There Tur says that if the defendant resisted the judgment, he is not believed to say he paid and the creditor collects from encumbered property. This does not imply that without resistance there is never encumbered-property collection. Rather, if the debtor is absent and the creditor seeks to collect directly from purchasers, we normally plead repayment for him unless there is some basis to know the debt remains unpaid. Resistance supplies such a basis by presumption. If the debtor himself is present and admits nonpayment, Rosh and Tur permit collection from purchasers without invoking resistance. Thus the two rules address different evidentiary postures. Rosh's law of resistance in the first chapter of Bava Batra does not derive from Rif's interpretation of the Bava Metzia passage. Had Rosh adopted Rif's interpretation, he would have quoted it there as he usually does. Instead it is an independent evidentiary rule: one who openly refuses to comply with beit din is presumed not to have paid. This distinction is important. Once we accept Rosh's framework, Bach's later limitation also requires examination. Bach says: if the debtor admitted in Nisan that he still owed the debt, and admitted again in Iyar that he had not paid, the creditor cannot collect from property sold at the end of Nisan. He may collect only from property sold after the Iyar admission. I do not understand. If we fear collusion and possible payment, then even purchasers after Iyar should be protected because payment may have occurred after the Iyar admission. If we do not fear collusion and accept the debt as unpaid, the lien should run from the first judicial admission in Nisan, not from the later confirmation. There is no principled middle position. Bach says his distinction follows Raavad cited by Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 12, part 2. I searched Raavad's words and find the opposite. Raavad says that once the parties stood in judgment, the obligation immediately has written-loan force because the proceeding is public. He then adds that where there are witnesses that the defendant did not pay after judgment, or the defendant admits nonpayment, or the creditor imposed an oath that he did not receive payment, or the defendant resisted the judgment, beit din can write a document without the defendant's consent. If the defendant later says he paid, he is not believed because the matter is now documented both for repayment and for encumbered-property collection. Raavad's wording "immediately" indicates that for purchaser collection the lien dates from the original judgment. The later proof of nonpayment merely allows the court to issue the written judgment and blocks the defendant's personal repayment claim. It does not create a new lien date. This is exactly contrary to Bach's distinction. Raavad's final remark also explains the Bava Metzia passage. At first R. Zeira says that where beit din told him, "Go pay," he can later say he paid. Later the Gemara says that if the creditor comes to write, they do not write. Why mention writing if the debtor would remain able to say he paid even against the written judgment? Raavad answers that once beit din has verified nonpayment and properly issues a written judgment, the document itself prevents a later repayment claim. Thus the first rule concerns the period before writing. The second explains why writing is withheld unless the court has reason to know the debt remains unpaid. This is straightforward. Gidulei Terumah's difficulty with Raavad therefore disappears. Raavad supports neither Bach nor Shach. He supports the position that the judicial proceeding can have immediate public-lien force while the debtor may still personally claim payment until a written judgment is properly issued. This is essentially the position Sema attributes to Tur and Rosh. There remains some uncertainty whether Rosh and Tur apply the same rule to a simple admission before beit din as to an adjudicated dispute. Rosh's main question comes from the rule that "standing in judgment" creates written-loan force. He does not ask from the separate law of judicial admission. Perhaps a simple judicial admission is different. Where the debtor came specifically because the creditor summoned him to obtain a formal acknowledgment, the very purpose may have been to create a public obligation. But where the parties came to resolve some broader dispute and the defendant happened to admit one item, perhaps no one intended a written judgment. Tur's wording, however, after discussing an admission before three, says that a beit din admission functions as an admission "to collect from encumbered property." This sounds like the same rule. Sema tries to read that phrase as a separate general remark, not connected to the prior discussion. The language is difficult to bear that interpretation. Accordingly, even Rosh and Tur likely extend the principle to ordinary judicial admissions where the tribunal had proper authority to adjudicate. Still, because Rambam and many other authorities disagree, the law is at least doubtful. Shach is therefore correct in siman 79 that Mechaber should not have presented Rambam's rule too absolutely, because many authorities do not accept that a summons alone eliminates the repayment defense even without a written judgment. Rosh and Tur certainly do not adopt Rambam's exact formulation. At minimum, a possessor can say kim li according to the authorities who allow the repayment claim. The practical structure is therefore: According to Rambam and Rif as interpreted by Baal HaTerumot, a summoned or compelled judicial proceeding can itself create stronger written-loan status, while an unsummoned compliant proceeding does not. According to Rosh and Tur, the decisive distinction is not summons but the difference between the debtor's personal repayment defense and purchaser collection from a public judicial obligation. According to Raavad, the judicial proceeding immediately creates public lien force, but a properly written judgment is needed to extinguish the debtor's later repayment claim. These are distinct approaches and should not be blended. Sema was right about Rosh and Tur but not about Rambam. Shach was right that many authorities reject Sema's attribution to Rambam, but his attempt to reinterpret Rosh and Tur through later purchasers does not solve the underlying problem. Because the Rishonim are divided, the possessor can rely upon the view that preserves the repayment claim where no properly written judgment exists.
Perhaps he paid him. Rambam's wording is imprecise if read literally. According to Rambam, even if beit din knows at the very moment the creditor asks for writing that payment has not yet occurred, the court may still be unable to write where the defendant was not summoned in the manner Rambam requires. Rambam treats judicial admission and judicial judgment under one framework. In both, where the defendant was not summoned, beit din does not write. This is obvious regarding an admission made in the same session. At the moment he admits, everyone knows he has not yet paid, yet the Gemara says there are forms of admission that are not written. Rambam later writes that the wise men of Spain would tell a creditor, when the debtor admitted before beit din, "Do not accept payment except before witnesses." Why did they not simply ask beit din to write a document immediately, while nonpayment was certain? Evidently the court could not always write. Shach and Sema likewise recognize that according to Rambam, absence of payment alone does not automatically permit writing. Why then does Rambam explain the refusal to write by saying "perhaps he paid"? The answer is that the original enactment was based on the ordinary repayment concern. The Gemara says that where the debtor is believed to say he paid, therefore if the creditor comes later seeking a written judgment, beit din does not write. Rambam unifies this with the law of judicial admission. Since courts do not ordinarily write immediately in the weaker class of proceedings, allowing later writing would often create a risk that payment occurred in the interim. Chazal therefore established a general rule that such proceedings are not converted into written obligations. Once the rule was fixed, we do not write even in the unusual case where the court presently knows payment has not yet occurred. There are two reasons. First, lo plug. The enactment was stated categorically. Second, because the ordinary rule is that such a proceeding does not produce a document, a defendant who appeared there did not come on the assumption that beit din would create a written loan against him. The court should not now strengthen an oral-level obligation into a written one without the procedural conditions that authorize it. Thus "perhaps he paid" explains the basis of the enactment, not every individual application. This is also why the same rule applies to an admission: the court does not convert it into a written loan merely because nonpayment happens to be certain at that moment.
And even according to the view, etc. Rama writes this to answer a difficulty in Tur. Tur cites Raavyah that a debtor can say "I paid" even against a written judicial ruling. Yet Tur also says beit din does not write a judgment later because perhaps the debtor already paid. If he remains believed to say he paid even against the written judgment, what harm is there in writing it? Rama answers that beit din must avoid degradation of the court and an appearance of falsehood. Writing a judgment after payment could make the judicial record appear to assert a liability that no longer exists. The Gemara itself is not difficult. One can interpret the statement, "If the creditor comes to write, they do not write," as referring not to an ordinary judgment but to an adrachta, an enforcement writ. Where the defendant resisted until the court had to issue such a writ, he is certainly not believed to say he paid in the ordinary way. An ordinary judgment, according to Raavyah, may still be written. Rashi's language fits this. Thus Rama's concern is needed primarily to reconcile Tur's own formulation. Sema asks from Tur in siman 79. There Tur says that if the creditor comes to beit din for writing, the court does not write because perhaps the debtor already paid and the creditor will collect a second time through the judgment in his hand. That sounds as though the debtor would not be believed to say he paid against the written judgment. Sema answers that perhaps the debtor will foolishly say, "This never happened," become established as a liar when the judicial record is produced, and then lose the repayment defense. According to that explanation, even if beit din presently knows nonpayment, perhaps they should still refrain from writing because he might later deny the entire proceeding and become a proven liar. Shach already objects, correctly. In siman 69 we sometimes fear that a person will forget and deny a fact that was not firmly impressed upon him, such as where he signed a blank paper later filled in by another. But a major judicial proceeding and formal judgment are not the kind of thing one casually forgets. How can we assume a person will falsely say it never occurred? Moreover, the proceeding has publicity and can affect purchasers, as Tur himself says. The better explanation is this. Originally, even according to Raavyah, Chazal did not permit beit din to write a judgment later because if the debt had already been paid, writing it would create degradation of beit din and appear false, as Rama says. Once that rule was established, it generated an additional evidentiary consequence. If a written judgment is later produced, we presume beit din acted properly. They would not have written unless they had established that the debt remained unpaid at the time of writing. Therefore, if the debtor claims, "I paid before that judgment was written," he is not believed. The written judgment itself carries a powerful presumption that the court verified nonpayment. Even a migo that he could say he paid afterward does not help against this strong judicial presumption. Thus Tur in siman 79 states the stronger consequence: writing can cause double payment because the debtor may no longer be believed to say he paid before the document was issued. This is not inconsistent with Raavyah's general rule that payment can be claimed against a judgment. That rule applies to payment allegedly made after the document was properly issued. The debtor remains able to say, "Afterward I paid." What he cannot say is, "I had already paid before beit din wrote it," because that would mean the court acted improperly. This resolves the contradiction cleanly. It also explains why Tur's rule is important. The court's refusal to write is not only to avoid appearance of impropriety. Once the document exists, it creates a strong presumption about the debt's status at the moment of writing and can materially prejudice the debtor. Therefore beit din must not issue it unless the facts justify doing so. If it is independently known that the debtor had not yet paid, then according to Raavyah's approach the court may write. The Gemara's reason for refusing is specifically the possibility of prior payment. This differs from Rambam's approach discussed above, under which even known nonpayment may not authorize writing where the procedural summons conditions were absent. The two systems should be kept distinct.
