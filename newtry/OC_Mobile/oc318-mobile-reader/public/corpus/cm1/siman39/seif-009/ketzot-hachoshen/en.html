Who came to beit din. The Tur, citing Tosafot, writes that once a debtor has stood in judgment, the creditor can collect from encumbered property because a court judgment generates publicity. But if the debtor claims payment, he remains believed unless there is proof otherwise. Taz and Shach explain that a later admission by the debtor cannot alone permit collection from purchasers because of concern for collusion. Why then is a guarantor different? In siman 129, if the borrower admits the debt remains unpaid, the creditor may collect from the guarantor, even though the borrower could be colluding with the creditor. The distinction is that the guarantor personally undertook the obligation. Once his liability is certain and he does not know whether the debt was paid, he resembles one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," and is liable even where he could not have known. A purchaser or heir did not undertake personal liability. Only the property entered under a lien. We therefore advance for them the seller's or decedent's possible claim of payment, treating it like a certain claim. Consequently, even if the seller now admits nonpayment, we suspect collusion when purchasers would be harmed. This is the same structural distinction seen in the law of one obligated to swear but unable to swear: the original litigant can be liable even where he could not know, while heirs are protected by the claims we advance for their predecessor. However, this protection should apply only where we can plausibly say the debt was paid before the purchaser acquired the property. If the purchaser certainly entered under an existing lien and only afterward payment is alleged, there is stronger reason not to advance the seller's later payment claim. The details depend upon the time and form of the alleged payment.
He is believed to say, "I paid." Nevertheless, once judgment has been rendered, the creditor may collect from purchasers. I was uncertain from what moment the lien created by judicial proceedings takes effect. Does publicity arise immediately when the parties stand before beit din, so that anyone purchasing an hour later is bound, or only from the next day, analogous to an undifferentiated dated document that ordinarily collects only from the next date boundary? Since a document written with hours can collect from the precise hour, it would seem that judicial publicity should likewise operate from the moment of judgment. However, Maharival, suggests that the rule, "standing in judgment is like a document," applies specifically where the creditor asks for a written ruling, because an ordinary kinyan stands ready to be written. If the basis is potential writing and documents in that locale do not record hours, perhaps collection begins only with the following day. There is proof in Bava Batra concerning a pit owner whose ox later dies. The Gemara explains collection from heirs through a case where judgment was rendered while the tortfeasor was still alive. Tosafot says the special enactment allowing collection from heirs in oral debts operates where one could also have collected from purchasers. If a court judgment took effect only the following day, then if the tortfeasor died and was buried that very day, the heirs should still not be bound. Yet the Gemara uses precisely such a case. This indicates that standing in judgment creates collection rights immediately, from the hour, unlike an ordinary document lacking hour notation. This also explains Tosafot's otherwise puzzling emphasis that the defendant died and was buried: the novelty is that even though he died the very day judgment was rendered, the court's ruling already had immediate publicity and collection force.
