When does this apply, that the admission, etc. The preceding seif discusses an ordinary admission where there had been no dispute at all, and distinguishes whether beit din summoned him or he came on his own. This seif adds that the same rule applies even where the parties had an actual dispute, claims, and counterclaims, came before beit din, and beit din ruled between them. The law still depends on whether beit din summoned him. Thus, the phrase "and the defendant said, yes" cannot mean that he fully admitted the plaintiff's entire claim, for that would simply repeat the previous case in Rambam and the Mechaber. Rather, it means that from the defendant's own words and his untenable arguments it became clear that the plaintiff was correct. This is simple, unlike the Shach.
As explained, etc. It appears that in his view, both concerning a judgment and an admission, if beit din summoned him he is not believed to say, "I paid," even if no written judgment or admission document has yet been issued, because an act of beit din is treated like a kinyan. The Shach in siman 79 writes at length that most authorities disagree and hold that until it is written he is believed to say that he paid. I likewise concluded in Tumim that since Tosafos, Rosh, and Tur disagree, it is a legal doubt and the party in possession may say, "I rely upon their view."
Whether they said, etc., "he is liable," etc. The word "liable" is not such a final expression that it necessarily means the judges will no longer reconsider whether there is some basis to exempt him. One might therefore have thought that he is not believed to say he paid, because he could have assumed the judges might yet find a ground for exemption. The ruling teaches that he is nevertheless believed. From this I also proved in Tumim that the case concerns an actual dispute and competing claims. Otherwise, if the defendant himself admitted the debt, what new basis for exemption would the judges later discover? This is obvious.
If he says, "I paid," he is believed. See Tumim, where I conclude that according to this view of Rambam and the Mechaber, one cannot collect even from purchasers, even if the debtor admits the debt, because we plead for the purchasers that he may already have paid. His present admission does not bind them. Indeed, even if it is known that he did not pay, one still cannot collect from purchasers, because the proceeding created no public notice and was not authorized to be written, so it has no status of a written loan, unlike the Sema. Tosafos, Rosh, and Tur hold that if the debtor admits he did not pay, one may collect from purchasers because the original beit din admission had publicity, and there is no concern for collusion so long as the claim was not otherwise impaired. Once he now admits that he did not pay, the publicity dates from the original admission and the purchasers caused their own loss by buying. This is unlike the Bach. If the debtor is not before us, however, one cannot collect from purchasers, because we plead for them that perhaps he paid. But if it is known that he refused to comply with the judgment, he is presumed not to have paid and one may collect from purchasers even in his absence. See Tumim, where this resolves the Tur's words.
Perhaps he already paid, etc. In Tumim I conclude that even if it is definitely known that he has not yet paid, according to Rambam and the Mechaber the judges may not write either an admission document or a written judgment unless he was summoned, because such a writing was never authorized. Rambam gives the concern that ordinarily he may already have paid as the reason the Sages did not establish a general rule permitting such writings. Once the Sages did not authorize them, even where this particular concern is absent they may not transform an oral debt into a written debt. See Tumim.
Even according to the opinion, etc. This resolves an apparent contradiction in the Tur. The Tur writes that a written judgment is not prepared because perhaps the debtor paid, and then cites Ravyah that a debtor is believed to say he paid against a written judgment. If so, why not write it? The Gemara itself is not difficult, because Ravyah can explain like Rashi that the phrase "when he comes to write" refers to an authorization for collection, not to the judgment itself. The Tur, however, requires the Rama's explanation here.
Perhaps he already paid. See Tumim, where I conclude that according to this opinion, if beit din knows he did not pay they may write. We do not suspect that the debtor might later deny the entire proceeding and become established as a liar, as Sema suggests. Why would he falsely deny something he remembers? An act of beit din is a matter a person remembers. If beit din wrote a judgment and it is now in the creditor's possession, and the debtor claims he paid before the date of that judgment, he is not believed, because we presume beit din acted properly and would not have written it had they not known the debt was still unpaid. Even though he now has a migo that he could say, "I paid afterward," a migo is ineffective against such a strong presumption.
