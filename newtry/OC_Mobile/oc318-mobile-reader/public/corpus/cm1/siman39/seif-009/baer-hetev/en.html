Yes. Meaning that although an admission made before beit din is treated like a documented loan for purposes of collecting from purchasers because a judicial act is publicized, this applies only where the borrower admits the debt or it is otherwise known that he did not pay. If he claims payment, he is believed. Thus the Sema. The Shach responds that the Sema appears to hold that even in a case where the borrower could claim payment and no written judgment is issued, the claimant nevertheless collects from purchasers. The Sema states similarly in siman 51, at the end of siman 70, and in siman 79, seif 12. The Shach rejects this, referring to his lengthy discussion in siman 79. He further writes that Rambam understands the ruling as applying specifically where the defendant merely said "yes," which constitutes only an admission and therefore allows a later claim of payment. Many poskim disagree. The primary rule is that an act of beit din is not automatically considered equivalent to a written document. If the parties leave court without a written judgment, the debtor is believed to say he paid unless the lender, in the debtor's presence, asked the court to write the judgment, in which case it is treated as though written. Thus the Shach.
Because he is believed. Darkei Moshe explains that writing the judgment would appear false. According to this, if it is still certain that the debtor has not paid, Rama apparently holds that a written judgment may be issued. In Perishah, however, the Sema argues that even then it should not be written, because perhaps the debtor will later pay, the claimant will sue him again, the debtor will answer "I never owed you," and the claimant will produce the written judgment, thereby establishing him as a liar, similar to the concern discussed in siman 79. The Shach rejects this. If so, no written judgment could ever be issued. Moreover, the Gemara in Bava Metzia explicitly shows that where it is certainly known the debt has not yet been paid, the court may write the judgment, as all the poskim imply and the Tur states expressly. Perhaps the Sema means a case where the defendant is not informed that a written judgment is being issued. One might have thought that because according to this opinion the debtor is believed to claim payment, a judgment should still be written; the rule teaches otherwise because of the concern that he may later be branded a liar. The Gemara and poskim, by contrast, concern a case where the defendant is informed, in which case the judgment may be written. Even so, the Shach concludes that the Sema's formulation remains unconvincing.
