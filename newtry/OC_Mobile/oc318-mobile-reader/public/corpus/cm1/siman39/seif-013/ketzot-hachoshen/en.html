The Rosh writes regarding a receipt that when a kinyan has been made, the receipt may take effect from its date even before it reaches the recipient, because the Rif distinguishes between sale or gift documents and documents of obligation. The Rif's formulation is difficult against the Mishnah, which says a receipt found in the street is not returned when the creditor does not admit it, and that wills, gifts, and receipts are not returned lest they have been written but never delivered. The Shach raises this and other questions. The correct distinction is between a document that itself creates a transfer of the underlying object and a document that records or releases an existing obligation. In the latter category, a prior kinyan can cause the obligation or release to exist independently of later delivery of the writing. Thus a receipt can operate from the kinyan even if the writing itself has not yet reached the other party. But sale and gift instruments that are themselves the mode of property acquisition ordinarily require delivery. This also helps explain the sugya concerning a writ of emancipation and the question whether the master later sold property acquired by the slave between the writing and delivery. An emancipation document is not mere waiver, because a slave's body is legally acquired by the master and must be transferred through an effective mode of release. Therefore delivery matters. Similarly, a sale deed written for a seller without the purchaser present does not itself transfer until proper acquisition occurs, unless an independent kinyan already created the sale. Where the writing merely records an earlier binding kinyan, concern for improper retroactive collection is reduced. The Rosh's distinction must therefore be understood functionally: in pure obligation and release matters, the operative legal event can precede delivery of the document; in property transfers whose very mechanism is the deed, the deed must reach the recipient. This also explains why the rules differ between later purchasers and creditors. The precise applications remain tied to whether the instrument is one of acquisition, evidence, or release.
