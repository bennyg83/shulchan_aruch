Even if the lender says, etc. Shach explains that we suspect the lender may be acting deceptively. Urim VeTumim asks that without the borrower's instruction the writing should be invalid as testimony from writing. The answer is that when witnesses prepare a deed for a borrower, they often do not witness the loan itself. Their testimony becomes operative upon authorized delivery of the document. If the borrower later says, 'Give it,' that delivery can complete the testimonial function. According to the view that signed witnesses acquire for the recipient retroactively, a document created contrary to the borrower's instruction at the time of signing can indeed be problematic. But a distinction remains between signing on a blank sheet, where there was no testimonial statement at all, and signing a completed instrument that later receives the borrower's instruction to deliver. In the latter case the later instruction can cause the prior signing to function retroactively as authorized testimony.
When is this said? In a deed containing a kinyan, for, etc. Shach asks against Rosh's understanding of Rif from the cases of a receipt and found documents. The best explanation is that even in a kinyan document, ultimate effect can depend on the document later reaching the recipient. A borrower who orders a document written in anticipation of a future loan generally does so on the condition that the loan actually occur. Signed witnesses may give the beneficiary retroactive priority, but the substantive condition, the loan itself, must still be fulfilled. The same is true of a receipt: the borrower may acquire the benefit retroactively from the date of signing, but only on condition that payment actually occurs. If the creditor later admits payment, that admission satisfies the condition and the receipt can operate retroactively. This differs from a deed of gift or emancipation where the instrument itself creates the transfer and, depending on the doctrine of signed witnesses acquiring for the recipient, actual arrival of the deed can complete the earlier transfer. If property was sold in the interim in a way that destroys the very transaction contemplated by the first deed, later delivery cannot revive that extinguished transfer. By contrast, a lien document can remain operative regarding other assets even if one field was sold in the meantime. This also answers the objections from emancipation documents. A sale of the slave's property in the interim does not necessarily destroy the emancipation document itself. Regarding receipts, the condition is repayment. If repayment later occurs, forgiveness can be treated as effective from the earlier time in a 'from now, upon fulfillment' structure. Accordingly, the concerns in found-receipt cases remain because perhaps repayment never occurred at all.
And they wrote for him. In a kinyan deed, if he simply said, 'Write,' the witnesses may ordinarily deliver the document to the lender. Therefore we do not suspect that he secretly meant, 'Write it only for me.' By contrast, with a receipt or with a non-kinyan deed where delivery requires a separate authorization, an unqualified instruction to write can leave room to suspect that he intended the document to remain with him. Even where a kinyan document gives retroactive priority without delivery, if the obligor later expressly protests delivery before it reaches the creditor, the creditor cannot collect from purchasers. The retroactive acquisition is conditioned on eventual permitted delivery, and his protest prevents completion of that condition.
