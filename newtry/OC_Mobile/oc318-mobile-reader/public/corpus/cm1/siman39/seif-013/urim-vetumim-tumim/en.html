And one who acquires through kinyan, etc. Maggid Mishneh explains that where the borrower made a kinyan before the lender was present, the lender cannot simply tell witnesses afterward, "Write the document and keep it until the borrower agrees," because until the borrower gives proper authorization there is concern that the writing is only an appearance of falsehood. Shach challenges this. He argues that we do not generally fear a mere appearance of falsehood where the underlying transaction is true. He also points out that the Mishnah's rule, "we do not write a document for the lender when the borrower is absent," must be understood differently according to Abaye. According to Rav Asi, who establishes the first clause as involving acquisition documents, one can understand why the second clause teaches that the lender cannot have a document written without the borrower. But Abaye holds that omission of warranty is a scribal error and does not establish the first clause only as a kinyan case. If the Mishnah's second clause merely taught the obvious rule that the lender cannot create a document without the borrower's consent, what novelty remains? It is forced to say that even for Abaye the Mishnah shifts between different types of documents without saying so. The better explanation is as follows. Maggid Mishneh's rule depends upon a concern of apparent falsehood. Shach rejects that concern. If so, according to ordinary law, where the lender says to witnesses, "Prepare the document and keep it until the borrower later agrees," there would be no reason to prohibit the writing merely because the borrower has not yet given his final instruction. That works according to Rav Asi in a case with kinyan. But according to Abaye, who explains why one may write for the borrower while the lender is absent through the principle that the witnesses acquire for the lender through their signatures, the timing of the signatures is crucial. "His witnesses acquire for him through their signatures" means that at the moment the witnesses sign, they act under the borrower's authorization and thereby create the creditor's right. If the lender had them sign merely as a draft, before the borrower ever instructed them to sign, the signatures were not made as the borrower's agents and cannot operate to acquire anything for the lender. Later borrower consent cannot retroactively transform those old signatures into signatures made under his command. The result could therefore be an antedated document. The lien date would appear to be the earlier date of signing, even though the borrower did not authorize the creation of the creditor's right until later. Abaye's principle cannot cure that. Thus the Mishnah teaches: One may write a document for the borrower while the lender is absent because, when the borrower instructs the witnesses to sign, the signatures themselves acquire for the lender. But one may not write for the lender while the borrower is absent, even if the lender says, "Prepare it now and give it to me only after he agrees," because the old signatures were not made under the borrower's command and therefore do not acquire from their date. This creates a genuine antedating concern. Now the Mishnah is meaningful under both Abaye and Rav Asi. This also explains why the Gemara's difficulty is directed only according to Rav Asi in the kinyan framework. Rav Asi does not use Abaye's "witnesses acquire through their signatures" solution. Where a kinyan already occurred, the underlying lien may already exist and there is less reason to fear antedating merely because the paper was prepared earlier. If we also reject concern for appearance of falsehood, why should the lender not prepare a document and hold it pending the borrower's final consent? The answer must then be that a standard kinyan intended for writing is perhaps effective only where the lender himself was present at the kinyan, or under some equivalent condition. That is the Ramban-based limitation. But this means one should not combine both stringencies indiscriminately. Either the rule is based on appearance of falsehood as Maggid Mishneh says, or it is based on Ramban's narrower view of when a kinyan stands for writing. The sources do not establish both together as cumulative requirements. Indeed, Rashba and Raah disagree with Ramban on the latter point. Therefore there is no basis to impose both concerns except according to Sema's unusually strict reading. At least where a kinyan was made in the lender's absence and the lender later asks witnesses to prepare a document to be held until the borrower authorizes delivery, there is substantial ground to permit once the underlying lien already exists and no antedating results. There is no "appearance of falsehood" because the debt and lien are real. Moreover, the very rule is disputed, and we should not invent a double stringency unsupported by the major poskim. I remain somewhat uncertain for another reason. If a document is written and signed entirely without the borrower's authorization, why is it not invalid as "from their writing" altogether? As I explained above, written testimony ordinarily requires either authorization that gives it documentary status or another recognized legal mechanism. If the writing was originally worthless, how can the borrower's later statement "write" validate it retroactively? Why should an instruction given an hour later differ from one given months later? If later authorization could retroactively turn worthless writing into a document, one could revive any old paper that had never had legal force. This problem actually supports the view I stated above in seif 3. The debtor's instruction "write" is not necessarily what makes the testimony itself valid in the first place. Where the writing was prepared in normal legal document form, it can already preserve the underlying testimony. The debtor's authorization is needed primarily because creating a written loan against him strengthens the creditor's collection rights and should not be done against his will. On that understanding, later consent on the same day can indeed authorize the documentary consequence without needing to "revive" dead testimony. The witnesses' evidence was always valid in substance; what was missing was permission to impose written-loan status. This fits Maggid Mishneh far better. Ramban may disagree conceptually and require authorization already at the time of writing, but then his view should not be combined with Maggid Mishneh's separate appearance-of-falsehood reasoning as though both were universally accepted. Shach, who rejects Maggid Mishneh here, later in siman 48 leans toward Rashi's view that even the operative text of a document should not be written before the borrower commands it. His positions are therefore not entirely consistent. Accordingly, I do not accept Sema's cumulative stringency. The halachah should follow the more moderate structure explained above. There is another consequence of this analysis concerning the apparent contradiction between Maggid Mishneh and Ramban. Maggid Mishneh's concern is that writing before the borrower's authorization may look false because, at the moment of writing, the paper states a transaction that has not yet been formally authorized as a written loan. Ramban's concern is different. He addresses whether an ordinary kinyan itself stands for writing when the beneficiary was absent. These are separate questions. If the kinyan itself already created the lien and is sufficiently public, then the document can later be written without creating a false substantive statement. The underlying obligation exists. If, however, the kinyan does not stand for writing in that posture, the creditor may still need the borrower's later command before the paper becomes a creditor document. One should not infer from the existence of the second problem that the first must also be present. This matters because Sema treats both as if they reinforce one another and therefore creates an unusually strict rule. But if Rashba and Raah reject Ramban's limitation, and Shach rejects the concern of apparent falsehood, what remains as a source for the combined stringency? Nothing clear. The practical halachah should therefore distinguish the cases. If the borrower already made a valid kinyan that, according to the accepted local practice and authorities, stands for writing, there is strong basis to prepare the document even if the lender was not present at the kinyan, provided the paper is not delivered in a manner contrary to the borrower's remaining rights. If there was no such kinyan and the lender merely asks the witnesses to prepare a form in advance, Abaye's antedating concern remains substantial because the signatures were not made under the borrower's authority. There is also an important conceptual difference between the substantive debt and the document's lien date. Suppose the borrower owes the money orally already. The witnesses later prepare a document without his instruction. Their writing may truthfully record an existing debt. Nevertheless, if the document is dated from the earlier day and later delivered as a written loan, it can improperly create collection from purchasers from a time when no written lien existed. Thus a document can be factually true yet legally antedated. This is precisely why the issue cannot be reduced to "appearance of falsehood." The paper may contain no factual lie at all. The problem is that its legal consequences can reach backward beyond the moment of authorization. Abaye's rule solves that only where the signatures themselves, made under the borrower's authority, acquire for the lender from their date. If the signatures were placed earlier without authorization, they cannot later be given that retroactive effect. This further supports the explanation above. By contrast, where a kinyan already created a lien from the earlier date, later writing merely memorializes a preexisting legal state. There is then no antedating. The later paper does not create a new retroactive lien; it records one already born through the kinyan. This is why the kinyan case is fundamentally different. One can also understand the dispute about writing a document for a seller or giver in the same way. In a completed sale or gift by kinyan, the property already changed hands. A later document can record that completed transfer. The grantor may still have a right to prevent creation of a stronger evidentiary paper if the original arrangement did not authorize one, but there is no issue of creating the property transfer retroactively. That is why Rosh can say the grantor may retract from the writing but not from the field itself. In a loan, the document can affect not only proof but also the creditor's rights against purchasers. The stakes are therefore greater, and authorization matters more. This difference explains why statements concerning gifts and sales cannot always be transferred mechanically to loans. Nevertheless, where the loan kinyan itself already created the relevant lien, the analogy becomes much closer. There is another indication from the rule that omission of warranty is a scribal error. If the borrower authorized a written loan, the document can collect from encumbered property even if the warranty language was accidentally omitted. That rule shows the legal force of the document depends not merely upon the words physically written but upon the transaction the witnesses were authorized to memorialize. Authorization therefore links the paper to the legal event. Where the paper was signed before any authorization, that link is missing. Later consent can perhaps authorize delivery prospectively, but it should not automatically make the old signatures operate retroactively from their date unless the underlying kinyan already supplied the lien. This is another reason Abaye's mechanism cannot be extended too far. Accordingly, the cleanest framework is: Where no prior kinyan or lien exists, signatures placed without the borrower's instruction cannot later be used to create a retroactive written lien from the signing date. Where a prior kinyan already created the lien, later writing can generally record that existing status, subject to the dispute whether the kinyan stood for writing in the creditor's absence. Where the writing is merely a draft or memorandum and no one seeks retroactive lien consequences, the concern is much weaker and can depend upon the broader dispute concerning written testimony discussed above. This framework harmonizes Abaye, Rav Asi, Rosh, Ramban, Maggid Mishneh, and Shach more naturally than imposing every stringency simultaneously.
Or if he wrote explicitly in the document that the property will be encumbered from now, etc. Shach leaves this law unresolved. Ran derives it from Rashi in Ketubot 19b. Rashi explains a case where witnesses say, "It was a trust document." He describes a document that had been written before any loan occurred, with the borrower already subjecting his property from now for a loan that might be advanced later. Since the lien begins immediately, the document is not antedated when the loan is eventually made, although there can be another prohibition involved in leaving such a document outstanding. Ran derives that if the borrower expressly subjects his property from now for a future loan, witnesses may write even when the lender is not presently there, although the document is not a standard kinyan document. Shach asks that Rashi's case involved the document being delivered immediately to the lender. The lien can therefore begin from now. Where the lender is absent and the document remains with the witnesses, what source is there that the same rule applies? One cannot answer that Ran means merely that the lender had not yet advanced the money but the document itself was already delivered to him. Ran does not need to teach that. Ran himself does not follow Rashi's special requirement that the money be seen or advanced at the same moment. Like most authorities, he holds that once a proper document is delivered to the lender, we need not supervise when the money is actually handed over. Nimukei Yosef cites him to that effect, and if the borrower says explicitly, "Acquire for the lender," it certainly works. Thus Ran must be teaching a case where the document can be written even before delivery to the lender. Shach's difficulty therefore requires an answer. The key lies in Rosh's objection to Rif in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. Rif explains "documents of acquisition" simply as documents containing a kinyan. Rosh asks: why should kinyan make any difference? Once a document says, "So-and-so borrowed one hundred from so-and-so," the borrower's property is subject to the creditor under the ordinary rules even without a kinyan. If publicity is the issue, the witnesses to the document create publicity, not the kinyan itself. At first glance Rosh's question is very difficult. The entire concern of the Gemara is that perhaps the borrower asked to have the document written but never actually borrowed. If no money was advanced and the document never reached the lender, what obligation or lien exists at all? My relative, the famous gaon Maharam Schiff, already raised this and left Rosh's words requiring study. One cannot explain Rosh's question by saying the borrower expressly admitted, "I already owe this creditor one hundred," because then the case is not the ordinary fear that he wrote in order to borrow later. Nor can we say the witnesses knew the money had already been advanced, because that empties the Gemara's concern of content. Rosh also follows Rabbenu Tam below in siman 40 that merely saying, "I owe you one hundred in a document," does not create a new obligation until a document reaches the creditor. Thus a bare oral statement cannot solve the problem. The correct explanation is that even Rav Asi, who disagrees with Abaye regarding publicity, can agree with the underlying principle that witnesses can create the substantive acquisition and lien through their signatures. There are two separate questions: When does the legal obligation or lien arise? When does sufficient publicity arise to allow collection from purchasers? R. Meir holds that signed witnesses make the document operative. According to that theory, once the witnesses sign under proper authorization, the substantive legal creation can occur through the signatures themselves. This is the core of "his witnesses acquire for him through their signatures." Abaye holds the signatures also create sufficient publicity immediately. Rav Asi can disagree only on that second point. He may concede that the substantive lien exists through the signatures, while holding that the public does not treat the lien as announced until the creditor receives the document or until some additional unusual circumstance causes publicity. This explains Rosh. Rosh asks Rif: if the kinyan merely creates a property lien, that does not distinguish the case. The witnesses' signatures already create the substantive lien under R. Meir's document theory. The real distinction must therefore be publicity. Rav Asi says ordinary signatures, before the creditor receives the document and before money is advanced, do not generate sufficient public notice. A special acquisition document is different because the borrower undertakes liability in an unusual form, such as subjecting himself regardless of whether the future loan is ultimately advanced. Such an unusual transaction generates publicity from the outset. Rosh therefore explains Rashi's "shtarei aknayata" as a document in which the borrower subjects himself or his property whether he eventually borrows or not. Rosh calls this unusual, and precisely because it is unusual, word spreads. Now Rashi's view in Ketubot becomes clear. If the borrower expressly says, "My property is encumbered to you from now for whatever loan you later advance," the substantive lien is created now by the signatures. The unusual immediate encumbrance also generates publicity. There is therefore no antedating. It is irrelevant whether the lender physically holds the paper at that moment, because the legal lien and the publicity both already exist. This is the foundation of Ran's derivation. Shach's objection assumes that delivery to the lender is what creates the substantive effect. According to the Rosh-Rashi structure, that is not necessarily so. The signatures can create the substantive lien; delivery matters mainly for ordinary publicity under Rav Asi. Where the document expressly creates an immediate encumbrance from now, the unusual nature of the arrangement supplies the publicity even without delivery. There is support from Ketubot 94b. R. Meir says that where one document was written first and another delivered first, the one written first can acquire priority because witnesses to the signatures are the operative act. Maharsha asks: according to Rav Asi, who does not accept "his witnesses acquire through their signatures" in the ordinary loan context, why should earlier writing matter? The answer must be that under R. Meir, where signed witnesses are the operative legal act, everyone accepts the substantive acquisition through signatures. Rav Asi's dispute is only whether ordinary pre-delivery signatures have enough publicity in a loan to collect from purchasers. Maharsha himself, in Bava Metzia 20a, suggests that Tosafot's statement that "his witnesses acquire through their signatures" is a rabbinic enactment applies according to R. Elazar, who holds delivery witnesses create the instrument. According to R. Meir, signatures are the operative act by law itself. If so, we should not multiply the dispute by saying Rav Asi rejects even R. Meir's basic mechanism. This also explains why Rosh and Rashi can speak of immediate encumbrance without a kinyan act independent of the document. The document itself, through its signed operative form, can create the commitment. Rosh's phrase "whether he borrows or does not borrow" means that the borrower makes a present absolute undertaking whose existence does not depend upon later advancement of money. The actual collection can still be related to the eventual loan or condition, but the property lien arises immediately. Because that is unusual, it has publicity. One can now answer another major question posed against Rashi. Why should Rav Asi ever need actual advancement of money if the lender already received the signed document? Is delivery of the paper not at least as strong as Abaye's theory that signatures alone acquire for the creditor? According to the analysis above, Rav Asi's concern is not substantive acquisition but publicity. Abaye says the signed document itself creates public notice from the moment of signing. Rav Asi says that in an ordinary loan, until the lender actually advances the money, people do not treat the paper as evidence of a real outstanding debt. Even if the lender physically possesses it, the public can think it was prepared for a contemplated loan that never occurred. Therefore he requires the loan to become real before ordinary publicity arises. But if the document itself creates an extraordinary immediate lien from now regardless of whether the loan is advanced at once, the unusual transaction is public enough from the outset. This is why Rashi's immediate-encumbrance clause matters even after delivery. Mishneh LaMelech asks why Rashi needs to say the property is encumbered from now if the lender already holds the document. He assumes delivery alone should solve the antedating concern. He overlooked that Rashi consistently requires the advance of money as well. Rashi's explanations elsewhere show this. At the beginning of Rosh Hashanah concerning dating documents by regnal years, Rashi explains a case where the loan was seen in Tammuz and the document was signed in Kislev, creating uncertainty whether the paper is antedated. If delivery of the document on the day it was written automatically cured the issue regardless of when the money was advanced, Rashi's example would not work in that way. The fact that it does shows Rashi requires the underlying loan to become real, not merely physical possession of the paper. Thus Ran's derivation is much stronger than Shach assumed. If the borrower creates an immediate encumbrance from now, the same reason that helps when the document is in the lender's hand also helps when it remains with the witnesses: the substantive lien and the publicity already exist. Still, although we have defended Ran within Rashi and Rosh, Rama should not have stated the law as unquestionably settled. The entire analysis depends on a major premise: even Rav Asi agrees that under R. Meir the signatures can create the substantive lien before delivery, and his disagreement with Abaye is only about publicity. Not all authorities accept that. Ramban, as cited by Baal HaTerumot and in his discussion at the end of Perek HaMegareish, holds more broadly that according to Rav Asi we do not say "his witnesses acquire through their signatures" even under R. Meir. He uses this to reconcile Rif, who rules that signed witnesses are operative, with Shmuel's rule that where two documents have the same date the court uses judicial discretion. If earlier writing automatically created the right, discretion would be unnecessary. Ramban answers that because we rule like Rav Asi, the right does not arise until the document reaches the recipient. This is explicit. According to Ramban, Ran's derivation from Rashi cannot be treated as universal. It depends on how one understands Rav Asi and the document theory. Thus Shach is correct in the bottom line that Rama's ruling is subject to dispute, although the reason is not that Ran misunderstood Rashi. Ran's law is coherent and well grounded within the Rashi-Rosh approach. The practical result is a genuine safek among the Rishonim. There is a further refinement. The entire theory that even Rav Asi accepts substantive acquisition through signatures is strongest under R. Meir, who holds signed witnesses make the instrument. According to R. Elazar, who holds delivery witnesses are operative, Abaye's "his witnesses acquire through their signatures" is itself a rabbinic enactment. Tosafot say so. If Rav Asi rejects that enactment, then under R. Elazar there is no reason to say the signatures themselves create substantive acquisition before delivery. Many poskim rule like R. Elazar in document law. Accordingly, even if Ran's rule is sound under R. Meir, its general application under our halachic framework becomes more doubtful. This also explains why Rav Asi's establishment of "documents of acquisition" may be forced into a special form where the borrower makes an absolute present commitment independent of ordinary document delivery. Under R. Elazar's system, a regular signed paper still needs delivery. Only an independent kinyan or another substantive present undertaking can create the lien earlier. This is another reason to treat Rama's ruling as doubtful rather than absolute. There is also a practical distinction between a document saying, "My property is encumbered from now when you later lend," and one saying, "I absolutely obligate myself from now whether you lend or not." The latter is a full present obligation and, according to Rashi's "shtarei aknayata" explanation, can clearly generate the unusual publicity Rosh describes. The former is only a present lien conditional on a future loan. Ran reads Rashi as permitting this as well. But according to authorities who require a complete present acquisition to produce publicity, the conditional form may be weaker. This helps explain why Rashi and Rosh often describe shtarei aknayata in the extreme form, "whether he lends or does not lend." They are not merely saying the property will be collateral for a future loan. They are saying the borrower creates a present absolute obligation so unusual that everyone recognizes its immediate legal effect. One cannot infer every conditional future encumbrance from that language without dispute. This distinction is important in applying Rama's ruling. Accordingly, the law should be treated as follows: Where the borrower makes a complete present kinyan or absolute present obligation, the witnesses may write because the lien and publicity begin immediately. Where he merely writes that property will be encumbered from now for a loan to be advanced later, Ran and Rashi support validity, but Ramban and other authorities create substantial doubt. One should therefore not extract from purchasers in a doubtful case contrary to the possessor's claim. The matter is a true safek among the poskim.
And they wrote for him. Bach, Sema, and Shach infer from this wording that Tur too requires the kinyan to have been made in the creditor's presence; otherwise an ordinary kinyan does not stand for writing. This is difficult according to Tur's own adoption of Rosh's interpretation of "shtarei aknayata." As explained above, Rosh does not define the phrase as an ordinary kinyan document. He explains it as a document creating an absolute present obligation, whether the future loan is actually advanced or not. If so, Ramban's proof from the Mishnah does not apply directly to Tur. Ramban reasons from the clause, "we do not write a document for the creditor unless the borrower is with him." He says this must be teaching that even where a kinyan occurred, if it was not in the creditor's presence it does not automatically authorize writing. But according to Rosh and Tur, the Mishnah's first clause is not speaking of an ordinary kinyan at all. It is speaking of the unusual absolute-acquisition document just described. Then of course the later clause still requires the borrower to say "write." Nothing can be inferred about an ordinary kinyan conducted outside the creditor's presence. One might try to derive Ramban's rule from the sale and gift clauses instead. There, Rosh agrees that a completed kinyan transfers the field immediately and the grantor cannot retract the property even if the document remains with him. Rosh says so expressly in responsum klal 66, and Tur likewise rules below in siman 250 concerning a gift made by kinyan. Yet it is forced to say the Mishnah uses "write for the borrower" and "write for the seller" in entirely different legal senses without indication. There is another proof that Rosh likely does not accept Ramban's requirement. In Perek HaSefinah, concerning "Acquire the field for so-and-so and write him a document," Rosh asks why the grantor can retract from the document if an ordinary kinyan stands for writing. The language "for so-and-so" implies the beneficiary is not present. Had Rosh accepted Ramban's rule that a kinyan outside the beneficiary's presence does not stand for writing, there would be no question. Likewise, in Perek Chezkas HaBatim, Rosh asks why after the party says "write and sign and give," and a kinyan is made, renewed consultation is needed, since an ordinary kinyan stands for writing. Again, the case can involve a beneficiary who is absent. Rosh does not distinguish. This strongly suggests Rosh rejects Ramban's limitation. Beit Yosef and Ir Shushan understand Tur this way, despite Tur later citing Ramban's view in siman 238. Tur may cite Ramban there as an additional opinion without adopting it as his father's own view. Accordingly, one should not infer from Tur's phrase "and they wrote for him" that he requires the creditor's presence at the kinyan. The matter remains disputed, and Rosh's language leans against Ramban.
And the creditor shall not acquire until the borrower instructs that it be given. This ruling requires study. Rama copied it from Tur. If the borrower says, "Make a kinyan and give the document into my hand," and the creditor acquires nothing until the borrower later orders delivery, then the Mishnah's rule, "we write a document for the borrower even when the creditor is not with him," can be explained simply: the borrower says, "Make the kinyan and give me the paper." Rosh says exactly this in responsum klal 66 when explaining Rambam's law of a borrower who deposited his document with a third party and died. Rosh says that in a loan, because the Mishnah permits writing for the borrower without the creditor, we must fear that perhaps no actual loan ever occurred. Rambam's case therefore involves a kinyan, because without a kinyan one would not write for the borrower alone. If so, the old difficulty against Rif returns in full. Rif holds that even in a kinyan document the creditor must ultimately receive the document. His later critics ask from the rule that if one finds a kinyan document in the street, it is returned to the creditor because the borrower already subjected himself. If physical delivery is still required, perhaps the document fell from the borrower and the creditor never acquired anything. The generation after Rif therefore explained the law differently, saying that the found document case proves a kinyan document does not require prior delivery. If Rama's rule is correct, their objection to Rif disappears. A borrower often has a kinyan document prepared and kept in his own hands before any loan occurs. If such a document is found, why should it ever be returned to the creditor without first asking the borrower? The concern is not rare. The Mishnah itself says this is the ordinary procedure: a document may be written for the borrower while the creditor is absent. The Gemara repeatedly worries about precisely that possibility in cases of found documents and parties with identical names. Therefore Rama's rule is hard to reconcile with the accepted interpretation of the found-kiniyan-document sugya. In truth, Tur and Rosh themselves do not prove Rama's broad rule. Their interpretation of "shtarei aknayata" differs from Rif's. They explain the phrase as an absolute obligation created whether the future loan is advanced or not. In that special document, there is no difficulty returning a found paper to the creditor, because the borrower is already obligated regardless of delivery. At the same time, Tur can still say that an ordinary kinyan made for a contemplated loan does not give the creditor rights in the physical document until delivery. Thus there is no contradiction within Rosh and Tur's own system. But we do not generally follow their narrow definition of shtarei aknayata. Most authorities understand it as an ordinary document containing a kinyan. On that understanding, the found-document sugya proves that the creditor's substantive right can exist even before physical delivery. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, says expressly that this question depends upon the dispute between Rif and the later authorities. Nimukei Yosef likewise explains Rashi's language in terms of an ordinary kinyan and says such a kinyan already creates the creditor's right. Or Zarua is even more explicit. Therefore Rama's unqualified statement should not have been copied as settled law. Beit Yosef did well to omit it. One might rescue Rama by reading his words more narrowly. Perhaps "the creditor does not acquire" means only that he does not acquire the physical document itself. The underlying debt and property lien have already become binding through the kinyan. This would be analogous to the rule that after a gift is acquired, the grantor can still retract from having a separate deed written while unable to retract the transferred field. Then the found kinyan document can still be returned, because the creditor already owns the underlying claim and the paper merely evidences it. The borrower can say, "Give the document to me," without retaining a migo that no debt exists. This interpretation would solve much. But Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit reads Tur and Rama more broadly. He writes that where the borrower says, "Give it into my hand," the witnesses may not give it to the creditor because the borrower did not complete his intent to subject himself until the loan would actually be advanced. If they deliver it earlier, they wrong him. That clearly treats the substantive obligation as incomplete, not merely ownership of the paper. Tur's language also tends toward that reading. Accordingly, the narrow rescue is difficult. There is another possibility. Even if the borrower becomes obligated through the kinyan from the beginning, while the paper remains in his hand he has a migo to destroy it or to say the condition for the obligation was never fulfilled. Once the document leaves his possession, that migo disappears. Then the creditor's position becomes practically enforceable without saying the legal obligation arose only at delivery. This can reconcile some formulations. Suppose the borrower conditioned that the loan would be advanced later but made the property lien effective immediately. If he still holds the document, he can claim the condition failed and destroy the evidence. If the paper is found outside his possession, however, his ability to make that claim is impaired. The found-document rule can therefore allow return even though delivery has evidentiary importance. Still, this is not the plain sense of Rama. The main conclusion remains that the matter is disputed. Under the majority interpretation of shtarei aknayata, a proper kinyan creates the substantive right even before delivery to the creditor. Rama's broader statement, that the creditor acquires nothing until the borrower orders delivery, is doubtful. One should therefore follow Beit Yosef's caution and not treat the ruling as universally settled. See further below in siman 55.
