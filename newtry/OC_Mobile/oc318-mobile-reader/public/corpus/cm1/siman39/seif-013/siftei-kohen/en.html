Even if the lender says, etc. The reason is that at the time of writing the statement is false, and perhaps the lender is acting deceitfully. So writes the Maggid Mishneh. The Beit Yosef objects that in the chapter Kol HaGet the Gemara states that we are not concerned that a document merely appears false. He answers that there the document did not appear false when it was signed. This is not correct, for the discussion there implies that even after it was signed we are not concerned. The Gemara derives this from a note that was used for a loan and then repaid, which may not be reused because its lien has been extinguished. The reason is the extinguished lien, not that it appears false. See there. I later found that the Lechem Mishneh, at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot Edut, raises the same objection against the Beit Yosef. I did find that the Ritva, in HaIshah SheNitarmelah and in HaKotev, writes that the rule that we are not concerned about an appearance of falsehood applies only to writing without signatures, whereas a signed document is invalid. He cites the cases of "when you sit at Hini" in Get Pashut and the power of attorney in Merubah. Nevertheless, this does not appear correct to me for the reason stated above. Moreover, several proofs in Kol HaGet and HaKotev indicate that even a signed document is not invalid merely because it appears false. This also follows expressly from Tosafot and the Rosh in HaKotev concerning a woman who said, "Write it for me and give it into the hand of a trusted person." See there. The Rosh's statement in HaIshah SheNitarmelah that once they signed it became an absolute falsehood is different. There the recital that "we sat as a court of three and established the identity" was truly false. At the time of signing they had not testified, and even after the testimony they had never sat as a court of three, for only two testified. The signature was therefore an absolute falsehood. This distinction is precise. The Gemara's wording there, "give it to me," supports Tosafot and the Rosh. The Rashba and Ran likewise explain in Kol HaGet and HaIshah SheNitarmelah that the cases in Get Pashut and Merubah are different because the statement remains false forever and never becomes true retroactively. Rather, the correct explanation is that of the Bach. Here, because the writing is presently false, we suspect that the lender is scheming, and therefore the document may not be written. In the other cases the act is done with the borrower's consent, so there is no concern for deceit. The Beit Yosef himself concludes similarly, stating that where it appears false there is concern that the lender is scheming, hoping the document will fall from their hands or that he will seize it before it reaches the borrower. See below, siman 46, seif 24, seif katan 57.
When does this apply? To a document containing a kinyan, etc. The Sema writes that Tosafot and the Rosh disagree and maintain that the witnesses, through their signatures, acquire the right for the lender from the time of signing, even if the document reaches him only later. The Tur states this in the name of Ri. The Sema further says that Rabbeinu Yerucham records a third view, distinguishing between something not presently in the recipient's possession, such as a loan document, where the rule that the witnesses acquire for him does not apply until the document reaches him, and something already under his control, where he acquires from the time of signing. The Sema appears to understand Rabbeinu Yerucham as saying that in a matter not in the recipient's possession, the signatures do not acquire for him retroactively even if the document later reaches him, and that he acquires only from the time of delivery. In my opinion this is incorrect. There is no third view here. Rabbeinu Yerucham states precisely the Tur's view, following Ri who rules like Abaye: when the document later reaches the recipient, the signatures acquire for him retroactively from the time of signing. His added distinction is only that when the subject matter is already under the recipient's control, he acquires from the time of signing even if the document never reaches him. The Tur says this expressly. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise writes without mentioning the Rambam's or Ri's opposing positions in that passage: "Regarding something not in his possession for which a document was written, such as a loan note, we do not say that the witnesses acquire for him through their signatures unless the document ultimately reaches him. But regarding something already under his control, he acquires from the time of signing even if the document never reaches him." His words and those of the Tur are taken from the Rosh at the end of Shnayim Ochazin, who infers from the Rif that according to Abaye the requirement that the document later reach the recipient applies only where the subject matter is not already his, such as a loan note. Where it is already under his control, such as a release, he acquires from the signing even if the document never reaches him. The Gemara, Rif, and Rosh prove this in the discussion of a receipt, where Abaye follows his own position that the witnesses acquire through their signatures. One who does not rule like Abaye does not apply this principle even to something already under the recipient's control. Rabbeinu Yerucham's words are therefore plainly according to Abaye, relying on his earlier conclusion that Tosafot rule like Abaye. I do, however, find the Tur difficult. He first says that there is no distinction between a sale and a loan and that in all cases the witnesses acquire through their signatures. He then says that where the right was already under the recipient's control, such as a release, he acquires from the signing even if the document never reaches him. This latter ruling necessarily follows the Rosh's explanation of the Rif, that Abaye's rule concerning a timely receipt applies even without later delivery. Yet Rashi, Tosafot, and the Mordechai require that the receipt ultimately reach the recipient. If the Tur rejects Rashi on that point, why does he say that there is no distinction between sale and loan? The Rosh reasons that if the Rif distinguishes between release and loan, he must also distinguish between loan and sale, holding that in a sale the signatures do not acquire for the buyer. It would therefore seem that the Tur understood the Rosh's final statement, "Rashi did not explain it this way," as the Rosh's conclusion against the Rif. If so, there should be no distinction between loan and release either, and even a release should require eventual delivery. Careful study of the Rosh shows that these two questions depend on one another. The Tur's ruling therefore requires study. One could answer with difficulty that the Tur independently accepted Rashi on one point and the Rif on the other, but this is very strained. The Rosh's abridgment also states the Tur's rulings, which implies that the Tur understood this to be the Rosh's own position. Perhaps he understood "Rashi did not explain it this way" as referring only to the Rif's distinction between sale and loan, not to the preceding distinction between release and loan. In my opinion this is not correct. Rashi explicitly requires eventual delivery regarding a receipt. The Rosh's comment refers to Abaye's statement that a timely receipt collects, and means that Rashi does not interpret it like the Rif. According to Rashi there is no distinction between release and loan, and consequently none between loan and sale. This appears clear. Rabbeinu Yerucham, by contrast, follows the Rosh as he understood him: he distinguishes between a loan and a release where the right was already under the recipient's control, and immediately afterward distinguishes between sale and loan. This is the Rif's view according to Abaye. Rabbeinu Yerucham apparently held that although the Rosh concludes that Rashi did not explain this way, the principal ruling remains like the Rif because the Rosh cites and analyzes the Rif at length. Yet the Rosh's own explanation does not appear correct to me. His assertion that the Rif distinguishes between loan and release lacks a clear source, and no other decisor explains the Rif so. The sugya can be read naturally as requiring eventual delivery in all cases. Had the Rif intended a special rule for a receipt, he should have stated expressly that a release differs from other documents; the phrase "it is a release" does not clearly express such a distinction. Moreover, the Rosh says that the Rif's limitation of Abaye's rule to a document that ultimately reaches the recipient does not apply to a receipt. But the Gemara itself lists receipts among the documents that are not returned because perhaps they were written and the maker reconsidered. It asks why reconsideration matters if the signatures acquire for the recipient, and answers that this is only when the document ultimately reaches him. The question concerning receipts thus remains against the Rosh's reading. The Rosh's further distinction in the Rif between sale and loan is also very difficult, since everywhere a lien resembles a sale. Regarding documents of kinyan, the primary ruling follows those who say that eventual delivery is unnecessary. Although the Ran in responsum 35 writes that even a kinyan document requires delivery, and attributes this to Rabbeinu Chananel, the Rif, and the Rambam, study of the Gemara yields many proofs to the contrary. The Baal HaItur rules that no later delivery is required and cites Rav Hai Gaon. The Rosh states this expressly in responsum, rule 66, section 3; the Tur cites him below in siman 243 and at the end of siman 250. Tosafot in Shnayim Ochazin regarding a found receipt, and in Get Pashut regarding writing a receipt for a woman, also imply this. Rashi, Ri Migash, Ramban, Rashba, Raah, Ritva, and many other authorities agree, as cited by the Nimukei Yosef, Maggid Mishneh, Rivash, and the Ritva's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef. The Rambam also does not disagree, as those authorities explain. This is the ruling of the Beit Yosef, and it is primary, contrary to the responsum of the Mabit. It further appears that the Ran himself later retracted. In Gittin, concerning the woman called Nefata, he writes that a replacement document is written after loss only for documents still requiring delivery, because the recipient acquires only when they reach him. But for a document not requiring delivery, such as one recording a kinyan, once it is properly written and signed the witnesses have completed their agency and may not write another. Since that case concerns a lost document, this can only mean that a kinyan document takes effect without ever reaching the recipient. The Rosh's suggested distinction also leaves Abaye exposed to the concern that a lender might wrongfully collect from purchasers who bought from the borrower between Tishrei, when the loan was purportedly made, and the later delivery. One would have to say that the Rosh agrees that a lender acquires even against later purchasers, and distinguishes only between an initial and later buyer. That is strained, because it makes a lender stronger than a buyer. The Mishnah's rule that a deed may be written for a seller without the buyer present also raises the same concern. Any special answer should have been stated. The sugya of a found deed of emancipation likewise argues against the distinction. The correct explanation of the Rif is therefore that although the receipt had not reached the husband until now, it effects a release because the woman presently admits it and directs that it be given to him now. The Rif thus agrees with Rashi and Tosafot: there is no distinction between release, loan, and sale. In every case, according to Abaye, the signatures acquire retroactively only if the document ultimately reaches the recipient. This is the primary ruling. In any event, it appears that the Rosh himself ultimately follows Rashi, as the Tur understood him, for immediately afterward the Rosh says that the statement of Rabbi Abba bar Memel cannot be reconciled with Abaye. According to the Rif's alleged distinction it could be reconciled, proving that the Rosh did not accept that distinction. See also siman 65, seif 14, seif katan 36. Although the Rif, Rambam, and other authorities rule against Abaye, Ri, the Rosh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the Tur rule like him. A possessor may therefore plead that the law follows these authorities. Thus, if a woman admits a receipt, her husband, as possessor, may say that the law follows Ri and his colleagues. Even if she sold her ketubah rights to others before delivering the receipt, the husband acquired the property designated for her ketubah from the time of signing, and it cannot be taken from him. It is surprising that the Rama did not mention this view. The Sema suggests that the Rama omitted it because the Nimukei Yosef says many great authorities follow the Rif. The Rama indeed says this in Darkei Moshe. But the Nimukei Yosef states only that many early authorities follow the Rif concerning kinyan documents and require eventual delivery. He does not say that many rule against Abaye generally. His silence may imply agreement with the Rif, but it is no proof. The same is true of his discussion in Get Pashut. A responsum of the Raavan cited by the Mordechai in Gittin implies that the law follows Abaye. Tosafot in Mi SheHayah Nasui report that Rabbeinu Tam ruled like Abaye, and imply that Ri did as well. The Hagahot Maimoniyot, Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, and Hagahot Mordechai likewise support this. Maharshal's language cited by the Bach also suggests that the law follows Abaye. Therefore a possessor may certainly rely on these authorities. If, however, the receipt never reached the husband, such as where it was lost after being found, even Abaye agrees that the signatures do not acquire for him. The property designated for the ketubah must then be given to those who purchased her ketubah rights. The same applies in every analogous case. I was later shown that the Ramban in Milchamot, at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, likewise rules that even for a receipt or other release relating to something already under the recipient's control, eventual delivery is required, and he explains the Rif exactly as I have. I rejoiced that I had independently reached the true view of that great authority, with praise to God. As to the definition of a kinyan document, the Rif's explanation is primary: even if it does not expressly say "whether he borrows or not," the presence of a kinyan ordinarily means that the obligor subjected himself immediately. The Nimukei Yosef says many early authorities agree. Rashi can also be understood this way, as Rabbeinu Yerucham and Rivash explain. The Rosh's objection is answered because one who authorizes a document with a kinyan ordinarily intends to encumber his property from that day and transfers the lien by kinyan even if no loan is ultimately made. A kinyan has publicity like a document, since an ordinary kinyan is made for writing. Purchasers who learn of the kinyan therefore know that the property was encumbered from that time. See Maharshach, volume 3, responsum 9.
Or if he expressly wrote in the document, etc. This ruling requires study. No earlier decisor distinguishes this case, and the Gemara implies that according to Rav Asi only documents containing an actual kinyan are effective in this manner. The distinction is also illogical. Since Rav Asi does not accept the principle that witnesses acquire for the recipient through their signatures, what difference does it make that the obligor wrote that the lien begins immediately? The document still did not reach the recipient until later. It is also unreasonable that the word "from now" should be stronger than delivery of the loan money in the witnesses' presence. Even when the money is delivered, the borrower's property becomes encumbered immediately according to the rule that a lien is of Torah origin, yet the document still has no effect until it reaches the lender. Thus nothing is effective until delivery of the document. Darkei Moshe attributes this ruling to the Ran in the second chapter of Ketubot and to the Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, and it is cited that way in the Shulchan Aruch. Nothing of this sort is implied by the Nimukei Yosef. The Ran does say it, but his words also require study. He claims that the ruling follows from Rashi's explanation of an accommodation document. The Ran asks why we do not fear that the lender will never lend and will nevertheless collect improperly from purchasers. He answers that if the borrower encumbers himself now, effective when the loan is made, the document may be written even though the lender is not present, and from here he derives the present ruling. I do not understand this. Rashi speaks of a document delivered immediately. If the borrower encumbers his property now upon the later loan, immediate delivery avoids the problem of antedating; the witnesses are merely forbidden to create an accommodation document. But where the lender is absent and the document is not delivered, no such rule follows. Perhaps the Ran's phrase "even though the lender is not with him" means only that the lender is not present to hand over the money immediately, not that the document is withheld. His language is abbreviated because earlier he had described Rashi as requiring the lender to be present and the witnesses to see the transfer of money. On this reading, the Ran means that the lender need not be present for the money, but the document must still be delivered to him that day. The Rama's ruling cannot be interpreted this way and therefore requires study.
They may not write it even for the borrower. The Nimukei Yosef writes in the name of the Ran that this applies only if the document will be given to the borrower or remain for some time in the witnesses' possession. If it is immediately delivered to the witnesses so that they acquire it on the lender's behalf, and it remains with them from that moment for him, it may be written. In that case the lender is legally considered present, since one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence. The Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema cite this as law. The Ran adds that the lender is then considered present in order to answer why the Gemara did not explain the Mishnah's permission to write a note for the borrower without the lender present in this manner. The answer is that legally this is a case in which the lender is present.
