Seif 1. Collection is made from encumbered property. Shach, seif katan 2, extensively rejects Maharshal's rejection of Tosafot's distinction between an obligation imposed by the Torah and an ordinary loan. Although the Shach is correct in rejecting Maharshal's particular proof, I nevertheless believe the law follows Maharshal and that there is no such distinction. A clear proof appears from Bava Kamma 43. The Gemara asks whether Rabbi Akiva does not hold his view in damages and cites a baraita. Rabbah and Rav Nachman resolve the passage, and the Gemara asks why it is not established, according to Rav Nachman, where the heirs collected land, and according to Rabbah, where they collected money. According to Tosafot's distinction, this is difficult. Bava Batra 175 implies that Rabbah, who says that an heir who collected land must surrender it, is challenged because he elsewhere holds that liens are not biblical. The Gemara answers that Rabbah was speaking according to the western scholars, whereas according to his own view the heir would keep both money and land. Why, then, should the Gemara ask regarding Rav Nachman that the case be one in which land was collected? Rav Nachman's position that a creditor cannot collect from slaves sold by the debtor rests, as Tosafot and the Rosh cited here by Shach explain, on his view that liens are not biblical. But that is in an ordinary loan. Here the obligation is damages, an obligation written in the Torah, which according to Tosafot is like a written loan. Rav Nachman should concede that its lien is biblical, and therefore where land was collected the creditor has a right in it from the debtor's lifetime. The Gemara should not have needed to establish the case as one of a divorced woman. Necessarily, there is no distinction between an obligation imposed by the Torah and another obligation. According to Rav Nachman, who holds liens are not biblical, they are not biblical in either case, and the Gemara therefore properly could have answered that land had been collected. Tosafot there also imply the same. They ask that the Gemara could have challenged Rabbah whether the heirs collected money or land, since according to him liens are not biblical. If Tosafot truly distinguished obligations written in the Torah, there would be no challenge to Rabbah in damages at all, because even if he denies biblical liens for loans he should agree that damages carry a biblical lien. Rather, the Gemara's difficulty depends on the statement that where a biblical lien exists, Rabbah nevertheless distinguishes between land and money in the inherited estate. Thus the question remains valid in damages as well. This also resolves Rashbam's difficulty in Bava Batra 124b and Tosafot's question there. Rashbam may follow Tosafot in Kiddushin that distinguish a Torah-imposed obligation, so the challenge to Rabbah would be specifically where money was collected, which explains why Rav Nachman is mentioned with land. Examine this carefully. I later found this question in Urim VeTumim.
