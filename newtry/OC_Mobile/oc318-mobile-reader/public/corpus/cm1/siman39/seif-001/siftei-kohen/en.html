He may collect from encumbered property, etc. The central question is when an admission or judicial ruling acquires the force of a written loan for collection from purchasers. A formal act of court, a kinyan, or a document written with the debtor's knowledge may create publicity and a lien, whereas a bare oral admission generally does not. The Gemara and the Rishonim distinguish between one who was summoned and refused to comply, one who voluntarily admitted, one whose liability was established by witnesses, and one whose own legal claim caused the court to rule against him. They also distinguish whether the court wrote the ruling before the parties left, whether the debtor knew it was being written, and whether the creditor seeks merely to prove the debt or to collect from encumbered property. The Rif and Rambam treat a coercive or adjudicated court obligation more like a written loan, while a voluntary admission remains an oral obligation unless properly recorded. The Rosh, Tur, Ramban, Ran, Baal HaTerumot, and other authorities debate the exact limits, but the governing principle is publicity: where the proceeding produces a recognized act of court, purchasers are charged with notice; where it is only a private acknowledgment, they are not.
