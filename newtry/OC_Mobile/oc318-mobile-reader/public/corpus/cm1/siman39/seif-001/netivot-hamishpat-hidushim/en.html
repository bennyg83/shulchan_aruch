Or he admitted. The Sema writes in the name of Hagahot Ashri that with an admission, a kinyan is ineffective unless the kinyan obligates him to pay at a specified time. See Beurim, seif katan 1, that the intent is that with a mere admission we do not say that omission of a lien is a scribal error or lender's error, and therefore the admission does not permit collection from encumbered property unless the lien was expressly stipulated. For this reason we do not apply the principle that an ordinary kinyan is intended for writing when the kinyan added nothing to the admission. But where they made a kinyan obligating him to pay at a fixed date, the kinyan had substantive effect, and we then say that it was intended to be written:
And he does not collect from encumbered property. This means property sold or given away after the loan. An oral loan has no public notice, and purchasers therefore have no reason to protect themselves. A recipient of a gift is treated like a purchaser, because had he not exerted himself for the donor, the donor would not have given him the gift. See the Shach, who concludes that it remains unresolved whether the basic lien of a debt is from Torah law:
Responsibility is a scribal error. There is a presumption that a person does not throw away his money for nothing. Therefore, even if we know that no express responsibility clause was stipulated, we assume that omission of the clause was the seller's or lender's error. This presumption does not apply to a guarantor or to a mere admission:
Even if it was not written. The Sema writes that this applies only when the witnesses to the kinyan are before us and remember the date of the kinyan. If they are absent, or present but do not remember the date, they cannot combine with other witnesses who remember the date to establish collection from encumbered property. Collection is then allowed only from the earliest date that all witnesses can state with certainty that the kinyan had already occurred.
