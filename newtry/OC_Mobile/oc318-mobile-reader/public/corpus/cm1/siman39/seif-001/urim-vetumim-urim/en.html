Or he admitted, etc. Sema cites Hagahot HaRosh that a kinyan does not help an admission unless the debtor also undertook to pay by a specified time. See Tumim, where I explain that under our rule that responsibility is an omitted standard term, that limitation does not stand.
And he does not collect from encumbered property. This means property sold or given away after the loan. An oral borrower ordinarily borrows privately and the debt has no public notice, so purchasers do not protect themselves. The same applies to a donee, because without having rendered some service the debtor would not ordinarily make him a gift, and therefore the Sages did not favor the donee over the creditor. Sema. See Tumim for the dispute whether property liens are Torah law even for oral loans and the Sages merely suspended them for public policy, or whether no lien exists by Torah law and the Sages created collection from purchasers only for written loans to encourage lending. I conclude that the principal law follows those who hold liens are Torah law and a litigant cannot claim certainty like the contrary view. For movable property, and all the more for slaves who can be removed or freed, the lien is not Torah law in the same manner.

Even if it was never written. Sema asks from siman 43, where Mechaber says that if a kinyan was made, a deed written much later is valid, seemingly implying that some deed must eventually be written. He suggests that here the kinyan witnesses themselves are present and remember the date and immediately generated publicity, whereas when they are absent we require that a deed ultimately be written lest the witnesses have forgotten to publicize it. Sema himself calls this strained. In Tumim I reject it. Indeed, if the kinyan witnesses do not themselves testify and other witnesses merely saw the kinyan, one cannot collect from purchasers because perhaps the debt was repaid, and the borrower's later admission cannot overcome such a defect because of concern for collusion. I therefore conclude that Rosh, Tur, and certainly Mechaber hold that kinyan alone suffices to create collection from encumbered property even if no deed is ever written. Since the creditor could demand a deed at every moment, the kinyan itself creates the same public notice as writing. The statement in siman 43 that even a deed written much later is valid is a greater novelty: not only does unwritten kinyan create publicity, but even when the deed is finally written in Tishrei with the date of the Nisan kinyan it is not considered falsely antedated, because the lien and its publicity genuinely began in Nisan. Shach likewise concludes that this is the majority view.
