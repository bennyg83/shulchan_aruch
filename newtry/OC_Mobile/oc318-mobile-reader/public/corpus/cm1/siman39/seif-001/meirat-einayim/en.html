Or he admitted before witnesses, etc. Hagahot Ashri in chapter Zeh Borer writes that a kinyan helps with an admission when they made a kinyan from him that he would pay his debt by a certain time. But if he simply says, "I hereby admit to you by kinyan before witnesses that I owe you," it is unclear upon what the kinyan takes effect, since he is not presently transferring anything. See the beginning of siman 40.
And he does not collect from encumbered property. Encumbered property means assets that the borrower sold or gave away after the loan. He cannot collect from them because purchasers did not know of an oral loan. Witnesses without a document do not create publicity, since the borrower ordinarily does not wish it known that he is indebted and the witnesses do not spread the matter. The same law applies to a gift recipient, because in these matters the recipient is treated like a purchaser, for ordinarily one gives a gift only in return for some benefit. Where, however, a sudar kinyan was made before witnesses, we say that since he granted the obligation with such generosity he presumably does not object to publicity, and the witnesses ordinarily spread the matter. Therefore the Mechaber concludes that with a kinyan alone, even if no document was written, collection may be made from encumbered property. This excludes the view of Nimukei Yosef and Rashbam. In Hagahot Darkei Moshe I proved that this applies specifically where the witnesses are before us and remember the time of the kinyan. If they are absent or no longer remember it, then even if the borrower admits that the kinyan occurred at that earlier time, and other witnesses saw the kinyan, those other witnesses did not create the original publicity. There is therefore concern that the actual witnesses forgot it, and collection cannot be made from purchasers from the earlier date unless a document in the creditor's possession records the date of the kinyan.

See above, beginning of siman 225. That is the correct reference. There the source of the rule of mistaken responsibility is discussed, both for land and movable property, with a distinction between property leaving the purchaser through unavoidable circumstances and otherwise. Those distinctions do not apply here to a loan. Even if an unavoidable event befell the borrower, every borrower received a benefit through the loan and remains responsible to return the lender's money. If he has nothing, the lender collects from encumbered property. A purchaser is different: he did not borrow the purchase money but exchanged it for land, and the seller already did his part by transferring the property. Therefore the seller is not necessarily responsible if the land later leaves the purchaser through an unavoidable event.
