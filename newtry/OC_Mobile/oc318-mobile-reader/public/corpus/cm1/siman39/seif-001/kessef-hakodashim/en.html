Seif 17, gloss. And he took possession of the collateral. A case arose where one person agreed to give another a sum under a permitted heter in exchange for the right to live in the latter's house. He paid part of the money and made a chazakah in the house, then wished to retract. If the heter was structured as an iska, one might argue that because the recipient of an iska can retract like a worker who may stop in the middle of the day, the arrangement is not a fully fixed rental resembling a sale. Although siman 39 indicates that chazakah ordinarily prevents retraction, perhaps the recipient's continuing right to withdraw from the iska would mean that the underlying money was never irrevocably committed. That argument is not correct. The Gemara in Bava Metzia concerning "your wheat was burned" recognizes an acquisition that binds one side even though the other retains a right to retract. Thus the payment can already be legally present and effective despite an asymmetric right of withdrawal. Moreover, the arrangement might have been structured as a permitted mortgage, which would plainly create a complete acquisition. Where the parties made a kinyan sudar that one will lend money to the other as an iska, the effect requires further study. If particular cash is present and the kinyan concerns lending that very money, it appears capable both of immediate acquisition and of creating an obligation, just as a kinyan can bind a person to lend or rent a specified movable item. If the money is not particularized or not yet in his possession, a kinyan sudar can nevertheless create a personal monetary obligation. The deposit half of an iska may be different. That half is not a rental benefit belonging to the recipient, and the recipient can ordinarily terminate his custodianship. Since the deposit itself gives him no independent right, a personal obligation to provide that deposit may not be enforceable in the same way. Once he can withdraw from the deposit portion, the loan portion may also fall because the two halves form one transaction. Conversely, just as the recipient can withdraw from the iska even after receiving the funds and compensation for his labor, perhaps the provider can also withdraw before actually delivering the money. Even if the customary labor compensation was already fixed through a kinyan of obligation, he may still be able to retract from placing the deposit itself. It is true that the recipient benefits from the deposit half because a larger capital base can yield greater profit, and when purchasing livestock combined capital can create efficiencies. Nevertheless, so long as the legal form remains a deposit, it is doubtful whether a binding obligation to provide it can be created. This entire question requires further study. At the end of siman 39, where a loan document has been written, we rule that the lender can still retract before handing over the money. According to authorities who disagree, perhaps the written instrument creates a personal obligation analogous to saying, "I owe you in a document." Such reasoning may not extend to a deposit. Likewise, where the kinyan involved a permitted housing arrangement conditioned on providing money through iska, no forbidden interest was intended. Even if the parties did not expressly say "iska," where everyone understood that a customary heter document would be executed, that understanding has legal significance. Still, if neither the labor payment nor the principal was actually delivered, the kinyan's precise effect requires further study. The Shach and Urim VeTumim write that if it becomes known that the borrower is not financially secure, the lender may retract. Perhaps this requires clear proof that the borrower was represented as secure. If the lender is in possession, he can raise that claim and the other side may be subject to the standard communal oath procedure. If the borrower is in possession, however, he can say, "I do not know that I became obligated to return anything now, for perhaps you were never particular about my level of security.
