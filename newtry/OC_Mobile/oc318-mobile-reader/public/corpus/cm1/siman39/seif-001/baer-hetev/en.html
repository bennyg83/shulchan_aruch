From encumbered property. The reason is that purchasers do not know of the loan, since witnesses without a document do not create publicity. Because ordinarily a borrower does not want it publicized that he owes money, the witnesses do not spread the matter. But if the borrower made a kinyan sudar before witnesses, we say that since he transferred the obligation generously, he certainly does not object to publicity, and presumably the witnesses publicized it. According to the Sema, this applies specifically when the original witnesses are before us and remember the date of the kinyan. Otherwise, even if the borrower admits that he made a kinyan sudar at that time, and even if other witnesses saw the kinyan, those others did not create the publicity. There is concern that the original witnesses may have forgotten the matter, and therefore collection from encumbered property cannot begin from the date of the kinyan unless the lender possesses a document recording that date. In that case he collects from that date. The Shach does not agree with this distinction. See there, where he discusses at great length whether lien by Torah law exists. He concludes: Nevertheless, I do not rely upon my proofs and determination, for a student like me cannot decide among such great mountains. At minimum, this law remains a doubt in the law.

Who made a kinyan. Hagahot Ashri writes that if a person says simply, "I hereby admit to you by kinyan before witnesses that I owe you," the kinyan does not take effect. The Shach writes that Bedek HaBayit challenges this and holds that the kinyan is effective even in such a case. The Bach reconciles Hagahot Ashri, and the practical law requires study. See Gidulei Terumah, gate 42, part 5, and above at the beginning of siman 40. All of this concerns collection from encumbered property. As against the debtor himself, it is obvious that an admission is effective even without a kinyan. Shach.
