Or if he admitted before witnesses, etc. Hagahot Ashri in Perek Zeh Borer writes that a kinyan helps with an admission only where the parties made a kinyan obligating him to pay by a specified time. But if he merely says, "I hereby acknowledge to you by kinyan before witnesses that I owe you," Sema writes that it is unclear what the kinyan takes effect upon, since he is not presently transferring anything. This follows the text before us and also appears to be Beit Yosef's text, because Hagahot Ashri concludes that we do not say the kinyan helps merely to prevent him from later claiming, "I paid before so-and-so and so-and-so, and they went overseas." On that reading, the conclusion is that a bare kinyan attached to an admission accomplishes nothing. According to Bach's reading, however, the text says the opposite: perhaps the kinyan does help by preventing the debtor from later making that payment claim. On that version, Hagahot Ashri does not conclusively rule that the kinyan is ineffective. Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit questions Hagahot Ashri more fundamentally. Why should the kinyan not take effect by creating a lien on property that was not encumbered by an ordinary oral loan? Bach answers that even an oral loan creates a Torah lien because we rule shi'abuda d'oraisa. Therefore the kinyan adds nothing. This is difficult. If so, even where the kinyan was made shortly after the original loan, once the money had already been given and the Torah lien already attached, the later kinyan should have nothing upon which to take effect. More importantly, Chazal presently removed collection from encumbered property for an ordinary oral loan. What difference does it make that Torah law once created a lien? Under the operative law, that lien has been removed for collection from purchasers. A subsequent kinyan can reactivate a property lien in a form recognized for collection. The correct explanation is that Hagahot Ashri follows its own position in Ketubot, at the beginning of Perek Af Al Pi. There, regarding the rule "if they made a kinyan before two, he need not say 'write,'" Hagahot Ashri says this does not fit Shmuel, who holds that omission of warranty from a document is a scribal error. According to Shmuel, one should still need an express instruction to write, because perhaps the obligor does not want the creditor to collect from encumbered property. This means Hagahot Ashri follows the view of Nimukei Yosef and other authorities that even though an ordinary kinyan is generally intended for writing, the kinyan itself does not automatically show consent to collection from encumbered property. Accordingly, Hagahot Ashri explains the rule that one who admitted before two and made a kinyan need not say "write" according to the authority who holds that omission of warranty is not a scribal error. On that view, writing the admission after the kinyan still does not create collection from encumbered property unless warranty was expressly undertaken. If so, Beit Yosef's question disappears within Hagahot Ashri's own system. The kinyan cannot be said to take effect by creating an encumbered-property lien, because under that very view no such lien is created through the kinyan and writing. Therefore Hagahot Ashri can fairly ask what legal object remains for the kinyan. But according to our halachah, that omission of warranty is a scribal error, and a kinyan can create a lien on property. We also rule that after a kinyan the document may be written. Accordingly, Beit Yosef's reasoning applies in practice: the kinyan takes effect through the property lien it creates. This is why the later poskim properly omitted Hagahot Ashri's restrictive ruling.
He collects from encumbered property, etc. Beit Yosef explains this on the basis that property liens are Torah law. Shach discusses at length in seif katan 2 that this remains a disputed legal question because major authorities disagree, and he inclines toward the view that the lien is Torah law, although he does not wish to decide against the dissenting authorities. I have already written that I do not presume to decide such a dispute merely by force of argument. Yet I do not know whether this is truly an appropriate case for one to say kim li, because Rif, Rambam, Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, Rosh, Tur, Ran, Raavad, and Maharshal all support the conclusion that shi'abuda d'oraisa. Shach did not see Rashba's novellae to Kiddushin, where Rashba states clearly that the halachah is that property liability is Torah law. Rashba also cites Raavad explaining that when Kiddushin says an oral loan collects from heirs because shi'abuda d'oraisa, this is the Gemara's own reason, not Rav Pappa's. Rav Pappa himself, at the end of Bava Batra, explains the rule through the policy concern of not closing the door before borrowers and therefore appears to hold the lien is not Torah law. The anonymous Gemara adds the Torah-law explanation because the final halachah is not like Rav Pappa on that conceptual point. This also accords with Raavad's glosses in Hilchot Erakhin. The responsum of Raavad cited in Baal HaTerumot does not prove the contrary. There he answers an opponent who argued that the lien is not Torah law and says, in effect, that if so then even a written loan would lack Torah attachment. He is rebutting the argument on its own premises. Rosh writes similarly. Ran in Perek Shevuat HaDayanim explicitly says that we rule shi'abuda d'oraisa. Tur likewise states this below in siman 88. Thus, even if Tosafot, Mordechai, and Baal HaItur could be read otherwise, the weight of authorities strongly supports the Torah-law view. In truth, even Tosafot do not clearly rule that the lien is rabbinic. Their discussions often aim only to reconcile Rav Pappa's statements. Rav Pappa says elsewhere that repayment of a creditor is a mitzvah and that the lien is not Torah law, while in Kiddushin he appears in a sugya that says oral debt collects from heirs because the lien is Torah law. Tosafot answer that Rav Pappa distinguishes between an ordinary loan and an obligation written in the Torah. That explains Rav Pappa. It does not establish that the final halachah follows Rav Pappa's conceptual position. Ramban, Rashba, and Ritva likewise explain Rav Pappa as holding shi'abuda lav d'oraisa yet rule against him because the halachah follows R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish. There is therefore no contradiction in saying Tosafot explain Rav Pappa's view without adopting it. A further indication arises from Bava Batra 125a. Rabbenu Tam explains the Gemara's challenge to Rav Nachman, who says that if heirs collected land in payment of their father's debt the firstborn does not take a double share in it. The Gemara challenges this from the rule that a creditor of the deceased can later collect that same land from the heirs. The question works only if both rules depend upon whether the creditor's lien is Torah law. If the rule allowing the creditor to collect from that land operated even when liens were merely rabbinic, then no contradiction would arise. Thus the Gemara links the status of collected land to shi'abuda d'oraisa. Since the practical law follows the rule that a creditor can collect land recovered by the heirs, there is strong support that the lien is Torah law. Rashba in Kiddushin seems to have this very proof in mind. Shach distinguishes between an obligation written in the Torah and an ordinary loan. But that distinction itself requires care. An obligation written in the Torah should not be stronger than an obligation whose lien was expressly written into a document. Rashba says in Gittin and Kiddushin that an express written lien resembles a Torah-written obligation. Indeed, Tosafot in Bava Batra 126a ask why Rav Pappa says a firstborn does not take a double share in a debt because the lien is not Torah law, when Rav Pappa elsewhere says a Torah-written obligation has Torah force. Their question makes sense because a written document with an explicit lien should at least equal a Torah-written obligation. This shows that even within Tosafot, a written express lien can have Torah character. One may ask from Rav Pappa's rule that we do not collect from minor heirs because minors are not subject to the mitzvah of repaying their father's debt. Commentators often say this follows because shi'abuda lav d'oraisa. If the lien were Torah law, why should the minors' personal mitzvah matter? But according to Tosafot's own distinction, this cannot be the whole explanation. For Torah-written obligations, where even they concede a Torah lien, Rav Pappa should then permit collection from minor heirs. Yet the Gemara's rule concerning appraisal of heirs' property does not distinguish ordinary debt from damages, valuations, redemption of a firstborn, and similar Torah-created obligations. The correct explanation is different. Even if shi'abuda d'oraisa, the primary obligation rests upon the borrower himself. Torah obligates him to pay, and beit din compels performance. The debtor's property functions as guarantor. Minor heirs are not personally obligated in mitzvot. When the principal debtor before beit din is not himself subject to the mitzvah, the attached property does not necessarily become collectible, just as a guarantor is not collected from where the primary obligation is unavailable in the relevant structure. Thus Rav Pappa's statement about minor heirs does not prove that he rejects Torah liens in every category. This weakens Shach's principal argument. There is an even stronger proof from Perek Yesh Bechor. R. Meir and R. Yehudah dispute brothers who divided their father's estate before paying the five sela'im of pidyon haben. R. Meir exempts them; R. Yehudah says the assets were already obligated. The Gemara explains that the case involves only five sela'im and turns on whether brothers who divide are treated as purchasers and whether an oral debt collects from purchasers. We rule like R. Yehudah that the assets were already obligated, and we also rule that brothers who divide are like purchasers. If an oral loan could never collect from purchasers because the lien itself were not Torah law, then every time heirs divided an estate, their father's oral creditors should lose completely. No beit din in Israel has ever understood the law that way. Once heirs divide, they are treated as purchasers for certain purposes, yet oral creditors still collect from inherited assets. This is a powerful indication that the underlying lien is Torah law and that the rabbinic limitation against collecting an oral loan from ordinary purchasers is a protection for market purchasers, not a destruction of the lien itself. Ramban struggles elsewhere to explain why a creditor can collect from heirs after partition despite the rule that brothers who divide are purchasers. He does not say that division simply destroys oral debt. The most coherent structure is this. Rav Pappa holds shi'abuda lav d'oraisa. On his theory, oral debt should not collect from heirs by strict law. Chazal allowed collection from heirs to avoid closing the door before borrowers, but did not impose that enactment upon ordinary purchasers. If brothers who divide are treated as purchasers, Rav Pappa's model would tend to leave them under the Torah baseline and exempt them. R. Yehudah disagrees because he holds shi'abuda d'oraisa. Under Torah law the debt would attach even against purchasers. Chazal protected ordinary purchasers because oral debt lacks publicity and otherwise commerce would be harmed. But brothers dividing an inheritance are not the kind of market purchasers for whose protection that enactment was made. There is no comparable concern that innocent outsiders relied upon public records and paid value. Thus the Torah lien remains effective against them. This explains why R. Yehudah says the assets were already obligated and why we rule like him. It also reconciles the practical rule that oral creditors collect from heirs even after partition. Maharshal in Bava Kamma leaves the combination of rulings difficult because we rule like R. Yehudah, Rav Pappa, and the view that brothers are purchasers. Maadanei Melech likewise gives strained answers. According to what I have written, there is no contradiction. We do not follow Rav Pappa's conceptual premise that the lien is merely rabbinic. We follow his practical rule that oral debt does not collect from ordinary purchasers, but for a different reason, namely the rabbinic protection of purchasers where no public notice exists. This also explains why the anonymous Gemara in Kiddushin can give shi'abuda d'oraisa as the reason oral debt collects from heirs even though Rav Pappa himself elsewhere gives the policy reason of not closing the door. The Gemara's reason reflects the final halachah, not Rav Pappa's personal theory. One could attempt a different defense of the rabbinic-lien view. Perhaps Chazal enacted collection from heirs to avoid closing the door, and although brothers after division are technically purchasers, Chazal still treated them as heirs for this purpose because the same lending policy applies. The rule in Bechorot that oral debt does not collect from divided brothers could then be limited to a Torah-written obligation such as pidyon haben, where the policy of encouraging loans is irrelevant. But this does not work well. The Gemara itself says the dispute turns on Rav Pappa's rule that an oral debt does not collect from purchasers. If the entire heir rule were a special lending enactment and the pidyon haben obligation were excluded because it is not a loan, then the classification of the brothers as purchasers would not explain the dispute as stated. Moreover, if Chazal applied a broad "do not close the door" enactment to all heirs but not to purchasers, we would still need to explain why they treated divided brothers differently from other heirs in one category but not another. That creates an artificial "lo plug" structure unsupported by the sugya. The Torah-lien approach is far cleaner. There is also a difficulty for Rif and Tosafot if one says Rav Pappa himself concedes Torah liens only for Torah-written obligations. The Gemara in Bechorot concludes that R. Meir and R. Yehudah either dispute Rav Pappa or dispute whether brothers who divide are purchasers. We rule like R. Yehudah and like the view that brothers are purchasers. That would seem to force rejection of Rav Pappa. If Tosafot's interpretation were that Rav Pappa himself says Torah-written obligations have Torah liens, then R. Yehudah's pidyon haben case should actually align with Rav Pappa, not oppose him. The sugya's formulation becomes difficult unless we distinguish Rav Pappa's own conceptual opinion from the anonymous Gemara's final explanation. This again supports Ramban, Rashba, and their colleagues. Maharshal asks another difficulty. Later in Bechorot, R. Yehudah says that where a father and son both require redemption, the son's redemption can take priority because a Torah-written obligation collects from encumbered property. How can the earlier sugya say R. Yehudah might agree with Rav Pappa that oral obligations do not collect from purchasers? Tosafot answer that the earlier Rav Pappa framework concerns ordinary oral obligations, while a Torah-written obligation differs. But then, why does the earlier pidyon haben case depend upon Rav Pappa at all, since pidyon haben itself is Torah-written? One can resolve this according to the Torah-lien model by saying the two disputes are not identical. The later sugya concerns whether a Torah-written obligation has the full status of a written document for collection even from ordinary encumbered property. The earlier sugya concerns brothers dividing inherited assets, where ordinary purchaser-protection policy may or may not apply. R. Yehudah can say: even if ordinary oral debt does not collect from outside purchasers, divided heirs are not protected because the underlying Torah lien remains and the market-protection reason does not apply. In the later case, he can additionally hold that a Torah-written obligation is strong enough to collect even from full encumbered property. Thus the two statements are compatible. Alternatively, one can say R. Yehudah argues to R. Meir on R. Meir's own assumptions: even if you do not treat the Torah-written obligation as a full written lien, at least admit that divided brothers should remain liable as heirs or because the lien attaches by Torah law. This is often the structure of Tannaitic debate. There is another possible establishment of the later father-and-son redemption case that avoids making Torah-written obligations identical to written loans in every respect. Suppose the father owned property at the time of an earlier written creditor's loan, later lost other assets, and then acquired new property after the son's birth. If he redeems himself first from the later-acquired property, the earlier written creditor may be able to take the originally owned property and leave nothing for the son's redemption. If he redeems the son first from the later-acquired property, his own earlier pidyon obligation can then reach the older assets because a Torah lien existed before the later creditor in the relevant order. This kind of priority structure can explain the later sugya without saying every Torah-written obligation collects identically from purchasers. The details are complex, but they show that the earlier conclusion does not force abandonment of shi'abuda d'oraisa. The central point remains: the body of the halachah and the majority of Rishonim support Torah liens. However, one major question raised by Maharshal remains: how can the Gemara say that R. Yehudah either disagrees with Rav Pappa or disagrees with the classification of divided brothers as purchasers, when later R. Yehudah seems to hold that a Torah-written obligation collects from encumbered property? Tosafot themselves raise two questions and answer principally that R. Yehudah's rejection of Rav Pappa is limited to Torah-written obligations. Their first question still appears difficult if one reads the earlier passage too mechanically. I believe the answer is that the later statement itself can be fitted into more than one of the earlier alternatives. If the earlier dispute is framed through Rav Pappa, then R. Yehudah can say that in this Torah-written obligation, shi'abuda d'oraisa gives stronger collection. If the earlier dispute is framed through whether divided brothers are purchasers, then the later priority case can be established through the timing and character of the assets without requiring ordinary purchaser collection. Thus the two Gemara establishments need not be identical. More fundamentally, if R. Meir and R. Yehudah already disputed the exact same proposition twice, one of the discussions would be redundant. It is more plausible that the later dispute refines the strength of Torah-written obligations, while the earlier dispute concerns the status of divided heirs. R. Yehudah can therefore say to R. Meir: according to me the Torah lien itself is enough, and according to your assumptions you should at least admit that these heirs are not ordinary protected purchasers. This resolves much of the difficulty. I have written at some length because shi'abuda d'oraisa is a major foundation in civil law and many later rules depend upon it. In my view, after weighing the authorities and the Talmudic structure, one should not move even slightly from the conclusion that property lien is Torah law. The majority of major poskim say so expressly, and the practical rules concerning heirs after partition are exceedingly difficult on the opposite view. Even where we rule that an oral debt does not collect from ordinary purchasers, that is a rabbinic protection based on lack of publicity, not proof that no Torah lien exists. There is an important limitation. The Torah lien primarily attaches to land. Movables do not ordinarily become subject to the same enduring Torah lien. At first glance this is difficult because the verse from which shi'abuda d'oraisa is derived speaks of the lender entering to take a pledge, which sounds like movables. Ramban in Bava Batra and Rashba at the end of Kiddushin indeed imply that conceptually Torah lien can relate to movables as well. Yet the majority of poskim do not treat movables as Torah-encumbered property collectible from heirs or purchasers, and no rule of priority ordinarily applies to them. The reason appears to be reliance. A lender does not rely upon movable property as enduring security because the borrower can hide it, consume it, sell it, or remove it easily. We therefore regard the lender as having extended the loan without reliance upon those movables. It is as though he waived any enduring lien upon them at the outset. The Torah structure of lien can apply in theory, but where the parties clearly do not rely upon the asset as security, the lien does not attach in the operative manner. This reasoning also explains slaves. Although slaves are compared to land in several laws, they are movable in the practical sense. The owner can remove them from place to place, and a creditor cannot safely rely upon them. Therefore, for purposes of enduring Torah lien, they resemble movables more than immovable land. This resolves Shach's objection to Rosh and Maharshal. Shach notes that they often rule shi'abuda d'oraisa, yet Rosh at the beginning of Bava Kamma says a creditor does not collect slaves from minor heirs because the lien is not Torah law. There is no contradiction. Land, which cannot be hidden or removed, is subject to Torah lien. Slaves, which can be moved and disposed of, are not subject to the same automatic reliance. The same point appears in Tosafot Bava Kamma 12a, where Ri explains Rav Nachman's rule concerning slaves through the absence of Torah lien. If Rav Nachman elsewhere appears to accept Torah lien for land, the distinction is exactly as stated. There is an additional reason concerning slaves. An owner can free a slave at any moment and thereby remove the slave from a creditor's lien. If the creditor truly owned a full Torah property right in the slave, the borrower should not be able to extinguish it unilaterally. The fact that emancipation can defeat the lien shows that the creditor's attachment is inherently weaker. This resembles the sale of a debt, where the original creditor's ability to forgive the debt shows that the purchaser did not acquire the underlying obligation with complete property ownership. So too, a creditor's attachment to a slave cannot be a fully vested Torah lien comparable to land. Land is different. Once land is subject to a creditor's lien, the debtor cannot simply consecrate it with ordinary monetary sanctity in a way that defeats the creditor, nor can he erase the lien by unilateral act. The creditor's attachment is therefore structurally complete. Thus, even while maintaining shi'abuda d'oraisa as the general rule, slaves and ordinary movables can remain outside its strongest application. This distinction removes the supposed contradictions and accords with the practical halachah. A further proof arises from the exact way the Gemara in Bechorot formulates the disagreement. The conclusion says that everyone can agree with the principle that five whole sela'im are required and not five half-sela'im. The dispute is then tied either to Rav Pappa's rule that an oral loan does not collect from purchasers, or to R. Asi's rule concerning the status of brothers after division. If R. Yehudah agreed both that brothers are purchasers and that an oral loan never collects from purchasers because there is no Torah lien, there would be no basis at all for his ruling that the assets were already obligated. Yet that is his ruling and the halachah follows him. Therefore one of the underlying assumptions must change. According to the explanation advanced here, R. Yehudah rejects Rav Pappa's conceptual premise. The debt lien exists by Torah law. The rule protecting ordinary purchasers is a later enactment for the sake of commercial security. Divided brothers are not ordinary market purchasers, so the enactment does not protect them in the same way. This is not merely a technical answer to one sugya. It explains a wide range of inheritance cases that otherwise become very difficult. Suppose a father dies owing an ordinary oral debt and his children immediately divide the estate. If partition converted them fully into ordinary purchasers for every purpose, then the creditor would lose as soon as they divided. No halachic system treats division as such an easy device to defeat the father's creditor. The reason is that their "purchase" status governs internal allocation between heirs, but does not erase the Torah lien already resting upon the estate. Only an outside purchaser who paid value in reliance upon the absence of a public document receives the special protection. This distinction is fundamental. Ramban's discussion of brothers as purchasers is also consistent with it. He explains that they divide subject to an implied condition that one brother should not lose everything while the others retain their shares when a creditor later collects. That presupposes the creditor's right continues after division. Had division automatically destroyed an oral debt, there would be no reason for such a condition. One might object that the Gemara sometimes treats heirs who divide exactly like purchasers. That is true for matters whose legal consequence depends upon title allocation between the heirs. It does not prove they receive every rabbinic market protection granted to strangers. The same word "purchasers" can operate differently depending on the purpose of the law. This is common in halachah. The purchaser-protection rule concerning oral debt is based on lack of public notice. An outsider has no reasonable way to know of an oral obligation and may pay full value innocently. An heir, by contrast, takes from his father without paying market value. The concern of protecting commercial reliance is therefore far weaker. There is no injustice in telling him that the property he inherited remains subject to his father's preexisting Torah debt. This is the conceptual heart of R. Yehudah's view. It also explains why the anonymous Gemara in Kiddushin says shi'abuda d'oraisa in connection with collection from heirs. If collection from heirs were merely a policy enactment to encourage lending, the distinction between heirs and purchasers would be harder to justify after partition. But if the Torah lien exists and only purchasers receive a special protection, the whole structure becomes natural. Shach relies heavily on the phrase "repayment of a creditor is a mitzvah" to infer that Rav Pappa rejects Torah lien and that we should therefore follow him as a later Amora. Even if Rav Pappa indeed rejects Torah lien, that does not decide the halachah. The question is which Amoraic and anonymous sugyot were accepted by the Rishonim. Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, Ran, and others expressly say we do not follow Rav Pappa on this conceptual point. The anonymous Gemara's explanation and the practical rulings of R. Yehudah carry greater weight. Thus the fact that Rav Pappa is later does not overcome the structure of the settled sugya and the broad consensus of authorities. One should also note that "shi'abuda d'oraisa" does not mean every debt automatically has the same public collection power as a written document. The Torah lien and the evidentiary form of the debt are separate questions. A written document creates publicity and allows purchasers to know that the property is encumbered. An oral debt lacks that publicity. Chazal therefore protected purchasers from oral debt even though the Torah lien conceptually exists. This is why one can simultaneously say: The lien is Torah law. An oral loan does not collect from ordinary purchasers. An oral loan does collect from heirs. And brothers who divide remain liable despite being treated as purchasers for other laws. There is no contradiction once the rabbinic market-protection enactment is separated from the Torah lien itself. This also answers the suggestion that perhaps an oral loan should collect even from a purchaser who actually knew about it. The enactment protecting purchasers was stated categorically. Once Chazal said an oral debt does not collect from purchasers because it lacks public form, they did not make the law depend on proving each buyer's actual knowledge. This is similar to other market enactments where the rule is formal rather than reconstructed case by case. Therefore, the fact that a particular purchaser knew does not prove the Torah lien was absent. It proves only that Chazal applied one uniform purchaser rule. This also helps with the distinction of Torah-written obligations. A Torah-written obligation can be more public in law, but not necessarily in fact. R. Yehudah may still treat it as stronger because Scripture itself creates the obligation and lien in a way analogous to an express written document. But that does not mean every Torah-written obligation must collect from every purchaser in precisely the same manner as a formal shtar. Different sugyot can distinguish between the existence of the lien, its priority, and the rabbinic protection of purchasers. The same conceptual separation is needed concerning a firstborn's double share in a debt. When a father dies holding a debt, the question whether the firstborn takes a double share in collected land can depend upon whether the father's right was considered sufficiently "possessed" at death. If the lien is Torah law, land collected later can be viewed as a realization of an existing property right. If the lien is only rabbinic, the collection is more easily viewed as a later-acquired asset and therefore merely expected. This is why the sugyot connect the firstborn question with shi'abuda d'oraisa. It is not merely an incidental association. The same underlying concept controls whether the creditor had a present property attachment before collection. This further confirms the Torah-lien position. The discussion of movables requires one more refinement. I wrote that the lender does not rely upon movables because they can be hidden or removed, and therefore it is as though he waived enduring lien upon them. This does not mean no Torah obligation can ever attach to a movable object. A pledge actually delivered to the creditor, or specific property expressly designated and transferred into a stronger security arrangement, can certainly create significant rights. The point is only that ordinary general shi'abud over the debtor's estate does not rest upon everyday movables in the same enduring way as land. The creditor cannot assume those objects will remain available. That is why there is ordinarily no priority among creditors in movables and why heirs may not be bound through them under the original law. The Geonic enactment expanding collection from movables of heirs is therefore understandable as an enactment responding to changed economic reality. In later commercial societies, wealth increasingly consisted of movables and money rather than land. Chazal's and the Geonim's later enactments can extend practical collection without proving the original Torah lien applied identically to all categories. This historical-economic distinction fits the Rishonim well. Slaves are especially illustrative. On one hand, Torah compares them to land for several rules. On the other hand, from the creditor's perspective they are unstable security. The owner can move them, sell them, and most importantly free them. The power of emancipation demonstrates that the creditor never held a fully vested proprietary interest. If he did, the debtor could not erase it by a unilateral personal act. This is why Rosh can say shi'abuda d'oraisa regarding land yet deny the same strength regarding slaves. There is no inconsistency. A similar analysis applies to debts themselves. A creditor can sell a debt, yet the original lender can forgive it according to many authorities. That power of forgiveness shows that the buyer did not acquire the underlying obligation with complete property force. Whenever one party retains unilateral power to extinguish the supposed lien, the purchaser or creditor's right is structurally incomplete. This is why the analogy to slaves is strong. Land subject to an ordinary creditor lien cannot be made free of that lien by a mere declaration of the debtor. The debtor may sell it, but the creditor follows it to the purchaser where the law permits. He may consecrate it in some forms, but the lien can still break through the consecration. That is a true enduring property attachment. Thus the distinction between land and movables, and between land and slaves, is not an exception forced upon the theory. It follows from the nature of reliance and permanence that defines the Torah lien itself. For all these reasons, the ruling of Beit Yosef that collection from encumbered assets rests upon shi'abuda d'oraisa is not merely one opinion among equals. It reflects the dominant line of the Rishonim and best explains the interlocking Talmudic rules.
And if there are witnesses who made the kinyan, etc. Sema asks from below, siman 43, seif 16, where Mechaber says that if a kinyan was made, then even if the witnesses did not sign until much later the document is valid. This seems to follow Rosh that writing must ultimately occur. If the witnesses never write, the kinyan alone would not suffice for collection from encumbered property. Perishah in siman 43 adds several difficulties against Tur. Tur follows Rosh in Bava Batra, where Rosh says: if you know the day of the kinyan, write the date of the kinyan, and the document is not considered antedated because, once the document is written, the witnesses publicize the lien from the time of the kinyan. This sounds as though later writing is essential. Yet in siman 51 Tur cites Rambam that one witness signed in a document and one witness to a kinyan can join to create the status of a written loan, even though the kinyan witness says the lender never came to ask him to write the document. Likewise, in siman 70 Tur treats a kinyan as a written loan. And in siman 88, if a debtor admits fifty with a kinyan and denies another fifty orally, the kinyan fifty is treated as though it were written and therefore does not create an ordinary partial-admission oath. These sources imply that kinyan alone, without later writing, can create the relevant written-loan effect. Perishah answers that Rosh requires later writing only because if no document was ultimately written, perhaps the witnesses forgot to publicize the kinyan. But if the witnesses are presently before us and testify that they did publicize it from the time of the kinyan, everyone agrees it works. Thus, in the other cases, the witnesses are before beit din and say the publicity existed. Perishah himself says this is strained because Rosh should have stated such an important distinction expressly. There is an additional difficulty from Even HaEzer siman 114. Tur writes concerning one who undertakes to support his wife's daughter: if he made a kinyan or wrote a document, she collects from encumbered property. This plainly treats kinyan alone as sufficient, without any mention of later writing or of the kinyan witnesses still being present. That is difficult for Sema's explanation. The explanation itself is also hard. If Rosh's case assumes the kinyan witnesses are no longer available, how do we know a kinyan occurred? If other witnesses now testify that they saw the kinyan, the debtor should in any event be believed to say he repaid, as explained below in seif 5 where witnesses other than the original kinyan witnesses testify to the transaction. If repayment can be claimed even within the original period according to the relevant view, how could one collect from purchasers? Even Sema, who elsewhere does not automatically plead repayment for purchasers where there is no defect, must concede that here the very absence of a document is itself a defect giving force to the repayment concern. Thus, if the debtor himself would be believed to claim repayment, certainly purchasers should be protected. We must therefore be speaking of the original kinyan witnesses themselves appearing and testifying. But then Sema's explanation collapses. Those very witnesses know whether they publicized the kinyan. At first I thought Rosh's reason might differ from Nimukei Yosef. Perhaps the issue is not that publicity disappears if no document is later written, but that failure to take a document creates a repayment concern. This could explain Even HaEzer 114, where the obligation may still be within its term and repayment is not plausible, and siman 51, where one written witness already prevents a simple repayment claim according to Rambam. Still, siman 88 remains difficult, unless one establishes it within the term, which is forced. More importantly, if Rosh truly required later writing as Nimukei Yosef does, Tur should have stated that explicitly, especially because many authorities disagree and Tur normally records his father's distinctive position. Therefore it appears clear that the text of Rosh should be read differently. Our printed text says, "It is not antedated, because once the document is written the witnesses publicize from the time of the kinyan." I believe one letter should effectively be read so that the meaning is: "It is not antedated because the document may be written, for the witnesses publicize from the time of the kinyan." The point is not that actual later writing creates retroactive publicity. Rather, because a standard kinyan stands for writing, the witnesses are authorized from the moment of kinyan to write a document whenever necessary. This makes the kinyan itself publicly significant from that moment. Now Rosh's statement becomes coherent. If no document is ever written, there is no antedating question at all. Collection rests upon the witnesses to the kinyan, who can say when it occurred. The novelty is that if a document is written much later but bears the original kinyan date, one might call it falsely antedated because the paper was actually written months later. Rosh answers that it is not false. Since the kinyan was destined for writing and the witnesses began publicizing from that day, using the kinyan date accurately reflects the effective public lien. Tur and Mechaber below in siman 43 can be read similarly. They say that where a kinyan was made, even if the document was not signed until much later it is valid. They do not mean later signature is necessary for the lien to exist. They mean even if the paper itself was created later, dating it from the kinyan does not make it antedated. Without any document, there is simply no antedating issue. There is a second way to explain Rosh that yields the same conclusion. Rosh says the witnesses publicize from the time of kinyan "because an ordinary kinyan stands for writing." Why does that fact create publicity? Because at every moment after the kinyan the witnesses are ready to write the document without needing a new instruction. The creditor can come at any time and receive it. The transaction is therefore treated, for purposes of publicity, as though the document is already capable of immediate existence. The essential publicity derives from this standing authorization, not from actual later writing. This resolves an apparent contradiction in Rosh. In Bava Metzia, regarding a found loan document, the Gemara asks how one may write a document for a borrower when the lender is not present. Perhaps it was written in Nisan but the loan did not occur until Tishrei. The Gemara answers: in documents of kinyan. Rif explains that the kinyan itself solves the problem. Rosh objects: what difference does kinyan make? Once the document says that so-and-so borrowed one hundred, the property lien exists even without kinyan because shi'abuda d'oraisa. If the concern is publicity so purchasers can protect themselves, it is the signed document that creates the publicity, not the kinyan. At first glance this contradicts Rosh in Bava Batra, where he says witnesses publicize from the time of kinyan. There is no contradiction. In Bava Batra, the kinyan has publicity because it stands for writing. The witnesses are ready at every moment to produce the creditor's document. In effect, the kinyan functions as a presently available document. In Bava Metzia, however, the actual document was written for the borrower and is not being delivered to the creditor. Rosh says even a fully written document does not create normal creditor publicity when it remains with the borrower and is not authorized for delivery to the lender. If the document itself lacks effective publicity in that posture, the prior kinyan cannot create more publicity than the document for which it stands. Thus Rosh says there: the witnesses signed on the document create the publicity, not the abstract kinyan. But the relevant publicity exists only where the document is available to the lender. His two statements are perfectly consistent. Accordingly, Rosh does not hold that failure to write later destroys the original publicity. The kinyan itself creates publicity from its date because it is intended for writing. Actual delayed writing merely uses that original date lawfully. There is therefore no reason to multiply disputes among the poskim by attributing to Rosh the stricter view of Nimukei Yosef.
