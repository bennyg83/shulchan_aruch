Maharshal reads the Rosh as holding that liens are biblical; the Shach argues that the Rosh holds they are rabbinic in ordinary loans, based in part on the Rosh's statement that a creditor does not collect from heirs' slaves because slaves are treated as movables in rabbinic law. Yet even according to the Shach, a debt written in the Torah has a biblical lien. This creates difficulties from Bava Kamma, where the Gemara discusses collection for tort damages and nevertheless treats slaves like movables. The solution is that according to either view, the distinction between land and movables in collection is substantially rabbinic. According to the view that liens are not biblical, land itself is not biblically encumbered and the Sages created the hierarchy. According to the view that liens are biblical, movables too are biblically subject to collection, as Rashba writes, but the Sages limited ordinary reliance to land and withdrew collection from movables of heirs. In rabbinic terminology slaves are included with movables, both for disadvantage and advantage. Thus even where the underlying lien is biblical, the Sages' classification of collectible property places slaves with movables. This also resolves several famous questions concerning partial admission and the oath of heirs. Tosafot asks that according to the view of biblical liens, a partial admission involving land should be an admission of a land lien and thus exempt from oath. One answer is waiver of the lien or absence of land. But how then does the Torah's rule, "the oath of Hashem shall be between them and not between the heirs," ever apply if heirs' movables are not subject to the debt? Rashba's approach answers that biblically even heirs' movables are subject to collection; the limitation is rabbinic. Similar reasoning resolves questions involving the testimony of a widow about her husband's death and her ketubah. Even if a single witness is biblically believed to permit remarriage because the matter is likely to become known, that witness is not biblically believed for the monetary ketubah. Her collection follows the rabbinic ketubah condition, so the question of land testimony remains rabbinic. The broader dispute over liens also intersects with the rule of collecting enhancements from heirs and with explicit agreements to encumber property. In my view, even according to one who holds liens are biblical, a debtor cannot independently create a new partial property interest merely by saying he encumbers assets. A lien that exists by Torah law arises automatically from Torah law, much as inheritance operates automatically. The debtor's own attempted additional lien can fail under the principle that one cannot make a partial acquisition in something that is not fully transferred. This explains why Abaye and Mar Zutra can accept biblical liens yet still limit collection of contractual additions, such as enhanced land, from heirs. The Torah's automatic lien exists according to the Torah's measure, while the debtor's attempt to enlarge it does not necessarily create a new biblical property interest. Rava may disagree and allow a more expansive contractual lien. We rule like the majority against Rava on this point. This also explains why tort claimants may collect superior land from adult heirs where Torah law itself fixed that measure, while an ordinary creditor who merely stipulated for enhancement does not collect the enhancement from heirs. A person can create a new personal obligation even where none existed before, and the Torah lien then follows that valid personal obligation according to law. But he cannot necessarily manufacture an independent fractional property lien. According to the view that liens are not biblical, collection from the debtor himself may still occur through coercion to fulfill the mitzvah of repayment, not because his assets are inherently encumbered. Ramban explains that beit din may descend to the debtor's assets as the least burdensome form of coercion to perform the mitzvah. This distinction also illuminates charity and fixed interest. Charity can involve an enforceable obligation with property consequences, while fixed interest is returned under a personal mitzvah of "your brother shall live with you" and does not necessarily create an inheritable property lien. Many details remain dependent upon the competing authorities, but the central distinction between automatic Torah lien, rabbinic collection rules, and personal coercion resolves the major difficulties.
