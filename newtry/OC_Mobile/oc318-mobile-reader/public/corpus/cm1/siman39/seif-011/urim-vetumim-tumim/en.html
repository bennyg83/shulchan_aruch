We are concerned regarding collection from purchasers, but from the debtor himself he collects. Hagahot HaRosh states this. Shach asks: if the debtor never told the witnesses "write," is the writing not invalid as testimony "from their writing and not from their mouths"? He answers that Hagahot HaRosh may follow Rabbenu Tam, who permits written testimony where the witnesses are capable of oral testimony, but Mechaber is difficult because Mechaber does not generally follow Rabbenu Tam. This follows Shach's approach of making the entire issue depend upon the dispute between Rashi and Rabbenu Tam. According to what I explained above, there is no difficulty. Even according to Rashi, a writing prepared in proper documentary form can preserve the underlying oral testimony against the debtor, even if it does not acquire full document status for collection from encumbered property. There is another reason as well. Here we know the witnesses are truthful about the underlying event. The only uncertainty is whether the debtor authorized them to write. The Torah's rule against relying upon written testimony applies where we know the writing itself is the only form in which testimony is being given without authorization. But where the authenticated writing clearly records a true event and we simply do not know whether the debtor said "write," why should we create a doubt that defeats a claim whose substantive truth is otherwise established? Against the debtor himself, the witnesses' statement proves the debt. We need not search for a speculative defect in the manner of writing in order to deprive the creditor of money that we know is due. Against purchasers the matter is different. Even if the debt is certainly genuine, collection from their property depends on whether a full written lien was created. If the debtor never said "write," then there is no written-loan lien against purchasers. Since we are uncertain whether that authorization occurred, we cannot extract from purchasers out of doubt. Thus the distinction is sound. Against the debtor, we know the substantive right belongs to the creditor and the written form is at most a technical concern. Against purchasers, the very legal basis for taking their property is uncertain. This is why Hagahot HaRosh and Mechaber can say the creditor collects from the debtor but not from encumbered property without relying upon Rabbenu Tam.
