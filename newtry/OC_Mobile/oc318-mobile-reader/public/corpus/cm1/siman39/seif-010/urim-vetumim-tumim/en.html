If he has a written judgment in his possession, etc. According to Rambam, this case must concern a proceeding in which the defendant was summoned. Without the required summons, beit din may not write at all, even if it knows he has not paid. If so, why speak of a written judgment that was issued improperly? Are we assuming beit din erred and violated the law? And if the writing was unauthorized, it should be no better than worthless paper, so of course the debtor can say he paid. On the other hand, if the defendant was properly summoned, Rambam says the judicial proceeding itself is like a written loan and the debtor cannot say he paid even without possessing a written judgment. Shach asks this and gives no satisfactory answer. One possibility is that the existence of the written judgment resolves a factual uncertainty. Suppose we do not know whether the original proceeding involved the required summons. If the creditor now produces a formal judicial judgment, we apply the presumption that beit din acted properly. Had the defendant not been summoned in the required manner, the judges would not have written. Therefore the document itself proves, presumptively, that the procedural condition existed. The debtor cannot answer, "They never summoned me," and thereby recover his repayment defense. This explanation is possible but somewhat strained. I believe there is another reason. Rambam understands a judicial proceeding as analogous to a kinyan. The Gemara in Bava Batra says that a kinyan is like a judicial act, and Shach discusses this comparison below in siman 79. Now consider the law above in seif 5. If the original witnesses to the kinyan are not before us and other witnesses merely testify that they saw a kinyan, while the creditor has no document, the absence of a document impairs the creditor's position. The debtor can say he paid. Why? Because if the kinyan normally stands for writing, the creditor's failure to obtain and retain the expected document is suspicious. The same reasoning applies to a judicial judgment. Suppose the judges who issued the ruling are not before us, and other witnesses merely testify that a judgment was rendered. If the creditor has no written judgment, even though such judgments are ordinarily written, his failure to produce it creates an evidentiary defect. The debtor can therefore say he paid. In my view, even if the same judges now testify orally about the prior judgment, if enough time passed that the creditor should have obtained a written ruling and we do not know whether one was ever given, the debtor can still be believed to say he paid. This follows the rule that a person who pleads against a judicial act is not ordinarily believed, but only where the judicial obligation is one for which no separate writing is normally expected. Where the law and practice produce a written instrument, failure to produce that instrument can defeat the claimant. The classic example is a ketubah. A woman ordinarily cannot say, "I lost the ketubah but it still existed," and collect as though the document were present where the absence of the expected writing is legally significant. The same principle can apply to a judgment. Thus Rambam may require the creditor specifically to possess the written judgment. The issue is not that the writing itself creates the initial liability. The judicial act does that. The writing preserves the strong evidentiary form expected from such an act. If the writing is missing, the claimant's proof becomes impaired and repayment is credible. All the more so where the original beit din is absent and only other witnesses testify about the old ruling. This, in my view, is the correct practical rule.
And if they did not tell him, he is believed to say, "I paid," etc. This rule cannot follow Rambam and Mechaber's own approach. According to Rambam, if the defendant was not summoned in the required manner, beit din has no authority to write the judgment at all. Merely telling the debtor afterward that a judgment was written cannot repair that defect. Even if nonpayment is known, the writing is unauthorized. Conversely, if the defendant was properly summoned, then according to Rambam the judicial proceeding itself already has written-loan force. He is not believed to say he paid merely because he did not know the creditor had physically received a written copy. Once summoned, he knows beit din is authorized to write. This is no different from one who tells two witnesses, "Write," or one who makes a kinyan before two. He cannot later say, "I did not know you would actually write, so I paid without a receipt." The responsum in Hagahot Maimoniyot from which this rule comes therefore follows the other authorities, not Rambam. Those authorities hold that so long as no written judgment has actually been issued, the debtor remains able to say he paid. According to them, if beit din writes without informing the debtor, his payment claim remains credible because he had no reason to protect himself with a receipt against a document he did not know existed. Therefore they require the court to notify him before writing. Rama's wording is imprecise because it sounds as though this rule continues Mechaber's Rambam-based approach. It does not. It belongs to the dissenting system under which the debtor remains believed to say he paid until a written judgment is properly issued and brought to his attention.
And some disagree, etc. Regarding the claim "I paid" against a written judicial ruling, it appears clear that Raavyah agrees that where there is a formal written admission before three judges and the defendant was summoned, he is not believed to say he paid. The Gemara says that such an admission is sometimes written, and the implication is that it has the force of an admission before two with a kinyan. Likewise, Bava Batra 40a says a kinyan is like a judicial act. If even against a full judicial act one could always say "I paid," what would the comparison accomplish? Raavyah's own wording confirms the distinction. He explains that a written judgment differs from an ordinary evidentiary document because it is written primarily so the judges need not later say from memory, "We ruled for this one." It is not created chiefly as proof of the debt. That rationale applies specifically to a judgment. A formal written admission, by contrast, is created precisely as evidentiary proof of the admitted obligation. It resembles an admission before two witnesses whom the debtor instructed to write, and against such a document the debtor cannot simply say he paid. Sema, seif katan 26, understands Rama's gloss as equating a written admission with a judgment and therefore applies Raavyah's opinion to both. That is incorrect. As I explained above, all of seif 9 concerns a judicial ruling, not a formal admission. Otherwise the distinction between "you are liable to give" and "go give him" has no place, and Rambam and Mechaber's repetition of the two categories would be unnecessary. Thus Raavyah's dissent applies to a judgment. It does not follow that he allows a repayment claim against a proper written admission. Rama tries to reconcile Tur, who rules both that beit din should not write because perhaps payment already occurred and that according to Raavyah payment can be claimed against a written judgment. Rama answers that writing after payment would still involve degradation of beit din and an appearance of falsehood. I explained above another possible reconciliation. Raavyah may agree that repayment against a judgment is uncommon. A person who pays while the judicial ruling remains intact would ordinarily tear it or obtain a receipt. Therefore, if the debtor himself claims "I paid," we do not extract money from him, because the burden of proof remains on the claimant. But if the debtor dies, perhaps we do not plead this unusual payment on behalf of the heirs. If so, the court must not write a judgment after payment may have occurred, because the creditor might later present it against the heirs, who would be unable to assert that unusual defense. This can also explain Tur in siman 79, who says the debtor may be forced to pay a second time through a judgment in the creditor's possession. His words can refer to a case where the debtor dies and the claim is then made against heirs, for whom we do not plead an uncommon mode of payment. This explanation is possible, though somewhat strained. It also helps answer Beit Yosef's question against Raavyah. Raavyah writes that a judgment is not principally written as evidence because the judges themselves can testify whom they ruled for. Beit Yosef objects that only two judges can testify; the law that a judge cannot say "I ruled for this one" applies when only one judge remains or when the judges disagree. According to the explanation above, the practical concern of death remains. Chazal worry about death of one person even where they do not generally fear the simultaneous death of two. Moreover, many courts sign a judgment with only two of the three judges, and a dissenting judge may not sign at all according to Raavyah's approach. If one of the two signatories dies, the remaining single judge cannot independently establish the ruling. The written judgment therefore has a real preservation function even if it is not intended as the primary proof of the debt. Still, this is not the clearest explanation. The stronger point is that Raavyah distinguishes judicial rulings from ordinary proof documents because the court's own institutional record is not equivalent to a creditor's ordinary shtar. That is enough to preserve his distinction. Another indication that Tur truly follows Raavyah comes from Rosh. Rosh explains that a debtor is normally not believed to say "I paid" against an ordinary document because of the rule, "Why is your document in the creditor's hand?" That logic applies naturally only to a document intended as continuing proof of the debt. A judicial ruling is different. Its purpose is to record the court's decision, not necessarily to remain in the creditor's hand until payment as security against a repayment claim. Thus even if the creditor still holds it, the question "why is your document in his hand?" is weaker. This supports Raavyah. If Rosh disagreed and treated a written judgment exactly like an ordinary shtar, Tosafot and Rosh's entire reconciliation of the Gemara would be unnecessary. They could simply establish the statement "standing in judgment is like a written loan" as a case where an actual written judgment was issued. Instead they distinguish between purchaser collection and the debtor's repayment claim. That strongly implies that even with a written judgment, the personal repayment defense may remain available. Accordingly, Tur did not invent Raavyah's rule. It fits Rosh's conceptual structure. From this it also follows, as I explained above, that Tur's system permits collection from purchasers based on the judicial publicity even where the debtor himself can say he paid. Shach's attempt to avoid that conclusion does not fit the sources. There is an additional proof from Bava Metzia 16b. The Mishnah says, "All judicial acts are returned" when found. The Gemara labors to explain this as referring to writs of collection and similar documents rather than ordinary judicial rulings. Why not read it simply as a lost written judgment? If, according to Raavyah and Tur, the debtor can still claim repayment against such a judgment, then returning it would not necessarily harm him personally. But it could harm purchasers if the judgment has public-lien effect, or heirs if we do not plead the uncommon payment for them. Therefore the Gemara cannot simply return it without concern. This strongly supports the distinction I stated above. At the stage where R. Yirmiyah challenges R. Abbahu from the phrase "all judicial acts," one might ask why an ordinary judgment is not included. The answer is that at that stage the Gemara assumes that once a document has been judicially authenticated, repayment is not feared, because otherwise the instrument would have been torn or a receipt taken. Under that assumption, all the more so a judgment could be returned. After the Gemara rejects the broad reading and limits the Mishnah to particular judicial writs, the ordinary written judgment remains subject to the concern described. Thus the sugya is coherent. Rama and later authorities report different customs. Ir Shushan says the practice follows the view that a debtor remains believed to say he paid against a judgment. Shach reports the opposite. The practical distinction should depend on how the proceeding occurred. If the defendant was formally summoned through beit din, as is usual in most real judgments, then Rambam and Mechaber say the proceeding itself has strong written-loan status, even before an actual written judgment. Certainly once it is written, the debtor should not be believed to claim payment according to that major line of authorities. Therefore the common practice of not accepting "I paid" in an ordinary summoned judgment is well grounded. But where the parties came voluntarily without formal summons, Rambam and Mechaber themselves say the court should not have written in the same strong manner. According to Rambam, such a paper lacks the full judicial-document status. According to Tur and Raavyah, the debtor can say he paid. Thus, in an unsummoned case, the debtor certainly has strong authorities supporting the repayment claim. Since Rambam, Tur, and Raavyah all support him in one form or another, who can extract money from the possessor against these major authorities? The correct practical distinction is therefore: Where the judgment followed formal summons and a true judicial process, the debtor ordinarily cannot say "I paid." Where there was no summons and the paper was nevertheless written, the debtor can claim payment. This accords with the major authorities and resolves the apparent conflict in reported custom.
