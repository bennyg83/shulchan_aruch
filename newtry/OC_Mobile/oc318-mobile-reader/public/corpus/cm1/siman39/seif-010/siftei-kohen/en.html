The borrower is not believed to say, 'I paid,' against a judgment in the creditor's possession. Bedek HaBayit states that this is the view of the Rif and Rambam, and Maharl ibn Chaviv proves it at length. Mabit rules otherwise, but his proofs are unconvincing. His argument from the Rambam's ruling that a written judgment is not cancelled by shemittah actually points the other way: if the debtor could freely plead payment, the debt would still require substantial collection and resemble an oral loan. The Semag and Bartenura, who follow the Rambam, therefore also imply that a plea of payment is unavailable against a written judgment. Since the Rif and Rambam hold that even a written admission defeats a later plea of payment, all the more so does a true judgment. Attempts to explain their concern merely as fear of duplicate collection, while preserving the plea of payment, force their words from their plain meaning. If payment occurs after a judgment was written in the debtor's presence, he must take the judgment back or ensure its cancellation; otherwise he caused his own loss. The suggested distinction between a judgment written before the parties leave and one written later is likewise weak, and could readily have been addressed by dating both the ruling and its writing. The Tur's citation of Raavyah here does not prove that he accepts Raavyah against the Rambam. The Tur cites him to reinforce only the narrower point that before any judgment is written a defendant who leaves court may plead payment. The Tur's later ruling in siman 79 follows the Rambam that once a judgment is in the creditor's hand, payment cannot be pleaded. This reading also explains the Tur's phrase 'and even if they wrote him a judgment, Raavyah wrote,' which introduces a further opinion without adopting it. Numerous authorities support Maharam's ruling that one cannot plead payment against a written judgment: Rabbi Yerucham, the Ran, Rabbenu Chananel, Rabbi Ephraim, Baal HaIttur, Rivam, Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ran, Ramban in Milchamot and Chiddushim, Baal HaTerumot and the Raavad, Rabbi Yeshayah of Trani, and Mahari ibn Lev. Nimukei Yosef expressly states that one who pleads payment after a court document says nothing, for a document created by the court without the debtor's instruction still has the law of a document. Ramban likewise writes that once the court writes the ruling, the debtor cannot plead against it, and that a recalcitrant litigant's ruling may be written and delivered. Rashi's term adrakhta also means a judicial ruling authorizing collection, not a lesser paper against which payment remains available. Distinctions invented between different types of judgments have no basis in the Rishonim.
