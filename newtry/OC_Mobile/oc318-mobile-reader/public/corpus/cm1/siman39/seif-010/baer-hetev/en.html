I paid. See the Shach's lengthy discussion of this law. He cites many poskim who disagree with the Mechaber, but concludes that in his view the Mechaber's ruling is primary: a debtor is not believed to claim payment against a written judgment.
They said. Responsa Maimoniyot states that if the judges themselves say they did not tell him that a written judgment had been issued, he is believed to say he paid. This appears true even where the judges' signatures can be authenticated independently of their testimony. One might ask why this differs from witnesses who say, "Our document was written on trust," in siman 46, seif 37, where they are not believed if their signatures can be authenticated elsewhere. The distinction appears to be that only witnesses are subject to the rule that once they have testified they cannot retract. A beit din may clarify its own judicial act. Certainly here the judges are not completely reversing their original statement. Shach.
Matter. The Mordechai explains that a written judgment is not created as an instrument for collection, but only to preserve a record of what the judges decided. Therefore the debtor may not have been careful to take it back after paying. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that the first opinion appears primary, contrary to the Ir Shushan, who says the practice follows the dissenting view. The Shach says he does not know the source for such a custom and has never seen it practiced in the places where he lived. Perhaps the Ir Shushan means only where the judges do not inform the debtor that they wrote a judgment, in which case such practice would make sense.
