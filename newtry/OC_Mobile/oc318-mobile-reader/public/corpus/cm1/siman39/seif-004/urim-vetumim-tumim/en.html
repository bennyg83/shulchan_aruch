Return my money to me, etc. In Responsa Rosh, at the end of rule 57, the Rosh cites Rabbeinu Tam's view that in every type of document, whether a sale document or a loan document, even after a kinyan the transferor may object to the writing of a document. The other party, however, may say, "Since you object to the document, return my money," and we listen to him. The Rosh concludes that according to Rabbeinu Tam there is therefore no practical difference from the opposing view except in a gift or sale involving a kinyan, where the transaction may thereby be undone. We learn two points from these words. First, if the transferor does not return the money, the witnesses may write the document against his will. He cannot both retain the money and prevent the witnesses from writing. If this were not so, there would be a major difference between Rabbeinu Tam and the opposing authorities even in loan documents. If the money had already been spent or lost, according to the opposing view the witnesses could write the loan document and the lender would collect from encumbered property, while according to Rabbeinu Tam there would be no document and the lender would have to pursue the borrower personally without any lien on property. It is therefore clear that Rabbeinu Tam holds that if the money is not returned, the borrower's objection is ineffective and the witnesses write the document against his will. There is thus no practical difference in a loan, because if he returns the money, even the opposing view agrees that no one can prevent repayment. The difference arises only in a sale, where the seller wishes to return the money and cancel the sale: Rabbeinu Tam allows this, while the opposing authorities do not. Second, we learn that after making such an objection the debtor is not believed to say, "I repaid." If he were believed, there would again be a major practical difference: according to Rabbeinu Tam, once he objected he could later claim payment, whereas according to the opposing authorities, whose position is that the objection is disregarded and a document is written, he could not claim payment, because the lender would still hold the document. It follows that even according to Rabbeinu Tam he may not repay during this period without witnesses or other proof. He cannot prevent the witnesses from writing and then later claim that he paid. This is clear. Knesset HaGedolah records a dispute on this point between Mabit and Tzeror HaKesef. As I wrote there, the correct view is that of Tzeror HaKesef, that the debtor cannot say he repaid. This is also proved from the Rosh's language. Chavot Yair, responsum 230, writes that his own inclination is likewise that the debtor is not believed to claim repayment, but he hesitates to rule without proof. He did not notice that Tzeror HaKesef and Mabit had already debated the matter and that the Rosh provides a clear proof, for otherwise there would be numerous practical differences. I later reviewed Mabit's responsum and saw that his reason was that one may rely upon the authorities who hold that after every kinyan the debtor may claim repayment, and his case concerned a later period of time. In such circumstances he concedes the point stated above.
But the witnesses are not permitted to write, etc. The Shach infers from this that even if they write merely as a memorandum they may not do so, and he uses this as support for his view that a writing made without the debtor's consent is not testimony at all. This inference is not conclusive. The intent here is that the transaction was a documented loan and the borrower accepted the loan on that basis. However, Responsa Rosh, rule 65, law 3, discusses witnesses who wrote that Reuven had admitted after an accounting that he owed a certain amount to Shimon. Reuven claimed that they had written the document without his knowledge. The Rosh ruled that because it was written without his consent, it cannot collect from encumbered property, for there was no kinyan and it is not a loan document but merely a record of an admission. Nevertheless, for that purpose it is fully valid testimony establishing everything that he admitted before the witnesses. The Beit Yosef cites this as proof that according to the Rosh, even a writing produced without the debtor's consent remains testimony with the force of oral testimony. See below, siman 61, where the Mechaber cites this ruling briefly. The Shach, because he firmly holds that such a writing is no testimony at all, attempts to reject this proof. He says that the case there is different because the debtor does not deny what the witnesses wrote. His words are not understandable. If the document is merely a worthless shard according to the Shach, then we judge only on the basis of the debtor's present admission. Why then did the Rosh say that it cannot collect from encumbered property because there was no kinyan? It would be worthless for collection in any event. And why did the Rosh conclude, "Nevertheless, for this purpose it is valid testimony to establish everything he admitted before them"? If the debtor now admits the matter, there is no need for their testimony; and if he denies it, according to the Shach he should be believed because the writing is worthless. It is therefore clear that the Rosh regards the writing as a valid evidentiary record for the purpose of preventing him from saying, "That never happened," like ordinary oral testimony, even though it is not a loan document that can create a lien on encumbered property. This is as I wrote above, and the Shach's words are not understandable.
