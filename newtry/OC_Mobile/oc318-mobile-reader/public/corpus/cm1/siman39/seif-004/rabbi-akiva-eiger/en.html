[Shach, letter 9] However, etc. Note: Urim writes that this is unrelated to the dispute between Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam; see there. One may explain that the Shach's reasoning is that whenever the document is written in proper documentary form, it has the status of a deed, and immediately upon the witnesses' signing it, their testimony is regarded as having been investigated in beit din, provided that at the time of signing it constituted valid testimony. This fits Rabbeinu Tam, according to whom written testimony can constitute testimony; the signatures therefore have the status of testimony, and by signing, their testimony is completed and the document becomes a valid deed. According to Rashi, however, this would not seem to apply, because there was never any oral declaration of testimony, since it is testimony from writing. It is effective only with the consent of the obligor, because the witnesses act as his agents. The general principle would thus be that whenever, at the time of signing, the act has the status of testimony, it is regarded as though the testimony was investigated in beit din. Nevertheless, even according to Rabbeinu Tam there is room for analysis. Although written testimony may be testimony, at the time of signing it is not effective as testimony because it was done outside the presence of the litigants, and likewise they may have signed at night. We must therefore say that because it has the status of a deed, it is superior to an oral declaration of testimony and does not require all the laws of testimony. If so, it should likewise be effective according to Rashi. See further.
