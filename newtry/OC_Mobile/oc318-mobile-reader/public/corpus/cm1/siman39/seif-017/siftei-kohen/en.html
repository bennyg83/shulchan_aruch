A borrower, etc.; he may retract, etc. Ri Migash and Ramban disagree. The Mechaber himself cites their position below at the end of siman 238, and his wording there implies that if it is established that the document was written with the lender's consent, the lender cannot retract. It is therefore somewhat difficult that he rules here without qualification. As to the practical law, Beit Yosef writes that reason inclines to Rashba, particularly because Rashba brings proofs for his view. The Bach rejects Rashba's proof from chapter HaIsh Mekadesh, Kiddushin 46b, arguing that everyone there agrees that money lent is given for expenditure, whereas our accepted law is that even if the very coins remain intact in the borrower's possession, he need not return those same coins. The Bach was not precise. Rashba's principal proof is from the authority there who holds that the borrower must return the very coins. Even the other authority, whose view we follow, says that he need not return them only because a loan is given for expenditure. Without that principle he would not disagree, even though the borrower's property became subject to the lender from the moment of the loan, since liens are of Torah origin. It follows that when the money is still in the lender's possession, where the rationale that a loan is given for expenditure does not apply, the lender does not become obligated to advance it merely because the borrower's property has already become subject to him. Gidulei Terumah, gate 48, part 1, asks what proof Rashba can bring from HaIsh Mekadesh, since there no document or other obligating instrument exists, whereas here the debtor's assets became subject through the document. This too is inaccurate, and he overlooked Responsa Rashba, siman 1054. Rashba there expressly addresses the objection: Since we hold that liens are of Torah origin, in the Gemara's case too the borrower's property became subject from the time of the loan even without a document. Yet one opinion still allows the lender to retract after the money came to the borrower. The dispute there certainly is not about whether liens are of Torah origin. Therefore, an existing lien does not by itself obligate the lender to part with his money. Nevertheless, it appears to me that this particular proof can be rejected. Perhaps there the lender is not trying to retract against the borrower's will, because the borrower also wishes to repay, as in the comparable law of a deposit discussed there. The dispute may be only whether the lender can demand return of those very coins while they still exist. In that setting one cannot say that the borrower's assets remain subject against his will. Rashba also proves his view from the rule that a document may be written for the borrower even when the lender is not present. The Bach answers that in the era of the Mishnah the heset oath had not yet been instituted, whereas today, despite the institution of heset, we do not abolish the Mishnah's rule. There was no need for this strained answer. Even today, if the lender truly promised to lend, he will truthfully admit it. If he did not promise, he can say that the document was written without his agreement and take a truthful heset oath. He cannot escape a heset oath in any event, as the Bach himself concludes. Gidulei Terumah makes the same point, and it is straightforward. Since Rashba's proofs are not conclusive, as I have explained, and the Mechaber himself later cites the contrary view of Ri Migash and Ramban, this law initially appears to be a doubt in practical law. Gidulei Terumah likewise implies that because the lender is in possession, money cannot be taken from him against the view of Rashba and the Tur, which means the matter is treated as doubtful. Responsa Mabit, part 1, simanim 200 and 213, likewise rules that the possessor can say kim li like Ramban and those who agree with him. Great authorities also disputed with Mabit there concerning one who sold wool for a fixed amount, wrote the buyer a document for the price, then the buyer died and the buyer's creditor seized the wool from the seller's possession. Mabit wrote that even Rashba would concede with respect to movable property. In my humble opinion this is certainly incorrect, as can be proved below from the law of one who borrows an axe. Those who disagreed with Mabit wrote conversely that even Ramban would concede regarding movable property. Their conclusion appears correct in the following sense: even according to Ramban, the seller is not specifically obligated to give those particular movable items. He may satisfy the obligation with other movable property of his choosing, as I prove below from the borrowed axe. Therefore, the buyer's creditor who seized that particular wool seized it unlawfully. It further appears primary that if the borrower dies, the lender may retract even according to Ramban. The same is true if circumstances arise in the interim that will cause the lender a loss, for example if the loan will no longer be secure. This is contrary to Mabit there and follows those who dispute him. Although their particular proofs are not compelling, reason supports them, because the lender presumably never agreed on such changed conditions. This is especially so because, even without this consideration, Beit Yosef already writes that reason inclines toward Rashba. Indeed, nowhere in the Gemara do we find that merely because one person's assets became subject to another, the latter thereby becomes obligated to lend him money. Upon further consideration, I conclude that Rashba's view should be accepted completely. My earlier suggestion that the borrower in HaIsh Mekadesh also wished to repay does not fit the plain sense of the sugya, nor Rashi's explanation there. Moreover, the Gemara immediately brings Rav Huna's rule concerning one who borrows an axe from his fellow: if he already chopped with it, he acquired the right to use it and the owner cannot retract, but if he has not yet chopped with it, he has not acquired that right and the owner may retract. That case necessarily concerns the owner seeking return of the axe during the agreed borrowing period against the borrower's will. The same is stated in Bava Metzia 99a: regarding retraction, if he used it the lender cannot retract, and if he did not use it the lender may retract. The law of the borrowed axe itself clearly supports Rashba. The Gemara there says that Rav Huna disagrees with Rabbi Elazar, who holds that just as meshichah was instituted for purchasers, so too it was instituted for custodians. Rabbi Elazar's view is our law because meshichah gives the borrower a completed right of use. But without that acquisition, even though the borrower already becomes liable for unavoidable accidents from the relevant time and his assets are thereby subject to the owner, Rav Huna would still allow the owner to retract. The Gemara itself distinguishes between liability and the right to retract. It asks whether Rav Huna means the borrower has not acquired liability for accidents, and answers that liability begins from the time of borrowing, so Rav Huna's rule must concern the owner's right to retract. Thus, even though the borrower's assets are already subject to the owner for accident liability, that lien does not compel the owner to continue furnishing the axe. One cannot limit the principle of Torah liens to loans of money. In many places the Gemara and poskim make clear that Torah liens apply to every monetary obligation, including damages and similar liabilities. Nor is it plausible to say that Rav Huna holds liens are only rabbinic. If that were the issue the Gemara should have said that his view conflicts with the authority who holds liens are of Torah origin, rather than saying that he conflicts with Rabbi Elazar. The Amoraic dispute over Torah liens is discussed at the end of Get Pashut, and this case would have been cited there had that been its basis. A careful reading of chapter HaShoel likewise makes clear that Rav Huna and Rabbi Elazar are not debating whether liens are of Torah origin. Rather, even assuming a Torah lien, the lien does not itself complete the lender's or owner's performance obligation. This is a clear proof for Rashba. I am surprised Rashba did not cite it directly from chapter HaShoel, although perhaps he included it within his proof from HaIsh Mekadesh, where Rav Huna's rule is also mentioned. It is also possible that he did not cite it because one could force an answer that although the lender may retract from lending that particular axe, he remains obligated to provide another axe of equivalent value because of the existing lien. The Gemara's expression that "the lender may retract" does not naturally support such a distinction, and Ramban and those who agree with him would apparently have to force such an explanation. Tosafot, the Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef in chapter HaShoel, as well as the Tur below in simanim 307 and 340, also hold that the borrower becomes liable for accidents from the time the owners withdraw, yet the lender may retract until the borrower definitively takes the item for use. Thus, even though the borrower's assets became subject from the earlier time, the lender may still retract. All the more so here. Rambam and the Mechaber disagree there regarding the precise point at which liability begins, but they do not disagree with the principle relevant here. Examine this carefully.
He may retract. Even if the document has already reached the lender, so long as the lender has not delivered the money he may retract. See the Tur and Beit Yosef. So writes Masat Binyamin in his work.
If the lender lost the document, etc. Darkei Moshe writes this in the name of Nimukei Yosef in chapter Get Pashut and says it is also implied by Maggid Mishneh, chapter 24 of Hilchot Mechirah. In truth, neither source says this. They discuss a receipt where the lender lost the original debt document and therefore must pay the cost of preparing a receipt, because otherwise the borrower would not pay. Since the receipt serves the lender's interest and the lender should have safeguarded his document, he bears its cost. The present rule is not stated there. Nevertheless, the present rule is correct on its own reasoning. See below, siman 54, seif katan 3.
