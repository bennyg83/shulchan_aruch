He may retract. Ramban holds that once the borrower's property became encumbered to the prospective lender through a kinyan, the lender can no longer retract from advancing the money, because in exchange for the lien he became obligated to lend. Rashba rejects this. He brings proof from Kiddushin, where a loan can remain subject to retraction even though custodial responsibility and property liability have already arisen, and from the rule that one who borrows an axe does not acquire the right of use until he actually begins using it, despite earlier custodial liability. At first glance, if a purchaser of land gives the seller a debt note for the purchase price, the note should act like money and acquire the land, because it creates a legal obligation to pay. But Rashba's approach shows otherwise. In kiddushin, giving collateral for a promised maneh does not effect kiddushin because "there is no maneh and there is no collateral." Tosafot and Rosh explain this because collateral cannot create an underlying obligation that does not yet exist. Rashba gives a different explanation: even where the promisor became personally obligated, so long as the actual money remains with him, it is still bound to him and has not become the recipient's money. Consequently, a debt note is not equivalent to cash for acquiring land either. The acquisition of property through money requires value that actually leaves the buyer's control. This is true even if property has become legally encumbered to secure his future payment. Therefore Rashba is consistent in allowing the lender to retract until the money is actually advanced. The Talmudic references to writing a note for the price do not prove otherwise, because they can concern a different category of obligation or a case where the debt has already been constituted through an effective monetary act.
And he took possession of the collateral. Rashba writes that if the lender actually entered and acquired possession of mortgaged land, he could no longer retract from lending, because by taking the mortgage he caused himself to become obligated to provide the money. But if the document alone was written and the lender had not yet acquired the land, no obligation arose. This follows Rashba's distinctive understanding of the rule, "there is no maneh and there is no collateral." According to him, a collateral arrangement can create personal liability, but it does not function as the purchase money itself while the promised money remains attached to the promisor. Therefore possession of the collateral can obligate the lender to advance the money even though it cannot itself serve as acquisition money in kiddushin. According to Tosafot and Rosh, however, collateral cannot create liability at all before an underlying debt already exists. The Shulchan Aruch applies that view in several places, including engagement securities, unless the parties transfer the body of the collateral itself. Therefore Rashba's ruling must be limited. Where the mortgage is actual land given for usufruct or in a form resembling rental, possession can create an independent benefit for which the lender becomes obligated to pay the corresponding consideration. But where movables or land are merely pledged as security for a loan not yet made, mere possession of the pledge does not, according to Tosafot and Rosh, obligate the lender to advance the money. This distinction was overlooked by some commentators.
