A borrower who came before beit din, etc. Shach asks why Mechaber here rules simply like Rashba, that the lender can retract before advancing the money, while below in siman 238 he cites Ramban that after the borrower has created the obligation the other party cannot retract. There is no difficulty. Many distinctions are possible between a sale and a loan. In a sale, once the seller cannot retract from the transfer, the purchaser likewise cannot retract, because the two acquisitions are reciprocal. In a loan, however, the borrower can always repay the money after receiving it. Since he can effectively terminate the continuing benefit of the loan by repayment, it would be inequitable to bind the lender before he has actually advanced the funds. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 48, also follows this approach. Rashba proves the point from Kiddushin 47a, where the Amoraim dispute whether a loan is given with a right of return while the money remains intact. Had the loan not been given for the borrower's use, one could have said it remains returnable. This shows that before the money reaches the borrower, everyone agrees the lender can retract. Bach objects that the dispute there is not whether a loan is generally given for expenditure. Everyone agrees that a loan is given for expenditure. The dispute is only whether, while the actual coins remain intact, they are legally still in the lender's domain or already in the borrower's. Shach answers correctly that the view which says the loan is not returnable treats the coins as already within the borrower's legal domain because they were given for his benefit and use. We need not extend that view farther than its case. Before the money ever reaches the borrower, even that authority can agree that the lender can retract. Still, Rashba's phrase "given for expenditure" is not perfectly precise. The real concept is that the object was given for the borrower's use and benefit. The same principle appears in a borrowed axe. Once the borrower actually begins using it, the lender cannot retract, even though the axe itself must ultimately be returned. Thus the decisive factor is that the borrower acquired the right of use. Shach says Rashba did not need to prove the law from the dispute about a loan and could have proved it directly from the borrowed axe: before the borrower uses it, the lender can retract, even though the borrower is already liable for accidents. But Shach's premise that the borrower's property becomes encumbered immediately upon receiving a borrowed object is incorrect. The property lien arises only when the accident or other event creating actual liability occurs, not merely at the moment of borrowing. This is the rule stated by Nimukei Yosef and Maggid Mishneh in the name of Rashba, and it is the basis for treating uncertainty whether the animal died while borrowed or rented as a case of uncertainty whether liability ever arose. According to one version in Ketubot, Rava holds that liability attaches from the act of taking the borrowed object. On that view one could indeed say that before the rabbinic enactment concerning meshichah the lender could not retract by Torah law, like the view of R. Elazar discussed by Tosafot. But our accepted law does not follow that line for general purposes. There is also a strong proof against Ramban's theory from the Gemara's own question. The Gemara asks whether the dispute concerning a loan being returnable should imply a corresponding Tannaitic dispute regarding the borrowed axe. If Ramban were correct that a binding property lien itself creates an exchange and therefore prevents retraction, the cases would differ. A loan would create an immediate land lien and therefore a reciprocal acquisition, whereas the borrowed axe, before any accident, would not. The Gemara could then answer that the cases are fundamentally different. The fact that it does not is a substantial argument against Ramban. Gidulei Terumah answers that Ramban speaks specifically of a written obligation that creates a property lien through the document. In the Kiddushin passage the discussion can concern an oral loan. If so, there is no comparable property exchange. That answer has force, especially because the entire Kiddushin establishment is made according to Rav, who holds an oral loan does not collect from heirs and therefore does not accept the full Torah-lien doctrine in that setting. Still, I found a more direct proof for Rashba. According to Ramban, if a purchaser buys specified movables, such as a quantity of wool, for one hundred and gives the seller a written obligation for the money, the seller's property lien should itself operate like land transferred in exchange. The specified wool should then be acquired through the reciprocal property exchange. Shach tries to limit this and says the seller can still give other wool of the same type. But if the specific wool was designated, or if no other wool exists, that limitation is difficult. Ramban's own reasoning is that a land lien is treated like land itself. If so, a lien exchanged for specified movables should acquire them just as land exchanged for movables does. Mabit, part 1, responsum 217, indeed says explicitly that according to Ramban the specific object can be acquired in that way. If so, Bava Metzia 77b becomes difficult. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel teaches how to prevent a party from retracting from a land sale after only part of the price was paid: the seller writes that he received two hundred and that the remaining eight hundred is a loan. Rashi explains that by converting the balance into a loan it is as though the money was received, and the land is acquired through money. According to Ramban, why is this explanation needed? Once the purchaser's debt of eight hundred creates a Torah land lien, that lien itself should be treated as land transferred in exchange for the field and should acquire the field as barter. The same question appears in the long responsum of Rif cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, and Ritva likewise asks how the land is acquired when the full price has not been paid. None answers that the purchaser's new debt creates a land lien which acquires the field in exchange. This is a strong indication that Ramban's theory should not be extended as Shach does. Shach adds that if circumstances materially change before the lender advances the money, everyone agrees the lender may retract, because it is clear he never intended to lend under the changed conditions. Certainly if the borrower has become financially unsafe, the lender may restrain the money even during an agreed term, all the more so before delivery. Likewise, if the borrower owns no land at all, Ramban's reciprocal-lien theory has no application. Ordinary movables do not carry an enduring property lien of the same kind. In such a case the lender certainly cannot be compelled to advance the money. Thus Rashba's rule is the sounder practical law. There remains the qualification suggested by Gidulei Terumah. Ramban's reciprocal-acquisition theory is strongest specifically where a written document creates a presently enforceable land lien. The Kiddushin passage, by contrast, can be read as an ordinary oral loan. In that setting, especially according to Rav, the full Torah lien does not operate in the same way. Therefore the Gemara's comparison to the borrowed axe does not necessarily refute Ramban's special rule for a written loan. Still, this only saves Ramban from the immediate proof. It does not answer the later commercial examples. If the written debt itself is treated exactly like land transferred to the lender, then every transaction in which a purchaser issues such a debt for specified property begins to resemble barter. Yet the Rishonim discussing land sale through deferred payment do not explain acquisition through that route. That silence is significant. The land-lien theory therefore should not be expanded beyond Ramban's precise case. This also explains why Rashba's proof from the loan-before-delivery question remains the better practical guide. Before the money is advanced, the lender has not yet conferred the benefit for which the borrower sought the loan. Nothing has been exchanged in a completed reciprocal sense. Once the funds are actually placed at the borrower's disposal, his right of use becomes real and the lender can no longer simply revoke that benefit while the loan continues. But before then, the lender retains control. The practical exceptions stated by Shach follow naturally. If the borrower's condition deteriorates, or circumstances change in a way that clearly was not contemplated, there is an obvious implied condition that the lender never undertook to advance the money in that new situation. Likewise, where there is no land to which Ramban's lien theory could attach, the lender cannot be compelled through that theory at all. Thus even within Ramban's conceptual framework the law is far narrower than a categorical rule that a promise to lend becomes irrevocable upon execution of a document.
