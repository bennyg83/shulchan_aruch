A borrower who came to beit din. The source in Baal HaTerumot concerns a deed of kinyan. Since the lender acquires the property lien immediately through the kinyan, even if the document has not yet reached him, Ramban holds that the lender can become personally obligated to provide the promised loan just as a buyer who acquires an object becomes obligated in the consideration he promised. The lender's later advance of money is then itself an oral debt owed by the lender to the borrower until performed. The crucial distinction is between acquiring the body of an object and creating a personal obligation to give an object. A kinyan can transfer ownership of the body of property, allowing the buyer to marry a woman with it, sell it, or otherwise treat it as his. By contrast, if one merely obligates himself to give another a particular object, the obligee does not yet own the object itself. Beit din can compel delivery, and only through the eventual acquisition does title pass. If the obligor sells the object before beit din transfers it, the sale can be valid, though he remains liable to provide the object or its value. Ramban treats acquisition of the lien similarly to acquisition of an object for which consideration was promised: once the borrower grants the lien, the lender becomes personally obligated to advance the promised money or merchandise. Rashba disagrees because a lien is not equivalent to acquiring the body of property. This distinction answers the proofs drawn from betrothal, borrowing an axe, and similar cases. Those passages concern ownership of the object itself, whereas here the issue is the personal obligation generated by acquisition of the lien.
