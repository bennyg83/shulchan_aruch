[Shulchan Aruch] But if he admitted concerning land. Note: It appears to me that this applies only where he admits that the land was always the other person's. But if he admits that he gave his own land to the other, the witnesses may not write. This is obvious according to the Rashba, Responsa, siman 1211, who explains that they may write because where he is not obligating anything of his own, consent of the obligor is unnecessary. In the case of an admission that he gave away his own land, they certainly may not write. Even according to Tosafot, who hold that a protest and a declaration of coercion operate by rabbinic enactment, one must explain that they permit writing an admission concerning land because ordinarily the person is pleased with it, since the writing causes him no loss and is therefore equivalent to his expressly instructing them to write. This also requires saying that the concern that he does not want many documents outstanding does not apply where the other has not taken anything of his, for then he is not really regarded as the holder of a document. Consequently, when he admits that he gave away the land, they may not write, because he may not want additional documents outstanding against him, as Tosafot explain regarding one who retracts concerning a field. True, according to Tosafot's reasoning that consent of the obligor is required, the case of retracting concerning a field is already explained because once he protests, the writing becomes testimony from writing, as Mishneh LaMelech concludes. One might therefore say that Tosafot do not invoke the concern of multiplying documents at all, and thus that in any admission concerning land they may write. Nevertheless, it appears that when witnesses acquired a field for a certain person they require either an instruction to write or a kinyan. What difference, then, is there between witnesses who saw him give the field and witnesses before whom he admits that he gave it? Therefore, it appears to me unlike Tumim; see there.
