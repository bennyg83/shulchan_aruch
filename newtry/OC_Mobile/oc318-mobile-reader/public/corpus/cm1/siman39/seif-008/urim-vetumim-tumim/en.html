And even if they are deposited movables, etc. Tur writes that if they are movables for which the person admitting liability is responsible from the moment of the admission, beit din may write the admission even without a kinyan. Lechem Mishneh and Bach raise many objections. The most correct reading is to emend Tur so that it says the person admitting liability is not responsible. Meaning: either he expressly stipulated in the deposit arrangement that he would be exempt from the ordinary laws of custodians, or the property is of a type to which the ordinary custodial obligations do not apply. Then writing the admission creates no improper additional risk. Even after the writing, he remains believed to say he discharged the obligation through a migo that the property was lost by an exempting accident. Nimukei Yosef notes that this may still require an oath. But even so there is no Torah oath here, because he never accepted ordinary custodian liability. At most he already faces a rabbinic hesset oath. Thus the writing does not materially worsen his legal position. Accordingly, it may be written without a kinyan. This reading is correct and clear.
For there is no concern here, etc. The permission to write concerning land should be limited. If the person admitted that the land had always belonged to the other party, or that he sold it to him in a transaction at which no witnesses were present, the admission may be written. But if there were witnesses to the original sale, the court should not write an additional document. Perhaps the buyer will first collect on the basis of the sale witnesses, because a purchaser whose sale is proven by witnesses can collect from encumbered property in the relevant circumstances, and afterward he will use the newly written judicial document to collect a second time. With purchasers, there may be no receipt available to protect against such double collection. See below in siman 41 and siman 117. This appears clear, and I am surprised the commentators did not discuss it.
