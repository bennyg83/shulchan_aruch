(In the Tur, seif 3: therefore, even though, etc.) The wording requires analysis. It implies that certainly where they did not witness the loan, they may not write a document. Why would one have thought otherwise if they did not witness it? Furthermore, why does the Tur conclude, "perhaps he does not want," etc.? Why express this as uncertainty when it is certain that a person does not wish harm to himself? It appears that the meaning is this. Certainly, if the witnesses were not present at the loan and the borrower later admitted that he had already borrowed from the lender, they may not write a document, because perhaps there was an express condition at the time of the loan that the lender would not take a document from him. Even if the witnesses were present at the loan and heard that no such condition was made, one might have thought that the lender certainly lent only on the understanding that the borrower would give him a document, and that if the borrower refused, the lender could compel him to return the money. The ruling therefore teaches that, since there is such a great difference between a written loan and an oral loan, perhaps the borrower would prefer to return the money rather than have a document written against him. This explains both the word "therefore" and the word "perhaps," which reflects a genuine possibility contrary to what one might otherwise have assumed.
