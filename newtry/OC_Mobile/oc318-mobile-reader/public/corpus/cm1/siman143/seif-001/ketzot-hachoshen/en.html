Nimukei Yosef writes that for a valid three year presumption it is enough that the challenger be in a place where protest could be made at the end of the three years. Even if he was initially able to protest and later became unable, the possessor does not obtain a presumption, because we may say the challenger intended to protest at the end but was prevented. This resembles the rule in prayer that one who reasonably thought he still had time and was later prevented can be considered coerced. The difficulty is that if the decisive point is the end of the three years, why did the Gemara need the challenger to say he was away at the markets? Even if he had originally been in the same country, the final period of market preoccupation should have excused him. The answer depends on how the market case is understood. Under the explanation that the challenger was actually unable to find time to protest, this is a genuine final coercion and Nimukei Yosef's rule follows. But under the explanation that the challenger merely forgot because of market preoccupation, the matter is different. If he knew of the possession earlier, simple preoccupation at the end does not excuse complete failure to protest. This explains Beit Yosef's comment that Nimukei Yosef's rule applies only according to the interpretation that the challenger had no opportunity to protest. The Shach's attempt to say that even one who originally knew may later plead that he forgot because he was busy is therefore difficult. Once he knew and had ample time, ordinary market distraction is not a sufficient excuse.
