And I did not know that you were holding my house. This proves that Mechaber understands the rule in Bava Batra 35, that where one owner was in Yehudah and the possessor in Galil there is no chazakah, established there in a time of emergency, to mean that even the possession itself is not heard during such a period. Tosafot there prove clearly, however, that the chazakah is heard and only the protest is not. If even the chazakah were unheard, how could the Gemara prove that the Sages hold a protest made outside the possessor's presence is effective? Perhaps such a protest is never effective, yet Yehudah against Yehudah establishes chazakah because news of possession is heard and the owner should come protest in person, while Yehudah against Galil does not because the possession is not heard. Evidently, even in another province the possession is heard; only the protest cannot be transmitted. The Rosh likewise writes on page 30 that once one possessed peacefully for three years, the chazakah is heard even in another province, as proved later. He alludes to Tosafot's proof from page 38, where the Gemara speaks of a time of emergency or of Yehudah and Galil being equivalent to one. Even then the possession is heard and only the protest is not. Yet the Rosh's own words on page 30 are difficult, as Tumim notes. At first he explains the owner's failure to protest by saying that he did not know the other was living in his house, implying that possession itself is not heard, but later he says that it is heard even in another province. Tumim answers that what matters is whether the owner knows at the end of the three years that the possessor is still living there. Here, because market days dispersed the people, no continuing rumor was generated that the possessor remained there, and because the owner lacked knowledge at the very end there was no chazakah. This is difficult. If so, even where caravans are common there should be no chazakah whenever the owner did not happen to know at the end. What difference would the availability of caravans make if in both cases he already knew of the possession? Why then do the poskim describe the external marketplace as a place where caravans are not common? The rule itself is also incorrect, as I explain below. Rather, it appears that the Rosh and Tur hold that Bava Batra 38 proves only a presumption that possession is generally heard even in another province and even during an emergency. But if the owner expressly claims, "I did not hear of the possession," he is believed when it is known that he was in another province. This also resolves Tosafot's question. The Gemara properly proves that protest outside the possessor's presence is effective, because otherwise why would the Mishnah state categorically that there is no chazakah between Yehudah and Galil? We do not automatically plead for the owner that he was unaware of the possession merely because it was an emergency. But if he expressly claims that he did not know, he is believed since he was in another province. This is proved from the case on page 30 where he says, "I was in the outside markets," and is believed. According to Rashbam, merely saying, "I had no time to protest," is insufficient, so the claim must mean that he did not know of the possession. Thus, where it is established that he was outside the province, he is believed to say that he did not know.
And he went to a place where protest could not be made. See Shach, seif katan 1, who writes that if the owner knew the other was possessing his property and later wishes to say, "I was occupied and did not know that you were still possessing my property," the claim does not help and the chazakah is valid. He says this is Beit Yosef's intent. At first glance these words are obscure. Beit Yosef, after citing Nimukei Yosef's rule, says that this ruling applies only according to the commentators who explain that the owner had no opportunity to protest. Darkei Moshe and Bach object that even according to the commentators who explain that he did not know of the possession, Nimukei Yosef's rule follows. If so, how does Shach's newly stated distinction answer their question? Moreover, Shach's new rule is itself unclear. It appears, however, that Shach's words are straightforward once Nimukei Yosef is understood. Nimukei Yosef infers that even if at the beginning of the three years the owner was in a place where he could protest, if at the end he was in a place where he could not, there is no chazakah. The proof is, as Tumim writes, that each year he spent thirty days at home, while the market occupation occurred only during the last thirty days. He therefore could have protested earlier. Further, the Gemara says only that during the entire thirty days of the market he was occupied, implying that immediately afterward he was no longer occupied. Shitah Mekubetzet explicitly writes that the occupation lasts no more than thirty days. In the earlier years, even if he was occupied during market time, once the market ended and he returned home he again had the ability to protest before reaching the external marketplace where a protest could not be heard. The final thirty days, however, coincided with the end of the three years. This proves that even if at first he was in a place where protest was possible, if at the end he was in a place where it was impossible, there is no chazakah. One should not ask why he needed to say that he was in the external marketplace. Since at the end of the three years he was occupied at the market and had no time to protest, that alone might appear sufficient. The answer is that when one voluntarily goes to a place where he cannot protest, he can say that when he left he intended to return and protest before the three years ended, as Darkei Moshe explicitly writes. Here, however, if the thirty market days themselves were known in advance to be the final thirty days of the three years, and he knew he would certainly be occupied throughout them, he should have protested before the market began. He therefore needed to say, "I was in the outside markets," meaning that in the second year he intended to return long before market day but was unexpectedly delayed by ones or the like. According to the second interpretation, however, that a person has time to protest during market days but may claim that because of market distraction he did not know the possessor was living in his house, Nimukei Yosef's conclusion cannot be understood as requiring knowledge specifically at the end of the three years. It cannot be that during the first years' thirty-day periods the owner was not distracted and knew of the possession, but because at the end he did not know whether the possessor still remained there, no chazakah arose on the theory that the owner must know specifically at the conclusion of the three years. If that were so, why would he need to claim that he was in the outside markets? Even if he had been home throughout the three years but happened to be distracted at the final market period and did not know that the possessor was still there, there would be no chazakah. Rather, those commentators who explain "I was occupied at the market" as meaning that he did not know of the possession must understand that throughout the market periods in all three years he remained distracted until he reached the outside market and therefore never learned of the possession during the entire three years. That is why there was no chazakah. But if at any point within the three years he knew of the possessor's occupation and only at the end did not know whether it continued, such as because of market distraction, chazakah is valid. Once an owner knows that possession has begun, he must be concerned that it may continue and must protest. His failure to protest supports the possessor. On this reading Beit Yosef and Shach are clear. If before the end of three years the owner went to a place from which he would be unable to protest and knew that he would not return to a place where protest was possible before the period ended, all agree that the chazakah is valid. Since he knew both of the possession and that he would later be unable to protest, he should have protested before leaving. Nimukei Yosef denies chazakah only where the owner can say, "I intended to return before the three years ended." If an unavoidable event prevented him from protesting at the end, then there is no chazakah, as in Ketubot at the beginning of chapter 2, where two years of possession were in the owner's presence and one year outside his presence because he fled for his life. Nimukei Yosef adds that even if he left voluntarily, so long as he can say that he intended to return before the three years ended, there is likewise no chazakah.
