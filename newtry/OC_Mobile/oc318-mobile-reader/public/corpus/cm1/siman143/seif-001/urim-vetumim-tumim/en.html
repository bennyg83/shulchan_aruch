I did not know that you were occupying my house. This refers to a case where he had another house in which to reside. Rashbam explains "I was occupied in the markets" to mean: I did not know that you were living in my house, such as where he had another house in that city at which he lodged. Some explain that although he knew, he had no opportunity to protest, but Rashbam rejects that explanation. At first glance, according to Tosafot below on 38a, even from the Galilee to Judea during an emergency, possession becomes known, as Tosafot explain that one investigates what happens to his property, whereas the protest itself may not become known. If so, one who was in distant markets all three years, even during an emergency as Rabbenu Chananel explains, nevertheless knew of the possession and knew that this person was occupying his property. During the final thirty days of the third year, when he returned to the country, he should therefore have protested. We would seemingly have to accept the other explanation rejected by Rashbam, that he was so occupied in the market that he lacked time to protest, and then it would make no difference whether he lodged in another house or in this one, because he simply had no time to protest. Yet according to this, the Rosh appears self-contradictory. He writes: "all thirty days I was occupied in the markets and did not know that you were living in my house, and when I was outside I was in distant lands where caravans were uncommon." This follows Rashbam, that because of the market's distractions he did not know of the occupation. But the Rosh then writes that it is more plausible that he need not bring proof, because once this person possessed the property peacefully for three years, and possession becomes known even in another country, as is evident below, the burden is on the owner to establish that he was in a place where his protest could not be heard, and therefore refrained from protesting. This plainly follows Tosafot's view, to which the Rosh alludes by "as is evident below," namely from the discussion of protest not made in the possessor's presence, where Tosafot prove that possession is known even from the Galilee, but protest is not. How then can the Rosh say that he did not know the other was living in his house, when he necessarily knew beforehand while in the distant markets? One would again need the reason that he lacked time to protest. This requires explanation. I believe the Rosh's position can be reconciled as follows. Even when one knows that another is possessing his property, until the possession reaches a complete three years there is no obligation to protest, because there is not yet a chazakah. The essential protest is therefore at the end of the three years, when he sees that the other has possessed his property for three years. If he is elsewhere and hears of the possession, he must indeed protest as the end of the third year approaches, since perhaps the possessor will complete three years. But one who was in the distant markets and intended to return to the country where his house was located at the end of the three years planned not to protest until he arrived and could investigate whether, at the completion of the third year, the other was still residing there. If the possessor had left earlier, no protest was needed. He therefore did not concern himself with protesting until he could ascertain whether the possessor was still there. When he arrived, intending to investigate, the distractions of the market caused him to forget to inquire whether the other was still there. Since he heard no report that the person was still living there, he assumed that he had left and therefore did not protest. In truth, the public was just as occupied in the market and so no report circulated. One must say something of this kind even according to the Rosh, for if occupation from afar ordinarily becomes known, why was there no report here? It must be that the market activity prevented the report from circulating. This is what the Rosh means when he writes, "I was occupied in the market and did not know that you were living in the house": he relied on the absence of any report and assumed that the possessor was no longer there. The Rosh's words are therefore consistent, though the matter still requires study. According to this, the Rosh clearly disproves the Shach in seif katan 1, who writes that if the owner initially knew that this person was possessing his property and later wishes to claim, "I was occupied and did not know that you were still possessing my property," the claim is ineffective and the possession is a chazakah. According to what has been explained, the owner here initially knew, yet because he did not know at the end, the possession was not a chazakah. The Shach's words are therefore contradicted by the Rosh, and this is clear.
If at the beginning he was in a place where he could protest, and he went to a place where he could not protest, it is not a chazakah, etc. These words originate in the Nimukei Yosef, who writes concerning the case, "I was in the distant markets," cited by the Mechaber, that from here it appears, etc. The Beit Yosef cites him and writes that this inference applies only according to those who explain the claim as, "I had no opportunity to protest." The Darkei Moshe writes that as a matter of logic the rule applies according to all views, because the owner intended to return but was then delayed and did not return. The Bach and Derishah write similarly. I do not understand this. If the matter follows merely from logic, the Nimukei Yosef should not have said that he derives it from the case of the distant markets, writing, "from here it appears," nor should the Rama have said, "from this one may learn." Indeed, the logic can be challenged. Since the owner sees that he is going to a distant place, he should not enter into this doubt, but should immediately protest. What difficulty is there in protesting before two people? The Gemara in Ketubot 17b somewhat supports this. It asks why the case should not be established where the possessor consumed two years in the owner's presence and one year not in his presence, such as where the owner fled. It does not say more simply that in the third year the owner went from Judea to the Galilee, so that the possession would not be a chazakah, teaching that despite his presence during the first two years the chazakah fails. This suggests that in such a case there actually would be a chazakah. The Nimukei Yosef therefore required a proof. The Beit Yosef accordingly writes that there is no proof from the cited case, because the owner never knew of the possession in a manner obligating him to protest, since we say that during all thirty days he was occupied in the markets and did not know at all that the other had occupied his property. Thus there is no basis for the Nimukei Yosef's inference. The Derishah answers that since the Nimukei Yosef derives from there that the owner need be in a place where he can protest only at the end of the three years, it follows that everything depends on the end of the third year. Therefore, if at that time he was in a place where he could not protest, he did not lose his right. But these matters do not depend on one another. Where at the end of three years he was in a place where he could protest, once he heard that the other had possessed his property for three years, why did he not protest? Yet from that very point one could equally reason in the opposite direction: since he knows that the other has possessed his property for two years and he is now going to a place where he cannot protest, he should not rely on returning, but ought to protest immediately, as stated above. I would have said that the Nimukei Yosef understood that the market day did not occur every year. The principal distraction of market day is precisely that it occurs only at distant intervals, as the Sema writes. If it occurred every year for thirty days, that would not be an uncommon interval. Rather, during the first two years the owner was present for thirty days without a market day, while in the third year a market day fell during those thirty days and he was occupied, so he did not know whether the possessor was still there in the third year. He therefore did not lose his right. On this basis the Nimukei Yosef could properly prove that even if during the first two years the owner was in a place where he could protest, as here where during the first two years he was present thirty days without distraction, nevertheless since in the third year he was in the distant markets and those thirty days were occupied, equivalent to being in the distant markets, the possessor does not acquire a chazakah. This would resolve the matter. Yet aside from the fact that this is strained in the Nimukei Yosef and there is no source for assuming such facts, I also do not understand his first inference, that even if the owner was for two years in a place where he could not protest, nevertheless if at the end of the third year he was in a place where he could protest, there is a chazakah. In that episode, the owner was in his own locality for thirty days every year. Were it not for his claim that market day occupied him, he was each year in a place where he could protest. The Derishah writes that there is nevertheless a proof because each year he was there for only thirty days. I do not understand this. Could one have imagined that throughout the entire three years the owner must be bound together with the possessor and never move his hand from his hand? Plainly, if at the end of each year he was there, heard and knew that the other had possessed his property during that entire year, this is considered that throughout the three years he was in a place where he could protest, for he could protest every year. There is another difficulty in the Nimukei Yosef. He writes regarding the Gemara's statement, "but you came for thirty days every year," that during the first years the owner could have said, "I thought I would come in the third year and protest," but now that he came also in the third year, why did he not protest? What did the Nimukei Yosef seek to establish by this? If his point is why the possessor troubled himself to bring witnesses that the owner came for thirty days every year rather than proving his presence in only one year, and his answer is that presence in the first two years is ineffective while presence in the third year is decisive, the question still remains. He needed witnesses only that the owner came during the third year. Testimony about the first two years was superfluous, as the Derishah himself notes. If it was in fact unnecessary and merely additional witnesses were brought, the original difficulty returns: what did the Nimukei Yosef gain from this analysis? I therefore believe that his view is as follows. Certainly, if for two years the owner was in a place where he could not protest specifically, meaning that he nevertheless heard of the possession, as Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Nimukei Yosef himself state in the discussion of one who was in Judea, but he could not protest because it was a time of emergency, then if in the third year he came to a place where he could protest, he must protest. He already knew that the other was possessing his property, but lacked the ability to protest. Once he eventually came to a place where he could protest, he should have done so. But if for two years he was in a distant place and did not hear of the possession at all, then even if in the third year he came to a place where he heard that the other was possessing the property, there still is no chazakah. Although he now heard that so-and-so was possessing the property, did he hear that the possession had continued for the entire three years? Who remembers whether this person has been sitting there for three years or not? This is a matter not imposed upon one's attention. Even when witnesses come before us, how carefully we examine them regarding continuity of possession for three years in proper sequence, as I explained at length in siman 140. All the more so, a mere report does not establish that the person has possessed for three years. Therefore there is no chazakah. The words of Rabbenu Chananel, cited in the Ramban's commentary on the passage "I was in the inner residences," indicate this distinction. He writes that during an emergency the owner must be with the possessor in the same country throughout all the years of chazakah, and he concludes that the owner must bring witnesses that throughout all three years he was in a nearby place, because a possession of less than three years is not a chazakah, and since part of the three years occurred during an emergency, those years do not count. The Ramban agrees, writing that Rabbenu Chananel ruled well in explaining that the owner must be with him in the country throughout all the years of chazakah. One could force the language and say that "all three years" only excludes presence at the beginning alone, while if he was present only at the end of the third year this is still called being there for the entire three-year period because the end of the third year closes the entire span. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 15 of Hilchot To'en, citing Ramban and Rashba, appears to say that it suffices to establish that the owner was in the country at the end of the third year. Nevertheless, the plain language of Rabbenu Chananel more strongly indicates that the owner must be there throughout all three years. It therefore appears that one must distinguish between a place where even the possession cannot be heard, in which case all three years are required, and a place where only the protest cannot be heard, in which case presence at the end of the three years suffices. Accordingly, the Nimukei Yosef inferred from the Rif's explanation of "I was in distant markets and did not know that you had entered my house" that the owner was so far away that it was impossible even to hear of the possession. The Rif did not give the simpler explanation that he could hear of the possession but was unable to protest, as the Rosh explains. The Nimukei Yosef was troubled: why, according to the Rif, was it necessary for the possessor to bring witnesses that the owner was in his home and city for thirty days every year? Regarding the third year, witnesses were indeed needed, as the Nimukei Yosef says, because the owner could claim, "I intended to come in the third year, and therefore did not protest." But why were witnesses necessary for the first two years? The Rif therefore explains that the owner claimed that he did not hear of the possession at all because he was so far away. In such a case, presence in the country only in the third year is insufficient. He must be there throughout all three years, as Rabbenu Chananel states. Hence the possessor brought witnesses that every year the owner was in the country for thirty days. Had the Rif instead explained that the owner was in a place where he heard of the possession but could not protest, then presence in the third year alone would certainly suffice, and there would be no need for witnesses concerning the first two years. The Gemara's trouble in producing those witnesses would then be unexplained. The Rif's wording is thus precise. This is the meaning of the Nimukei Yosef's statement, "from here it appears that the owner need not be in a place where he can protest except at the end of the three years." He specifically says a place where he can protest, not a place where he can hear of the possession, for in this episode, according to the Rif, he could not even hear the possession. Thus, specifically regarding ability to protest, all three years are unnecessary, but regarding awareness of the possession, the owner must be in a place where it can be heard throughout all three years. The inference is therefore sound. Had the law been that even the ability to protest was required throughout all three years, there would have been no need for the Rif to say that the owner did not know of the possession. It would have sufficed to say simply that he could not protest. The second law is likewise clearly proved from the need for witnesses regarding the third year. This shows that concerning testimony about the first two years the owner may say, "I intended to come in the third year and protest," even though he had gone far away to a place where even the possession could not be heard. Certainly the same applies where he went only to a place where the protest could not be heard. The Nimukei Yosef's words are therefore clear and compelling from every side. Still, although this distinction between hearing the protest and hearing the possession is logically persuasive, and Rabbenu Chananel's words almost clearly indicate that where the owner did not know of the possession, presence at the end of the three years alone does not suffice, I remain uneasy because I have not found explicit support for this distinction among the other authorities. There is also some difficulty from the aforementioned Gemara in Ketubot. It could have established its case where the possessor consumed for two years while the owner was in a place where he could not hear of the possession, and in the third year the owner was in a place where the possession could be heard and where he could protest, yet the possession still would not be a chazakah. This would teach a substantial novelty. Perhaps one must say that this was already taught by the Mishnah stating that when the owner was in Judea and the possessor in the Galilee there is no chazakah until they are in the same country, which Rabbenu Chananel interprets as requiring that they be together throughout all three years. The matter therefore requires study, and the Beit Yosef properly questioned the Nimukei Yosef. Nevertheless, even if one says that no proof can be brought from the Nimukei Yosef because his words are obscure, the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 15 of Hilchot To'en explicitly writes in the name of Ramban and Rashba that if the owner was with the possessor in the country at the beginning and middle, but at the end went to a distant place, the possessor did not acquire a chazakah. I therefore wonder why this law was made dependent on the words of the Nimukei Yosef. It is expressly the law of Ramban and Rashba, and the Ramban writes it in his novellae as cited above. There is no doubt in the ruling itself.
