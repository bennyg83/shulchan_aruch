While the merchandise remains in his possession. This is Rambam's exact wording in chapter 20 of Hilchot Mechirah. In the Derishah I proved that because Rambam states without qualification that the seller is believed whenever the merchandise remains in his hand, he does not follow Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rosh. They explain that the seller is believed while holding the merchandise because of a migo: if he wished, he could hand it to the person to whom he says he sold it. They therefore limit his credibility to a case where he received money from both claimants, or received from one and says he sold to the one who paid. But he is not believed to say that he agreed to sell to the second person who paid nothing, because that would be a migo against witnesses or against an overwhelming presumption. Rif and Rashi disagree. They explain that so long as the merchandise remains in his possession, the seller is believed even if he says he agreed to sell to the person who did not pay and that the other person forced money upon him against his will. Their reason is that while the merchandise remains with him, the seller takes special care to remember the truth so that he not become subject to mi shepara by falsely saying he agreed to a person whom he did not originally accept. Maggid Mishneh gives another reason: so long as the merchandise remains in his hand he is like a judge who is believed to say, "I ruled in favor of this one and against that one," as the Tur and Mechaber write above in siman 23. See the Derishah. The Tur cites both views without deciding and does not mention the Rosh's independent view here. It appears that Rambam too agrees with Rif and Rashi, which is why he states the rule generally and holds that while the merchandise is in his hand he is believed in every circumstance. It is true that according to Rif, even when the merchandise is no longer in his hand, if he received money from only one party he is believed like two witnesses to say that he agreed to sell to the other, because such a statement is so counterintuitive, namely that he agreed to the person from whom he received no money, that he certainly remembers it clearly and we do not suspect deliberate falsehood. Only when he received money from both, one willingly and one unwillingly, did the Sages say that once the merchandise is no longer in his hand he is not believed, because without the threat of mi shepara he may speak based on an impression rather than precise memory. The Rosh and Tur explain all this in Rif's name. Rambam writes generally that once the merchandise is no longer in his hand he has only the status of one witness. It may be that Rambam alludes to the same limitation: his phrase "he is like any other person, because he has no interest in the testimony" applies specifically where he received money from both. If he received money from only one, or received from neither but each claims to have acquired through meshichah or hagbahah, he still has a potential interest in saying that he agreed to the one whose money he received, since otherwise he would have to trouble himself to recover the property from the other, or perhaps one claimant is easier for him to deal with than the other. In the Derishah I wrote another possibility: Rambam follows the Gemara's wording, and the Gemara regards as obvious that where he received money from only one party he is believed like two witnesses for the reason just stated. However this is understood, Rama's gloss immediately below, "there is no difference whether he received money from one, etc.," appears at first to refer to the immediately preceding case where the merchandise is not in the seller's hand, and that would accord with no opinion. According to Rif he remains believed like two in the one-payment case; according to Rabbeinu Chananel, even while the merchandise is still in his possession he is not believed to say it belongs to the one who did not pay. It therefore appears that Rama's gloss refers to the first clause, where the merchandise remains in the seller's hand, and serves to explain Rambam and the Mechaber according to Rif, as I wrote. This is unlike the Levush, who confused the positions by giving the first clause the migo rationale and then adding that it makes no difference whether the seller received money from one or both. Those two propositions cannot coexist, as is clear from the Gemara, the Rosh, and Tosafos. Moreover, if the seller received money from only one claimant, he is personally interested in the testimony, as explained.
He is only one witness. The Tur explains: he is like an ordinary single witness, and can exempt from an oath the litigant whom he supports. If both parties are holding the merchandise, the person to whom the seller says he agreed receives half the merchandise and half the money without an oath, while the other receives half and must take a Torah oath. See there for additional details.
Therefore, if he took, etc. This too is Rambam's wording. "Therefore" refers to the immediately preceding rule that when the merchandise is no longer in his hand he is only a single witness. Rambam holds that when the merchandise is in his hand he was granted credibility equivalent to two witnesses, but when it is no longer there he is only one witness. Since his credibility is testimonial, meaning he testifies about what occurred originally and is not simply choosing now whom he favors, if he does not know from whom he first received the money he is like a person without testimony. The case is then governed like two people holding a garment and appearing before us, each saying "half is mine," where each takes an oath while holding a sacred object, etc.
Whether the merchandise remained in his possession. See the Derishah, where I brought proof from this that Rambam follows Rif, his teacher's teacher. This also resolves the Rosh's question against Rif. See there; there is no need to lengthen the discussion here.
