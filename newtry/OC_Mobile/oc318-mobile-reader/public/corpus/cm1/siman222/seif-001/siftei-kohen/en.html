The seller is believed to say, etc. There are several explanations of this matter in the Gemara. The principal explanation, in my opinion, is that the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia and at the end of the chapter Asarah Yuchasin asks the same question in one form: 'Let us see from whom he received the money.' Whether the merchandise remains in the seller's possession or no longer remains there, why is the seller's credibility needed? Ordinarily it is known from whom he received the money, and we presume that he agreed to sell to that person. Even when the merchandise remains in his possession, if the seller says that he sold it to the one who did not pay, he is not believed, because this is comparable to a migo against witnesses, as Rabbeinu Chananel explains. Certainly, where the merchandise is no longer in his possession, and both claimants hold it, if it is known from whom he received payment, we presume that he sold it to that person and that it belongs to him alone. Therefore the Gemara at the end of Asarah Yuchasin had to answer that he received money from both, one willingly and one against his will. The Gemara could likewise have answered that he received from one but it is unknown from whom, except that witnesses ordinarily know who paid, as the Ramban writes in Milchamot Hashem at the end of Asarah Yuchasin, and as the Ritva writes there. If the witnesses do not know from whom he received payment, however, then while the merchandise remains in his possession the seller is believed, through a migo, even if he says that he agreed to sell to the person who did not pay. Although he admits who paid him, he could have denied altogether that he received payment from that person. The Ramban writes this in Milchamot Hashem at the beginning of Bava Metzia, and the Ritva writes similarly in Asarah Yuchasin, unlike the Rosh there. The Ramban further writes that even where witnesses know from whom he received payment, the seller is believed in favor of the other person. This does not appear correct to me in light of the Gemara. Rather, the principal ruling is, with Heaven's help, like the Rosh: if witnesses or the purchasers establish from whom he received payment, we presume that he sold to that person. The Ritva likewise rules this in Asarah Yuchasin. It appears that Rabbeinu Chananel also agrees with all of this, and so, in my opinion, does the Rif, unlike the Rosh's understanding of Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rif. According to the Rif's text, the Gemara had to answer at the beginning of Bava Metzia that 'he does not know,' because the question 'Let us see from whom he received the money' includes the possibility of clarifying the matter through the seller. The Gemara therefore had to answer that he received from both, since otherwise witnesses or the purchasers could ordinarily establish who paid. It also had to say that he does not know, since otherwise, while the merchandise remained in his possession, the matter could be clarified through the seller according to the Rif's text. The Rosh asks why the Gemara was pressed to answer that the seller does not know. In my opinion this is no difficulty. On the contrary, why should the Mishna's case of buying and selling be limited specifically to merchandise no longer in the seller's possession? It can be established in every case, whether or not it remains in his possession. Where it is no longer in his possession, the Mishna applies straightforwardly; where it remains in his possession, it concerns a seller who does not know. This also appears to be the view of the Rambam and the Mechaber. They therefore write at the end: 'Consequently, if he received payment from two people, etc., and does not know from whom he received it willingly.' This implies that if he knew from whom he willingly received payment, then while the merchandise remained in his possession he would be believed like two witnesses. If it no longer remained in his possession but both parties held it, he would be equivalent to one witness. The party whom he supported would take the half already in his possession without an oath, under the rule that one supporting witness exempts from an oath, while the opposing party would have to take a Torah oath. Where the seller does not know, both parties instead swear under the rabbinic enactment. There are many practical differences between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath, as explained above in siman 87. The Rambam and Mechaber therefore specify that he received from two people. If he received from only one, it would ordinarily be known from whom he received payment, the seller's testimony would not be needed, and even if he knew, his testimony would not be accepted against the established fact. Once witnesses or the purchasers establish who paid, we presume that the sale was to that person. The Rama and the Ir Shushan also agree. Their statement that there is no difference whether he received money from one or from two means only that there is no difference regarding the seller's formal credibility. It rejects Rashi's explanation, and the Rif as understood by the Rosh and Tur, that where he received from one he is believed like two witnesses even after the merchandise left his possession because responsibility for the matter rested upon him and he therefore remembers. They teach that there is no such distinction regarding his status as a witness. Nevertheless, where it is established by witnesses or by the purchasers that he received from one, there remains the separate presumption that he intended the sale for that person. This is indicated at the end of the Rambam's and Mechaber's words, as explained. The Sema's objection to the Rama and Ir Shushan is thereby removed. See the Kesef Mishneh and Bedek HaBayit. Although their language there is somewhat confused, it supports my explanation. Examine all of this carefully, for in my opinion it is the principal practical ruling.
He is regarded as one witness, etc. Hagahot Ashri at the beginning of Shnayim Ochazin writes that nowadays, since the oath of hesset exists, the seller is not treated as a witness. The opposing party can require him to swear and say, 'Give me the article that I purchased from you,' and a witness who must swear is not accepted as a witness, as explained above in siman 121, seif 9. The Maadaney Yom Tov writes that the Rosh and the other poskim state this law without saying that it no longer applies nowadays, which indicates that they hold it remains applicable. In his view, even today the seller bears no oath obligation. The case of two people holding an item is established even as a sale. If each claims that the seller delivered it to him, neither has any claim against the seller, since each admits that the seller delivered it. If each claims that the seller did not deliver it to him but delivered it to the other, each thereby admits that he had not yet performed meshichah, so the seller may retract and is subject only to the curse of mi shepara. Rashi writes in Kiddushin, regarding merchandise remaining in the seller's possession, that the seller must be exact so that he does not retract from one, return his money, and give the merchandise to the person to whom it was not sold, thereby incurring mi shepara. Since only mi shepara is involved, the Maadaney Yom Tov argues that this does not prevent him from serving as a witness. Moreover, he says, the case necessarily cannot involve meshichah, because if one purchaser had performed meshichah the item would already be in his domain and he would have no claim against the seller. More strongly, the present case concerns merchandise no longer in the seller's possession. Rashi writes in Kiddushin that where the merchandise is no longer in his possession, mi shepara does not apply and the seller does not impose upon himself the duty to remember. According to this, one who is subject to mi shepara might indeed be disqualified as a witness, because he cannot complete his testimony through speech alone, for the same reason that a witness who must swear is disqualified, but here there is certainly not even mi shepara. These are the words of the Maadaney Yom Tov. In my opinion they are incorrect. The case can readily be one in which a purchaser performed meshichah and then left the item with the seller, who subsequently gave it to the other claimant. His citation from Rashi proves nothing, because Rashi follows his own explanation, under which the issue is whether the seller imposes upon himself the duty to remember. Rashi also did not write that mi shepara is absent when the merchandise no longer remains in the seller's possession, as the Maadaney Yom Tov quoted him. He wrote only that the seller does not impose upon himself the duty to remember after the merchandise has left him. See Rashi there. Mi shepara can certainly apply even when the merchandise is no longer in his possession. According to the Rosh and the other poskim, who explain the issue as whether the seller is believed as one witness, it plainly concerns a case where each claimant says that he acquired the item through meshichah, a sudar acquisition, acquisition together with land, or another valid mode of acquisition. Otherwise the issue would not depend upon witness testimony at all. If neither acquired it, and only mi shepara applies, the seller plainly may sell it to whomever he chooses by a mere declaration. Rather, it appears to me that the Rosh and the poskim did not address the oath issue. They meant only that the seller is not inherently interested in the testimony and therefore has the status of one witness. They spoke of a case in which he need not swear, such as where both parties released him from any oath. Even according to the view above in siman 121, seif katan 52, that release from an oath is ineffective, that applies only where the borrower alone released the witness, because we suspect that he will testify for the person who released him. Here both parties released him, so the release plainly works. Where they did not release him, the Rosh and the poskim indeed agree with Hagahot Ashri that he is not accepted as a witness. This appears correct to me. As a general practical ruling, where a person is subject only to mi shepara, he may still serve as a witness. See above, siman 121, seif katan 51. I later found that Shiltei HaGiborim, chapter 3 of Bava Metzia, end of page 65, writes that Hagahot HaRosh proves the following: although mere words do not complete a sale, after institution of the oath of hesset, if Reuven claims, 'You concluded a transaction with me concerning a certain article and sold it to me,' and Shimon denies it, Reuven may require Shimon to take a hesset oath. This is so even if the article remains in Shimon's possession and Shimon would owe nothing had he merely said, 'I retract.' These words are astonishing. How can he be required to take a hesset oath regarding a matter for which he would owe nothing even upon admission? Hagahot Ashri must therefore mean as I explained above.
