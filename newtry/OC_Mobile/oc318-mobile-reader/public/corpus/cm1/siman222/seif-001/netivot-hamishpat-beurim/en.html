The seller of the merchandise is believed. Shach, seif katan 1, says that if witnesses know he received money from one of the two claimants but do not know from whom, the seller can be believed, while the merchandise remains in his hands, to say he intended the sale for the person who did not pay, through a migo that he could have denied receiving money at all. In my view this is difficult. Ramban uses a migo only where there are no witnesses at all that money was received. Where witnesses know he received money from one of them but not which, Ramban gives a different rationale. Consider the second buyer. If he admits that he did not pay, then the factual matter is established through his own admission, and we may infer that the seller sold to him only if the legal rules allow it. If he claims that he was the one who paid, while the seller says the other paid but he intended to sell to this claimant anyway and now wishes to return the money to the other, the proposed migo is a migo from one person to another. We generally do not say that a person should be believed because he could instead have stolen from one person in order to benefit another. Maharit explains that perhaps he is unwilling to wrong one person in order to help the other. Although the seller’s present false statement could itself wrong the other claimant, ordinary people may rationalize taking property where full payment is returned; “you shall not covet” is often misunderstood as applying only without payment. It is therefore not a valid migo to say he could have committed a clearer theft from one person and handed the benefit to another. There is also a strong presumption that a person does not sin for someone else. Rama should therefore be understood as ruling like Ramban for substantive reasons, not through this migo. If only money was given and no full kinyan occurred, the seller remains a litigant concerning the object and can say which buyer he intended. His admission itself can transfer ownership, because a formal admission can operate as a kinyan. Even if the payer performed a kinyan, the seller may say, “I never consented to sell to you; your act of acquisition was ineffective, while I intended to sell to the other.” Since the seller retains the prior ownership chazakah, that claim is like any defendant saying, “It never happened,” and he is believed with a heset oath. The real dispute arises where both claimants already performed valid acts of acquisition and witnesses heard the seller direct one of them to acquire but do not know which. The object has certainly left the seller’s ownership and belongs to one of them. The seller is then merely a witness identifying ownership. In that case the authorities who disbelieve him rely on the presumption that he intended the sale for the person who paid. Ramban too may agree. Where the merchandise is no longer in the seller’s hands, Rama follows Ramban that the seller is not believed as owner and has only the status of one witness. This is the primary ruling, as Taz also concludes. Finally, when the seller received money from one person but says he intended the sale for the other, he is believed only if he says that from the outset he did not consent to the payer’s purchase and that the money was forced upon him. If he admits that he initially consented to the sale and the buyer later performed meshichah, or says only that he retracted before meshichah and was willing to accept mi shepara, he is not believed without witnesses proving the retraction before meshichah. Tur and the other poskim imply this by limiting the rule to a claim that the money was initially given against his will.
