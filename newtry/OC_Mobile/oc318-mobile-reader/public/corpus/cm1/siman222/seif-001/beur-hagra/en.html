He is like, etc.; his law is, etc. The Tosefta says that when the sale is no longer in the seller's possession, he is like any other person. Rif cites this at the beginning of Bava Metzia. Rabbenu Hananel explains the Kiddushin passage to mean that if the seller accepted money from only one claimant, he is not believed to say he sold to the other, even while the article remains in his possession, because such a claim is a migo against witnesses: we presume that he sold willingly to the one whose money he accepted. In Bava Metzia, where the article is no longer in his possession and he accepted money from both, he is nevertheless treated as one witness, obligating one claimant to take a Torah oath and exempting the other. Rif explains that the Mishna concerns a sale still in the seller's possession; the Gemara asks why the court does not determine from whom he took payment, and answers that this is unknown. Rosh and Tur understand Rif like Rashi: if he accepted money from only one, he is believed even after the article left his possession; the limitation applies only when he accepted from both. Beit Yosef, however, maintains that Rif follows Rabbenu Hananel, and this is the principal view. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch likewise omit any special rule based on accepting from one alone. Their distinction is that while the article remains with him the seller is believed as two witnesses, whereas afterward he is like one witness, following Rabbenu Tam, because he has no personal interest. Rama inserted Rif according to Rosh's interpretation, applying it to the opening clause. Yet on that approach the concluding clause should also be limited to one who accepted from both; if he accepted from one, he would be believed as two witnesses. This is difficult, and Rashi's view is an isolated one. Rambam's formulation is better understood like Rabbenu Hananel: the Gemara's question applies whether or not the article remains with the seller. If the court knows from whom he accepted payment, that claimant is presumed to be the purchaser. When it is unknown to the court but known to the seller, the seller's degree of credibility depends only on possession of the article, not on whether he received from one or both. This is the ruling of Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, and Shach adopts it.
(Supplement) He is believed, etc. Rashi holds that the seller is always believed as two witnesses unless the sale has left his possession and he accepted money from both. Rabbenu Hananel holds that he is believed only when the sale remains with him and he accepted from both; if he accepted from one he cannot say that he sold to the other. Rabbenu Tam understands the Gemara's question as seeking at least the testimony of one witness. Ri offers another explanation. Rambam appears to follow Rabbenu Hananel in treating the question as applying to both clauses, with the seller's knowledge rather than the court's knowledge being decisive. The practical distinction is only whether the article remains in his possession: while it does, he is believed as two witnesses; afterward, as one witness. There is no legal distinction between payment from one and payment from both. This is the principal ruling. (End of supplement.)
(Supplement) He is believed, etc. Rosh records four approaches: Rashi and what he understands as Rif's view, Rabbenu Hananel, Ri, and Rabbenu Tam. His concluding synthesis combines them. Where the payor is known independently, the article belongs to him regardless of possession. Where only the seller knows, Rabbenu Hananel limits the seller's credibility; where the article is no longer with the seller, Ri and Rabbenu Tam treat him like an ordinary witness. Several readings in Rosh require correction, as Beit Yosef explains. (End of supplement.) See cited sources 50, 81.
