The seller is believed. Kiddushin teaches that the owner of the sale is believed to say, "I sold to this one and not to that one," when the item remains in his hand. Once the item is no longer in his hand he is not believed. The Gemara asks why we cannot simply see from whom he received the money and answers that he received money from both, one willingly and one against his will, and no longer knows which was which. Rashi explains that while the merchandise remains in the seller's hand, he pays close attention because he must avoid giving the object to the wrong buyer and thereby becoming subject to the curse of "Who exacted payment." Once the item is no longer in his hand and both buyers are holding it, he no longer has the same reason to remember. Rosh asks that even if the seller remembers, after the item left his possession he is still only one witness. Ran answers that according to Rashi, if the seller received money from only one buyer he remains believed even after the merchandise left his hand, at least while the buyers are still before him, analogous to a judge who remembers the litigants. Maggid Mishneh understands Rashi similarly. This limitation is difficult. At the beginning of Bava Metzia Rashi says that even though the seller is generally not believed once the merchandise is out of his hand, if he received money from only one buyer he certainly remembers from whom he received it. His wording suggests that this remains true even after the buyers have left. Indeed, Rashi explicitly says that when the seller took money from both, he may fail to remember once they have gone, because he had no continuing duty to distinguish which payment was voluntary and which forced. The implication is that where he received money from only one, he remembers even after they left. This requires study. Rabbeinu Chananel takes a different approach. Where money is known to have come from only one buyer, there is a strong presumption that the seller agreed to sell to that payer. Even while the merchandise is still in his hand, the seller cannot overcome that presumption by saying he intended the other buyer, because his migo is a migo against a presumption. This explains the Gemara's question, "Let us see from whom he took the money." Shach concludes that where the merchandise remains in the seller's hand he can be believed through a migo that he could have denied receiving the money from the payer, following Ramban in Milchamot. But if the fact of payment is established by witnesses, or even by the buyers' own admissions, we presume he agreed to the payer. I do not understand why the seller's migo should be stronger than the buyer's corresponding migo. If the buyer admits that the other buyer paid, why should he not be believed through a migo that he could have denied that fact? The point remains unresolved.
He is a single witness. Maggid Mishneh understands Rambam to mean that the buyer who contradicts the seller must take a Torah oath because the seller is one witness, while the buyer supported by the seller would be exempt from the rabbinic oath. Kesef Mishneh says he found no clear proof that Rambam holds the latter exemption. Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah is explicit that if the seller knew the buyer and testified, his testimony would have the status of one witness. The opposing buyer would then take a Torah oath because of that witness, rather than merely the Mishnah's rabbinic oath. Rambam adds that neither buyer would receive more of the garment unless another witness joined the seller. His failure to say that the supported buyer is exempt implies that the seller does not exempt him from the rabbinic oath. The seller's testimony serves only to create a Torah oath against the opponent. Kesef Mishneh also understood Rambam as following Rashi, that where the seller received money from only one person he may always be believed. That too is not evident from Rambam's wording. The Gemara's question in Bava Metzia can be explained differently. Suppose two parties each say, "The whole garment is mine," and witnesses prove with certainty that half belongs to one. The remaining doubt concerns only the other half, so the proven owner receives his certain half and the disputed half is divided. He therefore receives three quarters and the other receives one quarter. So too, if the seller is one witness that the whole item was sold to Reuven, the opponent must swear against that witness regarding the whole. If he swears only the Mishnah's formula, that he owns no less than half, he has contradicted the witness only regarding half. As to the other half, where he did not swear against the witness, the single witness stands like two and that half belongs entirely to Reuven. The opponent therefore receives only one quarter. Rosh gives an analogous rule concerning identifying signs plus one witness: until the party swears to contradict the witness, the witness stands as though two. According to the Shulchan Aruch there, the oath can be omitted where it would accomplish nothing because the object remains deposited in any event. Here, however, swearing against the seller as to the whole changes the result and enables the buyer to receive half. Therefore it is not a purposeless oath. This explains the Gemara's question. If the seller is one witness, how can the Mishnah say each party swears only that he owns no less than half and then they divide? Unless the opponent swears against the seller concerning the whole, he should receive only one quarter.
For he is not interested in his testimony. Hagahot Ashri writes that nowadays, when hesset applies, the seller may be considered interested because the losing buyer can swear him and say, "Give me the merchandise I bought from you." A witness who himself requires an oath is not a valid witness. The Shach suggests that the buyer might have pulled the item from the seller, left it there, and the seller later gave it to the other buyer, and therefore the seller would need to be released from an oath. The decisors do not state such a special exemption, and their silence suggests another explanation. Even if one buyer says, "I bought it from you by a valid kinyan and you later gave it to the other," the item is now held by the other buyer. The seller can no longer return the very object and would at most owe its value as of the wrongful taking, payable in money or best quality property. The present dispute between the buyers concerns the physical object, not a monetary difference against the seller. He therefore has no monetary interest in which buyer receives the object, provided there is no price differential. Hagahot Ashri may be speaking where the object was bought cheaply and the seller's monetary exposure changes depending on the testimony. Even there, however, the seller should not necessarily be disqualified. While the merchandise was in his hand he was believed even without a migo, as Ramban explains. If the claimant says, "I bought it from you and you gave it to the other," the seller's delivery can itself be treated as reliable proof that he acted correctly, much like a shalish whose trust survives even after he delivered the escrowed item. Rashba writes that once it is known the shalish delivered the item, his possession and prior authority show that he delivered it according to the escrow terms. Thus if the buyer admits the seller was the one who delivered the object, the seller can remain reliable. If the buyer says he seized it from the seller or from the other buyer, then the seller obviously has no personal monetary interest and remains one witness. This explains why the decisors did not automatically disqualify him.
Whether he took money from one. According to Rabbeinu Chananel, when the seller received money from one buyer we presume he agreed to sell to that buyer. If he says he actually agreed to the other buyer, his statement can still function as one witness. A single witness is stronger than ordinary presumptions for purposes of creating a Torah oath. Wherever two witnesses would obligate payment, one witness obligates an oath, and two witnesses can overcome even strong presumptions. Rabbeinu Chananel's limitation applies to the earlier case where the seller's merchandise is still in his hand and he would otherwise be believed like two witnesses through his special credibility. There, if he says he agreed to the nonpaying buyer, his migo cannot overcome the strong presumption arising from the payment. But once he has only the status of one witness, the witness can testify even against that presumption and obligate an oath. This is the meaning of Rama's statement that there is no difference whether he took money from one. Even if he testifies that he agreed to the other buyer, his testimony retains the force of one witness.
