Seif 2. The owner of the merchandise is believed. The rule that a seller is believed while the merchandise remains in his possession requires study regarding a non-Jew or other persons disqualified from testimony, such as a minor old enough to conduct small transactions, and especially where two disabilities combine, such as a minor non-Jew. The Shach in siman 149 writes that a defendant may sometimes be required to take a hesset oath based on a non-Jew's information. Perhaps even there two weaknesses would be insufficient, or perhaps the oath is imposed only because the information creates some supporting indication. The seller's special credibility may be weakened even by one disqualification. It is possible that the seller's credibility was not stated as an independent law but is learned from the rule that a person is believed concerning matters in his control, as in issur law, where even a woman may be believed. This requires study. It is likewise unclear whether the rule applies to land where the seller or landlord still exercises practical control. If the rationale is migo, the same migo can exist with land. If the rationale is that the seller carefully remembers the transaction, that too may apply. But if the rationale is that he functions quasi-judicially because the merchandise is physically in his hand, perhaps it applies only to movables. If the rule based on careful recollection is itself merely a rabbinic enactment, a doubt concerning the scope of the enactment should perhaps be resolved narrowly, especially because the language of the Sages expressly concerns movables. Only the migo rationale clearly extends to land.
