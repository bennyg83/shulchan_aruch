Where the sale is still under his control. From the unqualified wording it appears that even if he took money from one party and says that he consented not to that party but to the other, who did not pay, he is believed. This follows the Rif and Rashi, who explain that his credibility is based on the fact that he is careful to ascertain the truth so as not to incur the sanction of “He Who exacted punishment,” or because while the merchandise remains in his possession he is like a judge who is believed to say, “I awarded it to this one.” This is unlike Rabbeinu Chananel, who explains that his credibility rests on migo, according to which he is not believed to say that he consented to the one who did not pay, since this would be a migo against witnesses, for we are regarded as witnesses that he consented to the one who paid. It is also possible that this view maintains that even after the merchandise leaves his possession he is believed like two witnesses for the reason above, and that the unqualified statement that he is like one witness concerns a case in which he took money from both parties. This is hinted at by the statement that he has no personal interest, because if he took from only one party and says that he consented to the one who paid, he certainly has an interest, since perhaps it would be difficult to collect from the other. It must therefore concern a case where he took money from both. If he took from only one, he is believed in either event: if he says that he consented to the one who paid, no special credibility is required, since there is a presumption that he consented to him; and if he says that he consented to the one who did not pay, since he states the less plausible account he certainly took care to ascertain the truth. According to this, the Rama's statement that there is no difference whether he took from one party is difficult, for it follows neither opinion. According to the Rif, even after the merchandise has left his possession he is believed like two witnesses where he took from one party, while according to Rabbeinu Chananel he is not believed to say that he consented to the one who did not pay even while the merchandise remains in his possession. One must therefore say that the Rama's statement applies only to the first clause, where the merchandise is still in his possession, but in the latter clause, when he took from only one party, he is believed (Sema). The Shach writes that the principal view is that of Rabbeinu Chananel, and he is not believed to say that he consented to the one who did not pay. This applies specifically where there are witnesses, or where it was established through the buyers, who gave the money. But where it is known only from the seller who paid, even if witnesses know that one of them paid but do not know which one, the seller is believed by migo. When the Rama writes that there is no difference whether he took from one party, he means that when it is not known who paid, the seller is believed to say to whom he consented or who paid. But where witnesses know who paid, he is not believed to say that he consented to the one who did not pay. This is hinted at in the conclusion, “Therefore, if he took from two and does not know,” for if he took from one, presumably it is known from whom he took, and then we say that he presumably consented to that person and the seller is not believed to say that he consented to the other. Nevertheless, his status is that of one witness, because one witness is effective against such a presumption. So writes the Ketzot HaChoshen. It is clear that the principal practical rule is as follows: if there was only payment of money, and even if one party performed meshichah but the seller contradicts him and says that he did not consent to his sale, that party's kinyan is of no effect. The seller is believed because, when he contradicts the claim, the claim is directed against the seller himself, and the seller has both possession of the money and the presumption of prior ownership. Consequently, he can transfer the item to another by a valid kinyan. But where both parties already performed a kinyan before witnesses, and there are witnesses that the seller consented to one of them and told him, “Pull and acquire,” but they do not know which one, it is then known by witnesses that the merchandise has already left the seller's domain and certainly belongs to one of the two. The seller's assertion that he did not consent to one rather than the other is then merely testimony between them. Therefore the principal view follows Rabbeinu Chananel, that he is not believed to say that he consented to the party who did not pay. Even where it was established only from the seller who paid, there is nevertheless no migo. Certainly where it was established from the buyers who paid, the Shach himself writes that the seller is not believed. But even where each buyer claims that he paid and the seller says that Reuven paid while he consented to Shimon, this is not a migo on the theory that he could have said that Reuven's money belonged to Shimon; see there for the reason. If it is known that he consented to Reuven when Reuven paid and performed meshichah, all agree that the seller is not believed to say that he retracted after payment but before meshichah unless he proves this through witnesses.
He is like one witness. If both parties are holding the object, the party whom the seller supports is exempt from an oath, and the opposing party takes a Torah oath (Sema). This applies specifically where each claims that the seller gave it to him and that the other is holding it unlawfully, so that each thereby exempts the seller from an oath. But where one claims that the seller sold it to him by kinyan sudar and the seller was negligent and gave it to the other, the seller himself owes an oath in response to that claim and is therefore a witness who requires an oath, who is not a valid witness. This applies specifically where the claimant asserts that he made a kinyan. Mere payment of money, although according to the buyer's claim it subjects the seller to the sanction of “He Who exacted punishment,” does not disqualify the seller from serving as a witness. See Beurim: this applies specifically when the merchandise is worth more than the money paid. If the merchandise is worth only the amount that is being returned to him, the seller is not a witness who requires an oath, because there is no denial of money.
Therefore, if he took. Meaning, since the case in which both parties hold the item and each takes an oath can arise here only by rabbinic enactment, it can occur only in this manner, namely, where he took money from both and does not know. If he took from only one, even if he does not know, we nevertheless say that he certainly consented to that party. The authorities dispute only where the seller contradicts this; where the seller himself does not know, all agree that he presumably consented to the one who paid. It must also be a case where he does not know, for if he does know, at times he is believed like two witnesses and at times like one witness, in which case the opposing party takes a Torah oath.
