(Seif 1.) The owner of the sale is believed. There are two principal approaches to this law. According to the Rosh's and Rabbeinu's understanding of the Rif, the seller's having taken money from one claimant is an advantage, because the payment causes him to remember to whom he sold, even if he says that he consented to sell to the person who did not give the money. The reason appears to be that when he accepted the money he necessarily considered in what capacity he was taking it, whether to retain it or to return it. Since he would need to know to whom it should be returned, the money causes him to remember the truth. They therefore explain the Gemara's question, “Let us see from whom he took the money,” as asking that even after the object has left his possession he should be believed, because taking the money enables him to recall the essence of the transaction. The answer is that the case concerns one who took money from both and does not remember which payment he accepted willingly. Thus, possession of the object adds no special credibility in favor of one claimant where he took money from both; but where he took from only one, his credibility is the same whether the object remains in his possession or not. Rabbeinu's text must therefore be emended, for the present order is reversed. The correct reading is: According to the Rif, whenever the object remains in his possession he is believed, whether he took money from both, one voluntarily and one against his will, and does not know which was which; and if he took money from one, even where the receipt is known through witnesses, he is believed to say that he consented to the other. Otherwise the text is astonishing, for how could he be believed against witnesses? Nor would the later phrase, “to the one who did not give the money,” fit a case where he accepted money from both. “Known through witnesses” means that the receipt of the money is known, but not which payment was voluntary. Rabbeinu's wording, “or which of them gave the money against his will,” rather than simply “which gave it against his will,” teaches that we do not require two distinct points of knowledge, namely that one gave willingly and the other unwillingly. Knowledge of the latter alone suffices. I have seen one who sought proof for the uncorrected text from the Ramban in Milchamot, who writes that even if a seller accepted money from one in the presence of witnesses and willingly, he is believed to say that he had sold to the other before or afterward, who acquired the garment by pulling. This is no proof. The Ramban discusses a case where witnesses know only about one party and nothing about the other; it is therefore possible that the other truly acquired it. Rabbeinu's text, by contrast, states expressly that witnesses establish that the second person's payment was taken against the seller's will, and he surely cannot be believed against them. The emendation is therefore clear. Rabbeinu Hananel and the Rosh disagree with the Rif in two respects. First, for them taking money from one is a major disadvantage when the seller testifies that he consented to the other, for the circumstances themselves testify otherwise. Second, when the object is no longer in his possession, the seller has no credibility beyond that of an ordinary single witness. Only while the object is in his possession, and he has a migo, is he believed more than an ordinary witness. They therefore explain the Gemara's question concerning the money as referring to a case where the object remains in the seller's possession: Why should he be believed, even then, to say that he consented to the opposing claimant where the adverse circumstance is that he took money from only one? The Gemara answers that he took money from both and we do not know to whom he consented. Only then is the seller believed while the object remains in his possession, since he knows the truth. The Rambam, however, states without qualification that the seller is believed while the object remains in his possession and is not believed after it leaves his possession. If he agreed with Rabbeinu Hananel, he should have explained the adverse effect of taking money from one. If he agreed with the Rif as understood by the Rosh, he should have explained that after the object leaves his possession he is believed where he took money from only one. The Sema suggests that the Rambam hinted to this by writing that after the object leaves his possession he is believed only as a single witness. The Sema says this must refer specifically to one who accepted money from both, since if he accepted from only one he does not testify even as a single witness. This is not persuasive. The Rambam would hardly omit the novel rule that after the object leaves his possession he can sometimes be believed more than a single witness and leave it merely hinted. Moreover, why must he have any personal interest? The case may be one in which each litigant brings the purchase money and places it in his hands, so the witness has no financial interest. The Rambam's description of the seller as a single witness then means simply that his law is that of any other single witness. The Sema further writes that the Rambam considered it obvious that if the seller took money from one he is believed more than a single witness and therefore did not need to state it. This is difficult, for the Rambam expressly says that once the object leaves his possession he is only a single witness because he has no personal interest. This teaches that he at least retains the advantage of any other witness and is no worse. How much more necessary would it have been to state that he is sometimes believed even more than a single witness because he took money from one. It therefore appears to me that the Rambam follows a third approach. Once the object leaves the seller's possession, he is never believed more than a single witness, under any circumstances. The Gemara's question, “Let us see from whom he took the money,” concerns a case where the object is no longer in his possession. The question is that the claimant against whom he testifies should at least be obligated to take a Torah oath, while the claimant whom he supports should be entirely exempt, since the seller supports him. The Gemara answers that he took money from both and does not know which he accepted willingly, so there is no testimony at all and each claimant swears under the enactment of the Sages. The Sema cites this explanation in the name of Baal HaMaor and writes that it is also the Rambam's view. It appears clear to me that the Rambam likewise understood the Rif in this manner. The Rif never granted a seller whose object has left his possession any credibility beyond that of an ordinary single witness. The Rif writes that where the sale no longer emerges from beneath the seller's hand, he is not believed to say, “I took from this one willingly and from that one against my will.” The Rosh inferred that if he took money from only one he is indeed believed. The Rambam, however, did not read the Rif that way. The Rif merely used the exact expression stated in the Gemara, which discusses taking from both. In such a case the seller has no credibility beyond that of an ordinary witness. The Rif then asks that at the beginning of Bava Metzia it appears that if the seller knows, he is believed more than an ordinary witness. The Rif answers that the passage there concerns the object still in the seller's possession. Once it has left his possession, he has no advantage over another witness. In the chapter Asara Yuhasin, the Rif's text likewise reads, “and it is not known.” This removes the Rosh's objection there against the Rif. The conclusion of the Rif's words at the beginning of Bava Metzia also proves this: where the object no longer remains in the seller's hand but both claimants are grasping it, the seller is not believed to say, “I did not sell it to this one,” unless another witness joins him, as in ordinary testimony. This indicates that he has no advantage in any respect over a single witness. This is, in my humble opinion, the correct understanding of the Rif, followed by the Rambam. It is also the Rama's intent when he inserts into the Rambam's ruling, “whether he took money from one,” etc. The Sema wondered at this and therefore moved the gloss to the opening clause, where the object remains in the seller's possession, following his understanding of the Rif. According to what I have written, everything is resolved. The Rambam's words retain their plain meaning, exactly as the Rama cited them, and the Rif's words are likewise well explained according to the Rambam's understanding. This appears correct.
