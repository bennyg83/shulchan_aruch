(Seif 4, in the gloss.) I am the buyer, etc. Rabbi Mordechai Yaffe, followed by the Sema, writes that this claimant must swear. I am astonished that two such lions should say this, for it appears that no oath is required. The source is Kiddushin 63, concerning a father who says, “I betrothed my daughter, but I do not know to whom,” and one man comes and says, “I betrothed her.” He is believed. We rule there like Rav Asi that he is believed even to marry her. Rashi explains that a person would not be so brazen as to say before the father who accepted the betrothal, “I am the man,” because he fears that the father may contradict him and say, “I know that you did not give me the betrothal money.” This law concerning the sale of a field is derived from there. If this reasoning suffices to permit a woman whose marital status is uncertain, where no oath is relevant, it certainly suffices in the less stringent field of monetary law and grants him the property without an oath. This also follows from the language of the Hagahot Maimoniyot in chapter 9 of Hilchot Ishut: “It appears to the Riva that he is believed, because the seller admits that the property is not his. The claimant can say to the seller: Do you not admit that it is not yours? Go your way. If another comes and refutes me with witnesses, I will return it to him.” Thus, the seller is not his litigant at all. Who, then, can impose an oath upon him? In my humble opinion there is therefore no place for this oath, which is why neither the Hagahot Maimoniyot nor the Rama mentioned it.
