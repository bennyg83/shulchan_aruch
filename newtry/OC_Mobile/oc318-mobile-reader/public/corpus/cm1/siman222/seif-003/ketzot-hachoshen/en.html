And he swore falsely. Where the underlying obligation itself arose through a transgression, such as theft from five people, or, in Even HaEzer, prohibited kiddushin by intercourse creating the relevant monetary obligation, he must pay each claimant because the liability originated in the wrongful act. Here, by contrast, the sale itself was made permissibly. The later false oath does not by itself create a civil payment obligation. If he later repents and admits, the Torah's laws of a false custodial oath and denial of entrusted property impose restitution. But so long as he does not confess in repentance, the false oath alone does not generate an independent monetary obligation. One should not object that one who wishes to fulfill his heavenly duty must pay anyway. The earlier rule that one should pay each claimant to satisfy Heaven is only a measure of piety. Here, once he confesses after a false oath concerning entrusted property or a hand placed upon another's property, Scripture itself imposes restitution. Since the liability then arises from the transgression, he must pay each claimant, like one who stole from five people. Without repentance and confession, however, there is no such additional payment obligation. The original sale was lawful.
