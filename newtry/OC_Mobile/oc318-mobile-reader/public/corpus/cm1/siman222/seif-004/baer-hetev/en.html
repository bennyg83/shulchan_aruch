Taz writes: Rama MiFano and, following him, Sema state that an oath is required. I am astonished that two such lions should say this, for it appears that no oath applies here. This rule is found in Kiddushin 63 regarding kiddushin: if a father says, 'I betrothed my daughter but do not know to whom,' and one man comes and says, 'I am the one who betrothed her,' he is believed. We rule there like Rav Asi that he is believed even to marry her. Rashi explains that a person would not brazenly say before the father who received the kiddushin, 'I am the one,' because he fears that the father may contradict him and say, 'I know that you did not give me the kiddushin.' This law regarding sale of a field is derived from there. If this rationale is strong enough to permit a woman whose marital status is doubtful, where no oath is relevant, then certainly it suffices for the lesser matter of money. This is also implied by Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Ishut chapter 9, which says that he is believed because the seller admits that the property is not his. The claimant may tell the seller, 'Do you not admit that it is not yours? Go away. If someone comes and refutes me with witnesses, I will return it to him.' Thus the seller is not his litigant at all. Who, then, could impose an oath upon him? It therefore appears to me that there is no place for this oath, which is why Hagahot Maimoniyot and Rama do not mention it. Taz.
