We do not listen to him. Nevertheless, after payment he may require the other party to take a heset oath, as stated below in siman 88, seif 22. See there.
Likewise, those who swear and collect, etc. He cannot reverse the oath, etc. Tosafos in Perek HaKosev, Kesubos 88a, and the Agudah there, wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak that he can reverse it. This also appears from Rashi there, 87b, who wrote that Rami bar Chama initially thought it was a Torah oath, with the practical difference being that it could not be reversed. The Mordechai in Perek HaSholeach likewise wrote this in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak, and Tosafos in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim, Shevuos 41a, wrote the same. The plain sense of the Gemara there also appears to support this. Although I write below, seif 13, seif katan 38, that the passage also addresses a heset oath, that is because the Gemara placed the question, what is the difference between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath, after Rav Nachman's enactment, showing that the discussion includes heset. Yet since the Gemara asks generally what difference there is between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath, rather than what difference there is between a Torah oath and Rav Nachman's oath, and answers that a Torah oath cannot be reversed whereas a rabbinic oath can, this indicates that every rabbinic oath may be reversed. Indeed, Shaarei HaRif writes that the Gemara there concerns a Mishnaic oath, not heset. Although I disagree with him below in seif 13, that is only because the Gemara also concerns heset. It nevertheless also concerns a Mishnaic oath. As for the Rif and Rosh in Perek HaKosev, who write that the defendant may say, if you wish to collect, swear, and if you do not swear you do not collect, while I will not swear because you have no claim against me, one can answer that even regarding heset the defendant could say, I will not swear. Rather, we place him under a ban. Here too, if the defendant does not wish to accept the reversal and swear, although we do not descend to his property, we nevertheless place him under a ban. This appears correct according to Rashi, Tosafos, and those who agree with them. It also appears that even those who, by strict law, should not collect even with an oath, but the Sages enacted that they collect with an oath, such as a hired worker, one who was robbed, one who was injured, and the like, may, according to Rashi, Tosafos, and those who agree with them, reverse the oath. Although the Ran in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim, cited by the Beit Yosef in seif 16, distinguishes such cases, that follows his approach that the Gemara's question concerns heset alone, and therefore he writes that we learned no rule of reversal regarding a Mishnaic oath. But Rashi and Tosafos clearly explain that the Gemara concerns both heset and Mishnaic oaths. If so, the Gemara's unqualified reference to a rabbinic oath includes every Mishnaic oath. The reason appears to me to be that even where strict law would not allow the claimant to collect even with an oath, once the Sages of the Mishna enacted for his benefit that he collect with his own oath against the defendant's will, they certainly also enacted for his benefit that he be able to reverse that oath onto the defendant against the defendant's will. This is a sound point. Examine it carefully. This is how the view of Rashi, Tosafos, and those who agree with them appears to me, and it is also explicit in Shaarei HaRif, Shaar 17. See there. It further appears that if one who is obligated to take a Mishnaic oath in order to collect has already seized property, even if he seized it in the presence of witnesses, he may say, I rely on Rashi, Tosafos, and those who agree with them, and I will not return it until you take that oath by reversal while holding a sacred object. The plain sense of the Gemara also supports Rashi and Tosafos. Moreover, many Geonim hold that even a Torah oath may be reversed. Although I wrote above in seif 10 that if one seizes and refuses to swear, we place him under a ban according to all opinions, that is where he refuses to swear altogether and also refuses to reverse the oath. Here, by contrast, he does reverse it. Although accepted practice follows the Rif, Rosh, Rambam, Tur, and those who agree with them, that a Mishnaic oath required for collection cannot be reversed, it may be that the accepted practice was so only because one could not impose an oath in a case of doubt, which would be a leniency for the defendant, as I write below in seif 13. There is another practical difference between Rashi, Tosafos, and those who agree with them, and the Rambam and Tur. If the borrower has one witness that the note was paid, the holder of the note must swear. According to Rashi and Tosafos he may reverse that very oath, which arose because of the witness, and the witness can then no longer serve as a supporting witness. According to the Rambam and Tur, however, he cannot reverse that very oath. He can only say that he does not wish to avail himself of the rabbinic enactment and will stand like any oral claimant. But where he became obligated to swear because of the witness, he cannot say this, for if he does so and his claim is treated as an oral claim without a document, the defendant will be entirely exempt because he then has a supporting witness who exempts him from an oath, as I write below in seif 12. Examine this carefully.
Oaths that arise from uncertainty may not be reversed, etc. It appears to me that where one swears and is exempt by rabbinic law on a definite claim, while holding a sacred object in a manner resembling a Torah oath, all agree that he may reverse that very rabbinic oath, with the sacred object, onto the claimant, who must then swear and collect. Examples include one who claims, regarding a deposit evidenced by a document, that he returned it, where he is believed only through a migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable accident, and therefore must swear that he returned it in the manner of a Torah oath, as below in siman 296, seif 2; an employer who disputes a hired worker regarding the amount fixed, according to the Rosh, Rambam, and Tur below in siman 89, seif 4; or one who tells a hired worker, I paid you, where there is one witness to the hiring, according to the Ramah and the second opinion in the gloss below, siman 89, seif 2. The same applies to all similar cases. One should not infer otherwise from the Rambam in chapter 1 of Hilchos To'en, who writes that only a heset oath can be reversed, but a Torah oath or a rabbinic oath that resembles a Torah oath cannot be reversed. There he only means to exclude those who swear and collect or those who swear on an uncertain claim, as is explicit from the Rambam there. He does not address the category I described, where one swears and is exempt on a definite claim. The proof is that he writes there: one who is obligated to a rabbinic oath among those who swear and are exempt, such as those who swear on an uncertain claim, or one obligated to heset, and who refuses to swear, is placed under a ban, etc. Had he meant to include those who swear and are exempt on a definite claim, he should have listed them as well. Rather, the matter is as I wrote. There is another proof. The Tur and Mechaber also hold that a rabbinic oath of one who swears and collects cannot be reversed, yet they write that those who swear and are exempt by rabbinic law on an uncertain claim cannot reverse the oath because the claimant himself advances only an uncertain claim. This implies that were that reason absent, they could reverse it. The Tur's ruling in the name of the Ramah proves the same. He writes that if the claimant says, swear to me that you truly suspect me of this, the law is with him, because there is no oath imposed on him that he is unable to swear. This implies that when an oath is imposed on him and he is able to swear, the defendant may reverse that very oath, with a sacred object, onto him. This is also explicit in the Sema, seif katan 25. Examine carefully. The Beit Yosef likewise writes explicitly at the end of seif 16, regarding the Rambam's statement that those who swear on an uncertain claim cannot reverse the oath, that perhaps the Rambam's reason is that in truth he could reverse it like heset if it were possible for the claimant to swear, but because the claimant advances an uncertain claim, he cannot swear, and therefore the oath cannot be reversed. In my view there is no uncertainty here. This is plainly the Rambam's reason, as is evident to one who studies his words. There is yet another proof, for the Rambam's very reason that one who swears and collects cannot reverse the oath is, as he writes there, because the defendant can say, swear and collect as the Sages enacted for you, and if you do not wish to swear, go away. The Tur writes the same. This derives from the Rif in Perek HaKosev regarding a single witness who testifies that a note was paid. The Rif writes that if the debtor is clever he can bring the matter to a Torah oath, and explains that the practical difference cannot be reversal, since even though the present oath is rabbinic it cannot be reversed. A rabbinic oath is reversed only from defendant to claimant, because the defendant can say, you have no claim against me. If you speak truthfully and have money due from me, swear and collect. Thus, if the claimant wishes to collect, he swears and collects, and if not, his claim falls away. But when the claimant is the one obligated to swear, he cannot reverse the oath onto the defendant, because when he says, swear that you owe me nothing, or that you paid me such and such, and be exempt, the defendant can answer, I do not seek to swear and thereby become exempt. Although that would be advantageous to me, I do not seek it. Rather, if you wish to swear and collect, very well; if not, go, for you have no claim against me. The Rosh writes likewise. This reasoning clearly implies that in the category I described, those who swear and are exempt by rabbinic law in the manner of a Torah oath can reverse it. The opening words of the Rif and Rosh also indicate this, since they say that a rabbinic oath is reversed only from defendant to claimant. All this appears entirely clear to me. There is a further possibility: the oaths I described may themselves be included in the category of heset, because the Rambam calls every oath not explicitly mentioned in the Mishna heset. I wrote similarly below in siman 89, seif 5, regarding the Rambam. The proof is that Rashi writes in Perek Kol HaNishba'in that even before Rav Nachman there was a heset oath in certain matters. The Mordechai in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh likewise records in the name of earlier authorities that in the days of the Tannaim a heset oath was enacted in some matters. Thus the Rambam can also hold this way. Accordingly, when he writes that no oath may be reversed except heset, he includes the oaths I described, since they are within the category of heset. Examine carefully. It further appears that the Rambam says only heset can be reversed because, although the oaths I described can indeed be reversed as they stand, with a sacred object, the claimant can say, I do not require you to hold a sacred object, as the Rambam and Tur write immediately below in seif 12. The same applies here. He can say so, and we are certainly not dealing with fools, for it is obvious that he will waive the sacred object so that the defendant can reverse upon him only a heset oath, as Mahari Chayyun writes in his rulings, siman 32, and the Rama in his gloss. Consequently, there is no practical difference in reversing such an oath. This is why the Rambam says that the only oath whose reversal remains exactly as the defendant reverses it is a heset oath. This is clear.
If he claims, let him swear to me that he truly suspects me of this, etc. The Rama writes in Bedek HaBayis: there is reason for wonder, since this is an uncertain claim and one does not swear on such a claim. Perhaps he means merely a general ban and was imprecise. The Bach disagrees and explains that an actual oath with a sacred object is intended. This is also explicit from the Rama's gloss, the Levush, and the Sema, and follows from the wording of the Ramah, who says that no oath rests upon him that he is unable to take. As to Bedek HaBayis's question that this is an uncertain claim, it appears to me that although ordinarily one does not swear on an uncertain claim, here, since the partners' oath was enacted specifically to be taken on suspicion, because one suspects that perhaps the other retained something, the same type of uncertain oath can be reversed. The claimant can be required to swear that he indeed suspects him, even though the defendant cannot know with certainty that the claimant does not suspect him. Perhaps this is also the Bach's intent. Examine carefully.
Nevertheless, etc. It appears that even after he has reversed it, he can still say, either you take the lighter oath, or I will take that lighter oath myself, meaning without holding a sacred object. For why should the defendant no longer retain the choice and lose anything from his original position? So writes Mahari Chayyun in his writings, siman 32. In all of this there is a practical difference only regarding the partners' oath. Examine carefully.
