He cannot shift it. The Shach writes that many great authorities hold that a Mishnaic oath can be shifted to the opposing party. He adds that there is a practical distinction even according to those who hold that a Mishnaic oath cannot itself be shifted. The claimant can always say, "I do not want the rabbinic enactment in my favor. Treat my claim as an ordinary oral claim and require the defendant to take hesset." The distinction arises where one witness testifies that the debt was paid. If the defendant is required to swear because the claimant shifted the original oath to him, then the witness who testified to payment is not considered a supporting witness, because that very witness created the claimant's oath in the first place. But if the claimant abandons the special collection mechanism and merely says, "Take hesset that you owe me nothing," then the witness stands independently on the defendant's side and exempts him. The Shach thus assumes as settled law that where one witness testifies that a note was paid, the noteholder must take the relevant oath, and if that oath is shifted to the debtor the same witness does not then exempt the debtor. This is logically persuasive, because otherwise money would effectively be extracted through a single witness who first creates the oath and then determines its reversal. There is also support from Tosafot and the Rosh. They explain the Gemara's advice, "If he is clever, he should bring her to a Torah oath," as useful because a Torah oath cannot be shifted. If one witness supporting the debtor automatically exempted him after a shift, their distinction would collapse. They themselves hold that a supporting witness ordinarily exempts. Thus they seem to agree that where the same witness created the original oath, he cannot also serve as the supporting witness after the oath is shifted. Maharam of Rothenburg, however, does not appear to understand the matter that way. Hagahot Maimoniyot to Hilchot Shevuot, chapter 11, cites him as I explained above in siman 84. Maharam notes that Rashi explains one view concerning an impaired ketubah as a Torah oath, with the practical consequence that it cannot be shifted. Yet when Rashi explains the phrase "if he is clever," he gives the distinction of whether the oath requires holding a sacred object. Maharam explains that Rashi distinguishes because in the case of one witness testifying that the ketubah was paid, if the oath were shifted, that very witness would support the debtor and exempt him. Thus the only remaining difference is the manner of the oath. Maharam therefore reads Rashi as holding explicitly that even though the witness originally created the oath, he can later operate as a supporting witness after the oath is shifted. Tosafot and the Rosh can perhaps be explained differently. The woman need not directly contradict the witness by saying she received nothing. She can say, for example, "I indeed received the money, but I returned it to the husband," or "That payment was for another debt," and similar claims. She can be believed on such a claim by virtue of a migo that she could have contradicted the witness outright and sworn. If the husband then demands an oath concerning that additional assertion, she can shift that oath to him, for the witness does not testify about whether the money was returned or allocated to another debt. In that respect the witness does not support him. Thus their statement that shifting remains meaningful need not prove the Shach's broader principle. At first glance one might add that since her credibility on the additional claim derives only from the migo of directly contradicting the witness with a Torah oath, the derivative claim should itself carry the same nonshiftable character as the original claim. Just as she could not shift the Torah oath had she directly contradicted the witness, perhaps she cannot improve her position by choosing a claim whose credibility comes only from that migo. Indeed, where one witness testifies to a specific object and the defendant says, "I seized it for another reason," the case can resemble Rabbi Abba's piece of metal, because she is not actually contradicting the witness about the physical payment. See above siman 58. It is possible that Maharam would agree with this and intended Rashi's explanation only in the setting where the additional claim is itself directly supported. Nevertheless, this is not the natural reading of Tosafot and the Rosh, and Maharam's understanding of Rashi also seems difficult on independent grounds. It is counterintuitive to say that an oath generated by one witness is lighter than the oath of an impaired note. In the one witness case the witness is actively declaring that the claimant was paid. Why should that oath require fewer formalities? What compelled Rashi to invent such a distinction between categories of oath? There is another textual difficulty. If according to the view that the witness generated a Torah oath the practical distinction from a rabbinic oath were whether a sacred object must be held, Rashi should have explained that distinction when the Torah oath was first introduced. Instead Rashi mentions it only in connection with the later phrase "if he is clever." This suggests a different structure. I therefore believe Rashi's position is as follows, in line with what I wrote above in siman 84. The Gemara considers the possibility that a woman who impaired her ketubah even by less than a perutah, or reduced it in a strategic way, might manipulate the facts merely to avoid the heavier oath. What manipulation would be gained if the husband could in any event say, "Swear to me"? The answer is that Rashi distinguishes between oaths expressly listed by the Mishnah and the later hesset type oath. The oaths enumerated in the Mishnah are accompanied by holding a sacred object, while hesset does not require that formality. Rashi appears to hold that even in the era of the Mishnah there existed lesser rabbinic oaths comparable in form to hesset. What matters is not chronology but whether the oath belongs to the formal Mishnaic class. Thus the claimant may prefer to structure the case so that she avoids the formal Mishnaic oath and faces only a lighter hesset style oath if the opposing party directly challenges her. Accordingly, this also explains why, when the Gemara asks what difference there is between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath, Rashi identifies the rabbinic oath with Rav Nachman's hesset rather than with every Mishnaic oath. Rashi does not necessarily call the formal oaths of those who swear and collect simply "rabbinic oath" for purposes of that contrast. His wording here, where he says "a rabbinic oath," therefore naturally points to the hesset type oath, not the full Mishnaic oath. This resolves the apparent contradiction between Rashi here and Rashi in Gittin, where Rashi requires holding a sacred object for certain Mishnaic oaths. It also explains the Rosh. In Shevuat HaDayanim the Rosh asks why the Gemara did not list holding a sacred object as a distinction between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath, and cites Rashi in HaKotev that in the "if he is clever" case the practical difference is precisely the formal manner of the oath. The Rosh then cites the Geonim that a Torah oath requires holding a sacred object, while hesset does not, but the oaths of those who swear and collect do require it. This would be contradictory if Rashi meant that no Mishnaic oath requires holding an object. According to what I have written, there is no contradiction. Rashi's lighter oath in the "if he is clever" analysis is the hesset type oath, not the full Mishnaic oath. It is possible, then, that Maharam himself agrees with the Shach's principle that one witness should not perform both functions, creating the oath and then exempting the other side after reversal. His difficulty was instead why Rav Pappa needed the special advice. If the husband simply says to the woman, "I do not rely on the witness. I merely demand that you swear hesset," and she tries to shift that hesset, the witness would independently support the husband. Rashi therefore needed to explain that the real advantage of Rav Pappa's strategy is to force the heavier Torah form of oath. Tosafot and the Rosh need not face this exact question. Perhaps, as I wrote above in siman 84, the case concerns a debt within its due date, where hesset cannot ordinarily be demanded without the witness. Or perhaps if the husband makes no use of the witness and demands only hesset, the woman is willing to swear because the oath is light and does not require holding a sacred object. But if he formally sues on the basis of the witness, she may prefer to shift the oath to avoid later being branded disqualified if both the husband and witness establish her falsehood. Thus a Torah oath blocks the strategic reversal in a way hesset does not. In any event, the underlying logic of the Shach is sound: a witness whose testimony is the very source of the oath cannot ordinarily serve as the supporting witness that eliminates the reciprocal oath after a shift. There is also strong support from the Rashba, cited by the Ran in Perek Shevuat HaEdut. In a case where the defendant says, "I know about fifty and do not know about fifty," the rule is that because he cannot swear he must pay, and the opposing party does not need to take an oath. This is how the Shulchan Aruch rules in siman 75 seif 13. The Gemara's discussion of Rabbi Abba's one witness case likewise indicates that the opposing party does not need a new oath. Yet the witness who testified against the defendant is certainly a witness supporting the claimant. The Rashba elsewhere, responsum 1061, recognizes that a supporting witness can exempt from a rabbinic oath. Why then is there not even a theoretical reciprocal oath here from which the witness exempts? The answer is the Shach's principle: the witness cannot be counted again as a supporting witness in a procedural chain that his own testimony created. The Shach further understands Rashi and those who hold that a Mishnaic oath may be shifted as extending the rule even to cases where Torah law would not otherwise allow the claimant to collect at all and the Sages created a special right to collect through an oath, because the Gemara says broadly that rabbinic oaths may be shifted. This requires study. Rashi's own explanation of "rabbinic oath" in Shevuot as Rav Nachman's hesset suggests that the statement need not include every special Mishnaic collection oath. Indeed, if every Mishnaic oath were included, the hired worker's oath would also be shiftable. But that is disputed, as I discuss below in siman 89. The reason for the hired worker's enactment is that the employer is occupied with his workers and may honestly be mistaken. Forcing him to swear may therefore lead to a false oath. It is difficult to believe the Sages allowed the worker simply to shift the oath back to the employer and recreate the very danger the enactment was designed to avoid. Accordingly, we should not unnecessarily broaden the power of reversal. In collection oaths whose entire substantive right is a special rabbinic enactment, reversal should not be assumed merely from the general phrase that a rabbinic oath can be shifted. Nor can the possessor simply say kim li otherwise, because the purpose and structure of those enactments point strongly against it.
On a doubtful claim, etc. The Shach writes that where a custodian says, "I returned the deposit," and is believed only through the migo that he could have claimed unavoidable loss, his oath nevertheless has the status of a rabbinic oath and therefore can be shifted to the opposing party. I already challenged this in my rules of migo. This ruling is incorrect. Because the custodian's entire credibility on the return claim depends on a migo whose alternative claim would require a Torah bailee's oath, the present claim retains the legal force of that Torah oath. He cannot convert it into an ordinary rabbinic oath merely by choosing the return formulation. Accordingly, the oath cannot be shifted. See what I wrote there at length.
We do not shift it. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, siman 151, discusses two litigants who have claims against each other, where one is obligated in a Torah oath and the other in a rabbinic oath. He rules that we do not automatically require the person with the Torah oath to swear first. Rather, beit din should cast lots to determine the order. He brings a proof from the Mishnah asking, "Who swears first?" even though one party's oath is Torah level and the other's is rabbinic because of concern that he may have set his eyes on the object. The Mishnah still treats the order as a question, which he understands as showing that neither oath has intrinsic priority. I do not recognize this proof. The Mishnah is not a record of an unresolved debate between a questioner and answerer. It is explaining the law. "Who swears first?" means: one might have thought that the person obligated in the Torah oath should swear first, but the Mishnah teaches otherwise because of the concern stated there. The form of the Mishnah does not show that absent the special reason the order would be irrelevant. Even if we treated the Mishnah as presenting a genuine question about order, there would still be no proof. There are competing considerations. On one side, the person with the Torah oath should perhaps swear first because his obligation is more severe. On the other side, it may be useful to require the second party to swear first because if he refuses, the disputed deposit or object may be released and the first oath may become unnecessary. For example, if the deposited object is returned, there may no longer be any need for the first party to swear about its value. Thus the special facts themselves create a genuine reason to reverse the ordinary priority. The authorities who write that where the borrower owes only hesset, the lender should swear first because otherwise the borrower may swear and thereby frustrate collection, likewise show that order is governed by practical considerations and not merely by formal ranking. This is so even when the lender's oath is significantly more severe than the borrower's hesset. The rule teaches that beit din may structure the oaths to prevent an inequitable or pointless result. One might even read the Mishnah as saying that the specified party must swear first even if both litigants consent to the opposite order. Beit din does not permit an arrangement that creates a concern of desecration of Hashem's name or a needless oath. Thus the Mishnah certainly cannot prove that order is normally determined by lottery. In fact I found explicit support for the opposite view in the Ritva, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. On the Mishnah's question "Who swears first?" the Ritva asks why the answer is not obvious from the general rule that the lender should swear first concerning whether the collateral remains in his possession. He answers that because the borrower's oath is Torah level while the lender's is rabbinic, one might have thought the borrower has priority and should swear first if beit din chooses. The Mishnah therefore teaches that, because of the special concern in that case, the lender must nevertheless swear first. The Ritva thus states explicitly that absent the special reason, the litigant obligated in a Torah oath has the stronger claim to priority. Who can lightly dispute such a clear statement? Even so, the final order remains subject to the discretion of beit din. The Ritva says that the Torah oath would come first "if beit din wishes," showing that the matter is not rigidly mechanical. If judges see that requiring the other oath first is likely to produce a settlement or eliminate a needless oath, they may alter the order for a sound reason. The proper practical ruling is therefore that the Torah oath ordinarily has priority, but beit din may reverse the order when circumstances justify it.
