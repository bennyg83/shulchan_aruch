We do not listen to him. The opposing party may say, "Either follow Torah law and take the oath imposed upon you, or pay me. The Torah did not impose an oath upon me." Likewise, in a rabbinic oath taken by one who swears and collects, the defendant may say, "Either swear and collect according to the Sages' enactment or lose the claim. The Sages did not impose the oath upon me." Certainly this is so where by strict law the claimant could not collect at all even with an oath and the Sages created the collection right only upon oath. Such a claimant plainly cannot reverse the oath.
Likewise those who swear and collect, etc. The Shach holds that if the claimant has already seized property, he can say, "I rely on Rashi and Tosafot, who allow even these oaths to be reversed," and retain the property subject to that position. This is correct. But the Shach further appears to hold that even those who by strict law would not collect at all and only by rabbinic enactment may collect upon oath can reverse the oath. I do not agree, as I wrote in Tumim. The Shach also writes that according to Rashi, if the oath obligation arose because one witness testified that the debt was paid and the claimant reverses the oath, that witness is not then treated as a supporting witness for the defendant, because the same witness generated the oath in the first place. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length and agreed with the Shach as practical law.

If a custodian claims, "I returned the deposit," and is believed only through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident, thereby incurring a custodial oath, the Shach holds that he may nevertheless reverse the oath. In Tumim and in the general rules of migo I wrote that such an oath has the full status of a Torah oath and cannot be reversed at all.
To reverse it. If two parties sue each other and one is obligated in a Torah oath while the other is obligated only in a rabbinic oath, see Tumim. I concluded that the party bearing the Torah oath ordinarily must swear first. If, however, beit din sees that having the other party swear first is likely to bring the dispute to compromise, they may alter the order. See there.
That he suspects me of this. Although this is only an uncertain claim, the underlying oath itself also rests on uncertainty. Shach. According to this reasoning, where a person is sued on the basis of uncertain information received from one witness, he cannot reverse the oath by saying, "Swear that you truly suspect me," because the original oath is based on the claimant's definite reliance on the witness, while we do not impose an oath on the claimant merely from an uncertain counterclaim.
But if there is gilgul. If the defendant says, "I will swear concerning the rolled matter, but you swear concerning the principal claim," he may reverse only the principal claim where reversal is otherwise permitted. Likewise, he can say, "You swear that you truly suspect me concerning the matter you are rolling upon me," because a rolled oath may itself be imposed even on an uncertain claim.
