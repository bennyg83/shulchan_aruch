And likewise those who swear and collect. See Shach, seif katan 30, who writes that if the claimant seized property in the presence of witnesses, he may say that he follows Rashi and those who agree with him. This appears limited to categories of persons who by Torah law would collect without any oath and whose oath was imposed only by rabbinic enactment. If Torah law would not permit collection even with an oath, and the Sages merely granted a special right to collect upon swearing, then saying, "I follow Rashi and will shift the oath to the defendant," cannot help. If the claimant himself refuses the oath on which his rabbinic right depends, beit din cannot descend to the defendant's assets. The claimant must return what he seized because he took it without authority, as Sema seif katan 22 and Shach seif katan 25 explain. Beit din also cannot place the defendant under a ban, because the defendant can respond that he follows Rif and those who hold that this oath cannot be shifted. According to this reasoning, even if the defendant would otherwise have to take a Mishnah oath to be exempt, a claimant who seized without right would have to return the property if the defendant refuses to swear. Ketzot HaChoshen writes similarly. Tumim, however, rules that even where the required oath is hesset, one who seized need not return the property until the other party swears. This also appears from Shach, seif katan 38, who writes that one may retain seized property until the other party takes an oath with a sacred object. It follows that seizure is effective in all these cases.
That arise from uncertainty. See Shach, seif katan 31, who writes that rabbinic oaths imposed on a defendant in response to a definite claim are taken with a sacred object in the manner of a Torah oath. An example is one who claims that he returned a deposit evidenced by a written document. He is believed only through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable loss, and therefore his oath on the return is like a Torah oath. At first glance this is difficult. His credibility for the return comes only from the migo that he could claim unavoidable loss. Had he actually made that alternative claim, he could not shift the oath. Why should he gain the power to shift merely by claiming return? Otherwise he could manipulate the law by choosing the return claim solely to obtain that advantage. There is no difficulty. The defendant can say that the claimant's written deposit document cannot be stronger than an ordinary loan document. Even with a loan document the debtor could demand, "Swear to me that I did not pay you." All the more so with a deposit document he can demand, "Swear to me that I did not return it to you."
That he suspects me of this. It appears from Taz at the beginning of siman 93 that only in the case of partners, where one may impose an oath only when there is some concrete basis for suspicion, can the defendant demand that the claimant swear that he truly suspects him of the alleged misconduct. This implies that a creditor collecting from the assets of orphans, who must take an oath as a matter of law, cannot demand that the orphans swear that they actually suspect him of wrongdoing.
