And likewise those who swear and collect. The Shach rules that a claimant who has already seized property and is subject to one of the Mishnah oaths may say, according to Rashi and Tosafot, "I reverse the oath upon you, and I will not return the property until you swear." He further says that if the opposing party refuses the reversed oath, beit din does not descend to his property but places him under ban. In my view one must distinguish types of claimant oaths. Where Torah law itself would allow the claimant to collect without an oath, and the Sages merely added an oath, such as an ordinary creditor collecting on a document, reversal places the opposing party in a position where refusal to swear permits actual collection. The debtor's entire exemption depended upon the claimant's rabbinic oath. If the claimant validly shifts that oath to the debtor and the debtor refuses, the debtor has not established his defense and beit din may descend to his property. By contrast, in cases such as one who was robbed or injured, the claimant has no right under strict law to collect at all. The Sages created a special right to collect only through the claimant's oath. If the claimant reverses that oath and the defendant refuses, the refusal does not create an underlying Torah debt. At most the court can impose sanctions for refusing the accepted oath. This distinction also controls seizure. Where the claimant's underlying right exists independently and the oath is merely a rabbinic condition, seizure can support a claim that the defendant must swear before the property is returned. Where the claimant's entire right exists only through the special rabbinic oath, seizure cannot create greater rights, particularly where the validity of reversal itself is disputed. In doubtful seizure, the seizer cannot simply say, "I follow the authorities who permit reversal," when even beit din could not clearly descend to the defendant's assets. Accordingly, the Shach's broad rule should be narrowed according to the source of the claimant's right.
That arise from doubt. The Shach says that even a Mishnah oath imposed upon one who swears and is exempt can be reversed, because it is rabbinic, while only claimant oaths or partnership type oaths are excluded from reversal. Taz similarly allows reversal in some rabbinic defendant oaths. Rambam's language, however, is broader: only hesset can be reversed. A Torah oath or a rabbinic oath fashioned like a Torah oath cannot be reversed. The Shach acknowledges the wording but limits it to claimant oaths and partnership oaths. The language does not naturally bear that limitation. For a claim of return concerning a documented deposit, the issue also depends on whether the oath is truly Torah law. According to the Sema, the custodian is believed to say he returned the deposit only through a migo that it was lost through unavoidable accident, and he must therefore take the same Torah oath as in an accident claim. Such an oath cannot be reversed. According to the Shach's own view in siman 296 that the oath is merely rabbinic, he can more readily permit reversal. Still, even if the return oath is Torah law, the depositor can independently say, "Swear to me that you did not return it," just as a borrower can require a creditor holding a document to swear upon a payment claim. The custodian's migo is needed to exempt himself; the depositor's demand for an oath does not depend on that migo. This creates a structure resembling reversal even though the original custodian oath itself remains nonreversible. A similar distinction applies to an employer and worker. If the employer's exemption depends upon a migo, the Torah or rabbinic character of that migo based oath determines whether formal reversal is available. But the employee may have an independent claimant oath under the special wage enactment. The ancient authorities already dispute whether the return oath concerning a documented deposit can be reversed. This is not merely a modern inference and should be treated as a genuine early dispute.
