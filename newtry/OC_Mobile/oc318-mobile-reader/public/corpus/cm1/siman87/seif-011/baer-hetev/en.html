To reverse it. Tosafot in Perek HaKotev, citing Ri, says that the oath may be reversed, and Rashi there and Mordechai also imply this. The Shach reasons that even claimants who under strict law would not collect even with an oath, but whom the Sages specially permitted to collect through their own oath, such as a hired worker, may according to Rashi and Tosafot reverse the oath onto the defendant. Since the Sages enacted the original oath to benefit the claimant and force the defendant to pay upon that oath, all the more so they may allow the claimant to place the oath back on the defendant against the defendant's will. Therefore, if a person who was entitled to collect through a Mishnah oath seized property even before witnesses, he may say, "I rely on Rashi, Tosafot, and their supporters and will not return it until you take the reversed oath while holding a sacred object." The plain sense of the Gemara also supports this, and many Geonim hold that even a Torah oath can be reversed. Earlier the Shach wrote that one who seizes but refuses either to swear or reverse the oath is placed under ban; that is different because here he does reverse it. A further practical difference arises where a borrower has one witness that a debt document was paid. The holder of the document must swear because of the witness. According to Rashi and Tosafot, he can reverse that very oath onto the borrower, and the supporting witness no longer exempts the borrower from the reversed oath. According to Rambam and Tur, however, he cannot reverse it, but may only say, "I decline the Sages' enactment and proceed as an oral claimant." Once the oath was triggered by the witness, he cannot do that, because then he would be treated as an oral claimant without a document and the defendant would be fully exempt by the supporting witness. See Responsa Radbaz 202.
The doubt. The Shach writes that those who swear and are exempt through a rabbinic oath based on a definite claim, while holding a sacred object like a Torah oath, may according to all opinions reverse that same rabbinic oath upon the claimant, who would then swear and collect. Examples include one who claims return of a documented deposit and is believed only through a migo of unavoidable accident, a homeowner disputing the amount of wages with a worker according to Rosh, Rambam, and Tur, or an employer claiming payment to a worker where one witness establishes the employment according to the Ramah and the cited opinion in siman 89. The Shach brings proof for this conclusion.
That he suspects. Beit Yosef questions how an actual oath can be imposed where the underlying assertion is uncertain, since one generally does not swear on an uncertain claim, and suggests perhaps only a general cherem was intended. The Bach disagrees and reads the passage as requiring a true oath while holding a sacred object. The Rama, Ir Shushan, Sema, and language of the Ramah support the Bach. The Shach explains that although ordinary uncertain claims do not generate oaths, the partners' oath itself was instituted on suspicion, namely that perhaps one partner retained something improperly. Therefore the oath may also be reversed on an uncertain basis, requiring the other party to swear that he in fact suspects him, even though the defendant cannot know with certainty whether the suspicion is genuine.
Wishes. The Shach cites Mahari that even after the defendant has reversed the oath, he may still say, "You take the lighter oath, or I will take that lighter oath myself," meaning without holding a sacred object, because the original choice still remains with the defendant. The Shach says the practical difference concerns the partners' oath. The Taz interprets the passage somewhat differently as relating to the oath of suspicion.
We reverse. Meaning that reversal can remove the gilgul claim, but the defendant may tell the other party, "You swear on the principal claim and I will swear on the gilgul," or the reverse, or reverse both, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state explicitly in siman 94, seif 6 and siman 75, seif 16.
