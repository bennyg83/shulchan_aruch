From an oath. The Shach discusses this law at length and concludes that the prior undertaking does not exempt a person where he now owes an oath while holding a sacred object, but it can exempt him from a shevuat heset. The Taz explains the passage differently: it means he is not exempt from later swearing that he actually paid, as the Rama states. Even according to the opinion in seif 16 that a properly administered earlier oath can exempt him, here the original undertaking was not a proper heset oath because a handshake or solemn pledge lacks the formal admonitions used for heset. Hagahot Maimoniyot indicates that the reason is the greater stringency of an oath compared with a handshake undertaking. The Sema instead distinguishes between a prospective oath and a retrospective oath, a distinction found in Responsa Rashba, but the Taz says it cannot explain Hagahot Maimoniyot because that source makes the issue depend on the relative stringency of the oath.
Who says. The Taz notes that Hagahot Maimoniyot explicitly assumes both parties agree that there was a binding handshake undertaking to pay, and the defendant only later says he already paid. The Rama's phrase "and he denies" therefore means there is a monetary denial between them, not a denial that the undertaking ever occurred. The Sema's emendation implying denial of the very undertaking is not essential, because even when the undertaking is admitted the point is that a second oath may still be required.
Gift. In an older printing the Sema's errata suggests the case must be one where the defendant is otherwise exempt from heset, because a handshake undertaking is certainly no stronger than heset, and a person who previously took heset may still later be required to take a Torah oath. The Shach rejects this. Even if a Torah oath would now be applicable, the rule that inability to swear leads to payment applies only where the underlying facts create a monetary obligation. Here the claim is merely that the defendant undertook by handshake to give money. Without the oath itself he would owe nothing, because the handshake is not equivalent to a monetary lien or kinyan. Therefore there is no basis to impose either heset or a Torah oath merely about whether he made such an undertaking. Responsa Maharam states this explicitly. See also siman 81, seif 28 and the Taz's distinction.
