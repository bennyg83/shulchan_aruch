He is not exempt from the oath. The Shach rejects the Sema's explanation that an oath concerning the past is inherently more severe than one concerning the future, because earlier the Mechaber and Rama cited this very distinction as disputed. He identifies the source as Maharam in Hagahot Maimoniyot and says Maharam held that heset required holding a sacred object, while a handshake oath did not. Therefore, under our ruling that heset also does not require holding a sacred object, perhaps a prior handshake oath should suffice. There is no difficulty. Maharam stated the clearest practical distinction available under his own view, but even according to us a handshake undertaking is not equivalent to heset. Consider an oath that by law requires holding a sacred object. If a person swears without it, he must swear again, although the first oath is fully a Torah oath, violating "you shall not take" and carrying lashes and an offering if false. The only difference is that people regard the formal oath with the sacred object as more severe. All the more so does a mere handshake differ from heset. Violation of a handshake does not carry the legal consequences of a formal oath. Rabbeinu Tam even questions whether it is more than a covenantary undertaking. It is certainly lighter than a vow, yet the Gemara says that before Rabban Gamliel's enactment a widow could not simply substitute a vow for the required oath because a vow is regarded as lighter. Therefore the Mechaber's rule stands even under our form of heset. Maharam's reasoning did not depend exclusively on the physical sacred object.
To give him a gift, etc. The Shach cites the Sema's addenda that the exemption applies only where the defendant would otherwise owe heset, but if one witness creates a Torah oath he must still swear. The Shach objects that Responsa Maharam itself appears to say that even where one witness contradicts him, there is no second oath, because one does not swear an oath after already having sworn. Maharam's words indeed say this, but the rationale requires analysis. If a formal Torah oath with a sacred object is materially more severe than an earlier handshake, perhaps a person who is willing to violate the lighter undertaking would refuse to take the heavier oath falsely and would admit. This is the same reason that one who mistakenly swore heset when a formal Torah oath was required may have to swear again in the proper form. The answer is that where a person was already substantively obligated by his handshake to give the gift, we face a dilemma. If his claim now is true, there is no reason for any second oath. If it is false, he is already violating his prior undertaking. Were we to say that because the prior undertaking was procedurally defective it is treated as nonexistent, then the very obligation to give the gift would disappear as well. We cannot simultaneously use the prior undertaking to create the substantive claim and then ignore it for oath purposes. This distinguishes it from an ordinary debt that exists independently and where the Sages can disregard an improperly administered oath and require the proper one. This reasoning works especially for Maharam, who holds that even breach of an undertaking about future conduct can establish a person as suspect like a false past oath. According to authorities who say that an oath concerning future conduct does not create immediate disqualification because no falsehood left his mouth at the time of swearing, the dilemma is weaker. A person may be willing to violate the future undertaking but still recoil from a present false oath. For them the Sema's distinction between heset and a Torah oath has substantial force. Agudah's separate reason, that heset was never instituted merely to enforce an uncommon prior undertaking of this kind, also supports exemption only from heset, not from a Torah oath. Therefore the issue is genuinely disputed, and the Sema's limitation has a serious basis. There may also be cases where the handshake promise can be released by a competent authority after informing the other party, as in Yoreh Deah, so the entire analysis depends on the form of the undertaking. The sources may assume a case where such release is unavailable. One further proof shows that the exemption cannot be treated as certain for purposes of extracting money. In Shevuat HaEdut the Gemara discusses one witness where the opposing litigant is suspected of false oaths and asks whether the claimant will actually swear and collect. It establishes a case where both are suspected according to the opinion returning the oath to the party originally obligated. If a claimant who had previously sworn with a sacred object not to make a false claim in beit din were automatically exempt from any further oath, the Gemara could have used that simpler case. The claimant would then collect through the one witness without a new oath, and the witness would plainly have caused direct monetary liability. The Gemara does not do so because a prior future oriented oath may be enough to exempt from an additional oath only as a matter of doubt. Doubt can remove an oath from a defendant because doubts in oath law are treated leniently, but it cannot justify extracting money. For collection, a new oath remains necessary.
He is believed by a migo. This implies that if witnesses establish the handshake undertaking, he is not believed without a migo to say that the promise was waived. The Taz challenges this from Responsa Rosh concerning a man who swore not to leave without his wife's permission, then left and said that she waived the condition. The Rosh accepted the claim without a migo. There is no difficulty. A generic claim of waiver is ordinarily weak. Why should a creditor or beneficiary simply waive a valuable right? Without a migo, the defendant is therefore not believed. In the Rosh's case, however, the asserted waiver was entirely natural. A wife may well permit her husband to leave rather than keep him confined, and "a proper wife does her husband's will." The claim was not intrinsically improbable. The only possible question was whether an oath was still needed to verify it, and the Rosh said no further oath should be piled upon the prior oath. Thus the cases differ.
