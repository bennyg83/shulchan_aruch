One who gives his handclasp to his fellow, etc. Since, in my humble opinion, the Mechaber, Rama, and later authorities who followed them did not explain this matter correctly, I must discuss it at some length. At first glance the Mechaber appears to derive this law from the Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe at the end of this siman, section 29, citing Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 11 of Hilchot Shevuot. Maharam ruled concerning one who gave his handclasp to his fellow that he would pay what he owed and later claimed that he had paid, that he must swear. But if he vowed to give his fellow a gift, gave his handclasp, and later denied having done so, he is exempt without an oath. The Rama appears to understand it this way, and the Sema likewise cites Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 11 of Hilchot Shevuot, as the source. Yet according to their understanding, Maharam's reason for requiring an oath after a claim of payment is difficult. They apparently understood that an oath concerning a future act is unlike an oath concerning a past act, as the Levush and Sema write, and the Bach states explicitly. But this is incorrect. Maharam holds that there is no distinction between a future and past oath. Moreover, if that were the basis, the Mechaber and Rama would contradict their own ruling above, seif 16, citing an opinion that makes no such distinction. It appears that the Mechaber and Rama instead understood Maharam's reason to be that a handclasp is not as stringent as an oath. They therefore wrote simply that one who gave a handclasp is not exempt from an oath. This corresponds to the Beit Yosef above, section 24, citing Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 16 of Hilchot Ishut, and the Mordechai at the beginning of Shevuat HaDayanim in Maharam's name. Reuven and Shimon formed a business partnership. Reuven said, “Give me your handclasp that you will divide with me everything you earn.” When they later divided the profits, Reuven demanded an oath that Shimon had not earned more. Shimon answered, “I already gave you my handclasp, which is like an oath, and I will not swear again.” Maharam ruled for Reuven. The Beit Yosef writes there that Responsa Rashba indicates that even if he originally took a formal oath, he must swear again at the end, because an oath concerning the future is unlike one concerning the past. It follows that according to the Mordechai, the reason is that a handclasp lacks the full stringency of an oath. Thus the Mechaber's ruling here derives from Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 16 of Hilchot Ishut, and the Mordechai, not from chapter 11 of Hilchot Shevuot as the Sema records. Yet this too is difficult. A handclasp is enforced just as an oath is enforced, asmachta does not apply to it, and throughout halachah it is treated as an oath. Why should it not count here? One who examines Hagahot Maimoniyot there will see that it says only that a handclasp is not equivalent to an oath taken while holding a sacred object. The Mordechai likewise quotes Maharam that although a handclasp is an oath, the partners' oath requires holding a sacred object. A dayan who administers an oath by the God of Israel without placing an object in the oath-taker's hand is deemed to have erred in an explicit Mishnah and must repeat it. Maharam concludes that the Sages trusted a partner only through an oath with a sacred object. His words expressly show that the handclasp is insufficient only because the required oath demands holding an object. They also show that he does not distinguish between a future and a past oath. It is therefore clear that Maharam's ruling cited by the Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe from Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 11 of Hilchot Shevuot, rests on the same reason. Maharam follows Rabbeinu Tam and those who require holding a sacred object even for a hesset oath, as I cited above in seif 13. Since the debtor must now swear while holding a sacred object that he paid, his earlier handclasp does not exempt him, because it lacked that element. Hagahot Maimoniyot itself states this explicitly: from the rule that a dayan who administered an oath without a sacred object must repeat it, Maharam ruled that one who gave a handclasp to pay his debt and later claimed payment must swear, although the creditor admits the handclasp, because even without the handclasp he was already obligated, whereas one who vowed to give a gift, gave his handclasp, and denies it is exempt without an oath, because according to his claim he never became obligated except by the handclasp. Maharam's own responsum, Prague edition, siman 996, likewise explains that where Reuven already owed Shimon money and gave a handclasp merely to strengthen the obligation, a later claim of payment requires an oath with a sacred object. But where, absent the handclasp, Reuven owed Levi nothing, he is not made to swear. Thus, according to our ruling that a hesset oath does not require holding a sacred object, the earlier handclasp exempts him from hesset. This is contrary to the apparent ruling of the Mechaber, Rama, and later authorities, whose broad language implies that even from hesset he is not exempt. That is incorrect. He is not exempt only from an oath that requires a sacred object, but he is exempt from hesset. Although according to Rashba's reason, that a future oath is unlike a past oath, even hesset would remain, Maharam and the Rosh disagree, and the Mechaber and Rama themselves cite their view above, seif 16. Especially since in oath matters we rule leniently for the defendant, as I wrote above, seif 3, he cannot be required to take a hesset oath after already giving a handclasp. In the old editions of the Sema, in the errata at the end of the volume, it says that this means exemption from hesset, because that is the oath discussed in the source. The Sema reasons that a handclasp is no stronger than hesset, and one who already swore hesset may still be required to take a Torah oath, because he did not swear in the legally prescribed manner and a person may be more cautious regarding a severe oath. His words are incorrect. Even if the present case would otherwise entail a Torah oath, a Torah oath can be imposed only where an admission would create monetary liability. Here the claimant says that the defendant gave a handclasp to give him money. Without that oath there is no debt. The oath is not a lien or acquisition that itself creates ordinary monetary liability; the obligation is only religious, by force of the oath. It makes no difference whether the proposed oath is hesset or a Torah oath. Do not be misled by the wording of Agudah in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, quoting Maharam that hesset was not enacted for this, for he means that even hesset was not enacted. Maharam's responsum, Prague edition, siman 996, states explicitly that even if one witness testifies that the handclasp occurred, the defendant, who denies the witness and describes a different handclasp, need not swear to contradict him, because the handclasp itself was the oath and there is no oath after an oath. Maharam distinguishes between a case where witnesses, or the party's admission, establish that he gave the handclasp and now refuses to perform, in which case he is compelled to perform, and a case where he says that he already fulfilled it, in which case he is believed without another oath, even if the other side has one witness. Maharam then distinguishes a preexisting debt, where the debtor would have had to swear with a sacred object even without the handclasp, from a gift, where absent the handclasp there was no monetary claim. The principle that wherever two witnesses obligate payment one witness obligates an oath does not apply here, because that rule concerns money where no oath was already taken. Here he already swore, and there is no oath upon an oath. If one witness said, “You swore while holding a sacred object to complete this sale,” and he denied swearing, we would not make him swear again, although two witnesses would obligate him. The gaon's responsum, Maharam concludes, proves that enforcement applies specifically when there are two witnesses. The Sema evidently did not see this responsum.
