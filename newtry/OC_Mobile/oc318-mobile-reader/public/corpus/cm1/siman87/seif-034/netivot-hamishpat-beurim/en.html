One who gave his hand in solemn undertaking. Tumim asks as follows. The Gemara asks why we do not say that since a person is suspected regarding money, he should also be suspected regarding an oath. It answers that we do not say such a migo of suspicion because perhaps he has an old doubtful loan against the claimant. People may refrain from taking an oath when in doubt even though they do not refrain from retaining money in doubt. Here, however, since he already took a lenient oath and did not refrain because of the doubt, he should certainly be suspected of violating a stringent oath as well. Why, then, does he swear again? The same question applies above in seifim 13 and 14, where one swore without holding a sacred object and is required to swear again. In my view the matter can be resolved. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 108, s.v. trei kefilei, write that one suspected regarding oaths is disqualified only rabbinically. At first glance this is difficult from the first chapter of Bava Metzia, where the Gemara asks concerning the Torah's obligation that one who admits part must swear, why we do not say that one suspected regarding money is also suspected regarding an oath. This seems to show that the disqualification is Torah law. We must therefore distinguish. By Torah law, one who violated an oath once is not presumed to continue doing so in the future. But when the question is whether one presently intending to steal money would also presently swear falsely, the Gemara can ask that if he is prepared to steal now he may likewise swear falsely now. The Sages, however, decreed that a person who once violated an oath is not accepted for another oath until he repents and it becomes clear that he has repented. By Torah law he remains fit for an oath, although for testimony the Torah explicitly teaches that he is disqualified. Accordingly, if a person was obligated in a stringent oath but took only a lenient oath, the lenient oath is legally equivalent to no oath at all and he must return and take the stringent oath. We do not say that because he was suspect concerning the lenient oath he is suspect concerning the stringent oath. By Torah law, we do not establish him as presently suspect merely because of a past transgression, and he therefore remains obligated in the Torah oath. Nor do the Sages disqualify him and shift the oath to the opposing party, because it has not been established that he is actually suspect. Perhaps he in fact swore truthfully. Since the Torah itself did not deem a lenient oath sufficient, but required the stringent oath because it suspected that one might violate the lenient oath while refraining from the stringent one, there is no reason for the Sages to undo the Torah's oath obligation when they do not know that he is a person disqualified from swearing. In a case where he swore to pay, the migo of suspicion likewise does not apply. If he fixed a payment date in the oath and that date has already passed, we cannot reason from his past violation to the oath he must take now, for the reason just given. Rabbinically, he would indeed have the status of one suspected regarding oaths. If he did not fix a payment date in his oath, there is likewise no migo of suspicion, because we can say that he is merely delaying payment temporarily.
