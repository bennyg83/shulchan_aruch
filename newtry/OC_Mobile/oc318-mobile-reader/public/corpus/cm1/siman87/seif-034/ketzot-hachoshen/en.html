One who gives his hand. The Shach explains Maharam bar Baruch as holding that a formal handshake does not discharge the oath because the handshake lacks the sacred object, and perhaps Maharam also requires a sacred object even for hesset. I do not accept this explanation. A handshake can certainly have the gravity of an oath for purposes of prohibition and a person who violates it can become suspected. Yet it is no stronger than a ban, which likewise has serious prohibitory force but does not discharge a judicial oath. The point is that the Sages required the actual form of an oath because it is more intimidating. Even where hesset does not require a sacred object, the threatening force of the oath itself remains necessary. Maharam's comparison to a judge who administered an oath without the required sacred object means that where the court departs from the established form of intimidation, the oath must be retaken. Responsa Maimoniyot itself indicates that even Rabbeinu Tam's stringent treatment of a handshake did not make it absolutely equal to an oath. Later generations often accepted a handshake precisely because they feared the punishment of an oath, much as the Sages allowed a widow to make a vow rather than swear. Thus the very custom shows that an oath is considered more severe. Moreover, Rabbeinu Tam's special stringency concerning a handshake is most intelligible regarding a promise for the future, where the handshake is a covenantal pledge of one's word. A handshake concerning a past fact does not have that same character. Therefore one who merely gave a handshake that he had not been paid has not necessarily fulfilled a judicial oath concerning the past. The later custom of using handshakes for past facts may be a later stringency, but it does not alter the basic law.
If he says that he gave him his hand. Maharam bar Baruch rules that where the entire obligation arose only through the handshake, no additional oath is imposed. The Shach explains that an oath does not itself create a monetary acquisition; it only obligates the person to fulfill what he swore. Therefore a later denial is not a direct monetary claim suitable for oath. Rivash states similarly that even if everything is included in the oath, there may still be no monetary claim, because admitting the oath would not itself allow beit din to seize property. Yet this rationale is difficult. One can be required to swear even regarding claims where beit din does not directly descend to property, such as prohibited interest or certain rabbinic obligations. The better explanation is connected to the rule that if the defendant is already suspected through violating the prior oath, administering another oath has no purpose. Maharam bar Baruch follows the view that violating a future oath disqualifies a person from swearing. The second oath therefore cannot be imposed. This explains why the Mechaber omitted Maharam's special exemption and simply ruled that a handshake does not discharge an oath. The Mechaber follows the view that violation of an oath concerning the future does not automatically make one suspected for judicial oaths. Accordingly, even where the monetary obligation arose through the handshake, another oath can still be required. The Rosh's responsum also supports the disqualification rationale: he says that because the person already swore not to leave without permission, if he lies now he is already violating that oath and there is no point in administering another. Even this rationale is not entirely free of difficulty, because a person may sometimes retain disputed money due to an old uncertain loan and yet refrain from a present oath until the uncertainty is clarified. Nevertheless, it explains the structure better than saying there is no monetary character at all.
