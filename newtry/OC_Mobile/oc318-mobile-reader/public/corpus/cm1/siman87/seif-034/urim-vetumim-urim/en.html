The handshake oath, etc. Sema explains that the case is where he gave a handshake oath that he would pay, and now claims that he paid. He is therefore obligated to take a hesset oath, because at the time of the handshake oath he had not yet sworn falsely. Only afterward would he violate his undertaking, and this is lighter in his eyes. By contrast, if he must now take a hesset oath, falsehood would leave his mouth in the very act of swearing, and a person is more careful about this. According to this, the matter depends on the dispute above in seif 16. Also, if he now gives a handshake oath that he already paid, it should suffice according to all views. Shach challenges the entire ruling. In his view, Maharam, the authority for this teaching, follows his own opinion that a hesset oath requires holding a sacred object, whereas a handshake oath does not. According to us, however, even hesset does not require holding a sacred object, so the distinction does not apply. See Tumim, where I wrote that there is no difficulty. A handshake oath nevertheless remains lighter than hesset, and therefore according to all views even if he now gives his hand that he already paid, he is not exempt from hesset. See there.
To give him a gift. Sema holds that this applies specifically to hesset, because hesset was not instituted for such a claim. But if there is partial admission, or one witness whom he contradicts, he must swear. Shach disagrees and holds that there is never an oath here, since absent the handshake oath there was no obligation whatsoever, and the obligation arises only from the handshake oath. There is therefore no oath after an oath. In Tumim I wrote that this depends on the dispute whether an oath concerning a past event and one concerning the future are governed by the same law. According to the view that they are not alike, Sema is correct to distinguish hesset from a Torah oath. Consider this carefully. It also depends on whether release from the original oath is possible. See there. See also Tumim, where I concluded that even according to the view that when he first swore he need not swear again at the end, that applies when the oath exempts him. But one who swears and collects may not collect by virtue of the earlier oath, and must swear at the time of collection. See there.
With a migo. The reason he is not believed to say, "You forgave me," even where no migo is needed, such as where he gave the handshake oath before witnesses, is that forgiveness is an inferior claim. See Tumim.
