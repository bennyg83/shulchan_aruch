(Ibid., but if he said, etc.) There, in the glosses to Maimonides, this matter is concluded as follows: “If a person vowed to give a gift to another and gave him his hand upon it, but then denies it and says, ‘I did not give my hand,’ he is exempt without any oath, because according to his own statement he never became obligated to him except through the giving of the hand.” It appears that the explanation is that there is no distinction here between an oath and giving one’s hand. Rather, since the entire obligation arises only from the giving of the hand or from the oath, beit din does not impose an oath concerning an oath. This differs from the first case, where he is already subject to an oath by virtue of denying a monetary claim, under the enactment of the Sages, and therefore he cannot exempt himself by giving his hand, as will be explained below. According to this, the same rule applies if he advances other claims regarding a giving of the hand or an oath: no oath is imposed concerning them. This is so even if there are witnesses to the giving of the hand or to the oath, because the original obligation arose only by force of the oath, and therefore he may say, “You released me from the oath.” This is likewise stated in a responsum of the Rosh, principle 10, responsum 2, concerning one who swore to his wife that he would not leave without her permission, and later left and claimed that she had released him from the oath. The Rosh ruled that no oath applies to this claim, because if he is lying he is already violating his original oath, and one oath does not take effect upon another oath. That case concerned an oath made before witnesses, as is apparent to one who examines it carefully. It is therefore difficult why Rama writes here that he is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have denied the matter entirely, which implies that where witnesses are present he is not believed. Why should that be so? He can still say, “You released me from the giving of the hand,” as stated. In Mordechai, at the beginning of Bava Metzia, this ruling appears in a case where one person claimed that he had not given his hand to give the item unconditionally, but only subject to specified conditions. Mordechai ruled that he is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have said that he never gave his hand at all. There is no difficulty there, because he claims that the condition existed at the very time he gave his hand. He can therefore be believed only where there were no witnesses present at that time. If there were witnesses to the giving of the hand and they knew nothing of the condition, their testimony would indicate that there was no such condition. It was with respect to that claim that Mordechai wrote that he is believed through a migo that he never gave his hand, meaning that no witnesses are present. It is possible that Rama’s reference to this migo applies only to a claim that the undertaking was subject to such-and-such a condition, in which case witnesses must not have been present. But the claim, “You released me,” requires no migo. Even if witnesses saw the giving of the hand, he is later believed to say, “You released me,” as the Rosh wrote. This appears to me plainly correct.
