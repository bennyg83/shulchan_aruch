Seif 6. (One witness supports him.) If a person has a single witness supporting his exemption from the sanction of mi shepara, it appears that the witness is effective according to all opinions. Even if the other party presently holds the money, he must return it immediately, because apart from mi shepara the money unquestionably stands to be returned. Since one supporting witness can exempt from a serious oath, it is reasonable that he can also exempt from this rabbinic sanction. It seems that mi shepara cannot be imposed on the basis of a single witness where the Sages never enacted such a rule. The sanction itself is a rabbinic enactment. Although Torah law recognizes acquisition by money, once the Sages suspended the acquisition through money, the remaining rule of mi shepara is entirely rabbinic. Any doubt concerning a rabbinic enactment should be resolved leniently for the defendant. This remains true even where the money is in the other party's hands, since it is otherwise unquestionably due to be returned. Certainly it is true where the other party is not in possession. One might argue conversely that if a single witness cannot impose mi shepara, perhaps he also cannot remove it, because the same evidentiary limitation works both ways. Nevertheless, mi shepara is lighter than an oath. An oath can be a Torah obligation, whereas mi shepara, even though the original monetary acquisition may have Torah roots, is only a rabbinic sanction. If, hypothetically, a single witness could impose mi shepara, the argument for a supporting witness to exempt would be even stronger, paralleling the familiar kal vachomer that if one witness can obligate an oath, one witness should certainly be able to exempt from it. The poskim write that mi shepara can be pronounced even in the third person and outside the transgressor's presence. Accordingly, much of this discussion has limited practical difference, because the claimant can state the formula in the third person before beit din even if the judges themselves do not pronounce it. Even if the judges are silent, it may be viewed as though the claimant himself has said it, and the defendant's assertion that he is exempt does not necessarily prevent the claimant from speaking. However, if the witness testifies that before the transaction the defendant openly disclosed an intention that would justify his later change of position, then perhaps the formula condemning one who fails to stand by his word does not apply. Although the earlier wording was changed, the single witness establishes that the change was permissible. It appears that the relevant "word" is the legally effective commercial commitment recognized by Torah law. If he knows that he had disclosed his intention and acts accordingly, he properly discharges his obligation according to all views. It also appears that where a general acceptance of a ban would otherwise rest on the statement of a woman who is interested in the matter, the testimony of another woman can exempt him from that acceptance. If a person speaking innocently can generate an oath, as the Shach writes in siman 149, then such an innocent statement supporting the defendant can likewise exempt from that oath. Whether an innocent statement supporting the defendant exempts from an ordinary shevuat heset, or from an acceptance of a ban based on substantial grounds, requires study. The same question applies to a woman's statement. At least where the only reason the defendant became suspect was the statement of one woman, even if she was disinterested, it appears that an interested woman can neutralize the basis for the acceptance of a ban. Certainly this is so where the original obligation itself arose only from an interested woman's statement.
