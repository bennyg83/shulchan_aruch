And according to the one who holds that collateral, etc. The Tur comments on the Rambam's ruling in the case of an exchanged item, where the Rambam requires a Torah oath, that perhaps the Rambam holds collateral is not considered heilech. The Beit Yosef asks why the Tur says "perhaps," since the Rambam explicitly follows his teacher Ri Migash, who holds that collateral is not heilech. The Derishah correctly explains that the Tur's uncertainty is not about the Rambam's general position on collateral. The question is whether this particular law of an exchanged item actually depends on the rule that collateral is not heilech. If the two laws are linked, then according to the Tur's own view that collateral is heilech the Rambam's ruling would not stand. If they are not linked, the Rambam's ruling could be accepted independently. The Perishah's explanation of why the two cases might differ is strained. It appears to me that the expression "it was exchanged for him" can bear two meanings. One possibility is that the custodian already knew before coming to beit din that the item in his possession was not the claimant's original object because it had been exchanged earlier, and he now tenders a different object in its place. In that case the tendered object is no weaker than ordinary collateral, and whether it is heilech should depend directly on the general law of collateral. The second possibility is that the custodian mistakenly brought the object to beit din believing it truly was the claimant's property and only then realized the mistake. In that case he never entered beit din intending to give the object as collateral or as payment. He presented it as the claimant's own property. What heilech can there be at the moment of his admission? He can in principle say that he delivered the object by mistake and seek its return. Even if, after realizing the mistake, he agrees to leave the object in the claimant's hands, that later consent does not make the original admission into heilech, because heilech must exist at the time of the admission itself. Accordingly, if the Rambam's case concerns an actual mistake discovered only afterward, it need not resemble ordinary collateral at all. The object was never initially delivered in the capacity of collateral. The Tur's uncertainty is therefore understandable. This requires further study.
