Its law is like every other monetary claim. A claim for damages caused through legally actionable indirect causation is treated like an ordinary monetary claim. Baal HaTerumot writes that if the holder of a document says its value was one hundred and the person who burned it admits only fifty, the latter takes the Torah partial admission oath. If he admits part and says he does not know the remainder, the rule of one obligated to swear but unable to swear applies. Maharshal writes that according to the view that liability for direct causation is rabbinic, no Torah oath applies. The Shach accepts this in simanim 386 and 388. In my view this is incorrect as a general principle. Rambam rules that a husband who admits part of a ketubah claim can incur a Torah oath even though Rambam holds the ketubah itself is rabbinic. Likewise, heirs who admit part of an oral claim can take a Torah oath even according to authorities who hold property liens against heirs are rabbinic. Ran says that where a claimant has already taken the special injury oath and thereby established part of the claim, denial of the remainder can create a Torah partial admission oath even though the claimant's collection right over the injury portion rests on rabbinic enactment. Rambam likewise applies Torah oath rules to disputes concerning acquisitions that themselves operate only rabbinically. The reason is that once the Sages validly vested the money in a person, their enactment becomes legally effective property through the power of beit din to declare property ownerless and transfer it. Denial of that valid monetary right can therefore generate a Torah oath. Maharshal's ruling concerning direct causation follows his separate view that rabbinic direct causation is a penalty and that one who admits a penalty is exempt. On that premise there is no ordinary admitted debt to support a Torah oath. But the Shach himself rejects Maharshal's penalty analysis and says that the rule of admitting a fine applies only to Torah fines. Once he rejects that basis, he should also reject the conclusion that no Torah oath can arise. Even a monetary obligation created by rabbinic enactment can support a Torah oath, and the rule of inability to swear likewise applies.
