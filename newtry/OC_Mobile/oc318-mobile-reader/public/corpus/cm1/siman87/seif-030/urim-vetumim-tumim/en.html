The law favors the one who seized. The Taz writes that if the defendant was initially obligated to take a Torah oath to contradict one witness and did so, and afterward the claimant seized property in circumstances where the claimant now has a migo, the claimant need not take any further oath. The Taz thereby challenges what the Rama wrote above, that a supporting witness does not exempt where the claimant's substantive credibility depends only on migo. The Taz attempts to distinguish between land and movable property, but his distinction is strained. Ultimately, if there is no true migo because the claimant is unwilling to swear falsely, why should the type of property change matters? According to what I wrote above, the real issue in these cases is whether one can attribute an alternative claim to both the litigant and the supporting witness without creating an impermissible two person migo. If the witness himself could support the alternative claim, then in some circumstances the migo can operate to remove the oath. That reasoning does not apply here. The witness already testified before the claimant seized the property, and his testimony was adverse to the defendant and created the original oath. Only afterward did the claimant seize and acquire a new migo. What migo does the witness now possess? Are we to imagine that he will return and give a second, different testimony merely to fit the claimant's later procedural position? We have never heard of such a retroactive or reconstructed migo. It is worse than an ordinary migo arising only after the fact. Therefore the claimant still requires the oath. The Taz's exemption cannot be accepted.
