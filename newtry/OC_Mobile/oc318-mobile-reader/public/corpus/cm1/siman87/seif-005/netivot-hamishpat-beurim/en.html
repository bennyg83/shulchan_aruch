From the denial claim. See Shach, seif katan 12. There is a textual error, and the correct reading is: "And in the first statement of Rabbi Chiya as well, he becomes liable only through his own claim, when he said, 'I never borrowed'; for had he said, 'I borrowed and repaid,' he would not have become liable." See also Shach, seif katan 13, concerning one who claims fifty from a loan and fifty for bodily injury, where the case is that the defendant admits that he took an oath. One might ask why he is not believed by a migo that he could have denied having sworn and denied everything. The usual answer of Rabbah, that a person is not brazen before his creditor, would seem unavailable here because he is brazen even now by saying that he did not injure him. This is not a difficulty. Since he admits that he swore and thereby became obligated to pay, he admits part of the monetary liability. That is less brazen than denying that he was ever liable even for that part. The alternative is therefore a migo of brazenness. See Ketzot HaChoshen, who correctly rules, unlike Shach, that even if after the claim the defendant wishes to swear concerning the injury, he is still considered one who admits part. This is explicitly stated by Ran at the end of his discussion. See there.
