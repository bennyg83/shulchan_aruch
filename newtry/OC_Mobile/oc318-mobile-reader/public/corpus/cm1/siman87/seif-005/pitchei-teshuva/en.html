The primary ruling follows the first opinion. Shaar Mishpat, above in siman 75 seif 23, seif katan 15, derives a new rule from the Rama there. The Rama rules that when a claimant says that a trustworthy person told him of the debt, even if that informant is his relative, he may impose a hesset oath. Since we rule that a supporting witness exempts from an oath by a kal vachomer, Shaar Mishpat reasons that for a hesset oath even a relative who supports the defendant should exempt him. If a relative can create hesset liability, all the more should a relative be able to remove that liability. He also finds support in Responsa Maharit, part 1, siman 151, which explains that a supporting witness exempts from hesset because 'a person does not sin for no benefit to himself.' That rationale may apply even to a relative, for even a father regarding his son can be described as one who does not sin merely for another's benefit. This proof requires study, however, because Nimukei Yosef at the end of Bava Batra states that this principle is said specifically with respect to consecrated property, not ordinary private claims. See what I write below in siman 104 seif 13, seif katan 11. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat siman 14, addressed this very ruling to the author of Shaar Mishpat himself, raised many objections, and concluded that a relative may create hesset liability but does not exempt from it. Machaneh Ephraim, Laws of Testimony siman 15, cited above in siman 35, likewise treats it as obvious that a relative does not exempt and questions only a person who was once a relative and later became unrelated. Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 14, cited above in siman 36 seif 1, also rules that a relative does not exempt.
