For example, he claims one hundred from him, and the defendant says, "I never borrowed," and later says, "I borrowed fifty and repaid you," etc. This is the reading in all printed editions of the Mechaber. The Sema, seif katan 14, writes that the text must instead read: "He says, 'I never borrowed,' and witnesses came and testified that he borrowed fifty, and he then said, etc." His reasoning is that although two witnesses have established the defendant as false in his original statement that he never borrowed, and anyone who says "I never borrowed" is treated as saying "I never repaid," the fifty is not considered a partial admission. The witnesses themselves did not establish that the fifty remained unpaid after the loan. The defendant becomes liable for it only because his earlier denial prevents him from later claiming repayment. Such liability, says the Sema, is not called a partial admission, and the defendant therefore takes only a heset oath on the balance. Rabbi Chiya's first rule, he explains, applies only where two witnesses testify, "We know that he borrowed fifty and still owes that fifty." The Sema says that careful study of the Rashba's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef shows no difference for this purpose between contradiction by one witness and contradiction by two witnesses. I examined that responsum carefully and do not understand the Sema's intent. The question there concerned a claimant who demanded one hundred, the defendant said that no such matter ever occurred, one witness testified that he had borrowed fifty, and the defendant then said that he had repaid. The Rashba answered that the oath of partial admission applies only where the defendant actually admits part. If he denies everything and becomes liable for part only by force of his own argument, that is different. Rabbi Chiya's first rule likewise concerns one who becomes liable through his own statement, because he said, "I never borrowed." Had he said, "I borrowed and repaid," he would not have become liable. Even so, the Torah oath on the remainder applies specifically where there are witnesses, by an a fortiori inference, but not otherwise. This responsum is also cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 79. I do not know what the Sema intended. Perhaps he was troubled because, in the Rashba's view, where the defendant says, "I never borrowed," one witness says he did borrow, and he later says, "I repaid," the rule is not necessarily that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay, unlike the Rambam. If so, why did the Rashba need to add that this is not a partial admission, since according to the Rashba the defendant might not be liable for that fifty at all? But there is no difficulty. The Rashba does not reject the Rambam in every respect, and he was also answering a questioner who followed the Rambam. He therefore explains that even according to the Rambam, although the defendant may have to pay the fifty because he is treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear, that payment does not turn him into one who admits part of the remaining claim. His liability for the fifty comes only from the legal consequence of his own argument, not from an admission or direct testimony establishing an unpaid debt. The practical difference arises whenever the rule of "obligated to swear and unable to swear must pay" produces liability for part of the claim. For example, if the claimant demands one hundred, the defendant answers, "As to fifty I do not know, and as to fifty there was never any debt," and one witness testifies that he borrowed fifty, then even though he must pay fifty because he cannot swear against the witness, this does not make him a partial admitter as to the balance. Similarly, in the case of the silver ingot seized before Rabbi Abba, if the witness does not know how much was seized and the defendant admits a portion, then although he must pay the amount he admits because he cannot take the required oath, he does not thereby become liable to a Torah oath on the remainder that he denies. The Rambam states this explicitly at the end of chapter 4 of Hilchot Gezelah. The Semag, Mordechai in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the Tur in siman 364 likewise record the dispute. Some say that because he admits the seizure and claims that what he seized was his own, and admits that twenty were seized while the injured party claims one hundred, he must pay the twenty he admits and take a Torah oath on the remainder as one who became liable for part. Our view inclines instead to a heset oath, because he did not truly admit any liability, only that he seized what he says was his own. The Sema is also difficult because the Rashba expressly says that Rabbi Chiya's first rule likewise concerns liability arising from the defendant's own statement, and he adds that even there it is specifically with witnesses, by an a fortiori inference, but not otherwise. This wording shows that one witness is insufficient to create partial admission, even if two witnesses would be sufficient. The Rashba cannot merely be discussing liability for the fifty itself, for then the expression "a fortiori" is inapplicable. The a fortiori inference concerns the oath on the other fifty, inferred from the defendant's own admission. Moreover, the Rashba's phrase "he said, 'I never borrowed'" fits my explanation better than the Sema's. The Rashba means that Rabbi Chiya's case is one where the defendant first says, "I never borrowed," witnesses then testify that he borrowed fifty, and only afterward he claims to have repaid. This is also how the Ran, Nimukei Yosef, and Ritva explain Rabbi Chiya's statement. Had he initially said, "I borrowed and repaid," before the witnesses arrived, their testimony to the loan alone would not obligate him. We cannot say that the witnesses testify that the fifty still remains unpaid, for then the debt would be supported by a lien on land, as the Ran, Nimukei Yosef, and Ritva explain, and the Rashba's a fortiori reasoning would still be understandable without that assumption. The Ran at the beginning of the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim states explicitly that Rabbi Chiya's case is not one where the witnesses testify that fifty is presently unpaid. If they did so, that fifty would be a debt secured by land under the principle that a Torah loan creates a lien, and the ordinary partial admission oath would not arise in the same way. Rather, the witnesses testify only that the loan occurred. The creditor cannot collect that fifty from purchasers, because we plead for them that perhaps it was repaid. But from the borrower himself the creditor may collect, because the borrower said, "You have nothing in my hands," and anyone who says, "I never borrowed" is treated as saying, "I never repaid." Purchasers are not bound by his personal argument and can say that perhaps he borrowed and repaid. The Ritva at the beginning of Bava Metzia writes the same: the witnesses testify only to the loan, the borrower had denied that it ever happened, and his denial prevents him from later saying that he repaid. Because his liability comes from his own admission, it is not treated as a direct admission concerning land. The Nimukei Yosef states this briefly as well. Nor is it reasonable to distinguish, where there are two witnesses, between a case where the defendant later says "I repaid" and one where he does not. The Ir Shushan also follows the Sema, but in my view the correct reading is as I have explained. Since no printed edition of the Mechaber contains the words "and witnesses came," it is better to correct the text to read "and one witness came," consistent with the Mechaber's ruling above in siman 75 seif 13 following the Rambam.
Through the argument of denial. The Sema explains this as where the defendant says, "I owe you nothing because the circumstances were such and such," mistakenly believing that those circumstances exempt him, but beit din does not accept his legal conclusion and therefore obligates him for part, as explained in the gloss to siman 75 seif 5. An example is where the claimant had told him regarding fifty, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," and he is sued for one hundred and says that he repaid. Similar cases are included. If he says, "You have no claim against me for fifty, and regarding the other fifty I do not know whether I repaid you," he is a partial admitter. Although he has not clearly admitted liability, his own response creates definite liability for part. So write the Rambam and the Ran in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. Likewise, if the claimant demands fifty arising from a loan and fifty arising from bodily injury, and the claimant has already taken the required oath regarding the injury while the defendant denies everything, the defendant is liable to a Torah oath on the fifty arising from the loan. Since the claimant already swore, we have the equivalent of witness testimony that the defendant owes him fifty, and this resembles witnesses establishing that fifty remains in his possession. If the claimant has not yet taken that oath, however, this rule does not apply, even if he is presently willing to swear as an injured party. So writes the Ran. His phrase that the defendant "denies everything" means that he denies causing the injury but admits that the claimant took the oath, or else the defendant denies everything and witnesses establish that the claimant took the oath. From this we may learn that whenever the claimant has already taken an oath that by law establishes liability for fifty, the defendant is liable to a Torah oath on the remainder. The Sema further cites the Rashba's responsum brought by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe at the beginning of this siman concerning one who claims, "I paid interest on your behalf," and compares it to the rule in siman 75 seif 5. He distinguishes this from a defendant who says, "I owe you nothing because today I paid you such and such and yesterday such and such," where calculation of his own words reveals that he still owes a balance. The Sema calls the latter a partial admission. I already wrote at length above in siman 75, seif katan 17, that I disagree with the Sema's distinction. Where beit din obligates the defendant because the law applied to his own account shows that he still owes part, such as where he says, "I paid interest for you," and comparable cases, he is liable to a Torah oath. Beit din is no less authoritative than witnesses. See there.
