One who becomes liable for part. The Shach cites Ran: if the defendant says, "Fifty I do not owe you, and as to fifty I do not know whether I repaid you," he is treated as partial admission even though he made no definite admission. His own response makes him liable for fifty. Likewise, if the claimant seeks fifty for a loan and fifty for an injury, and the claimant has already taken the special injured party oath concerning the injury, while the defendant denies everything, the defendant takes a Torah oath on the loan portion because the claimant's completed oath has established the injury portion. The Shach reads Ran as requiring that the claimant had already sworn before the litigation began. A closer reading of Ran shows otherwise. So long as the claimant eventually takes the required oath, even now or later, the defendant becomes subject to the Torah oath on the remaining part. Ran expressly compares this to Rabbi Chiya's rule: when witnesses arrive only later and establish part of the claim, the defendant cannot say his original response had already emerged exempt. The later testimony creates the evidentiary partial liability. The claimant's oath in an injury case has the same effect once actually taken. Therefore there is no need to force the case into one where the defendant denies the injury but admits the claimant had previously sworn. It can be the ordinary case where the claimant swears now before beit din. Once he does, we are as witnesses concerning that portion, and the defendant must take the Torah oath on the balance.
