Tosafot and the Rosh explain the advice "if he is clever, he should bring her to a Torah oath" by saying that according to the view that a Mishnaic oath can be shifted, the practical advantage is that a Torah oath cannot be shifted. But according to the view that a Mishnaic oath itself cannot be shifted, they must give a different explanation. Mahari asks: even if a Mishnaic oath cannot directly be shifted, the claimant can say, "I do not want the rabbinic enactment in my favor. Treat the note as an ordinary oral claim and require the debtor to take hesset." That would still create a practical difference from a Torah oath. Since Tosafot and the Rosh do not use this distinction, Mahari infers that they reject the rule that a claimant can abandon the special enactment and convert the matter into hesset. The Shach writes sharply that Mahari and Darkei Moshe erred. He argues first that no real difference is created. If the woman says, "I cancel reliance on my ketubah note and merely sue you orally, so swear hesset that you owe me nothing," then even if the husband later brings one witness and creates a Torah oath against her, she can simply pay and then sue the husband back, demanding hesset that she did not pay him improperly. The practical result would be the same. Second, the Shach argues that where the Mishnaic oath itself is shifted, the witness who originally testified that the ketubah was paid does not count as a supporting witness after reversal, because that witness created the oath in the first place. But if the woman entirely abandons the ketubah mechanism and simply demands hesset, the witness stands independently on the husband's side and exempts him. Thus the supposed practical distinction used by Mahari cannot work. Although there is room for the Shach's arguments, there is no basis to accuse Mahari and Darkei Moshe of error, Heaven forbid. As to the second point, they may simply reject the Shach's distinction concerning a supporting witness. They may follow Maharam, as I explained above in seif katan 12, that a supporting witness can exempt even when his testimony was the original source of the oath. Tosafot and the Rosh can then be explained as I wrote above, namely that the woman need not flatly contradict the witness but can claim that the money was for another debt or that she returned it, while the witness gives no support concerning that additional assertion. If so, their inference remains coherent. The Shach's first point, that she can always pay and then sue back by hesset, can likewise be answered. We rule that for an ordinary rabbinic oath, and certainly for hesset, beit din does not descend to the defendant's property if he simply refuses to swear. But where the woman still holds a valid ketubah note, she is in a stronger position. She can say: "I relinquish only the rabbinic presumption that lets me collect on the note without further challenge. Treat my claim like an oral claim and swear hesset. But if you refuse to swear, my note remains fully valid and I collect on it." She has not destroyed the note absolutely. She waives the special procedural advantage only on condition that the husband take hesset. If he does not, the note remains in full force. Therefore, if he refuses the hesset, beit din can descend to his assets by virtue of the existing note. Her position is thus materially stronger than if she first pays him and afterward sues him in an ordinary hesset action. In that later action there is no note supporting her and beit din would not descend to his property merely because he declined hesset. A rational woman will therefore not surrender money now merely to obtain a later ordinary hesset claim when she can presently retain the strength of her note and say, "Either swear or pay the note." This is a substantial practical difference and answers the Shach. One can also formulate Mahari's proof more simply. Why force Rav Pappa's case into unusual circumstances involving disqualified persons and similar complications? Suppose the ketubah is one thousand, the husband claims he paid five hundred, and one witness confirms payment of that five hundred. If the witness does not automatically force the woman into the full Torah oath mechanism, she can say she does not seek collection on the note as a note. She sues orally for the entire thousand and treats the written instrument as withdrawn for procedural purposes. The husband then admits five hundred and denies five hundred, which ordinarily creates a Torah partial admission oath. Just as a claimant who faces an all denying defendant can say "I do not want the special written instrument; swear hesset," so too one might think she can transform the partial admission setting into a Torah oath against the husband. If the husband first pays her and later sues her back, as the Shach suggests, he would ordinarily obtain only hesset. Thus Rav Pappa's special advice still has a clear procedural consequence. It is possible, however, that one should follow the Raah in Ketubot chapter 2, who holds that even where the claimant could plead payment and no note is physically being relied upon, a claim collectible only from land can still be treated as a land secured claim for oath purposes. If so, the example would require adjustment. Still, Mahari's general inference is not frivolous. For practical halachah I already wrote that the Shach's distinction concerning the same witness creating and later supporting an oath appears to be the stronger view. If so, Tosafot and the Rosh need not be interpreted through the "other debt" claim and Mahari's proof from them weakens. But it remains entirely improper to say that Mahari and Darkei Moshe simply erred. One point, however, appears clear to me as practical law. When a noteholder tells the debtor, "I waive my special right to collect without your hesset. Swear hesset that the note is not owed," and the debtor refuses, beit din may descend to the debtor's property by virtue of the note. The claimant never canceled the note unconditionally. She merely offered the debtor the opportunity to defeat it by taking hesset. If he declines, the note remains at full strength. I later found this very idea in the Raah on Ketubot concerning the phrase "if he is clever." The Raah writes that the practical difference is where the woman says she does not want to proceed through the note and treats it as though it were absent, suing one hundred or two hundred with certainty so that the other party is obligated in hesset. If the debtor says, "I will not swear," he can shift the hesset to her without requiring the formal holding of a sacred object, whereas the clever strategy brings her to a Torah oath with the full formal requirements. The Shach's question, why she cannot simply pay and then sue back, applies equally to the Raah. The answer must be as I have written: the procedural strength of the existing note remains available if the debtor refuses the hesset. This also helps explain Rashi. Rashi says the advantage of the clever strategy is that the Torah oath requires holding a sacred object, whereas the lighter rabbinic oath does not. Commentators asked why this is the relevant distinction when the oath could simply be shifted. But according to the foregoing, Rav Nachman's hesset exists in Rashi's version of the sugya. Rashi therefore needs to explain why the woman cannot evade the heavier oath by restructuring the claim into hesset. The answer is that even after reversal, hesset remains the lighter form without the sacred object, while the clever arrangement generates the full Torah form. Know also that when I came to Prague I saw judges rule that even a Torah oath can in practice be transformed so that the claimant takes hesset instead. I asked the elders and experts of the generation why they ruled contrary to the formal rule that a Torah oath cannot be shifted. They told me that this was a received local tradition. Perhaps the practice can be explained by the current procedure. In our time, before a person takes an oath he deposits the disputed money with beit din to remain in escrow until after the oath. Once the money is in beit din's hands, this can be regarded as a form of payment, comparable to the rule that one places designated money in the appropriate chamber. The original defendant can then bring a new claim against the recipient: "Swear hesset that I did not pay you improperly." The hesset is technically based on a new claim rather than a direct reversal of the Torah oath. There are objections to this explanation from several angles, but such is the established practice. If it is a received tradition, we accept it.
