(Ibid.) “Until he brings proof.” The Sema writes that if the defendant claims, “You waived the oath for me,” he is believed. I am astonished. If so, what did the Sages accomplish by instituting the heset oath? Every person will say, “You waived my oath.” The earlier rule that one was believed to say, “I swore,” presents no difficulty, because the claimant could protect himself by saying, “Do not swear except before witnesses,” just as one may say, “Do not repay me except before witnesses.” But if the defendant may say that the claimant voluntarily waived the oath privately, what can the claimant do? The entire enactment would be nullified. Tosafot raise analogous objections in Bava Metzia, chapter HaShoel, page 94, concerning one who says, “I do not know whether I owe you,” and therefore must take a heset oath, since otherwise everyone would make that claim. The Gemara similarly asks at the beginning of Ketubot, concerning “I found an open entrance,” what the Sages accomplished with their ketubah enactment, and Tosafot raise the same point in Ketubot, page 96, concerning orphans. Moreover, the Mordechai at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia writes: “Therefore, one who wishes to guard against all these devices should warn him not to repay except in your presence.” Yet the defendant could still say, “You waived it.” What would the warning accomplish? It therefore appears obvious to me that the defendant must take a heset oath that the claimant waived his oath. A claim that an oath was waived is equivalent to a claim that the money itself was waived, because if the money was not waived, the claimant may demand it again and say, “If you deny the debt, swear.” This differs from siman 93, seif 9, where a claim that, even if admitted, creates only an oath does not itself require an oath. There the claimant does not assert a definite monetary claim, but only a right to an oath. Here he definitely says that the defendant holds his money and must swear if he denies it. This certainly requires a heset oath. The Sema cites proof from the Tur in siman 82, but even according to the interpretation of Maharshal, which I have already explained differently there, the waiver claim only causes the creditor to swear. It certainly does not release the defendant entirely from every oath and thereby cause the creditor to lose. This appears correct to me.
