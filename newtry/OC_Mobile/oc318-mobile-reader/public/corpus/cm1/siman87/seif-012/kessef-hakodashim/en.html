Seif 27. (Among those who swear and are exempt.) In siman 87, seif 27, the phrase "if he is among those who swear and are exempt" excludes those who swear and collect, as the Sema writes in the name of Nimukei Yosef. This distinction applies specifically to shevuat heset. The Shach rules, following Rav Hai Gaon, the Rosh, Tur, Rashba, Nimukei Yosef, Ramban, and Rabbeinu Yerucham, that where a Torah oath was originally required and the defendant later says, "I already swore," he must take a shevuat heset to substantiate that assertion. But where the original oath itself was only a shevuat heset, we do not enact a further rabbinic oath on top of a rabbinic enactment. At first glance this raises a difficulty from Shevuot 41, where the Gemara asks what practical difference exists between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath. According to Rav Hai Gaon, why does the Gemara not answer that one who denies a prior shevuat heset by saying, "I already swore," is exempt, whereas one who says the same regarding a Torah oath must still take a shevuat heset? One might answer that the Gemara's category of rabbinic oaths includes also those who swear and collect, and in such cases a claimant who says "I already swore" cannot collect, as above. The Shach earlier, in seif 12, explicitly says that the discussion applies to all oaths. This works according to Rashi and Tosafot. But according to the Rosh, who holds that one who swears and collects cannot reverse the oath, the Gemara there must concern only shevuat heset, and the question returns, for the Rosh himself follows Rav Hai Gaon. The Beit Meir in siman 17 likewise writes that the Gemara did not mention the difference of holding a sacred object because it was discussing legal consequences rather than ceremonial details. Yet the distinction under discussion is itself a legal consequence. The same difficulty arises according to the Ran, who accepts all these rules, and according to the Rambam's inference that a claimant alleging robbery must take a special oath, as cited by the Shach in seif 13. There is another question regarding those who swear and collect. Why should we not believe a claimant who says that he already swore, if he can claim that he swore outside the defendant's presence? Although seif 23 rules that ideally he should swear before the other party, if he actually swore outside his presence he has fulfilled the oath. In many cases of swearing and collecting, then, he should be believed through a migo that he could say, "I swore outside your presence." The Rama in siman 296 recognizes a migo to exempt from an oath. Where the claimant holds a written instrument, however, the Shach in siman 82 explains that such a migo may be a migo to extract. Still, if the claimant alleges that he already swore, the opposing party would be saying, "I do not know whether I became obligated to you," because without the oath there is no enforceable claim at all. Even if the opposing party admits part of the debt, this should not create the rule that one obligated to swear but unable to do so must pay, because he was not expected to know whether the claimant took his oath. See Shach, siman 72, seif katan 51. Yet the difficulty remains: if the claimant says, "I swore outside your presence," the other party cannot impose an oath merely on the doubtful allegation that perhaps he did not swear. Even if he says, "I swore in your presence," perhaps he should be believed through the migo that he could have said, "I swore outside your presence," especially since the Rosh himself accepts a migo to exempt from an oath. It appears to me that the answer is the same as the view of several authorities cited by the Rama, that where the custom is to swear before the beit din attendant a person is not believed to say, "I already swore." Here too, his claim that he swore outside the other's presence contradicts the established practice that such oaths are taken before the opposing party, and he is therefore not believed. According to this, the Gemara could not give the proposed distinction as a universal difference between Torah and rabbinic oaths, because Torah oaths include the oaths of custodians and oaths triggered by a doubtful claim based on one witness, which are not necessarily taken in the claimant's presence. In such cases the defendant does have a migo that he swore outside the claimant's presence. The Gemara sought a difference applicable to all Torah oaths. In Or Zarua, Bava Metzia 17, I saw in the name of Raavad an explanation of the Gemara's phrase excluding an oath a person obligated himself to take. The case is a purchaser who has held a slave for less than the years needed for a presumption of ownership, where the seller could ordinarily recover without an oath, but the purchaser said, "I will swear." The purchaser is regarded as having waived his claim to the slave until the seller swears, and this waiver requires no kinyan. This is a strong proof for the Shach in Choshen Mishpat siman 241, seif katan 5. A final judgment in such a case does not render the purchaser an established denier. At first glance this is difficult, because it concerns one who swears and collects, and ordinarily a claim "I already swore" would not help him at all. According to the above, however, the case can be understood where he says, "I swore outside your presence," in which case the purchaser is uncertain whether payment has been made. This would imply that "I do not know whether I paid" can obligate even where the party was not expected to know, a point discussed in the Rambam, beginning of chapter 4 of Hilchot She'eilah, chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah, and Urim VeTumim siman 43. It would also imply that one may claim that he swore privately. This does not contradict my suggestion based on custom, because an oath voluntarily undertaken by a litigant has no fixed courtroom custom comparable to ordinary judicial oaths. Rabbi Yonatan, cited in Or Zarua there, expresses doubt regarding one who says he swore privately. I have also been uncertain whether the rule that one who denies an oath he voluntarily undertook does not become an established denier applies equally to a monetary obligation he voluntarily undertook. This requires study. The Rashba and Ran cited in Or Zarua there appear to conflict with the Shach's ruling that where the claimant makes a definite claim, the defendant must swear even when his defense is that he already took a shevuat heset. I later saw this discussed in Urim VeTumim, seif katan 20. It also requires study, according to those who recognize a migo to impose an oath, why the claimant should not be believed to demand an oath through a migo that he could simply have made a new claim against the defendant. See Shach at the end of simanim 36 and 39. What the Shach writes, that he wishes to swear that he owes nothing, also requires study, as does his reasoning in the following seif katan and in siman 93, seif katan 14. In seif 28 one can perhaps answer the Beit Yosef's question cited by the Sema by applying the rule of dividing a statement, treating the words as an admission relevant to the responsum. See there carefully.
