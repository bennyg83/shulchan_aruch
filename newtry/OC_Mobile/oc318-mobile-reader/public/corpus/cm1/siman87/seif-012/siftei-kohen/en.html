Mahari Isserlein discusses the view attributed to the Rambam's teachers that one who ordinarily swears and collects may say to the defendant, "You swear and be exempt." The Rosh's discussion of the rule that a clever defendant can bring a case into the category of a Torah oath does not refute this. That discussion concerns the formal shifting of an oath. Here the claimant says something more basic: "I waive the special enactment made for my benefit. Treat me as an ordinary claimant, and require the defendant to take the oath ordinarily imposed upon one who denies a definite monetary claim." This ruling is clear. A rabbinic benefit was not enacted so that its beneficiary must suffer through it. He may renounce the benefit and proceed under the ordinary law, in which case the defendant takes a rabbinic oath of denial and is exempt.
The defendant is exempt. The claimant may nevertheless impose a general ban upon anyone who wrongfully withholds his money. Some have the defendant say, "Vow to me by the life of your head," considering that lighter than a ban. This does not appear correct, for who has established that an undertaking by the life of one's head is less severe than a ban? The sources stating that such an undertaking is not an oath mean only that it lacks the legal status of an oath; they do not establish that it is necessarily lighter than a general ban.
