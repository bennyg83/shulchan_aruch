Whom it is possible to believe. See Baer Heitev, Responsa VeShav HaKohen siman 81 at length, and Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 69, discusses whether one witness who testifies that a husband waived his wife's ketubah oath exempts her from that oath. He initially writes that in this case all should agree that the witness does not exempt her. The dispute applies only to the Mordechai's case, which is the source of the Rama's ruling, where the defendant had a migo that he could have said, 'I purchased it from you.' There one can say that if he were lying, he could have made that alternative claim and the witness could likewise have falsely supported it. Here, however, the wife's primary oath concerns whether she was already paid, and the witness cannot testify to that. There is therefore no migo and, according to all opinions, she must swear. Chatam Sofer then questions the premise. Perhaps waiver of a wife's ketubah oath is unlike an ordinary claim that a debt instrument was forgiven. Forgiveness of a documented debt is considered a weak claim because people do not ordinarily forgive debts and a document stands for collection. But if a husband knows that he did not give his wife property in advance and trusts her, he should not force her into a potentially needless oath. In such a case the claim that he waived her oath may itself be a strong claim without a migo, and if one witness supports her, that witness should exempt her. He distinguishes further. If she claims that the husband waived the oath in the presence of witnesses and only one of those witnesses is presently available, the waiver itself may indeed be a strong claim, and the supporting witness can exempt her. But if she claims that he waived it privately between them, and she has one witness who merely testifies that the husband later told him he had waived his wife's oath, we cannot say that he waived because he trusted her and wished to avoid a needless oath. She would still need to swear that he made the waiver, so a private waiver would not have achieved that purpose. Rather, such a waiver must mean that he intended to forgive even any property she might actually have taken. That is a weak claim, requiring a migo. In that situation, even those who disagree with the Mordechai can agree that one witness does not exempt her from the oath.
