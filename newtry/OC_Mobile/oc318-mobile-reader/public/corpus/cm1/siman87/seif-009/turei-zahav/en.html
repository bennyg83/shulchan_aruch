(Ibid., “and the person imposing the oath shall answer Amen”) The Sema asks from siman 82, where the opposing party need not answer Amen. He answers that there the person wishes to leave the synagogue and therefore need not remain, whereas here he is present at the oath and should answer Amen to increase the fear of the oath. This distinction is astonishing, and the original question is unfounded. Here no oath is imposed until the opposing party first accepts the ban. Therefore, unless he answers Amen, he cannot compel the other person to swear, since the person may think he is not included in the ban when he does not answer Amen. Because the ban is what compels the oath, he must perform it in the strongest manner. The glossator notes that this should be considered together with the rule in siman 123, seif 9, that if the claimant is not in town the defendant is nevertheless sworn, because his oath is not delayed for the sake of a minor enactment; when the claimant is present, however, the ban must be stated clearly. Above, by contrast, the ban produces no new legal consequence. The claimant is merely granted permission to ban whoever robbed him, and the opposing party is under no obligation to answer Amen or even to be present. It therefore appears to me that where one liable for a heset oath reverses it, and the other party does not wish to swear and collect but only to impose an unspecified ban, the defendant need not expressly accept the ban or answer Amen. That ban likewise creates no new legal result; it merely permits the claimant to ban whoever wronged him. This is what appears from the language of the authorities, although the practice among judges is to require the defendant expressly to accept a ban that he owes the claimant nothing.
