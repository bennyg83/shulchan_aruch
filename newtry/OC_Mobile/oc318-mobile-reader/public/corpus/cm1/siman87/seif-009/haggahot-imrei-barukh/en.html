(Shach, seif katan 19) Except in the case of one suspected of false oaths, for otherwise anyone who has one witness, etc. Note: This is not what Teshuvot Maimoniyot says. I proved there that when a witness testifies against one suspected of false oaths, if we were to apply 'one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay,' then the single witness would be establishing a monetary obligation, whereas the Torah states that one witness shall not rise against a person for any iniquity or sin, but only for an oath. It follows from his words that where a witness testifies against a suspected person, the suspected person is exempt by Torah law. This resolves the difficulty of the Ran and Rashba at the beginning of chapter Kol HaNishba'in. They ask that, after explaining that the Mishnah lists among those who swear and collect only cases where according to strict law they would receive nothing, why does the Mishnah include the case of a suspected person, where his opponent swears and collects? By Torah law, one might have thought that the opponent should collect without an oath. According to Maharam's view the answer is clear: there is a case where the suspected person is liable to an oath because of a single witness. By Torah law the rule of 'one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay' does not apply there, because that would make the witness establish money. Rabbinically, however, they treated him as one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, and therefore his opponent swears and collects. According to this answer, however, a difficulty arises where both litigants are suspected. If we say that the oath returns to its original place, meaning to the person originally obligated, and because he cannot swear he must pay, then by Torah law he should not have been obligated to swear at all in such a case, lest the single witness thereby establish money.
