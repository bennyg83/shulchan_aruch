Not by a substitute Name. The Sema cites Meisharim that hesset consists only of a general ban rather than a formal oath. A difficulty arises from the shepherd case. The reason the opposing party swears and collects from a suspected defendant is that we do not administer an oath to a suspected false swearer. But a general ban can be imposed even on a suspected person, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule in siman 92. Even a direct ban can be imposed according to the Sema. If hesset is only such a ban, why not impose it directly on the suspected defendant rather than shift anything to the claimant? The answer is that under the final law we do not create one rabbinic enactment upon another. We therefore do not permit the claimant to take a new shifted oath merely because the defendant is suspected. Nor can we impose a formal oath upon the suspected defendant, because of the concern for a false oath. We may nevertheless place him under a ban, hoping he will withdraw from falsehood. If he persists, the sin of violating the ban is upon him, but the court has not administered a formal oath. Under the Gemara's initial assumption that one rabbinic enactment could be layered upon another, the claimant could say, "I do not trust this suspected person even to accept a ban. I will swear and collect." The defendant's willingness to accept a ban would not defeat the claimant because his very status is one of suspected dishonesty. The same answers the view cited by Mordechai that in some circumstances even an oath can technically be administered to a suspected person. The claimant may nevertheless refuse to rely upon that oath and invoke the shifted mechanism if the law permits it. Thus the Gemara's argument can be understood even if hesset itself is formulated as a ban.
