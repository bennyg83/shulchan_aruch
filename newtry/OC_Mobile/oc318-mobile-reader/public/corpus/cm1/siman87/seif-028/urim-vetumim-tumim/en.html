He is not disqualified on the basis of his own admission. The Tur writes that the Rif ruled in a responsum: if a person took hesset and afterward voluntarily admitted part of the claim, he is treated as disqualified from taking an oath. He must pay what he admitted, and as to the remainder the opposing party swears and collects. The Tur objects: how can a person become disqualified through his own statement? Rav Sherira Gaon writes that if he made the admission as part of repentance, he is exempt as to the remainder. If he did not admit by way of repentance, he pays the admitted amount and swears on the rest, because a person is not disqualified from an oath by his own admission. The Rif certainly did not overlook the explicit Gemara in Sanhedrin concerning one who says, "So and so sodomized me willingly." We divide his statement, because a person cannot render himself wicked. The Bach therefore explains that when the Rif says the opposing party swears and collects, he means only that the opposing party has the option to say, "A litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. You yourself admitted that you are disqualified, so I will swear and collect." This is the position of the Ramah cited by the Tur below in siman 92 without recorded dissent. It appears that the Baal HaTerumot understood the Rif this way as well. In Gate 10, Part 1, law 3, after citing the Rif, he writes: "It requires study why the Rav called him disqualified, since there are no witnesses contradicting him. If it is because of one witness, one witness does not render him disqualified," and so on. His wording is puzzling. Why discuss one witness at all? He should simply have asked the Tur's question that a person is not rendered disqualified by his own admission. Gidulei Terumah labors to explain this. It therefore appears that the Baal HaTerumot understood the issue conceptually. The rule that a person cannot disqualify himself may be explained by saying that he is not believed against himself because a person is considered related to himself. If that is the entire reason, then whenever we independently know that what he says is true, he really is disqualified. Witnesses exist only to establish facts that are otherwise uncertain. This resembles the principle that witnesses were created only because people may lie. According to that understanding, the Ramah's rule below in siman 92 is logical. The opposing party can say: "Your own admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses for the monetary aspect. You admitted that you swore falsely and are disqualified. I will therefore swear and collect." No one can prevent him from relying on the admission. But there is another possible understanding. The Torah may have decreed that a person does not become legally wicked or disqualified unless two witnesses establish the offense and remove him from his presumption of fitness. Under this approach, even if the admission is factually true, it does not create the legal status of a disqualified person without two witnesses. If so, the opposing party cannot transform the admission into disqualification merely by invoking the rule that a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses for monetary matters. At first glance this second explanation appears compelled by the Gemara. The person says, "So and so sodomized me willingly," and another witness joins him to execute the perpetrator. Since the speaker is related to himself, we divide his statement and disregard the part that makes himself wicked. But what of the second witness? That witness also testifies that the first witness was willingly sodomized. If the second witness is lying, how can we rely on him? If he is telling the truth, then his own testimony establishes that his fellow witness is wicked and disqualified. There should therefore be no valid pair of witnesses at all. Indeed, the entire testimony might fall under the rule that if one member of a group of witnesses is related or disqualified, the whole testimony is invalid. One might answer that we also divide the second witness's words, but the principle of dividing testimony because a person is related to himself applies only to one's own self incriminating statement, not to testimony about another. The Rosh writes this at the beginning of Makkot. This apparent dilemma would support the rule that even if the second witness speaks truthfully, one witness alone cannot legally disqualify the first. The first witness remains qualified until two witnesses establish his offense. The testimony can therefore stand. However, I found that the Raavan explicitly interprets the case differently. He says that the second witness does not testify that the first person was sodomized willingly. He testifies only that the perpetrator sodomized him, without saying whether the victim consented. If the second witness also testified to willing participation, then the second witness would indeed be testifying that his fellow witness is wicked and there would not be two qualified witnesses. The Raavan discusses this at length. If the Raavan's interpretation were accepted, the previous proof would fall away. Yet the plain language of the other authorities does not indicate such a limitation. The Rambam states the rule without distinguishing the wording of the second witness. There is also evidence from responsum Rashba 1236. The Rashba distinguishes between one who says, "I committed adultery with your wife," and one who says, "Your wife committed adultery with me," based on the law of divided testimony. He asks why the Gemara does not use examples such as "I sodomized so and so willingly" or "I had relations with his wife." According to the Raavan's reading, such examples would not work, because the second witness would necessarily have to testify to the perpetrator's willing sexual act, and the act of the male participant is presumed intentional because erection requires awareness, as the Rashba himself writes. The second witness would therefore be disqualifying the first. The Rashba's proof thus indicates that the Raavan's limitation is not accepted. Tosafot in Makkot 6, s.v. "the sodomized person," likewise appears to say explicitly that the second witness can testify that the first was willingly sodomized and nevertheless the first is not disqualified, because one witness does not make him disqualified. The Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 115 similarly rule that if one witness says he committed adultery with another man's wife and another witness corroborates the adultery, they can combine to prohibit the woman to her husband, even though according to the second witness the first witness participated in the sin. The first witness does not become disqualified on the testimony of only one other witness. This supports the second explanation: legal disqualification requires two witnesses as a decree of Torah law. Even where one witness's testimony makes the offense factually plausible, the person retains his legal presumption of fitness until two witnesses remove it. If so, the Ramah's rule cited below in siman 92 is difficult. Why should the opposing party be allowed to say, "You admitted that you are disqualified, so I will swear and collect"? The admission may establish the monetary facts but cannot create the legal status of disqualification that the Torah reserves for testimony of two witnesses. This, I believe, is exactly what the Baal HaTerumot meant in objecting to the Rif. When he says, "If because of one witness, one witness does not render him disqualified," he is invoking the root case from which the law is derived, namely "so and so sodomized me willingly." There too the structure is effectively that only one external witness testifies to the self incriminating portion. The Baal HaTerumot therefore abbreviates the point and relies on the known source. His words are precise. The fact that the litigant admits the conduct does not change the legal requirement for two witnesses before disqualification. Accordingly, the opposing party should not be able to swear and collect merely because the defendant admitted conduct that, if established by two witnesses, would make him disqualified. This differs from the Shach, who understands the Ramah's ruling in siman 92 as undisputed and therefore questions why the Mechaber writes it as "there are those who say." According to the Baal HaTerumot, there is a genuine dispute. One should not object from the Gemara's question, "If he is suspected regarding money, is he not also suspected regarding an oath?" That question can be understood in two ways. First, within the same factual incident, if we are prepared to suspect him of one prohibition for the sake of money, perhaps the same motivation should make us suspect him of the oath prohibition as well. This does not mean that a formal status of disqualification has already attached. Second, that is precisely the Gemara's initial assumption. At the conclusion, when we rule that one suspected regarding money is not automatically suspected regarding an oath, formal disqualification still requires the established halachic criteria, including two witnesses. Tosafot discuss this point. Thus the Gemara does not prove that an admission alone creates legal disqualification. This appears to me to be the proper understanding of the Baal HaTerumot's objection to the Rif. Still, I do not fully understand the Tur's strong objection to the Rif. The dispute between the Rif and Rav Sherira Gaon may depend on the two explanations of dividing testimony discussed by the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Makkot, which I analyzed above at the end of siman 33, seif katan 11, and in siman 34. One interpretation says that when the person says "so and so sodomized me willingly," we accept the factual core that he was sodomized but disregard only the word "willingly." The statement is divided into a valid part and a self incriminating part. According to the Rashba's responsum, if the words cannot be divided factually, as where a man says "I committed adultery with your wife," then one cannot simply preserve the accusation while discarding the self incriminating content in the same way. The Raavad gives another interpretation. Even when the factual sentence cannot be split, the speaker is simply not a witness against himself because he is a litigant with respect to his own status. Testimony can therefore be accepted regarding others while disregarded regarding himself. Apply this to our case. The defendant first swore hesset denying the claim and later admits part of the debt. If we accept the admission fully as a monetary admission, then it follows inescapably that he swore falsely. There is no factual way to say, "The admission is true but the oath was not false." If the oath was truthful, there is no valid admission. The two consequences are inseparable. According to the Rashba's narrower model of divided testimony, where an indivisible statement both helps others and makes the speaker wicked, the entire self incriminating consequence may not be capable of being severed while preserving the rest. One of the two aspects must give way. In a monetary setting the stronger rule may be to accept the admission, because a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses, and then permit the opposing party to treat him as disqualified for purposes of the remaining oath. This can explain the Rif. It is strained to answer that monetary admission and personal disqualification can always be separated simply because one arises through the doctrine of admission and the other through witness law. We have no clear source for drawing such a distinction when the very same indivisible fact necessarily entails both consequences. Rav Sherira Gaon, by contrast, may follow the Raavad's model. The defendant's statement is accepted as a monetary admission against himself, because that is the law of a litigant's admission, but it is not accepted as testimony establishing his own wickedness or oath disqualification. In that respect a person cannot testify against himself. Thus he pays the admitted amount but can still swear concerning the remainder. The two rulings can therefore reflect the two established understandings of dividing testimony. The Rif's position is not so astonishing as the Tur suggests, even though the practical ruling may depend on which conceptual model is accepted.
