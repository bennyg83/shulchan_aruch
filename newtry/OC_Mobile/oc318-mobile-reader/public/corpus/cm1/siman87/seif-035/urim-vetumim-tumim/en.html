That he already returned it, etc. The Sema wishes to emend the text to "he already took possession," because if the dispute concerns who should distribute the poor people's funds, there is no denial of the poor people's underlying money. But if the text reads "returned," we rule that one can take an oath concerning funds dedicated to the poor. The Taz writes that this applies where the community or beit din, who act as the hand of the poor, bring the claim. The individual depositor himself cannot impose the oath, because the defendant can say, "You are not my litigant." Perhaps the community or beit din could sue him directly, though the present seif does not discuss that situation. The beginning of the Taz's distinction is correct, but I do not understand the end. How can the community or beit din bring a definite claim? They do not know whether the property was returned. If they rely on the depositor's statement, the depositor is interested in the outcome, because the defendant says he returned the property to him and that could make the depositor personally liable to replace it. An interested witness does not create even the status of "I was informed" for purposes of an oath, as I wrote above in siman 75. More fundamentally, if this depositor himself had authority to distribute the funds to the poor, then the defendant has a simple migo. What substantive claim does the depositor have concerning whether the property was returned to him? The defendant can say that he distributed the funds directly to the poor, in which event he would be exempt from an oath. The Rama accepts migo even to exempt from an oath. Therefore he should likewise be exempt when he says he returned the property to the depositor. There is no difficulty in the text.
