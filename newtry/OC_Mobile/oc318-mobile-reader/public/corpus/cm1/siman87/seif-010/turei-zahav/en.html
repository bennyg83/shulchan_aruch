(Seif 25) “Nevertheless, he must swear to him.” This is difficult from the first chapter of Kiddushin, page 28. Where one claims against another, “You are my slave,” the Gemara asks that the claimant should be placed under a ban, since the Mishna teaches that one who calls another a slave is placed under a ban. Why is this different from saying, “You are my robber,” when here too he is claiming his own property from the defendant by asserting that he purchased him? The defendant should swear that he is not owned by the claimant. We must apparently say that, according to the claimant’s allegation, the defendant is not eligible to take an oath, and therefore we do not administer one. The glossator rejects this entirely. A Canaanite slave is certainly eligible for an oath; in all legal matters he is compared to a woman, and women take oaths, since Scripture equates woman and man for all laws, as stated in siman 96. The same applies to a Canaanite slave, and women and slaves are likewise equivalent concerning testimony and credibility. The original question is also mistaken. Calling a man a slave disqualifies him from the Jewish community, and the Gemara expressly says that one who calls another wicked may descend against him to his livelihood, although beit din does not adjudicate that insult. A monetary claim, by contrast, does require an oath, and the defendant may not retaliate as though called wicked, since the very fact that the court administers an oath proves the distinction. The Jerusalem Talmud, cited by Maimonides in Laws of Slaves, chapter 25, states that beit din administers an oath to a slave, and the Rama rules likewise in siman 89. One might have distinguished between a slave who swears and collects, where the owner’s money should not be taken on the slave’s oath, and one who swears and is exempt. One might also have read the Jerusalem Talmud rhetorically, were it not for these authorities. In truth, however, an ordinary slave is presumed to be a thief and kidnapper, as stated in Ketubot and Bava Batra, “If he turned out to be a thief or kidnapper, the sale is valid,” and is therefore disqualified from an oath. If, however, he is known to be upright, like Tavi the slave of Rabban Gamliel, he is unquestionably valid for an oath even though invalid as a witness. Returning to the Taz’s argument, one cannot answer that every disqualification connected with money may stem from an old debt about which the person rationalizes, and that an oath will cause him to withdraw, whereas a bodily status such as slavery renders him wholly ineligible according to the claim. If so, no robber would ever be disqualified, because he could excuse himself by alleging an old debt. Rather, once he seized property as robbery, there is no such justification, similar to the explanation of the ingot of Rabbi Abba. The glossator answers that the ingot case is different, because the defendant is obligated to swear against the witness and cannot do so, while one witness alone does not disqualify him. This is clear.
