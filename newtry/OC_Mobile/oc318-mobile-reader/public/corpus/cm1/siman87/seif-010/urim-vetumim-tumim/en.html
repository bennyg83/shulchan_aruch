The Shach writes that Piskei Rekanati expresses doubt about a case involving a person disqualified from taking an oath, where the opposing party is therefore required to swear and collect. If the opposing party seized property but then refused to take the oath, do we remove the property from him or allow him to retain it? The Shach reasons that we remove it. It appears to me that this can be proved. Tosafot and the Rosh hold that in the case of two disqualified litigants involving a note, if the noteholder is the one who would have to swear, the debtor must nevertheless pay in the circumstances they discuss. Why? If the noteholder's inability to swear were no worse than a person who could swear but simply refuses, and if seizure by the person who would otherwise collect were effective without the oath, then the debtor here should likewise retain his own property. The claimant is disqualified and cannot complete the shifted oath, while the debtor is already in possession of his own money. How could beit din extract it? The fact that payment is nevertheless required demonstrates that possession or seizure does not dispense with the oath. If the person who must swear and collect does not take the oath, his seizure is ineffective and the property is removed. This is simple.

This does not create a difficulty with the Rif's explanation of "if he is clever," namely that the husband should arrange matters so that if the woman later seizes property she will have to take a Torah oath rather than merely a rabbinic oath. One might ask: if she seizes money in the presence of witnesses, has she not already collected her ketubah? The husband can then immediately sue her on the basis of the witness who saw the prior payment and require a Torah oath that she did not collect twice. Why is any special strategy needed? The answer is that the woman may simply refrain from taking the present oath and wait until the witness dies or travels overseas. She can then seize the money, and the husband will no longer be able to generate the Torah oath because the witness is unavailable to testify before beit din. Therefore, if the husband is clever, he does not wait. He pays her immediately and then immediately sues her while the witness is still alive and available, thereby creating the stronger Torah oath.
