If he seized first. Rif explains the Gemara's advice to transform the wife's oath into a Torah oath by noting a practical difference. With a Mishnah oath imposed upon one who collects, if she has already seized property and refuses to swear, the property may not be removed from her. With a Torah oath, by contrast, beit din can descend to the property. Pnei Yehoshua asks: if she has already seized movables, why is all the elaborate second payment before witnesses needed? The husband can simply sue her for return of those movables. With one witness supporting him, this should become an ordinary defendant's oath rather than an oath of one who collects. The answer is that if she holds the movables only as collateral, the ketubah itself remains unpaid and continues to be a land lien. A one witness dispute about whether the ketubah was paid is therefore still fundamentally a dispute concerning a land lien, and one witness does not directly create a Torah oath regarding land. If the husband actually transfers the movables to her in complete payment, extinguishing the ketubah, then the land lien is gone and the analysis changes. But so long as the movables are merely held as security, the document remains in full force and the claim retains its land character. This is supported by the rule that denial of a documented loan is treated as denial of a land lien even though movables are also subject to ordinary collection. Likewise, if a specific movable is designated to secure the loan, the existence of that movable security does not remove the debt's land lien character. The sugya of testimony concerning a widow who has seized movables does not refute this. There the Gemara can establish that the husband had no land, so the claim is no longer a land lien in practice. Accordingly, the Rif and Tosafot can speak of seizure of movables as collateral without automatically turning the matter into an ordinary movable claim.
