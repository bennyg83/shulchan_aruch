If he first seized, etc. This refers to money, not movables, as explained in Even HaEzer 97:11. It appears that if he seized produce from land with a known market price, sold it, and retained the money, we likewise do not remove the money from him, because the concern of 'who appraised it for you?' no longer applies. This is supported by Rosh in the chapter *Chezkas HaBatim* regarding Rabbah bar Sharshom. The seizure here occurs in the presence of witnesses, so there is no migo involved.
We do not remove it, etc. This applies to persons who by Torah law would be entitled to collect without an oath, where the Sages merely imposed an oath as an additional safeguard. If such a person seized before swearing, we do not remove the property. But categories such as a hired worker or a robbed person, who by Torah law would not collect even with an oath and whose entire right to collect was created by rabbinic enactment, must return what they seized if they refuse the required oath. Likewise, if a defendant reversed a hesset oath onto the claimant and the claimant seized but refuses to swear, we remove the property. Similarly, where the defendant is suspected and the law is that the opposing party swears and collects, if the claimant seized but refuses to swear, we remove it. See Tumim.
From him. Sema writes that if the claimant holds a debt document, no general ban is imposed because the document is in his hands. Shach already rejected this. As explained above in siman 82, a ban is still imposed; we simply do not descend to his assets. Know also that the rule that seizure prevents removal applies to ordinary rabbinic oath cases. But where one witness testifies that a document was paid and that witness is still present, the claimant now possesses the money together with a witness who saw the payment. Since he seized in order to satisfy his document, he must take a Torah oath against that witness. He has no excuse. The rule therefore concerns a case where the witness is no longer available. See Tumim.
