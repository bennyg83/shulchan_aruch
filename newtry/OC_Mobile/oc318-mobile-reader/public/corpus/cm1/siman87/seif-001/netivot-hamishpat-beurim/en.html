See Shach, seif katan 3, through his statement that a deposit is deemed in the domain of its owner, from which he concludes that even if the deposited item is physically in the custodian's house, an admission concerning it is treated as "here it is" and does not generate a partial admission oath. At first glance this is difficult from the beginning of the chapter HaBayit VeHaAliyah. There, if one recognizes some of the stones, the Gemara says it is a partial admission, and if the other party says concerning some of them, "I do not know," he is liable under the rule that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay. The same rule appears below in siman 144. According to Shach, it should be a case of "here it is." It therefore appears that only a deposit is treated as "here it is," because wherever the deposit is located it remains in the owner's legal domain. A stolen object is different. If one is sued for two utensils that he stole and admits one of them, even if it is physically in his house it is not "here it is," because stolen property does not stand in the owner's domain until it is actually returned before beit din. Indeed, where property was stolen and the owners did not despair, neither the thief nor the owner can consecrate it, because it is not in either party's effective possession. Since it is not in the owner's domain, the admission is not "here it is." Although Bava Kamma 105 says that once he admits, it is as though the owner said, "Let it remain for me in your possession," Tosafot there explain that this is only where the owner remained silent and departed. This resolves the case in HaBayit VeHaAliyah. There the stones must have been moved into that party's domain, and the case is one where he moved them as an act of theft. If he moved them without the owners' permission, he presumably intended to take them as his own, and therefore the admission is not "here it is." If they simply fell by themselves into one party's domain, it appears that there is no partial admission at all, even according to the opinion that "here it is" is liable. A partial admission exists only where the claim concerns a loan or deposit that places a personal obligation upon the defendant to pay or return. If Reuven says to Shimon, "These two animals that entered your property, or that are standing in the marketplace, are both mine," and Shimon says, "One is mine and one is yours," this is not a partial admission, because there is no personal obligation upon him. They merely dispute ownership of a particular object. Therefore the Gemara's case must specifically concern one who moved the stones. Nor is there a difficulty from the Mechaber's ruling in seif 3 that where one says "here it is" as an evasion, he is liable for a Torah oath. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 4 write that one who says "here it is" is effectively denying the entire claim, and a person is not ordinarily brazen enough to deny the whole claim. One might therefore ask: even if his statement is evasive, he is still in effect denying the whole claim and acting brazenly, so why should he not be believed by a migo that he could have denied everything? The answer is that there remains a greater degree of brazenness in denying the entire claim and returning nothing than in admitting part and immediately returning that portion. Thus the alternative remains a migo of brazenness and does not exempt him.
