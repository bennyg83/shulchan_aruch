Partial admission. Suppose Reuven claims, "I entrusted two cows to you as borrowed," while Shimon admits one was borrowed but says the other was rented. Both animals are still alive when the claim is first made, and afterward both die through unavoidable accident. I was uncertain whether this creates the Torah oath of partial admission. At the time of the original plea there was not yet a present monetary denial, because both animals were intact. The difference between borrowing and renting mattered only if an unavoidable accident later occurred. One might therefore say that the first plea emerged exempt, and a later event cannot retroactively turn it into partial admission. Ran indeed writes that partial admission must exist at the moment the plea leaves the defendant's mouth. On the other hand, Bava Kamma shows that a denial can count as denial of money even where the present object itself is still intact, if the defendant's legal position would make him liable upon a later event. Rava discusses stolen chametz after the festival and compares it to a guardian who says, "I am only an unpaid custodian," thereby exempting himself from theft and loss. The denial concerns the future monetary liability, and the Gemara treats that as denial of money. The same concept can apply here. The defendant's statement that the second cow was rented rather than borrowed is presently significant because it determines who bears unavoidable accident. It is therefore a monetary denial already now, even before the accident actually occurs. Still, so long as the cows remain alive, the oath need not yet be administered. This resembles a debt whose obligation depends on a future condition. The admission and denial can exist now, while the time for the oath arrives only when the condition creating actual payment occurs. If the cows later die through unavoidable accident, the oath can then become due. There is, however, another complication. We rule that a custodian's property is not subjected until the negligent or obligating event. Before then there may be no actual monetary obligation at all. That may distinguish the custodian case from an ordinary debtor whose future payment is already fixed. The matter therefore remains open.
Ready in his hand. The Shach writes that the rule that "here it is" applies specifically to a loan, while with a deposit even if the object is in the custodian's house it is treated as though already in the owner's domain, because wherever an intact deposit is, it remains in its owner's possession. A related case follows. If one claims, "You sold or gave me two measures of wheat from this storehouse," and the other admits only one measure, this is partial admission even though the subject resembles a deposit or specified stock. Because the admitted wheat is not itself identified, the defendant can choose which portion to give. It is therefore not "here it is." Nimukei Yosef proves the principle from the dispute over a large or small slave together with clothing. Even where the defendant admits a garment, if the specific garment portion is not identified and he can select the three cubits from wherever he wishes in the cloth, the admission is not treated as "here it is." This also raises a question on the opening sugya of Bava Metzia, where a divided garment is treated as "here it is" even though the admitted half is not predesignated. The distinction may be that there the defendant has no power to select which part to give. Beit din itself divides the garment. Where the admitting party retains the right to choose which portion will satisfy the admission, the object is not yet placed in the claimant's hands and therefore is not "here it is."
A pledge is not considered "here it is." Ritva writes that giving collateral for the admitted portion does not count as "here it is." Even if the defendant wishes to give the pledge in complete payment, he ordinarily cannot force a creditor to accept property instead of money. Even if one assumes that when the debtor has no money he may be able to pay with other property, an item requiring appraisal is still not "here it is." It follows that even if three factors are present, namely the debtor intends complete payment, the pledge's value is fixed, and the debtor has no cash, it still need not qualify as "here it is" under our law. Ritva's discussion assumes only hypothetically that another object might be forced upon the creditor when no money exists. According to our ruling, the creditor may still say, "I will wait until you have money." Therefore the offered object does not become present payment merely because the debtor lacks cash. The Shach seems more inclined to call such a case "here it is," but Ritva's language points the other way.
A pledge is considered "here it is." The Shach ultimately rejects the view that a pledge qualifies, even when it was given at the time of the loan. He distinguishes personal handwriting or a debt instrument whose existence cannot be denied, saying the admission there concerns the very money claimed, whereas a pledge can be lost through accident and can also require an oath before collection. The argument from an oath is not persuasive. Formal debt instruments can themselves require an oath before collection, yet many authorities still call the admitted written portion "here it is" because the debtor cannot deny the obligation. The Shach's stronger point is that a pledge is only security and does not itself become payment. The creditor's money claim remains outstanding. This supports the rule that a pledge is not "here it is."
