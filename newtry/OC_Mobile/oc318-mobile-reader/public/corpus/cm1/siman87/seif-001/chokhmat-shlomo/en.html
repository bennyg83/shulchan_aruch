Seif 1, in the gloss: Some say that collateral is considered “here it is,” for even a document can be considered “here it is.” See Shach, who writes that this applies only where the creditor has permission to sell the collateral without valuation by beit din or by any other person; otherwise it is not “here it is.” At first glance this is difficult from the Gemara in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, which says, concerning the other teaching of Rabbi Chiya, “The Tanna also taught this, for what he is holding is ‘here it is,’ and nevertheless the Mishna requires an oath.” What proof is there? Siman 138 explains that if the disputed item cannot physically be divided without loss, the parties divide its monetary value. Certainly if one party wishes to sell quickly for less while the other wishes to wait for a higher price, the second can object; beit din must appraise the item and arrange its sale. Thus the Mishna may concern an indivisible item that requires appraisal by beit din, which according to the Shach would not be “here it is.” It is difficult to answer merely that the Mishna speaks generally and therefore includes divisible property. It therefore appears that the Shach’s limitation concerns only collateral. The body of collateral is not yet the creditor’s property. According to several authorities it would not ordinarily count as “here it is” for precisely that reason. Even according to those who do regard it as “here it is,” that is because the creditor can sell it and take his money. Therefore he must have power to sell it himself, without a remaining need for valuation. By contrast, where the item itself already belongs to the claimant, it is certainly “here it is” even if valuation remains necessary. A practical difference follows. If one litigant alone is holding an indivisible object and says, “Half is mine and half is yours,” the admitted half is still considered “here it is.” Similarly, if he admits, “I sold you one object at whatever value experts determine,” and he tenders that object while the claimant says, “You sold me two objects,” he is exempt from a Torah oath on the ground of “here it is,” even though appraisal is still required. This appears simple and correct in my opinion.
