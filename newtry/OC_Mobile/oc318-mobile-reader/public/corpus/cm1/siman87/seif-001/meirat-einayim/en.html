Where he could have retained it with the claim, 'It never happened,' etc. This excludes a case where he borrowed money and the lender said, 'Do not repay me except before witnesses,' or where an object was deposited with him before witnesses and the witnesses or beit din now saw the object in his hands before the lawsuit. In such cases he cannot make those claims concerning the identified object. The same applies where the claimant demands items ordinarily lent or rented and witnesses currently saw them in the defendant's possession. In all such cases he lacks the ordinary migo that supports possession. The rules of hesset and of a partial admission therefore depend on whether, at the time of the claim, the defendant had a legally available full-denial defense regarding the very property in dispute. Where the property was seen in his possession in circumstances excluding 'I returned it' or 'it never happened,' that supporting migo falls away.
If he admits part, etc. The Ran writes in chapter Shevuas HaDayanim about one who claims one hundred zuz from another, fifty as a loan and fifty for bodily injury, and the defendant denies everything. Although the injured person is entitled to swear and collect, this does not make the defendant a partial admitter until that oath has actually been taken. Once the injured person has sworn, however, the defendant who continues to deny everything is treated as one who admits part, because the oath establishes the fifty as though witnesses testified to it. It is comparable to a claim of one hundred where the defendant denies everything and witnesses establish fifty.
A partial admission, etc. The Torah oath of a partial admitter, the oath generated by one witness, and the rabbinic hesset oath, together with their rationales, were explained at the beginning of siman 75. The reason is not that a person is suspected of monetary wrongdoing but not of a false oath. The Gemara concludes that people regard theft and false swearing as comparably severe. Rather, a partial admitter is presumed not to intend theft; he is temporarily evading payment and intends to pay later, so the Torah imposes an oath that prevents this evasion. A total denier ordinarily has the presumption that a person does not brazenly deny his creditor completely, and therefore has no Torah oath. The Sages nevertheless imposed hesset because perhaps he is temporarily withholding money due to an uncertain older claim. An oath forces him to confront the possibility that, once the uncertainty is resolved, his oath would prove false retroactively.
Only through the witness. See the end of siman 75 in the Tur and in the Mechaber, seif 23.
Whether concerning a loan or a deposit. This excludes Rashi's view, cited by the Tur, that the Torah exemption of one who denies everything applies specifically to a loan because the lender did him a favor and therefore he is presumed not to brazenly deny him, whereas no such favor exists with a deposit. The ruling teaches that there is no distinction, in accordance with the majority of poskim.
Even if he says, etc., and the other says, 'It never happened.' This is not limited to a case where he admits receiving the loan but says he repaid it, or says that he has an offsetting claim, in which case even by his own account there was once a monetary relationship and he must take a hesset oath on denying the balance. Even if he says the transaction never occurred at all, the same rule applies. So writes the Tur.
As explained above in siman 75. See the Tur and Mechaber there from seif 17 onward.
If he says, 'Here it is,' etc. Because he immediately gives the amount he admits, with respect to the remainder he is treated as one who denies everything and receives the law of a total denier.
And even if he gives him collateral, etc. Since the collateral will not remain permanently with the claimant and may be redeemed tomorrow, giving collateral is ordinarily like a mere admission that payment will later be made. Rivash therefore writes that if he authorizes the claimant to sell the collateral immediately and take the admitted amount from its proceeds, it is as though he gave the money at once.
For even a document is considered 'here it is.' The Tur likewise uses this language. Since he says 'for even a document,' it implies that a document plainly has the status of immediate tender. This is derived from Bava Metzia, where a lender holding a document that says the borrower owes 'sela'im' or 'dinars' without number claims five and the borrower admits only two. He is not treated as a partial admitter because the two are already established by the document, which encumbers the borrower's property, so they are treated as 'here it is.' From this one can infer that even where the claim was oral, if the defendant admits fifty and immediately delivers a secured document for that amount, it may have the same status. The Ir Shushan's objection that collection on a document can be difficult does not refute the Gemara's rule. The opposing view that collateral is not 'here it is' can distinguish a document already held by the creditor, upon which he originally relied, from new security now being offered.
