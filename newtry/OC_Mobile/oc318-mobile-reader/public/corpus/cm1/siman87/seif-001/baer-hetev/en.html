With a claim. This excludes cases where the defendant cannot use the ordinary defenses of total denial or partial denial, such as where the lender said, "Repay me only before witnesses," or where an item was deposited before witnesses and is now seen in the defendant's possession before litigation, or where the claim concerns items ordinarily lent or rented and they are seen in his possession. In such cases he is not believed by a shevuat heset merely through total denial. See siman 72. Later simanim 133 through 135 discuss a garment delivered to an artisan, an animal, or a minor slave, where the usual rationales also may not apply. Sema.
Part. The reason for the Torah oath of a partial admitter is that we assume he does not intend outright theft but merely delays payment, intending to pay later. The Torah imposes an oath because he will not swear falsely. One who denies the entire claim is not liable to a Torah oath because a person is presumed not to be so brazen toward his creditor. The Sages nevertheless instituted a shevuat heset out of concern that he may be withholding payment because of an uncertain older claim he believes he may have against the plaintiff, intending to retain the money only until the truth becomes clear. The oath deters this because if he later learns that he had no such claim, his oath would retroactively have been false. See the beginning of siman 75 and siman 92. Sema.
Contradicts him. Why is the case of two witnesses who prove that he owes part of the claim not also counted here, as in siman 75, seif 4? The Shach explains that there the defendant had begun by denying a monetary claim in a manner that established him as a false denier, and the opposing party then swears and collects under the special rule stated there.
The witness. See siman 75, seif 23. The Ran discusses one who claimed one hundred, fifty as a loan and fifty for bodily injury, while the defendant denied everything. Although the injured claimant is entitled by rabbinic enactment to swear and collect the injury amount, before he actually swears that does not yet count as an admitted fifty. Once he swears, however, the defendant is treated as a partial admitter because we then have compelling proof that fifty is owed. The case resembles a claim of one hundred denied entirely while witnesses establish liability for fifty. Sema. The Taz argues that the Ran may therefore disagree with the ruling in seif 5 that a partial admission inferred only from the defendant's own denial does not count as partial admission. In the Ran's case the liability for fifty arises only through rabbinic enactment after the injured party's oath, yet it still counts; all the more so, a Torah-level liability inferred from the rule that "one who says I never borrowed is as though he says I never paid" might count. Since the Mechaber rules in seif 5 that it does not, the Taz says the Ran's rule may not be accepted, which may explain why the Rama omitted it. See the cited Geonic responsum and Responsa Maharam Mintz 93.
In my house. This applies specifically to a loan. With a deposit, if the defendant says, "The item is in my house," that is heilech, because the deposited property remains legally in its owner's domain. The Shach says this follows from the authorities and is the principal law.
Pledge. Because the pledge will not remain with the creditor permanently and may be redeemed today or tomorrow, merely holding it resembles an admission rather than immediate payment. Rivash therefore writes that if the creditor gives the debtor authority to sell the pledge immediately and take from its proceeds the amount the creditor admits, it is as though the money were handed over at once. The Sema abbreviates here. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite Rivash that this is only where he may sell without permission from beit din or any other person. If valuation is still required, it is not heilech.
Heilech. The Shach cites many authorities who agree with the Mechaber that an ordinary pledge is not considered heilech and inclines to rule that way. He adds, however, that where the pledge was taken after the loan, he earlier concluded in siman 72, seif 2 that the creditor acquires it fully even regarding unavoidable accidents. In that case it is heilech. Ritva, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef likewise indicate this. See Responsa Mabit I, 318 and the cited supplement to Beit Yosef.
Document. The Sema writes that if the plaintiff orally claims one hundred, the defendant admits fifty, and immediately gives him a debt document secured by property for the fifty, that too has the law of heilech. The Ir Shushan objects that a document should not count as heilech because collection remains difficult even with a document. The Shach responds that Bava Metzia explicitly treats a document as heilech in the case of "selaim, dinarim." Rashi explains that the debtor's land is encumbered through the document, and other authorities agree. The present case therefore must not depend on land encumbrance, or else the derivation from a pledge would make no sense. Rather, it concerns a debtor without land or a document that cannot collect from purchasers. The same analysis applies under our law, where an oral claim is not distinguished based on whether the debtor currently owns land, as stated in siman 88, seif 28. See also the Bach and the Taz's question.
