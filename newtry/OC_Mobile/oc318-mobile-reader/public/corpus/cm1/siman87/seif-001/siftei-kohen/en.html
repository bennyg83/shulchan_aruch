Likewise, if he, etc., and one witness contradicts him, etc. The reason the Mechaber does not also list a case where two witnesses testify that the defendant has part of the amount in his possession, as above in siman 75 seif 4, is that he began the clause by speaking of an object. With respect to an object, if witnesses established that he had denied possession falsely, he would be deemed one who was established as a liar, and the opposing party would swear and collect, as explained there.
If he says to him, "Here it is," etc. The Sema, seif katan 8, explains that since the defendant immediately gives the claimant what he admits owing, he is considered to deny the entire remainder. This wording is not precise. The Tur says that the admitted amount is ready in his hand before beit din to give to the claimant, which implies that he has not yet handed it over, only that it is ready for immediate delivery. Although the Baal HaTerumot writes that he pays it now before beit din directly from hand to hand, the Tur's position does not appear to require actual delivery. This is also explicit in the Ran in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, who writes that in a loan, so long as the money is in the defendant's hand and ready to be given to the claimant, it is considered "here it is" and he is exempt, because it is as though he had already given it. The Beit Yosef's text reads "as though he had given it in beit din," but this is a scribal error. The original word was "already," and a copyist mistook it for "in beit din." The Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia likewise writes as the Ran. It appears that even the Baal HaTerumot and Semag did not mean literally that the money must already pass from hand to hand. If it is immediately available to be given before beit din, it is as though it were already given.
Ready in his hand before beit din. This requirement applies specifically to a loan. With a deposit, whenever the depositary admits the claimant's ownership, even if the item is not ready in his hand and is standing in a meadow, it is considered "here it is," because wherever the deposited property is located it remains in the legal domain of its owner. So writes the Ran at the beginning of the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, the Baal HaTerumot in gate 7 part 2 in the name of the Baal HaMaor, and the Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en and the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot She'elah in the name of the Ramban and Rashba. The Nimukei Yosef rules this way at the beginning of Bava Metzia, and so too the Ritva in his novellae to the beginning of Bava Metzia. This also appears to be the primary reading of the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia in the discussion of the teaching of Rami bar Chama. See Tosafos there. The same must apply if the deposited object is in the depositary's own house, because the underlying reason is that wherever the deposit is, it is considered in the owner's legal possession. The authorities mention an animal standing in a meadow only because the Talmudic case concerns cows, which ordinarily may be in a meadow, and they teach the stronger point that even there the rule applies. Certainly it applies while the deposit remains in the depositary's premises. This is also demonstrated by the Baal HaTerumot, who records that the Raavad challenged the Baal HaMaor from the baraita, "I delivered to you a house full of produce; what you left remains for you to take," asking why this is not a case of "here it is." The Baal HaMaor answered that the produce had rotted through the depositary's negligence. The Raavad replied that this resolves produce, but what of money? The dispute is discussed at length in Sefer HaRivot. The Rosh and Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia, Tosafos and the Ran in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, and the Semag likewise explain that the produce had rotted through negligence, for otherwise the deposit would count as "here it is." This proves that even a deposit in the depositary's own house has that status. The Tur below, siman 88 seif 24, likewise explains the case as produce that rotted through negligence. Thus Tosafos, Rosh, and Tur also hold that a deposit in the depositary's home is considered "here it is," since wherever a deposit is located it remains in the owner's legal possession. The responsum of the Rashba, siman 1017, cited by the Beit Yosef above in siman 72, also implies this. I later found this stated explicitly by Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 3 part 2: Rashi explains "here it is" as meaning that the item is in my house. Other authorities say that for an ordinary debt the defendant must make immediate delivery before beit din, but with a deposit it is enough to say, "It is in my house," because every deposit remains in the legal possession of its owner. This is the primary view, and the same applies if it is standing in a meadow. See also what I write below at the end of siman 78, seif katan 24.
Even if he gives him collateral, etc. The Sema, seif katan 9, explains that collateral will not remain with the creditor permanently, since today or tomorrow the debtor may redeem it. It is therefore comparable to a mere admission, for today or tomorrow he may likewise pay. The Rivash therefore writes that if the debtor authorizes the creditor to sell the collateral immediately and take from its proceeds the amount admitted, this is as though he gave the money immediately. But the Sema abbreviates the Rivash's qualification. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite the Rivash as saying that perhaps if the debtor authorizes the creditor to sell the collateral and collect from it without needing permission from beit din or anyone else, it counts as "here it is." This clearly implies that if the creditor cannot collect from it without further authorization, then even permission merely to sell the collateral is insufficient. The Ritva, cited in Bedek HaBayit, likewise writes that if the collateral has a fixed value and the debtor gives it immediately as complete payment, and it is known that he has no cash with which to pay, it can count as "here it is." In his novellae to the beginning of Bava Metzia the Ritva writes that if one gives collateral for the admitted portion, it is not "here it is." Even if he wishes to give the collateral as final payment, it is not necessarily accepted as such, because he cannot compel the creditor to accept something other than money. The Ritva adds in the name of his teachers that even if the debtor has no cash and may therefore pay with another item, if the item still requires appraisal it is not "here it is."
And some say, etc., for even a document, etc. The Tur writes this in the name of the Ittur. I examined the Ittur in the laws of debt and in Maamar Kinyan and did not find this proof. In truth, it is no proof at all, as will be explained. In my view the primary ruling is that of the Ri Migash, based on the Ran's proof in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. See the Ran there, who cites the Baal HaIttur and disagrees. See also my comments below in simanim 100, 204, 207, and 241. The Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia rules likewise. The Ritva writes there that giving collateral does not count as "here it is." A promissory note has two independent features: the debtor cannot simply deny what is written in it, and the debt also creates a lien on land. Collateral may protect the claimant from loss, but at the moment of the claim the defendant could still have denied the underlying debt. Whenever the defendant could have denied the claim at that moment and does not actually hand over money, it is not "here it is." The Ritva likewise writes in Kiddushin, regarding the rule "there is no maneh here and no collateral here," that if one who admits part of a claim says, "Here is collateral for the amount I owe," this is not "here it is," because collateral is not itself payment. The Geonim likewise rule this way. The Rivash, responsum 396, cites the later authorities to the same effect and appears to agree. The Baal HaTerumot in gate 7 part 2 concludes in the name of the Ri Migash that collateral is not "here it is," adding that the master's practice was routinely to impose a Torah oath in such cases. The Rambam at the end of chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en and in chapter 4 plainly holds that collateral is not "here it is." The Maggid Mishneh writes that the Ri Migash stated this in the name of the Rif. Even the Raavad's objection to the Rambam appears directed specifically to the Rambam's case of "You owe me a maneh secured by this collateral," meaning collateral taken at the time of the loan. If the debtor first offers collateral only now at the time of the claim, the Raavad would agree that it is not "here it is." The Ritva in Kiddushin, and the Ran and Nimukei Yosef, expressly distinguish between collateral taken at the time of the loan and collateral newly offered at litigation. Nevertheless, above in siman 72 seif 27, seif katan 118, I concluded that even where the loan was originally made against collateral, it is not "here it is," following the Rambam. Although below in siman 88 seif 28 I write that a signed handwritten acknowledgment containing a credibility clause is treated as "here it is" because the debtor cannot deny it, following the Rambam and those who agree with him, that is different. There the debtor cannot deny the money itself that is being claimed, so there is no partial admission as to that component. Here, by contrast, he can still deny the monetary debt itself. Even though the claimant can ultimately collect from the collateral, this is not equivalent to immediate payment. If the collateral is destroyed through unavoidable accident, the claimant can still collect the money on the basis of the debtor's admission, and according to the view that a lender holding collateral is a paid custodian the same applies if it is stolen or lost. Moreover, even when the creditor ultimately collects from the collateral he may need to take a stringent oath. None of this applies to a signed acknowledgment with a credibility clause, where the debtor cannot deny the debt at all and must pay the money. However, collateral seized after the time of the loan stands differently. Above, siman 72 seif 2, seif katan 9, I concluded that such collateral is acquired by the creditor with a complete proprietary acquisition even as against unavoidable loss. The Ritva in Kiddushin, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef are therefore correct that it counts as "here it is." If the creditor later sues for one hundred and the debtor admits part, and the admitted portion had already been secured by collateral taken after the loan through legal process, that collateral is "here it is" according to all opinions. This is the clear rule.
For even a document, etc. The Ir Shushan writes that he does not find it reasonable for a promissory note itself to be called "here it is," since collection can be difficult even with a note. The Sema, seif katan 10, calls this astonishing, because the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia, in the case of "selaim, dinarim," explicitly treats a note as "here it is." In my view there is no difficulty. Rashi explains there that the note is treated as "here it is" because the debtor's land is encumbered by the note. The Nimukei Yosef and other authorities cited by the Beit Yosef below at the end of siman 88 hold that where the defendant admits fifty of a debt recorded in a document and cannot deny that fifty, he is ordinarily liable to a Torah oath on the remainder, and the reason the documented portion is excluded from the partial admission oath is the lien on land. Here, however, the Ittur's comparison of collateral to a note cannot rest on land encumbrance, for then there would be no analogy to collateral. The case must therefore concern one where the debtor has no land, or where the note is not collectible from encumbered property, and the same applies nowadays in contexts where we do not distinguish on that basis, as explained below in siman 88. Thus the Ir Shushan's point is understandable. See also the Bach and what I wrote above in siman 75, seif katan 11.
