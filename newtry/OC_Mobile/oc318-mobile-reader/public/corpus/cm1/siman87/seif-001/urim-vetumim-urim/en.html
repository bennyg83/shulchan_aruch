Where he could have retained possession, etc. This excludes cases such as where the lender said, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," or where movable property is of the type ordinarily lent and there are witnesses who presently see it in the possessor's hands, and many similar cases in which possession does not support the plea.
If he is one who admits part, etc. Suppose the claimant demands one hundred, fifty as a loan and fifty as damages for injury, and the defendant denies everything. Although the injured party's law is that he swears and collects, the defendant is not yet considered to have partially admitted under Rabbi Chiya's first rule, because the injury portion still lacks the claimant's oath. See Tumim, where I wrote that even if the defendant admits the injury itself he may not become a partial admitter, because he had no effective ability to deny that portion. But if witnesses testify that the claimant already took the required oath and the defendant still denies, Rabbi Chiya's first rule applies. Ran and Sema. See Tumim, where I also discussed the issue that the claim may be one secured by land.
One who admits part, etc. The Torah imposed an oath because a person ordinarily will not brazenly deny an entire true claim to the claimant's face. He therefore admits part, while concerning the remainder he may merely be delaying payment. See what I wrote in the booklet on migo and below in siman 92 at length.
Whether concerning a deposit. This excludes the interpretation attributed to Rashi that one who wholly denies a deposit may in certain circumstances incur a Torah oath. See what I wrote in the rules of migo concerning Rashi's intent.
And the other says, "It never happened," etc. The rule applies not only where he says, "I paid," which involves an acknowledged monetary relationship, but even where he denies the entire underlying event. The Gemara records two versions, and most authorities rule according to the version extending the rule to complete denial.
Here you are, etc. Meaning that the admitted amount is ready in his hands to be delivered to the lender in beit din. Even if he has not physically handed it over yet, once it is presently ready for immediate payment in beit din it is treated as though already paid. Shach, correctly.
Ready in his hands. This requirement applies to a loan. With a deposit, we rule like the authorities that the intact deposited object itself is always treated as "here you are" even if it remains in the custodian's home, and certainly if it is standing in an accessible field. But if the deposit depreciated in a way that creates a monetary obligation upon the custodian, it is no longer treated as immediate tender. Shach, and this is clear.
Even if he gives him collateral. Ordinary collateral is not the same as immediate payment because the debtor may redeem it with money and it still requires valuation. Rivash therefore writes that if he gives collateral and expressly authorizes the creditor to sell it freely without any beit din valuation, then according to all opinions it is treated as "here you are."
And some say that collateral, etc. The comparison is to a debt instrument. See Tumim. I agreed with the Sema that a debt instrument is treated as immediate tender only where the debtor owns land. If he owns no land, the document itself is not meaningful immediate payment, since many debtors possess documents and still fail to pay. The document is called immediate tender only because it presently subjects land to collection. If so, actual collateral in the creditor's hands is certainly stronger. The Shach inclines to the view that collateral is not treated as immediate tender. I discussed the matter at length in Tumim and concluded that it remains doubtful, so we cannot impose a Torah oath because of the doubt.
