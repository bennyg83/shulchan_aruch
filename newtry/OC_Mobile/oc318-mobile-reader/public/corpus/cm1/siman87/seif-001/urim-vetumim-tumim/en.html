If he is a partial admitter, etc. The Ran writes: if one claims one hundred, fifty as a loan and fifty as damages caused by the defendant, and the defendant denies everything, then although the claimant can collect the damages upon taking the special injured party oath, this is not yet treated as an admission to part of the claim because the claimant has not yet sworn. That is, the rule of Rabbi Chiya's later derivation does not apply. But if the claimant already took the oath establishing the fifty in damages and afterward sued for the full amount while the defendant denied everything, the case falls under Rabbi Chiya's first rule, because we are regarded as having witnesses that the defendant owes those fifty. This is the Ran's language, cited by the Sema. There is room for analysis according to the Rambam, who holds that an amount the defendant has no ability to deny is not considered an admission to part. If so, why does the Ran speak specifically of a defendant who denies everything? Even if the defendant admits the damages portion, it should not count as a partial admission because he has no effective ability to deny it once the claimant is entitled to swear and collect. One cannot answer that because the claimant still needs an oath the amount remains deniable. A written note also may require an oath before collection, especially where the creditor admits partial payment, and nevertheless it is treated as an amount the debtor cannot effectively deny. There is no proof from the Ran himself because he may simply reject the Rambam's rule. But according to the Rambam, the distinction must be that the injured party's oath is fundamentally different from the oath required in collection on a note. By Torah law, a creditor holding a valid note collects without an oath. The Sages imposed an oath merely to reassure the debtor. Therefore the debt itself remains one the debtor cannot deny. In the case of an injured party, however, Torah law gives the claimant no special credibility at all. The Sages created a special enactment allowing the injured party to swear and collect. Until that oath is taken, the obligation is not in the category of an amount the defendant is legally unable to deny. There is also a difficulty in the Ran's second case, where the claimant has already sworn. If beit din already adjudicated the matter and required the defendant to pay once the claimant swore, then the resulting obligation may be a land lien, because after judgment everyone agrees that collection can reach encumbered property. See below in siman 119 and siman 419. If so, the amount should not generate the ordinary partial admission oath because claims secured by a land lien are excluded. It is strained to say that the Ran speaks only where the defendant owns no land at all. Perhaps the Ran holds that one may plead payment even against a judicial ruling of this type. The defendant himself cannot make that plea here because he denies ever causing the injury, and one who says "I never borrowed" is treated as saying "I did not repay." Purchasers, however, can say that they are not bound by his denial and can claim that he may have paid. The Ran explicitly writes similarly in connection with Rabbi Chiya's first rule. See what I wrote above in siman 75. According to the view I explained in siman 39, however, that one cannot plead payment against a judicial ruling, the difficulty remains. Perhaps the answer is that a judicial ruling creates publicity sufficient for collection from purchasers only when beit din has entered a definite judgment ordering payment. Here beit din merely ruled that the claimant may swear and then collect. Purchasers who heard such a ruling need not refrain from buying the defendant's land, because perhaps the claimant will never take the oath. The later fact that he did swear may have occurred privately or outside beit din and lacks publicity. Likewise, because the original ruling was conditional, beit din may not publicize it as a fixed monetary judgment. There may therefore be no land lien against purchasers, leaving room for the partial admission oath. This requires study, because if so, an injured party who collects only after an oath may likewise be unable to collect from encumbered property. The matter requires further study.
For even a promissory note is considered heilech. The Eruach Shulchan asks that this itself is difficult, because even with a note collection can be difficult and uncertain. The Sema answers that the Gemara explicitly treats a note as heilech. The Shach responds that the Gemara's case concerns a debtor who owns land, as Rashi explains that all his land is encumbered. Therefore, says the Shach, if our case likewise concerns a debtor with land, how can the Tur derive the law of collateral from a note? In the note case there is a land lien. The Tur must therefore be speaking where the debtor has no land. If so, the Eruach Shulchan's question returns: why should the collateral be considered heilech merely because it is in the creditor's possession, when actual realization may still be difficult? The Shach therefore concludes that collateral is not heilech. The note case proves nothing, because a note may be different either because the debtor cannot deny it or because of the land lien. The Shach's analysis is not correct. The Tur plainly speaks where the debtor does own land. If he owned nothing, in what meaningful sense could the admitted portion be called heilech? Many debtors would then escape entirely with no one from whom collection could be had. The Tur's proof is instead from the initial stage of the Gemara in Bava Metzia 4. The Gemara sought to prove that heilech is exempt from the Torah oath from the case where the lender says the loan was three and the borrower says two, and the borrower is exempt from a Torah oath. At first the Gemara thought the reason was that the admitted amount is heilech. The Gemara later answers that the case is different because the written note supports the borrower, or alternatively because the claim is secured by land. At the initial stage, however, the Gemara had not yet invoked the exemption of land claims from the Torah oath. Tosafot, s.v. "one does not swear," and the Shitah Mekubetzet explicitly explain that according to that initial assumption one could still have thought that a Torah oath applies even to land. Nevertheless, the Gemara considered the admitted portion heilech because the creditor already had a land lien. Rashi says expressly that all the borrower's land was encumbered. Thus even on the assumption that land itself could generate a Torah oath, the fact that the admitted debt was already secured by land made it heilech. The Tur therefore reasons all the more strongly that where the creditor physically holds movable collateral, the admitted portion is heilech. The Ritva argues that collateral should not be heilech because it still requires appraisal and because the debtor can redeem it with cash. But those considerations apply no less, and indeed perhaps more, to an ordinary land lien. Encumbered land also requires valuation and the debtor may satisfy the debt with money. Yet the Gemara's initial assumption still treated the secured amount as heilech. The Tur's proof is therefore sound. The same point follows from the Gemara's question: according to the view that heilech is exempt, why did the Torah need a separate verse excluding land from oaths? One might ask that the verse is needed for a claim based on a note secured by land, where the exemption would otherwise derive from the Torah's exclusion of land. The Gemara's question assumes instead that even if land claims were in principle subject to an oath, a promissory note secured by land would already be exempt as heilech. This again shows that the existence of the land lien itself creates heilech status, and if so movable collateral in the creditor's possession should certainly do so. One could press the matter by saying that a note differs because "a note standing for collection is regarded as already collected," so the creditor is treated as though the land were already in his possession. Although we rule like Beit Hillel that a note standing for collection is not generally considered collected, Tosafot in Sotah 25 explains that where the note is entirely free of doubt everyone may agree that it has that effect. Still, this is strained and is insufficient to reject the Tur's straightforward proof. The Tur's words are clear. The Shach further suggests that a note is heilech because the debtor cannot deny the written obligation. Many great authorities have already asked what inability to deny has to do with heilech. They are distinct concepts. A claim can be heilech even where denial is factually possible, because heilech concerns whether the admitted amount is already effectively in the claimant's hands or secured to him. See Tosafot in the chapter Yesh Nochalin concerning the case where a claimant swears and collects half from unencumbered property. Tosafot asks that the fifty which the debtor admits should be heilech. Tosafot answers that because witnesses testify that the entire debt was paid, the admitted fifty cannot be collected from purchasers, and therefore it is not heilech. There the debtor certainly can deny, and indeed the witnesses support the assertion of full payment. Nevertheless Tosafot initially viewed the admitted fifty as heilech solely because of the possibility of collecting from encumbered property. This demonstrates that the essence of heilech in this context is the existing land lien, not the debtor's inability to deny. See also what I write below in siman 88. Even the Rosh, who says at the beginning of chapter 2 of Ketubot that heilech applies where the debtor cannot deny, means that only in a case of a land lien does inability to deny combine with the secured character of the claim to produce heilech. I discussed this there at length. Accordingly, the Tur's reasoning is clear, correct, and compelled by the Gemara. Nevertheless, the principal authority for the proposition that collateral is not heilech is Ri Migash, and he gives a different reason: "If there is no maneh, there is no collateral." The Ran in Shevuot and Nimukei Yosef in Bava Metzia explain his point. One might object that when collateral is given at the time of the loan, the lender acquires a possessory right through the money advanced. But where the collateral is delivered only later, what act of acquisition transfers a right in it? If the only act is physical transfer, Ri Migash can say that absent a presently effective monetary acquisition the collateral does not become equivalent to payment, and thus it is not heilech. On this rationale the Tur's proof from a promissory note is not decisive, because a note can create a lien on the debtor's property at the time of the loan itself, when the money is advanced. Likewise, if collateral is delivered immediately at the time of the loan, it should indeed be heilech. The problem concerns collateral delivered only afterward. Yet this very point is disputed. The Mechaber rules below at the end of siman 204 that an outstanding loan can serve as valid consideration for a Torah level acquisition, just as cash can, and that one who retracts may incur the mi shepara sanction. If so, even later delivery of collateral can be acquired through the existing loan obligation. Therefore the matter is not decisively resolved. The considerations incline toward treating collateral as heilech, and because there is doubt concerning a Torah oath, we are lenient. There should therefore be no Torah oath. The Shach's conclusion is not decisive, while the Tur's words remain correct. See what I write below in siman 88.
