(Shach, seif katan 1) And with respect to an object, he was established as a false denier. Note: Even in a claim concerning a specific object, the Mechaber's rule can apply. For example, the claimant says, "I lent or deposited this particular object with you." The defendant admits that the item came to him as a loan or deposit, but claims that afterward he purchased it from the owner. He would have been believed by a migo that he could have denied receiving it as a loan or deposit, or by a migo that he returned it. Then witnesses come and testify that the item came into his hands as a loan or deposit and that they have now seen it in his possession. He therefore has no migo and is not believed to claim that he later purchased it. Rabbi Chiyya's first rule applies, for by the witnesses he is obligated to return the object to the claimant. Yet he is not established as a false denier regarding his claim that he later purchased it. His account may in fact be true; we simply do not believe it because he lacks a migo. Even though in such a case the claimant may have to take a shevuat heset that he did not sell the object, this differs from the case of fifty for injury, where the claimant acquires the money only through his oath. Here the oath is not the basis of collection, but merely a shevuat heset contradicting the defendant's claim, like any total denial. According to the Mechaber in siman 133:5, as understood by the Shach there, the claimant first takes the item without an oath, and only afterward can the defendant impose a shevuat heset upon him. The same applies to Rabbi Abba's seized ingot: after returning the ingot because the defendant is one obligated to swear but unable, he may impose a shevuat heset that the ingot did not belong to the seizer. Yet there is still liability for a false oath of testimony when one adjures the single witness concerning the ingot. The shevuat heset does not make the witness merely an indirect cause. The Ran's entire foundation concerning the fifty of an injury, that it is not treated as partially established money, is that the claimant does not receive it except through his oath, because "who says he will swear," as stated in Shevuot 32. It follows that where there is only a shevuat heset, which is not treated as an indirect cause for purposes of the oath of testimony, the present case likewise has the status of the ingot. According to the Shach below, siman 133, seif katan 8, the Mechaber requires return first, and only afterward may the defendant impose a shevuat heset; he cannot hold back the return until the oath is taken. Even according to the Sema and Tur, who imply that return occurs only after the claimant swears, there is still reason to say that the oath does not make the obligation merely indirect. The principle "who says he will swear" makes the claim indirect only where the claimant has no right to collect except by virtue of the oath itself. A shevuat heset, by contrast, is merely a procedural restraint to prevent the claimant from later evading the oath. This is why the Mechaber there holds that against heirs no oath is required, since there is no certain claim and after the item has been returned no shevuat heset can be imposed. Even according to the Rama there, who requires an oath even against heirs and therefore appears to place the oath before return, one can say that this is only where it is not known that the object came into their father's hands as a loan. In the case described here, where witnesses establish that the object originally came into the possessor's hands as a loan or deposit and have now seen it in his hands, while the defendant only claims that he later purchased it, he must return it without any oath. Only after return may he impose a shevuat heset, because through the witnesses the item remains presumptively in the ownership of the lender or depositor wherever it is found, as though it never left his domain. There is therefore a full monetary obligation under Rabbi Chiyya's first rule. Even though the defendant maintains his claim that he purchased the deposit, since the law awards it to the claimant on the basis of the two witnesses who establish the original deposit and present possession, the judicial ruling has the force of witness testimony for purposes of Rabbi Chiyya's first rule. This is clear to one who understands the Shach, siman 75, seif katan 19, where he cites the Gemara, "and the tanna likewise taught it; since he seized, we are witnesses," even though there too the other litigant maintains that what his fellow seized actually belonged to him. Since the law nevertheless maintains possession in the seizer, that legal determination has the force of witnesses for imposing a Torah oath as in a partial admission. So too here, the law keeps the deposit in the depositor's ownership wherever it is found.
