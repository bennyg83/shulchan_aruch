To rule. The Shach writes: Where the defendant is one who is unable to swear and therefore must pay, a supporting witness does not exempt him. I proved this in siman 72, seif 15. See there. He further writes that Terumat HaDeshen, siman 334, ruled that a supporting witness exempts from an oath even in a case of partial admission, a watchman's oath, or where one witness contradicts him. See there, where he discusses the proofs at length. None of his arguments are convincing to me. Rather, it appears that one witness is set against the other and their testimony is removed as though it were absent, leaving the defendant obligated in the Torah oath arising from the watchman's liability or partial admission. See there, where this is proved. See also Responsa Maharit, siman 151.
And he collects. The Shach writes that this is the view of many authorities and is the primary ruling, except in the case of one disqualified from swearing. Otherwise, anyone who has one witness could extract money from a person disqualified from taking an oath. This appears in Responsa Maimoniyot, which brings proof from the Gemara in Shevuot. See there. The Taz challenges the Rama: Responsa Maimoniyot derives this law from the difficulty posed by the case of the piece of metal of Rabbi Abba, where we rule that the person from whom it was seized does not need to swear. From the case of "I know about fifty" it proves that the claimant collects with an oath. Why should the cases differ? It answers that the case of Rabbi Abba's piece of metal is different because there is one witness supporting the person from whom it was seized, and a supporting witness exempts even one who ordinarily swears and collects. According to us, however, who rule in siman 75, seif 13, in the case of "I know about fifty," that the claimant need not swear at all and only an unspecified ban is imposed, the proof of Responsa Maimoniyot falls away. See there.
The supporting witness. The Sema asks: When the claimant has one witness supporting him, the defendant is obligated in a Torah oath and cannot reverse it. He answers that the case is where the claimant agreed to swear and collect. Another answer is that it can occur with slaves, documents, or land, or where one was entitled to collect and reversed a hesset oath onto the other party, saying, "I do not want this enactment," as stated in seif 12, and the person upon whom the oath was reversed has a witness supporting him. He is exempt. The Sema further writes that perhaps, after the oath was reversed upon him, he learned of a witness who supported him, and the defendant cannot say, "Had I known this, I would not have reversed the oath," because since he was unwilling to swear even a hesset oath, all the more so he would not have sworn a Torah oath against the witness. His words imply that once he reversed the oath he can no longer retract. This is incorrect, as stated at the end of siman 22, that so long as the oath has not been taken he may retract. It is difficult to say that this case differs merely because he later produced a supporting witness. Rather, it appears to me that even after bringing the supporting witness he still does not wish to swear and says that his fellow should swear through the reversal of the oath. We then rule that the fellow is exempt without an oath, since this party refuses to swear and the fellow has a supporting witness. This is also clearly implied by the words of Maharam, the source of this ruling. Perhaps the Sema agrees in practice and means only a case where the reversal was made irrevocable, such as through a formal act of acquisition. Even then there is room for analysis, because perhaps he can say that he did not make the acquisition with this circumstance in mind. This requires study. Shach.
To believe him. The Shach writes that this ruling requires study, because the words of the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia explicitly imply that even here a supporting witness exempts him. See there. He records an incident in the holy community of Vilna where one person seized an article belonging to another and claimed that the other owed him a certain sum. He had one supporting witness, and we ruled that he could retain the article without an oath. Reason also supports this. Since we say that one witness with respect to an oath is treated like two witnesses with respect to monetary liability, just as two witnesses impose liability in every circumstance, so too one supporting witness exempts in every circumstance. See the Taz, who explains this ruling and writes that a case came before him in which one person had a supporting witness and was exempted from an oath. Afterwards another single witness came to contradict the first witness. He ruled that the second witness could not undo the first exemption, based on the principle that wherever the Torah believed one witness, and as stated in Even HaEzer siman 17 regarding testimony concerning a woman's husband's death after she was permitted to remarry on the basis of one witness.
