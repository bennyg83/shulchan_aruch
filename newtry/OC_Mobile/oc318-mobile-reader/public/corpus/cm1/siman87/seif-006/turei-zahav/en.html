(See there) “Only a heset oath.” The Beit Yosef writes here the following general rule: a Torah oath, and a Mishnaic oath imposed upon one who swears and collects, cannot be reversed onto the opposing party. A heset oath, however, and a Mishnaic oath imposed upon one who swears and is exempt, such as those who swear upon an uncertain claim, may be reversed, except that the opposing party cannot swear where the claim itself is uncertain. From this it appears to me that in siman 89, seif 5, where a worker and employer dispute the wage that was fixed and the employer swears while holding a sacred object and is exempt, the employer may reverse the oath onto the worker. This is a rabbinic oath imposed upon one who swears and is exempt, and every such oath may be reversed where reversal is possible. Moreover, this benefits the worker, for when worker and employer dispute whether he was paid, the worker swears and collects. The words of Mahari, siman 33, are now clear. He writes that a rabbinic oath other than heset, which requires holding a sacred object, may according to all opinions be reversed, and that the claimant may say, “I do not require you to hold a sacred object,” so that a severe oath will not be reversed upon him. Many have struggled with this, since only a heset oath is ordinarily reversible. According to what I have written, it refers to a worker’s dispute over the agreed wage. By strict law the employer would take only a heset oath; the Sages required him to hold a sacred object merely to appease the worker. This oath therefore remains within the category of heset. The Rama cited Mahari’s ruling regarding an oath on an uncertain claim. I have written what appears correct to me. Bedek HaBayit writes that the expression “let him swear against the person whom he suspects” is surprising, since the claim is uncertain. Possibly it means only that he may impose an unspecified ban, and the language is imprecise.
