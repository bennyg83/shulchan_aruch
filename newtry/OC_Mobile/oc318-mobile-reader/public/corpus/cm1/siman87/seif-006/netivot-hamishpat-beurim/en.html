The first view is primary. See Shach, seif katan 15, who adds that where two groups of witnesses contradict one another we disregard the testimony of both and treat the case as though neither testimony exists. Tumim asks: where a document is involved and two witnesses say it was paid while two say it was not paid, the defendant is exempt from payment because the two against two creates a doubt. In a case of doubt whether an oath is required, the rule is likewise that one is exempt from the oath, for when the authorities dispute whether an oath is required, the defendant is exempt. Why, then, when there are two against two should he be obligated to swear? Ketzot HaChoshen labors over this difficulty, but there is no problem. Monetary liability is different. We know that the Torah obligates payment only where the liability is established with certainty, not where it is doubtful. Although we collect on a document even when we do not know whether it was paid, that is because the document makes the case equivalent to certainty that it was not paid. Therefore, once beit din itself is in doubt concerning the document, whether the claimant or defendant is telling the truth, there is no monetary liability. Two against two is a Torah level doubt, so beit din is uncertain whether the claimant or defendant is correct, and monetary liability cannot be imposed. An oath, however, is different, because the Torah itself imposes an oath precisely in a situation of doubt, where beit din is uncertain whether the claimant or defendant is telling the truth. If there are also two against two, the uncertainty has not become qualitatively stronger. Before the witnesses appeared there was already a doubt whether the claimant or defendant was truthful, and the Torah imposed an oath on that doubt. The presence of two against two concerning the same factual uncertainty does not eliminate the oath. This differs from a dispute among the halachic authorities whether the Torah imposed an oath in this category at all. There the uncertainty is in the law of the oath itself, whether the Torah created an obligation to swear, and therefore the defendant is exempt. Tumim writes something approaching this, but bases the matter on the claimant's own uncertainty and then questions his reasoning in a case where the oath arises from a definite claim. In my view the explanation is as I have written.
He swears and collects. See Shach, seif katan 19, who writes that this is except where the defendant is disqualified from taking an oath, for otherwise anyone who has one witness could extract money from a person disqualified from swearing. At first glance this is difficult. Shach writes in seif katan 30 that an oath generated by a witness is not removed by that same witness. If so, this rule would not apply to one witness against a person disqualified from swearing, and how do we know that where the defendant admits part and one witness supports the claimant, the defendant is not relieved of the oath? It appears, however, that Shach's concern is valid. Otherwise anyone could lend one perutah to a person disqualified from swearing and then sue him for a large sum. The defendant would admit the small amount and become one who admits part, and the claimant could then extract the large sum through one supporting witness without taking an oath. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 5, s.v. shekenegdo, express a similar concern.
And he reversed it onto the opposing party. See Sema, seif katan 18, through his statement that the claimant agreed to swear and collect. At first glance this is difficult, because Shach writes in seif katan 30 that where an oath was generated by a witness and then reversed, a supporting witness does not exempt the person from the oath. Perhaps here the original oath was a Torah oath, which cannot by law be reversed. Rather, where the defendant refuses to swear, the law is that he must pay and the claimant need not swear at all. If the claimant nevertheless voluntarily accepts an oath, we do not treat it as a formally reversed oath, but as a voluntary undertaking to swear where the law imposed no oath upon him. A supporting witness can exempt him from such an oath. This differs from Shach's case in seif katan 30, which concerns a Mishnah oath that may legally be reversed. There the oath arises through the witness, so the same witness whose testimony generated the oath cannot serve as a supporting witness to remove the oath he generated. Still, there is a difficulty. If the witness had already testified in his presence and the claimant then accepted the defendant's proposal, "Swear and collect," why should the supporting witness exempt him? The reversal was undertaken on the understanding that he would swear and collect, and it is contrary to reason to allow beit din to award him collection without the oath. It therefore appears that if the witness had already testified before the reversal, and beit din imposed the oath on the defendant because of that witness, and the defendant said to the claimant, "Swear and collect," and the claimant accepted, the witness does not exempt the claimant, for the reason stated. Sema's second answer must instead build upon his first answer, where the witness came only afterward. Sema had asked that the defendant should be able to say, "I did not reverse the oath on this understanding." His meaning is that even if a kinyan had already been made transferring the oath to the claimant, making it as though the claimant's legal position were to swear and collect, and then one witness appeared, why should we not exempt the claimant from the oath? The difficulty remains that the defendant reversed the oath only because he thought it was a rabbinic oath capable of reversal. Once it becomes known that it was a Torah oath that cannot be reversed, the supposed reversal turns out never to have been legally effective, and the claimant likewise could retract from it. Sema answers that since the defendant was unwilling to accept even a rabbinic oath, there is a clear presumption that had he known it was a Torah oath he certainly would not have accepted that stricter oath and would instead have agreed to pay. Sema's second answer concerns a case where the claimant originally agreed to swear and collect even though, without the later witness, the oath was already a Torah oath, such as where the defendant admitted part. The defendant reversed that Torah oath by agreement and the claimant consented to swear a Torah oath and collect. Since both parties agreed, it is as though the claimant became obligated in that Torah oath. When a supporting witness later appears, he exempts the claimant. We can no longer object that the reversal was discovered to be invalid because the oath was actually a Torah oath, since it was a Torah oath from the outset and both parties knowingly agreed. This is the meaning of Sema's second answer. But if a witness first testifies and beit din thereby obligates the defendant in an oath, and the defendant then tells the claimant, "Swear and collect," and the claimant agrees, the claimant cannot collect without swearing. In that case the witness does not exempt him.
But only by virtue of a migo. See Shach, seif katan 21, who asks from the Rosh. Rosh writes that Rabbeinu Yonah asked from the statement in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in. Tumim answers this well. With respect to a document, we do not rely on migo in the ordinary sense. Rather, because the defendant could have exempted himself with another claim against the document, he is not expected to have been careful to eliminate the document, and the claim "I returned it" is itself a good claim. Sema writes similarly above in siman 46 seif katan 2, and with Hashem's help my intent there accords with his. Shach also asks from Mordechai's rule concerning collateral, that where the holder has a supporting witness he collects without an oath, even though his credibility regarding the collateral itself rests only on a migo. In my view there is no difficulty. The rationale of the authority Shach cites is that when one is believed only by a migo, we examine the alternative claim he could have made. If under that alternative he would have been obligated to swear, then even under his present claim he must swear, because his credibility comes only from a migo of a claim that itself carries an oath. Moreover, if he were exempt from an oath under the present claim, he would be manipulating the law and there would be no genuine migo. With collateral, however, he is believed by a migo that he could have claimed, "I returned it," and under that alternative claim there is no oath. His oath regarding the collateral is imposed only because a migo is not ordinarily sufficient to exempt from an oath, or because he is seeking to collect, as the authorities explain. Therefore, when he has a supporting witness, the witness can exempt him from the oath, since under the alternative claim available to him, namely "I returned it," he likewise would not have been required to swear. When Mordechai writes that he collects without an oath, his intent may be that he collects without the stringent oath required of one who swears and collects, which must be taken before collection. Here, if there is a supporting witness, he may collect without that oath. A heset oath after collection, however, as Shach writes in seif katan 73, may still be required even according to Mordechai, because he is not believed about the money merely by the fact that he took it before witnesses. His credibility rests on the fact that he originally had a migo, and the supporting witness does not necessarily exempt him from that later heset oath.
