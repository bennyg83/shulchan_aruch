The primary rule follows the first opinion. Terumat HaDeshen holds that where one witness supports the defendant against another witness who would otherwise create an oath, the supporting witness can exempt even in partial admission and custodial oath cases. The Shach disagrees. He says the opposing witnesses cancel one another and the original oath obligation remains. He extends this even to two pairs of contradicting witnesses: remove the contradictory testimony and leave the oath that would have existed without them. But if one witness first created the Torah oath and a supporting witness arrived later, the Shach allows the supporting witness to exempt, because the supporting witness is himself biblically effective. This position conflicts with the Shach's ruling in Yoreh Deah concerning a piece of uncertain forbidden fat. There he says that once one witness was believed and transformed the matter into definite prohibition, a later opposing witness no longer has equal force under the principle that wherever the Torah trusts one witness he is like two. Yet here the Shach does not apply that principle once the first witness created the oath. Maharam of Rothenburg's derivation of the supporting witness rule indicates the Shach's Choshen Mishpat position is more correct. Maharam argues by an a fortiori: if one witness can impose an oath on a possessor, a supporting witness should be able to remove it. If the first witness, once accepted, always became like two against a later witness, the supporting witness rule would fail whenever the two came successively. Yet Maharam's derivation is phrased broadly. My brother Rabbi Yehudah offered a distinction. In matters of prohibition, once one witness establishes the object as definitely forbidden, the factual status itself changes. A later witness then comes against an established certainty. In monetary law one witness does not establish the money as belonging to the claimant. He creates only an oath. The supporting witness can therefore be treated as supplying the equivalent of the oath and removing that procedural liability. As to two against two, the Shach's statement that we simply erase both pairs is difficult. In monetary law conflicting pairs create a genuine doubt. We do not treat them as though no testimony ever existed. The Shach himself rules elsewhere that where two say a document was paid and two say it was not, the document cannot simply collect as though all witnesses vanished. Rabbi Yehudah explains that if the defendant already had an independent Torah oath, such as a custodial oath or partial admission oath, the later two against two creates a doubt about the underlying money but does not create a doubt whether the oath was ever imposed. The original oath remains until discharged. A monetary doubt can preserve possession without extinguishing a preexisting oath. This is analogous to the Mishnah concerning a disputed cow or donkey, where despite a genuine monetary doubt sufficient to support possession, a partial admission oath can remain. With one witness against one witness, however, the first witness's only function was to create an oath and the second directly contradicts that evidentiary basis. The resulting doubt is therefore a doubt in the oath itself, and doubt concerning an oath is resolved leniently for the defendant. This supports Terumat HaDeshen in the specific one against one case, though the matter remains delicate because an opposing witness ordinarily nullifies a witness only where they are of equal standing.
And this is the proper ruling. The Shach says that where a defendant is already in the category of one obligated to swear but unable to swear, a supporting witness cannot exempt him, because at that stage the liability is money and one witness cannot remove a monetary obligation. He derives this from the Rosh's discussion of a custodian who entrusted the object to another custodian. In my view this is not the correct distinction. When one witness obligates a defendant who says, "I do not know," we do not ask how one witness can impose money. The witness comes only to impose an oath. The payment follows automatically because the defendant cannot swear. The same should work in reverse. A supporting witness removes the oath, and once there is no oath there is no derivative payment. Maharam's a fortiori supports this: if one witness can bring a possessor into the rule of "unable to swear and therefore pay," all the more should a supporting witness be able to remove that consequence by removing the oath. The supporting witness is effectively the equivalent of the oath itself. The Rosh's custodian case has a separate basis. A custodian who entrusts the object to another and then cannot swear is liable not merely because of the procedural rule of inability to swear. He is also in the substantive position of "I do not know whether I repaid you." By transferring the deposit without permission, he created the uncertainty after liability had already attached. Even authorities who do not apply the general rule of inability to swear can make him liable on that substantive ground. Nimukei Yosef explains similarly that if a borrower gives the borrowed animal to an unauthorized messenger and later does not know how it died, the borrower is in the position of one who does not know whether he discharged an existing obligation. That is direct monetary liability, not merely derivative payment from failure to take an oath. A single supporting witness cannot remove such a direct monetary burden. But where the defendant's only liability is produced by the inability to take an oath, the supporting witness should exempt him. Shitah Mekubetzet expressly rules that even in Rabbi Abba's piece of metal case a supporting witness that the seized object belonged to the defendant can exempt him. All the more so in ordinary cases of a suspected swearer or one who says he does not know. This is the better rule.
He collects without an oath. I was uncertain about the rule that a claim concerning movables can generate oath extension to land. If a supporting witness testifies only concerning the land, can that witness remove the extended oath on the land? A single witness cannot directly impose an oath regarding land. Maharam's a fortiori for a supporting witness says that if one witness can impose an oath upon a possessor, all the more can one remove it. But here the witness could not have imposed a direct land oath in the first place. It is true that one witness can indirectly cause land to enter an oath through oath extension if the witness testifies concerning movables and the land claim is then attached. That does not prove a witness who speaks only about the land has the power to remove an extended oath. Since one witness is not trusted directly concerning land for oath purposes, the matter remains doubtful.
And he reversed it upon the opposing party. The Sema explains that the party first reversed the hesset oath and only afterward discovered that he had a supporting witness. He cannot then say, "Had I known, I would not have reversed the oath." The Shach objects that before the opposing party actually swears, one can generally retract from a reversal. Why should the existence of a supporting witness change the rule of retraction? It appears to me that once a supporting witness exists, the matter is like final judgment. Ramban writes that when a litigant says, "I will pay," the statement can itself be treated like a completed ruling. What remains to be done is only execution. Where a party's position still depends on an oath that may never be taken, the judgment is not final. But once a supporting witness arrives and removes the remaining oath, there is nothing further needed from the party who reversed. The matter is therefore completed. More fundamentally, by reversing the oath the party relied upon the claimant's oath and accepted that oath as determinative. Had the claimant sworn, payment would follow. A supporting witness is treated as the equivalent of that oath. Thus when the witness arrives, it is as though the opposing party had sworn the very oath the first party chose to rely upon. At that point retraction is no longer available.
