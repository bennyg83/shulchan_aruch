Some say that the supporting witness exempts him. See Tumim. I distinguished with respect to a general ban. If the claimant asserts certainty, he may still impose a ban upon the defendant, but if the claimant is uncertain there is no ban because one witness supports the defendant.
And the principal ruling, etc. Terumat HaDeshen discusses a paid custodian where one witness says he was not negligent and another says he was negligent. Terumat HaDeshen holds that we do not simply neutralize one witness against the other and require the custodian to swear. Rather, the supporting witness exempts him from the oath. The Shach disagrees and requires an oath. See Tumim. I wrote that at least with a custodial oath and similar obligations, where the claimant himself cannot assert with certainty that the supporting witness is lying, the witness prevents imposition of the oath. If the supporting witness appeared first, then in every area he prevents a later witness from creating an oath, as the Taz writes, because the first witness is treated like two for purposes of the existing procedural status and the later single witness cannot displace that. If the oath creating witness came first and the supporting witness appeared only afterward, I wrote in Tumim that this is disputed among the authorities and requires practical study.
The same applies where his handwriting, etc. Meaning that even if the claimant says, "I made a mistake in my own writing and the written statement is erroneous," he is not believed, because his writing functions as a supporting witness. If he does not even allege error, the ruling would be obvious and teach nothing.
The same applies to one who swears and collects. See Tumim. I concluded that this exemption by a supporting witness applies specifically to those who by Torah law would collect without an oath and whose oath was added rabbinically. Where by Torah law they could not collect at all even with an oath and the Sages created a new right to collect upon oath, a supporting witness does not remove the oath. See there.
If he has one supporting witness. If the defendant says, "I do not know," in a case where that uncertainty ordinarily makes him liable because one who cannot swear must pay, a supporting witness does not help. Once his own uncertainty has already created a monetary obligation, one witness cannot overturn that monetary liability. Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia. See Tumim for my explanation. If the defendant is a person suspected of false oaths, so the opposing party ordinarily swears and collects, and the suspect has one supporting witness, see Tumim. I concluded that the supporting witness appears to exempt the suspect, particularly where both litigants are themselves suspect.
He collects, etc. If the defendant is suspect and the rule is that the opposing party swears and collects, while that opposing party also has a supporting witness, he nevertheless must swear. Otherwise any person could extract money from a suspect through a single witness. Shach in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot. See Tumim. I wrote that the cited case concerns a situation where the original oath obligation itself arose because of one witness. The defendant was obligated to swear to contradict that witness, but because he is suspect the oath shifts to the opponent. In that circumstance the same witness cannot also exempt the opponent from the shifted oath, for then one witness alone would extract the entire sum. But if the defendant was independently obligated in an oath and a separate witness supports the opponent, there is room to say that the supporting witness exempts the opponent. See there.
And he reversed it, etc. The Sema asks: if there is one adverse witness, the defendant is obligated in a Torah oath to contradict him, and a Torah oath cannot ordinarily be reversed. He answers that the case concerns land or documents, or that the claimant expressly agreed to allow reversal. The Shach writes that the defendant initially did not know of the witness and reversed the oath, after which the claimant produced the witness and the defendant now demands that the claimant swear. The ruling teaches that he cannot force that result: either the defendant himself swears or the claimant collects without an oath. If after the reversal the claimant produces a supporting witness that would ordinarily allow him to collect without oath, can the defendant retract the earlier reversal even in a case where the reversal had otherwise become binding, such as through a kinyan? The Sema holds that he cannot retract. The Shach remains uncertain. In truth it is a substantial doubt and requires study.
But if he is believed only by virtue of a migo. The reason is that had he actually made the alternative plea on which the migo rests, he would have been required to swear. His present plea should not be strengthened beyond the legal force of that hypothetical plea, so he too must swear. The Shach disagrees and cites a practical ruling made in Vilna where a person held another's object claiming a debt, had one witness supporting him, and was exempted from an oath. See Tumim. I distinguished that where the fact supported by the witness is one concerning which the opposing party cannot personally know that he is lying, while the alternative migo plea would be a matter that the opponent would know to be false, the supporting witness does not remove the oath and the possessor must still swear.
