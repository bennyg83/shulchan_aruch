Seif 20. (If he said, "I am prepared to swear.") The prohibition against accepting upon oneself in beit din an oath concerning something untrue, meaning even saying that one is prepared to proceed to such an oath, is not counted separately among the mitzvot. At present I require study whether this prohibition is from the Torah or only rabbinic. Its absence from the formal count is not decisive, because some prohibitions are branches of other mitzvot, as Sefer Charedim explains. Once a person accepted upon himself to take an oath that over several years he had not earned even a small amount, even though everyone regarded it as overwhelmingly evident that he had earned considerably more. At the time it was impossible to save him from this stumbling block because the parties would not compromise at all, and he immediately undertook to go to the oath. I think the proceeding was then conducted without three judges. Perhaps the prohibition discussed here applies specifically before a panel of three, whether its source is Torah or rabbinic. Although a single arbitrator whom the parties accepted can in some respects function as three, the prohibition may have been fixed only for an oath accepted before a formally constituted beit din of Torah law, such as three judges, a fully ordained expert, or an expert acting as agent of the ordained judges, but not where the adjudicative authority rests only on the parties' acceptance of a single person in place of beit din. This requires study. I did everything possible at the time to prevent the person from actually swearing. I warned the beit din attendant and the person who would read the oath formula not to permit him to utter the full formula and not even to read it before him, but merely to test him with one or two words that did not yet constitute an oath. They altered the wording. I then embarrassed him through a severe rebuke and warned him to repent. Later, through a fire, Heaven protect us, he was left without his real property.
