The primary ruling is like the first opinion. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 334, writes that one supporting witness exempts a litigant from an oath even in a case of partial admission or a custodian's oath where another single witness contradicts him. He discusses this at length. His proofs do not appear convincing to me. Rather, one witness should be set against the other and both testimonies disregarded as though neither existed. If the defendant was independently liable to a Torah oath because he was a custodian or because he admitted part, he remains liable to that oath. A proof can be brought from the Tur and Mechaber at the end of simanim 46 and 82, where one witness says that a condition existed and another says there was no condition. We set one against the other and the defendant takes a heset oath. Yet the Tur and Mechaber hold that a supporting witness can ordinarily exempt from an oath. It is strained to say that heset is uniquely different. If anything, the opposite is more plausible, because the Ramban, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef hold that a supporting witness exempts from rabbinic oaths and from a custodian's or partial admission oath in some contexts, as discussed in the Ramban's Sefer HaMilchamot and the Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia. It further appears that when two groups of witnesses contradict one another, we likewise disregard both groups. If without either group the litigant would have been liable to a Torah oath, such as a custodian's oath or a partial admission oath, or to a heset oath, he remains liable to that same oath after the testimonies cancel each other. If, however, a person initially became liable to a Torah oath solely because of one witness, and after beit din ruled that he must swear another witness came and supported him, the supporting witness nevertheless exempts him. The Nimukei Yosef writes that the Ran reached this conclusion. Although I do not find the Ran's reasoning entirely clear where he says that because no act was performed on the basis of the first witness, witnesses who arrived one after the other are treated as though they arrived together, since Rashi in Sotah, Tosafos there, Tosafos at the beginning of Kerisos, and other sources appear to say that whenever they arrive successively the first is treated like two and the later one like a single witness, even before action is taken, nevertheless those sources concern a case where Scripture grants the first witness the force of two but does not similarly strengthen the second. For example, in Sotah the Torah gives the witness who says "she became defiled" the credibility of two, but does not give equal two witness force to one who later says "she did not become defiled." Similar cases underlie the other authorities. See what I wrote in Shach to Yoreh Deah, siman 127, seif katan 13. The Ran follows his own view that a supporting witness does not exempt from a Torah oath. He therefore needed the additional reasoning that no act had yet been performed. Otherwise, once the Torah empowered the first witness like two to impose a Torah oath, the later supporting witness would be one against two. According to our ruling, however, that a supporting witness exempts by an a fortiori inference, namely if the Torah trusted one witness to impose an oath, certainly one witness can be trusted to remove it, as stated by the Rosh, Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia, Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 61, and many other authorities, then the supporting witness is also trusted by Torah law to the same extent as the witness who obligates. The case therefore resembles two against two. Whether they arrived together or successively, one witness is set against the other and both are disregarded. The same applies in reverse. If beit din exempted a litigant from an oath because of a supporting witness and afterward another witness arrived and contradicted that supporting witness, we set one witness against the other and disregard both. If the litigant would have owed a Torah oath or heset without either witness, he must now swear it. We do not say that because beit din already exempted him on the strength of the first witness, the witnesses are treated as successive testimony under the rule in the chapters HaIshah Shenitarmelah and HaIshah Shalom that the later witness is one against two. There the first witness has credibility equivalent to two while the second does not. Here, regarding oaths, the later witness is legally as credible as the first. This is clear in my view. See the responsum of Maharit, siman 151.
And this is the proper ruling. Nevertheless, where the litigant is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and therefore must pay, a supporting witness does not exempt him from payment. I proved this above in siman 72 seif 15, seif katan 59. See there.
The same applies when there is his handwriting, etc. The wording is awkward, and there was no need to state this here. It is obvious that the litigant's own handwriting is stronger than a witness. Even an authority who holds that a supporting witness does not exempt from an oath would agree regarding the litigant's own handwriting, for his handwriting is like one hundred witnesses, as explained above in siman 71 seif 9, seif katan 23, and in many other places.
And some say the same applies, etc. This is found in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 61, and in the responsa of Maharam of Rothenburg and in the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia cited below. Mahariv likewise rules this way in responsum 148. He writes that ordinarily, if there had been no witness to the amount expended, R. David would have had to swear how much he spent and then collect. But in that case one witness had been present when R. David reached the compromise with the duke and knew how much was expended. Therefore R. David did not need to swear, because a supporting witness exempts from an oath, as ruled by Rabbeinu Tam and Maharam at the beginning of Bava Metzia. This is the primary ruling.
Where one swears and collects. This does not apply to one who is disqualified from swearing. Otherwise anyone with a single witness could extract money from a person disqualified from swearing. Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 61, says this explicitly and brings a proof from Shevuos, page 32b.
And he has a supporting witness, etc. The Sema, seif katan 18, asks: If the claimant has one supporting witness, does that not itself impose a Torah oath upon the defendant, in which case the defendant cannot reverse the oath onto the claimant? He answers that the case is where the claimant voluntarily agreed to swear and collect. One may also establish the case in claims involving slaves, documents, or land, where the ordinary Torah oath generated by one witness does not apply. Another possible case is where a person was entitled by rabbinic enactment to collect with an oath, but he waived that enactment and reversed a heset oath onto the opposing party, saying, "I do not want this enactment," as below in seif 12, and then the opposing party has a supporting witness regarding the matter on which the oath was reversed. The Sema further suggests a case where only after the claimant reversed the oath did he discover that he had a supporting witness. According to the Sema, the defendant cannot then say, "Had I known this, I would not have reversed the oath," because one may tell him: If you were unwilling even to take heset, all the more so you would not have taken a Torah oath against a witness. This implies that after reversing an oath he cannot retract and say that he himself will swear. In my view that is not correct, for above at the end of siman 22 we rule that so long as the oath has not actually been taken, one may retract. It is strained to say that the arrival of a supporting witness changes that general right of retraction. Rather, the case here is where even after the supporting witness arrives the party who reversed the oath still does not wish to swear and insists that his opponent swear because the oath had already been transferred to him. We rule that the opponent is exempt without an oath. Once the first litigant insists on leaving the reversed oath upon his opponent, that opponent now has a supporting witness and is exempt. This is explicit in Maharam's responsum cited in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 61: If Reuven became obligated to swear to Shimon and said, "You swear and collect," and Shimon has one witness supporting his claim, Shimon collects without an oath if Reuven does not wish to swear. See there. Perhaps the Sema agrees in practical law and meant a case where the reversal was made irrevocable, such as by a formal kinyan, as discussed at the end of siman 22. He would then be teaching that the party cannot say, "I made the kinyan only on the assumption that you would personally swear, not that you would be exempted by a supporting witness." Even this requires further consideration, because perhaps he can indeed make that argument.
This applies specifically where the defendant asserts a claim, etc. This ruling requires study. The Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia clearly implies that a supporting witness exempts even where the litigant's credibility depends on a migo. Rabbeinu Yonah challenged the rule that a supporting witness exempts from an oath from the statement in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in that one who deposits with another before witnesses need not receive it back before witnesses, while one who deposits by written document must return it before witnesses, meaning in order to be exempt from an oath. Even without witnesses, the depositary would be believed with an oath to say, "I returned it," through a migo that he could have claimed that an unavoidable accident occurred. Rabbeinu Yonah asks why the Gemara requires return "before witnesses," since one supporting witness should suffice to exempt him from the oath. The Rosh answers that the Gemara uses the usual expression "before witnesses" because elsewhere it says that one who lends before witnesses need not be repaid before witnesses. Here too, the phrase means legally effective testimony, and even one witness suffices. Thus the Rosh expressly holds that a supporting witness exempts even where the litigant would otherwise be believed only by virtue of migo. I later saw in the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia that if Shimon holds property of Reuven and has a supporting witness, Shimon may retain it without an oath because the witness supports his exemption. Maharam ruled this in responsa. Necessarily Shimon's independent credibility there depends on a migo, for otherwise he would not need to swear and collect even without the witness. Yet Maharam still holds that the supporting witness exempts him. If so, the Rama's formulation appears inconsistent with Maharam's own rulings in the Mordechai. One could force a reconciliation, but it appears better to say that Maharam's restrictive statement in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim concerns land specifically, because there the migo is being used to extract land from its established possessor. His wording there supports this limitation. If so, Maharam agrees with the Rosh in ordinary monetary cases. A case of this kind later occurred here in the holy community of Vilna. One person seized a utensil from another and claimed that the other owed him a certain amount, and he had one witness supporting him. We ruled that he could collect without an oath. This also follows logically. Since for purposes of an oath one supporting witness is treated like two witnesses are treated for purposes of monetary liability, then just as two witnesses establish the matter regardless of whether the party also has a migo, so too one supporting witness should exempt from an oath regardless of the migo. Examine this carefully.
