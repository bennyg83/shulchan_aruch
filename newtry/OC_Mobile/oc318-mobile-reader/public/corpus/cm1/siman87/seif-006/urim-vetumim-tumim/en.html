And one witness supports him. The Shach writes that the Tur's words at the end of siman 84 show that even when one witness supports the defendant, a general cherem may still be imposed. However, responsum Mahari Weil 92 states explicitly that where one witness supports him there is not even a general cherem. It is difficult to distinguish by saying that where the defendant would otherwise be subject to an actual oath, the supporting witness merely exempts him from that oath but not from cherem, whereas where he would otherwise face only cherem, the witness exempts him even from that. Such a distinction has no rationale. If the testimony of one witness is strong enough to eliminate cherem, it should do so even in a case where an oath would otherwise have been required. And if it is not strong enough to eliminate cherem, why should it do so merely because cherem was the only sanction that existed before the witness testified? The proper distinction appears different. Where the claimant asserts a definite claim, one supporting witness does not remove the basis for a general cherem. The claimant stands and cries out with certainty that he is owed the money, and one witness is not sufficient to erase his definite allegation entirely. But where the claimant himself is uncertain and the defendant is supported by one witness, there is no meaningful claim remaining. The claimant does not know that the defendant owes him anything, while a witness affirmatively supports the defendant's version. What basis for doubt remains that would justify even a general cherem? Therefore in that case he is exempt even from cherem.
There are those who say that it exempts him, etc. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 334, writes concerning the oath of bailees that if one witness testifies that the bailee was negligent and another says he was not negligent, we do not say that the witnesses cancel one another and require the bailee to take the ordinary bailee's oath. Rather, the one witness who supports him exempts him from the oath. The Shach objects from the Tur above in siman 46 and in chapter 2, concerning one witness who says there was a condition in the transaction and another who says there was no condition. There the Tur treats the witnesses as canceling one another and requires a hesset oath. The Shach asks: if a supporting witness is not strong enough to exempt from hesset, how can he exempt from a Torah oath? But the Shach appears to contradict what he himself wrote above in siman 46, seif katan 109. There he explains the Ramah, meaning the Ramah MiPano or the cited authority, as holding that when one witness says there was a condition and one says there was no condition, the defendant must take a Torah oath to contradict the witness against him. The second witness is not a true supporting witness, because all he says is that there was a condition, but his testimony does not necessarily establish that the borrower owes nothing at all to the lender. Therefore, according to that authority, the second witness does not exempt him. The Tur, by contrast, holds that at minimum the two witnesses contradict one another. See there. If so, there is no difficulty against Terumat HaDeshen. In the condition case there may be no affirmative witness establishing complete exemption. The two testimonies simply neutralize one another, leaving room for a hesset oath on the underlying claim. In Terumat HaDeshen's case, however, one witness clearly testifies that the bailee was not negligent at all. According to what I wrote above in siman 46, however, that this is not the plain meaning of the Ramah and the Tur, especially in the parallel case where one witness says the debt was paid and one says it was not paid, there is indeed some support for the Shach's objection. Even so, I am surprised by the Shach, because he himself writes in seif katan 10 in the name of the Rosh and Tur that wherever there is a genuine doubt whether an oath is required, we do not impose the oath. Just as a possessor can say kim li in a monetary dispute, he can invoke a recognized doubt concerning an oath. This is clear. If two witnesses say the bailee was negligent and two say he was not, the testimony is in equilibrium. We cannot extract money, because the possessor can rely on the witnesses who exonerate him. Why should an oath be different? If the exonerating testimony is true, there is no oath at all. The only uncertainty arises because another witness contradicts it. Why impose an oath from doubt when the defendant can say, "I rely on the witness who says I was not negligent"? The Shach goes further and says that even if two witnesses say negligent and two say not negligent, we place the pairs against one another and still require an oath. This follows his own approach that for oath purposes there is no meaningful difference between one witness and two. But this very proposition is difficult. A dispute among authorities is no stronger than two contradictory sets of witnesses. See Tosafot in Perek Chezkas concerning "On what do you rely, on this document?" Yet where leading authorities dispute whether a particular oath exists, we do not impose it from doubt. Why then should we impose an oath where two witnesses say there was no negligence and therefore no oath exists at all? Just as doubtful testimony cannot extract money, it should not create an oath. One might distinguish between uncertainty about the legal rule requiring an oath and uncertainty about the underlying facts. For example, where a note is impaired in the presence of witnesses and there is a legal doubt whether the Sages imposed an oath, the uncertainty does not concern whether the claimant is telling the truth. It concerns only the scope of the rabbinic enactment, and there we say that because of doubt no oath is imposed. Here, by contrast, the uncertainty created by the witnesses is precisely whether the defendant's factual assertion that he was not negligent is true. That is the very sort of factual uncertainty for which an oath was originally imposed. The contradictory witnesses simply reproduce the original uncertainty, so perhaps the oath remains. This distinction is possible, but it is weak. It is especially weak in categories of oath that are not imposed on mere doubt but only in response to a definite claim. What definite basis remains when one witness affirmatively supports the defendant? Beit din may be uncertain which witness is correct, but the claimant himself may likewise have no knowledge that negligence occurred. In Terumat HaDeshen's case the claim is the ordinary bailee's oath, which arises from uncertainty. If the claimant himself does not know whether the exonerating witness is truthful, why should beit din impose an oath from uncertainty? The Rosh repeatedly explains that if the defendant refuses an oath he may ultimately have to pay. The defendant can therefore say that he relies on the witness who supports him. For this reason Terumat HaDeshen's ruling appears persuasive. The Tur's case can still be explained. By Torah law a signed note needs no validation, so the note remains effective unless rabbinic validation is lacking. The testimony of the witness who says there was a condition can defeat the note sufficiently to require validation, but the opposing witness who says there was no condition is stronger in one respect because Torah law initially sustains the written instrument. Thus, although the note may be neutralized for collection, the defendant still cannot necessarily escape a hesset oath. Apart from such a special feature, I do not see a basis to impose an oath when one witness clearly supports the defendant. In any event, even according to the Tur, that is where the claimant asserts with certainty that the witness who says there was a condition is lying. The claimant's definite assertion is itself enough to support a hesset oath. If tomorrow he simply sued, "You owe me a maneh," the defendant could not avoid hesset merely because another witness supports him in some collateral aspect. But where the claimant's own assertion is uncertain, as in Terumat HaDeshen's case of a bailee and alleged negligence, the claimant may not know whether the supporting witness is correct. Why should an oath be imposed from doubt? In this respect an oath should not differ from monetary extraction. And behold, above in siman 46 I wrote the view that if the witness who obligates the defendant to swear appears first, his testimony is treated, for the purpose of creating the oath, like two witnesses. Even if a supporting witness later appears, the later testimony is like the word of one against two. The Ran also appears to take this approach, as cited by Nimukei Yosef. Nimukei Yosef adds that beit din must first have ruled on the basis of the initial witness, though the Shach has already challenged that requirement. See Even HaEzer siman 17, Beit Shmuel seif katan 109. According to that view, the reverse should also be true. If the defendant would otherwise be subject to a Torah oath and a supporting witness arrives first, then even those who disagree with Terumat HaDeshen should agree that a later witness who contradicts the supporting witness is like one witness against two and cannot recreate the oath. The Taz indeed writes this. The Shach disagrees, reasoning that because both the power to obligate an oath and the power to exempt from an oath are Torah based, the two witnesses always stand as the equivalent of two against two for oath purposes, even if one came before the other. I found support for the Shach in the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia. The Rosh proves that a supporting witness exempts from an oath. Otherwise, one could challenge Rabbi Chiya's first derivation by saying: what is unique about a person's own admission and the testimony of one witness? In those situations one witness cannot later exempt him if another witness contradicts the first; similarly, even two witnesses cannot overturn a litigant's own admission because an admission is like one hundred witnesses. By contrast, in Rabbi Chiya's case, where witnesses establish fifty, if an equal pair later contradicts those witnesses there is no testimony and no oath. At first glance, however, if one witness obligates the oath and another later contradicts him, why should we say the first witness cannot be neutralized? Ordinarily it is one against one, leaving no testimony. The only way the Rosh's proof works is if one says that because the first witness already arrived and created the oath, his testimony is treated as two for that purpose and the later witness cannot uproot it. Yet after the Rosh proves that a supporting witness exempts, the same challenge should remain. A person's own admission certainly cannot be overcome even by two witnesses, and perhaps an initial witness who creates an oath likewise cannot be overcome by a later supporting witness. The Rosh nevertheless regards the supporting witness as an answer. This indicates that because the Torah gives one witness power both to obligate and to exempt from an oath, temporal priority does not transform the first into an unassailable pair. For oath purposes the two witnesses are treated symmetrically. These are essentially the Shach's words. If so, the same should apply in reverse when the supporting witness appears first. The later obligating witness should still neutralize him, because both powers are Torah based. This also explains why the Tur in siman 46 may disagree with the earlier authority and follow his father the Rosh, holding that where one says there was a condition and one says there was none, no Torah oath arises merely because one witness came first. See what I wrote there, although I concluded that the Mechaber appears to follow the other view. Even so, I do not fully understand the Shach's principle that because both sides are Torah based we never say that the first witness has the force of two. In Yoreh Deah siman 39, where a slaughterer says an animal was treifah and another witness contradicts him, if the witness declaring treifah came first to beit din he is treated as two and the later witness does not help. Yet one witness is also believed by Torah law in matters of prohibition. Thus the fact that both witnesses operate in a Torah domain does not itself prevent the first from acquiring enhanced standing. It therefore seems better to understand the Rosh differently. The general rule derived by the Rosh is that one witness who supports a litigant exempts from an oath. This rule includes even an oath that arose from another witness. In other words, one witness "stands" with respect to an oath both to create and to remove it. All kinds of oaths are included. This does not necessarily mean that in every setting temporal priority is irrelevant. On the contrary, where the supporting witness arrives first, he may be treated as two and a later contradicting witness may not restore the oath. This appears to be the correct practical ruling. Even apart from this, Terumat HaDeshen holds that where one witness supports the defendant there is no oath even if the witnesses arrive together. I wrote above that his reasoning is persuasive. Certainly, then, where the supporting witness arrived first, there should be no oath according to all views.
The same applies to one who swears and collects. The Taz asks that Maharam derived this rule from the case of the piece of metal in Rabbi Abba's sugya. There the opposing party was not required to swear before collecting because one witness supported him, and thus Maharam inferred that a supporting witness can exempt even a person who otherwise would swear and collect. But Maharam follows his own view that whenever a defendant cannot take the required oath, the opposing party swears and collects. According to our accepted ruling that in such a case the opposing party need not take an oath at all, Rabbi Abba's case provides no proof that a supporting witness can exempt someone who is in a true "swear and collect" category. The Taz's question is strong. However, according to the Rif's approach to the case of two people holding a garment, where each swears and takes half and that oath is counted among the category of those who swear and collect, there is support. Rabbenu Tam explains the Gemara's suggestion to examine from whom the seller took the money in a manner that assumes a supporting witness can exempt from the oath. This shows that such a witness can sometimes exempt even where the litigant is technically swearing in order to collect. For practical halachah, however, a distinction is clear. Where a creditor holding a note or a similar claimant is entitled by Torah law to collect without any oath, and the Sages imposed an oath only as an additional safeguard, one supporting witness certainly exempts him. A Torah level supporting witness should be at least as powerful as an oath that is merely rabbinic, and the fact that the claimant is taking money does not change this, because by Torah law he was entitled to collect without an oath in the first place. But where the claimant's very ability to collect arises only from a rabbinic enactment conditioned on an oath, such as a hired worker or a victim of robbery, Torah law would not permit collection even with an oath. The Sages created the right to collect and required an oath as part of that enactment. In such a case a supporting witness should not substitute for the oath, because the Sages did not enact extraction of money on the basis of one witness. We should not extend their enactment beyond what they actually created. They allowed collection through the specified oath, not through a supporting witness. This distinction is sound and correct. See below in siman 89, where I bring support for it. Although responsum Mahari Weil appears to imply that even in such a case a supporting witness exempts from the oath, the practical ruling should follow what I have written, especially because the claimant seeks to take money from one who is presently in possession and can say kim li that a supporting witness does not suffice in a true rabbinic "swear and collect" enactment.
He collects, etc. The Shach writes in seif katan 15 that where the rule is "because he cannot swear, he must pay," one supporting witness does not exempt the defendant. Once the defendant is unable to take the required oath, the obligation has become a monetary obligation rather than an oath obligation. What can one supporting witness accomplish against an existing duty to pay? These are essentially the words of the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia, where he answers the Ramban's question in Milchamot concerning a bailee who transferred the item to another bailee. The first bailee is not believed through the second bailee's oath. The Ramban asks why the second bailee's testimony should not support and exempt the first. The Rosh answers along the lines just stated. There is also some proof from Shevuot 32b. The Gemara says that everyone agrees in the case of one witness involving a person who is disqualified from taking an oath. It does not give the simpler example of one who says, "I know about fifty and do not know about fifty," who would ordinarily be required to pay because he cannot swear. If a supporting witness could exempt him, that would have been an obvious case. This suggests that once the liability has become monetary, one witness cannot help. Still, this proof is not conclusive, because the case may simply be identical in substance to Rabbi Abba's piece of metal and the Gemara need not list every variant. Nevertheless, the Rosh's rule points strongly in this direction. Yet a difficulty remains. If so, the Rosh's own challenge to Rabbi Chiya's first derivation seems unresolved. The Gemara should still be able to ask: what is unique about a person's own admission and one witness? In those settings a later witness may be unable to negate the existing obligation. For example, if one witness testifies that the defendant owes a maneh and the defendant responds, "I do not know," he must pay because he cannot take the oath. If another witness later contradicts the first, the second is not merely a supporting witness capable of undoing an already fixed monetary liability. Likewise, if the defendant says, "I know about fifty and do not know about fifty," and another witness contradicts his partial admission, the testimony cannot undo the litigant's own admission, because an admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses. By contrast, in Rabbi Chiya's case where two witnesses establish liability for fifty and the defendant says he does not know whether he owes any of the hundred claimed, the result would ordinarily be a "cannot swear, must pay" obligation. But if another pair of witnesses comes and contradicts the first pair concerning the fifty, the first testimony disappears and the entire oath structure falls away. Thus the Rosh's original distinction would still seem available even if a supporting witness cannot undo a monetary obligation. His question therefore remains. This allows a better explanation of what I wrote above in seif katan 5 concerning a supporting witness and temporal priority. I had difficulty there with the idea that if the witness who creates the oath arrives first, the later supporting witness is merely one against two. The present analysis suggests a different route. The Gemara challenges Rabbi Chiya's derivation by saying that one witness is different because on the portion the defendant denies he must swear. The Gemara answers by deriving the relevant feature from gilgul shevuah. Once the derivation is framed through gilgul, the Rosh's challenge should likewise be understood in a gilgul case, not in a simple one witness case standing alone. Suppose one witness testifies that the defendant owes a maneh. The defendant denies it and is therefore obligated to swear. The claimant then rolls into that oath an additional claim of another maneh from an unrelated matter. A second witness later comes and contradicts the first witness concerning the original maneh. Even if the original witness came first and retains sufficient force to sustain the original oath, the second witness is nevertheless a supporting witness with respect to the additional rolled claim, because the first witness never testified about that additional matter at all. If a supporting witness exempts from an oath, the gilgul cannot continue as to that second claim. By contrast, where two witnesses create the initial fifty in Rabbi Chiya's case and another pair later contradicts them, there is no underlying oath left at all. Once the first testimony is canceled, there is no basis for gilgul. Thus the Rosh can prove from the comparison that one supporting witness must have power to exempt from an oath, including from the rolled portion, without requiring us to say that a later supporting witness always uproots an earlier witness on the very matter to which both testified. This also resolves our current difficulty. One cannot frame the counterexample by saying that the defendant answered "I do not know" concerning the very matter established by the first witness, because the Gemara's derivation is not based on that kind of case. The basic comparison already fails there for other reasons. If the defendant's "I do not know" concerns the additional matter brought in by gilgul, then according to the Rosh's discussion in the sugya of a borrowed and rented animal, the rule "because he cannot swear, he must pay" does not apply to a liability arising solely through gilgul. See there. Accordingly there is no fixed monetary liability for the supporting witness to overcome. The entire difficulty disappears. Examine this carefully.
Without an oath. See what I wrote in the preceding seif katan that where the rule is "because he cannot swear, he must pay," one supporting witness does not help. There is, however, room to doubt the law of a person disqualified from taking an oath who has one witness supporting him. Should we say that here too the witness does not help, or does he exempt him? Reason and logic favor exemption. In the ordinary case where the defendant says "I do not know," his inability to swear immediately produces a monetary liability. Once the obligation has become money, what can one witness accomplish? A person disqualified from swearing is different. He cries out that he is prepared to swear, but beit din prevents him because of concern that he may take a false oath. If beit din knew that his oath would be truthful and faithful, he would be allowed to swear. Here there is a witness standing beside him whom the Torah itself trusts for purposes of an oath. Why should we make the disqualified defendant lose when, for this limited purpose, the witness gives us the very assurance that the oath would have supplied? There is an additional distinction. When the defendant answers "I do not know," the monetary obligation may attach immediately. A person disqualified from swearing, however, does not necessarily owe money at once, because the opposing party ordinarily must still swear before collecting. Yet this distinction is unnecessary. The first rationale is sufficient. This is precisely the position of our great teacher the Beit Yosef in siman 28. He rules that if a non Jew owes a Jew an oath, and the non Jew is treated as disqualified from taking the relevant Jewish oath, but has one witness supporting him, the witness exempts him. The Shach in siman 75 expresses surprise at this ruling, as though the Rosh's discussion cited above had escaped the Beit Yosef's attention. In truth the Beit Yosef's ruling is sound and contains no error. And thereby the sugya in Shevuot 32 is clarified. The Gemara says that all agree in the case where the opposing party is disqualified from taking an oath, and all agree in the one witness case of Rabbi Abba. The Gemara explains the first case as one where both litigants are disqualified, so the oath returns to the one originally obligated and, because he cannot swear, he must pay. The second is the well known case of Rabbi Abba's piece of metal. Tosafot leaves a difficulty. The rule that when both parties are disqualified the original defendant must pay because he cannot swear is itself associated with Rabbi Abba's approach. Why then did the Gemara not say simply that both statements follow Rabbi Abba, namely the case of the disqualified opposing party and the case of one witness? Tosafot does not resolve this. According to what I have written, the passage can be explained. Abaye's statement that all agree concerning a disqualified opposing party can be true even according to Rav and Shmuel, who disagree with Rabbi Abba's general rule that one who cannot swear must pay. One example is where the disqualified claimant holds a promissory note and the borrower dies. According to Rav and Shmuel, as the Rosh explains, the claimant may lose because he cannot take the required oath. But if that disqualified claimant has one witness establishing that the deceased borrower still owed the money, or the debt was within its due date so that no oath is required, he can collect. The witness who wrongfully suppressed his testimony would then be liable for causing the loss. Thus the principle can operate even according to Rav and Shmuel. Another example is where both litigants are disqualified and the disqualified claimant holds a note against the other. According to the view that does not say "because he cannot swear, he must pay," the claimant would normally lose. But if the claimant has one supporting witness that the note was not paid, such as a witness before whom the debtor admitted nonpayment, or the debt is within its due date, the claimant can collect. The witness's testimony therefore matters even without Rabbi Abba's general monetary rule. Since our accepted halachah follows Rabbi Abba, the Gemara naturally explains Abaye's statement in the form that fits Rabbi Abba, namely the case of two disqualified litigants where the oath returns to the original defendant and he must pay. But Abaye's broader proposition need not depend exclusively on Rabbi Abba. By contrast, the specific case of Rabbi Abba's piece of metal has no application according to Rav and Shmuel. This is why the Gemara attaches Rabbi Abba explicitly only to the latter case. The passage is thereby well explained. This also resolves the position of the Ramah and Rabbi Yeshayah, who hold that when a disqualified bailee is sued on an uncertain claim, the claimant collects without an oath. The same should apply where the claim is based on one witness. See what I wrote in siman 92 and the Shach there, seif katan 11, which indicates that the same law applies to the bailee's oath. One might otherwise ask why the Gemara framed the agreed case as both parties being disqualified rather than the simpler case of a single disqualified defendant sued on the basis of one witness. According to those authorities, the claimant would not need to swear at all, so the Gemara's question "who says he would swear" would not arise. It would be strained to answer, as I suggest later in siman 92, that the Ramah concedes specifically in a one witness case. According to what I have written, no such forced answer is needed. The Gemara could indeed have used a case involving one disqualified defendant. It chose the case of two disqualified litigants because it wanted an illustration in which either party might be the one seeking the witness's testimony. For example, the claimant may have one witness establishing liability, and because the defendant is disqualified he would ordinarily have to pay. But the defendant may possess another witness supporting him and exempting him. The defendant then needs that witness to testify. The structure fits the Gemara's discussion without multiplying separate cases. This is clear. The same analysis also resolves the difficulty I raised above in siman 75 concerning one answer of Tosafot in Shevuot, namely whether Abaye accepts the rule "because he cannot swear, he must pay." Tosafot in Ketubot indicates that he does. According to our explanation the statements can be harmonized. Accordingly, for practical halachah the possessor can say kim li like the Beit Yosef. We have no decisive proof against him. Moreover, the rule that when a defendant is disqualified the opposing party swears and collects is only a rabbinic enactment, as the Gemara in Bava Metzia says that the Sages did not create one enactment on top of another. It is extremely remote to equate this rabbinic mechanism with the Torah rule of "because he cannot swear, he must pay." Where do we find that the Sages intended their enactment to operate even in the face of one supporting witness whom the Torah trusts for oath purposes? This is especially compelling where both litigants are disqualified. In such a case one supporting witness should certainly be effective. Otherwise the result becomes circular. After the defendant pays, he could turn around and sue the claimant with one witness that the money was paid improperly. That would generate an oath against the claimant, who is also disqualified, causing the oath to return and producing another "cannot swear, must pay" result. The parties would simply reverse positions. The Rashba uses a comparable anti circularity consideration in his novellae to Kiddushin concerning the rule that the same people cannot function both as agents and witnesses in a way that produces a pointless reversal. Therefore, in the case of two disqualified litigants, the supporting witness must certainly have force. Examine this carefully.
An oath. The Shach writes that there is an exception concerning a person disqualified from taking an oath. If the claimant has one supporting witness, that witness does not exempt the claimant from the oath that ordinarily precedes collection, because otherwise anyone could extract money from a disqualified person merely by producing one witness. Maharam says the same in Teshuvot Maimoniyot and brings a proof from the Gemara. The source, however, speaks specifically where the claimant's one witness is what originally created the oath obligation against the disqualified defendant. That witness caused the entire mechanism. Because the defendant is disqualified, the oath is shifted to the claimant, who then swears and collects. If the very same witness were also allowed to support and exempt the claimant from his oath, the claimant would extract money from beginning to end solely on the basis of one witness. No disqualified defendant could protect his property. The Gemara in Shevuot supports this, for in the case of one witness against a disqualified defendant it asks, "Who says the claimant will actually swear?" This implies that an oath is still required even though the claimant is supported by the witness. But where the oath did not arise because of that witness, for example a partial admission or a bailee's oath where the claimant has a definite claim as discussed below in siman 92, why should a separate witness supporting the claimant not exempt him? The witness did not create the oath. The claimant is not extracting money from the disqualified defendant solely through one witness. The oath obligation existed independently. There is therefore no concern that disqualified persons would have no way to preserve their property. This is especially plausible according to one answer of Tosafot, which explains that the shifted oath imposed on the opposing party in the case of a disqualified defendant was itself enacted out of concern for the disqualified person. That rationale has no force where a separate witness independently supports the claimant. More strongly, if one accepts the Shach's later principle that a single witness never performs two functions, both creating the defendant's oath and exempting the opposing claimant, then the Gemara provides no special proof concerning disqualified defendants at all. The same rule would apply to an ordinary defendant whose oath was shifted for some other reason. A witness cannot both create the oath and then serve as the claimant's exemption from the reciprocal oath. In that event the Gemara's case teaches only the general rule against giving one witness two procedural functions. It therefore remains possible that where the claimant's oath arose independently, such as through a partial admission or a bailee obligation, a separate supporting witness exempts the claimant even against a disqualified defendant. This rule requires further study.
But if he is believed only because of a migo, etc. The Shach asks that this seems to contradict the Rosh. The Rosh asks concerning a deposit documented by a written instrument, where the custodian must return the item in the presence of witnesses. Otherwise, even though the custodian could claim that the item was lost through unavoidable accident, he would still need an oath. The Rosh asks why two witnesses are necessary. Let the custodian return it before one witness, and that witness will support him and exempt him from the oath. But if a supporting witness does not help where the defendant's credibility on the underlying assertion exists only by virtue of a migo, the Rosh's question is difficult. The custodian's claim "I returned it" is itself believed only because he has the migo that he could have claimed unavoidable loss. The Shach therefore rules against the Mordechai and holds that a supporting witness can exempt even where the substantive claim depends on migo. At first glance, however, one might have thought the opposite and preferred the Mordechai. What migo does the custodian have? He is precisely trying to avoid an oath. If he were to claim unavoidable loss, he would have no supporting witness for that assertion and would have to swear. He therefore claims return in order to avoid swearing. How can an alternative claim that requires an oath furnish a migo to support the present claim? Indeed, the Gemara explains that a custodian who says "I returned it" ordinarily requires an oath even though he could have claimed unavoidable loss, because he may be choosing the return claim specifically to escape the oath. The same problem should apply when he has one witness supporting return. I initially thought the matter might be explained as follows. When the Sages first ruled that with a written deposit the custodian is nevertheless believed to say "I returned it" because he could have claimed unavoidable loss, they also required him to swear on the return claim. Once that halachic rule became established, the return claim itself gained independent credibility. The existence of the document no longer creates the ordinary presumption "if you paid, why is your note still in the creditor's hand?" In a deposit case, everyone knows that the custodian can establish return through an oath. He therefore has no compelling reason to insist on retrieval of the instrument at the moment of return. Once the presumption based on the document disappears, the custodian's return claim is believed as an ordinary claim, no longer merely by a continuing migo. This would explain Tosafot in Bava Batra 70. Tosafot asks according to Rabbenu Tam, who holds that the bailee's oath requires some admission, how the Gemara can speak of the bailee's oath in the case of a written deposit and conclude that a person who deposited by note must receive it back in the presence of witnesses. Tosafot asks what oath remains. If the only issue were hesset, then two witnesses should not be necessary. One supporting witness would suffice to avoid hesset. According to the Mordechai, if the custodian remained believed only through the migo of unavoidable loss, perhaps two witnesses would indeed be necessary because one witness would not exempt him from the oath attached to the migo based claim. Tosafot's question therefore suggests that once the law recognizes the return claim, one witness can suffice. This could also explain why Rashbam and Rivam infer from the Shevuot passage that without witnesses the custodian is not believed at all even with an oath. The Ramban asks why they read so much into the language. Perhaps the Gemara merely means that if he wants to avoid an oath he should return in the presence of witnesses. But Rashbam and Rivam may have understood "witnesses" literally as two. If one witness would suffice merely to avoid the oath, the Gemara's wording would be imprecise. They therefore conclude that where return itself is disputed, the custodian is not believed merely by swearing. Likewise, Tosafot there asks how one could ever have thought that the custodian is not believed to say "I returned it" by a migo of unavoidable loss, given the principle that when a debtor admits a note was written the lender must still validate it. At first glance this question is difficult. Even if a migo could support the return claim, why would the custodian willingly leave the document in the depositor's hands and expose himself to an oath? By contrast, where the lender must validate the note, the debtor may escape without any oath at all. According to the foregoing, however, Tosafot's question is aimed specifically at the Shevuot case where one supporting witness is available to remove the oath. The practical concern about swearing then disappears, making the migo comparison stronger. Even so, all this is strained. What basis do we have to say that the migo disappears entirely once the Gemara has ruled that return is believed? The general rule remains that where the migo of unavoidable loss is unavailable, for example because the item was in a public setting where that claim is impossible, the custodian is not believed to say "I returned it." That indicates that the migo remains the true source of credibility. There is also the Shach's difficulty from the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia. There, a lender claims "I lent you money" and the defendant denies the loan. The defendant's denial is treated as also denying repayment. One witness testifies that the loan occurred. If the lender has seized property of the defendant, the Mordechai says he may retain it without an oath because the witness supports him. Yet the effectiveness of the seizure depends on a migo that the possessor could have denied the seizure or claimed return. Nevertheless the supporting witness exempts him. This contradiction requires explanation. Therefore, it appears that the Rosh's case should be understood through a combined migo involving both the custodian and the witness. If the witness tells the truth that the custodian returned the item, what oath remains? His testimony directly supports the custodian. If we suspect that the witness is lying because the written instrument remains in the depositor's hands, then both the custodian and the witness could instead have said that the item was lost through unavoidable accident. That alternative would have exempted the custodian with the witness supporting the claim. This is not an impermissible migo involving two people. With respect to the witness himself, we may say: if he intended to lie, he could have chosen the claim of unavoidable loss, which would not be contradicted by the depositor's knowledge. Tosafot in Kiddushin 42 writes that where there is a financial benefit the parties are sufficiently careful to coordinate their statements, and it is not necessary that both derive the benefit personally. It is enough that the defendant's money is at stake. In my novellae to Ketubot, in the sugya of witnesses who say "this is our handwriting," I likewise demonstrated that a litigant and a supporting witness are not always treated as an ordinary two person migo when the same considerations apply. One can still distinguish cases involving an embarrassing or brazen alternative. The principle that parties coordinate in a financial matter applies to the person whose money is at risk and who therefore may be willing to make a bold claim. But a neutral witness, who has no financial interest, may be unwilling to make a brazen statement that the opposing party knows is false. He would prefer a form of testimony whose falsity the opposing party cannot directly know. This distinction resolves the sources. In the Rosh's deposit case, if the witness lies by saying the item was unavoidably lost, the depositor cannot directly know that the witness is lying. That is therefore an easier lie for a neutral witness than saying, "The custodian returned it to you," which the depositor personally knows to be false. The witness thus has a meaningful migo and can support the custodian. Likewise in the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia, the witness says the loan occurred while the defendant denies it. The defendant already regards the witness as lying to his face. The witness could equally testify to the other facts supporting the claimant's possession, such as that the property was seized properly or later returned. Those alternatives involve no additional degree of brazenness from the witness's perspective. Therefore the supporting testimony can exempt the claimant. But in Maharam's case in Perek Chezkas, where a son holds land of his father and says he saw a deed in which his father gave the land to his mother, with one witness supporting him, the son has a migo that he bought the land from the heirs. The neutral witness does not have the same migo. To testify falsely that the son bought from the heirs would be an audacious statement that the heirs themselves know is false. By contrast, to say that he saw an earlier deed from the deceased father to the mother is a matter the heirs may not know. A neutral witness who is lying would prefer the latter type of claim. We therefore cannot attribute the son's purchase migo to the witness, and the supporting witness does not remove the oath in that case. Maharam's ruling is thus correct and does not contradict the Rosh. For practical halachah, the Shach is correct where the supporting witness himself has a viable alternative claim that is no more brazen from his perspective. But where the defendant's migo rests on a claim that would require the neutral witness to make a more audacious assertion directly contradicted by the opposing party's personal knowledge, that migo cannot be attributed to the witness. The precise scope of the distinction still requires study.
