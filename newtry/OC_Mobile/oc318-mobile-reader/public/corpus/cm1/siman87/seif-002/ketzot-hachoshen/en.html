In a matter he cannot deny. Bedek HaBayit questions this ruling. He had already accepted the view that "here it is" means actual delivery before beit din. Saying, "The object is in my house and I will give it to you," is not enough. If so, how can an obligation the defendant simply cannot deny be called "here it is"? Bedek HaBayit therefore suggests a different reason. Partial admission exists only where the defendant's own admission creates the liability. If he admits something he could not legally deny in any event, his words add nothing and are not the Torah's partial admission. This explanation fits the principle that a litigant's admission is trusted by the Torah through the verse, "which he says, this is it." The Torah oath arises where the defendant himself supplies part of the claimant's proof. If, however, he hands over fifty before beit din, the claimant no longer needs his admission at all. That is why true "here it is" is exempt. The Gemara's language that the admitted coins are as though already in the lender's hand reflects exactly this point. Under this approach, however, the sugya at the opening of Bava Metzia becomes difficult. The Gemara proves that "here it is" is exempt from a case where the defendant admits two while a document already establishes those two. If the reason there is simply that his admission is unnecessary because he cannot deny the document, why call it "here it is"? The answer is that both laws share one foundation: the Torah oath of partial admission requires that the defendant's own mouth create the admitted liability. Where the claimant already has the money in hand, or where an independently binding document establishes it, his admission is unnecessary. The Gemara therefore groups them under the same principle. A further consequence follows regarding Rabbi Chiya's oath based on witnesses. If the defendant's own admission would not create an oath because the obligation is already independently certain, testimony of witnesses should not create a greater oath. Rabbi Chiya's derivation is that testimony should not be weaker than admission, not that it should be stronger. This explains why witness testimony obligates where the defendant denied everything and witnesses establish fifty, because there the debt was not otherwise a presently certain obligation from the court's perspective. But where the liability is already fully established and cannot be denied, neither admission nor witness testimony generates a new partial admission oath. This leaves Rambam's ruling concerning two disputed debt instruments difficult, as Kesef Mishneh notes. If one set of witnesses establishes one instrument and another set the second, why should the defendant swear on the balance when the admitted or established amount is already undeniable? The Shach answers by distinguishing admission from witness testimony, but that does not fit Bedek HaBayit's own principle. Kesef Mishneh's difficulty therefore remains substantial.
