In the later generations. The Tur, citing Ramah, writes: From the time of the later sages who came after the Talmud, the practice was changed, etc.
They abolished an oath using the Divine Name. Meaning, even for an oath of the judges.
And the custom became to administer the oath with the formula 'accursed.' The Tur writes that they enacted that a person be placed under an 'accursed' formula before ten, because an imprecation has the force of an oath. The Mechaber omits 'before ten.' It appears that Rashi and the Tur required ten here because they held that other formal oaths also require ten, as Rama cites in seif 17. Since we do not rule that way for ordinary oaths, the Mechaber omits ten here as well. Ramah likewise does not mention ten. In Perishah I wrote that for an oath of the judges administered by an 'accursed' formula, the Divine Name or an appellation should still be mentioned. The reason given is that 'accursed' has the force of an oath, based on the Gemara's proof from Yehoshua's words, 'Cursed is the man before Hashem,' where the Divine Name appears. The Tur likewise writes in Yoreh Deah siman 237 that an 'accursed' formula is equivalent to an oath provided it mentions the Name or an appellation. Even with the Name, however, it is not identical to swearing by the life of Hashem, concerning which Scripture says that Hashem will not cleanse the offender. An imprecation invokes a curse upon oneself before Hashem and is less direct. Hesset, by contrast, does not require a true formal oath at all and can be satisfied by an ordinary ban, so it may be administered without mentioning the Name.
