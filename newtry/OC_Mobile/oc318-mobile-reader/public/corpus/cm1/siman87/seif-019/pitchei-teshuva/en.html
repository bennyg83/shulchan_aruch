It writes that even according to the Shach, who says that nowadays the custom is to administer a hesset oath as an actual oath, this applies only to ordinary people, where the oath serves to instill fear. With a Torah scholar, Heaven forbid that one should threaten him in that manner. The author testifies that throughout his career he never ruled that a Torah scholar who denied an entire claim must take an actual hesset oath, but only required acceptance of a ban. He states that throughout Poland the custom for a rabbi or rosh yeshivah is to treat hesset as a private acceptance of a ban before an agent of beit din, and that the same custom prevailed in the western lands. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat end of siman 45, in a response by the author's uncle Rabbi Sender, likewise states that this custom had become widespread on the basis of Knesset Yechezkel. Knesset Yechezkel further writes that in his case the distinguished Torah scholar could perhaps be exempt even from accepting a ban because one witness supported him and stated with certainty that the scholar did not remain indebted even by a perutah. Although the Tur in siman 84 and the Shach in siman 87 seif katan 14 write that a general ban may still be imposed, that may refer specifically to a case where the defendant admits the original debt and claims payment, so there is a concrete monetary basis to the claim. Where the defendant says that the alleged transaction never occurred, a supporting witness may exempt even from acceptance of a ban. Beit Ephraim also rules this way. Nevertheless, Knesset Yechezkel concludes that a Torah scholar should act so as to be clean before Hashem and before people and privately accept the ban in an honorable manner.
