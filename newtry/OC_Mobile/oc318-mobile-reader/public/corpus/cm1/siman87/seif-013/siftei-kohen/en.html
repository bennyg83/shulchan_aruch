Mahari Weil offers another explanation, namely that Maharik excludes a case of an error in discretionary judgment, where according to the Rosh the judgment would stand. That reading follows the Tur's presentation of the Rosh. I have demonstrated elsewhere, however, that where no irreversible act has yet occurred, all agree that a judgment based on discretionary error may be reconsidered. Consequently, the practical distinction here is only the Rama's stated one. In any event, as explained in the following comment, Maharik's classification of this ruling as an error in an explicit law is itself incorrect.
It is an error in an explicit law, and the ruling is reversed. Maharik so ruled, and the Rama and later authorities followed him. In my view, Maharik and those who followed him themselves erred. Maharik reasoned that all authorities agree that a rabbinic oath of denial does not require holding a sacred object, and therefore a judge who required it committed an error in an explicit law. That premise is false. Maharam and numerous authorities maintain that even a rabbinic oath of denial requires holding a sacred object, and the Gemara strongly supports them. Maharam derives this from the Gemara's question identifying the differences between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath. Since the Gemara does not list holding a sacred object as a distinction, the forms are equal in that respect. The passage concerns the rabbinic oath instituted by Rav Nachman, namely the oath of denial, as Rashi and the Ran explain. Maharam applies the same reasoning elsewhere, and Tosafot, Mordechai, the glosses to Maimonides, Mahari Weil, and other authorities likewise state that rabbinic oaths require holding a sacred object. The contrary interpretation, limiting the Gemara's question to the oaths enumerated in the Mishnah, is exceedingly difficult, because the question follows immediately upon Rav Nachman's enactment and because the laws identified by the Gemara fit the oath of denial. The Rosh and Ran, who exempt the oath of denial from holding an object, answer that the Gemara sought differences in legal consequences rather than in the ceremonial form of the oath, but that answer itself shows that the opposing view is substantial. Additional support appears from the incident of Rava's reed and from the case of the shepherd, where the Gemara compares the Torah oath and Rav Nachman's oath without indicating a difference in form. Thus, although the Geonim and most later decisors rule that the ordinary oath of denial does not require holding a sacred object, and common practice generally follows them, a judge who rules otherwise has not contradicted an explicit and undisputed law. At most he has erred in discretionary judgment. Where local custom requires holding an object, that custom may be followed, especially in a generation lax regarding oaths, and a judge may also impose greater solemnity according to the circumstances and the person. Accordingly, if a judge required an oath of denial with a sacred object and the defendant chose to pay rather than swear, the judgment stands and the payment is not recovered. Indeed, if the claimant has seized property, even in the presence of witnesses and without a migo, he may argue that he relies upon the authorities requiring a sacred object and need not return the property until the defendant swears in that form. The general practice of lighter oaths may reflect only the defendant's possession and the rule that uncertainty concerning an oath is resolved in his favor; once the claimant is in possession, that consideration is reversed. Therefore Maharik's ruling, and the Rama's adoption of it, are not correct.
