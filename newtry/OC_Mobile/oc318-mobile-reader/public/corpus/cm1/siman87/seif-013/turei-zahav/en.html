(Seif 30) “The law favors the person in possession, and he is believed through a migo.” To explain this, we must set out the underlying passages, because the Mordechai appears to contradict himself. In the chapter Chezkas HaBatim, the Mordechai cites Maharam concerning Serach daughter of Asher, who sued her uncle Reuven because he held the estate of his father Jacob. Jacob’s wife had not sworn concerning her ketubah, so the property belonged to Jacob’s heirs. Reuven claimed that Jacob had given all the property to Reuven’s mother. Maharam ruled that if Reuven had held the land for three years, then through the migo that he could have said, “I purchased it from you,” he is also believed to say that his father gave it to her, but he must swear. Although one witness supports him, he is not believed here without an oath. A supporting witness exempts from an oath only where the defendant would be believed from his own statement together with that of the witness, without any migo. Here the defendant is not believed merely because one witness supports him, because land remains in the possession of its original owner and cannot be removed through the defendant’s claim and one witness. His right depends entirely on the migo that he could have claimed purchase; since that claim would require an oath, his present claim also requires an oath. Yet in the first chapter of Bava Metzia the Mordechai cites Maharam as follows: if one witness testifies that Reuven borrowed from Shimon and Reuven denies it, then even though the witness does not know whether Reuven repaid, Reuven’s denial of the loan is equivalent to an admission that he did not repay, and he must swear against the witness. If, however, Shimon holds property of Reuven, Shimon may retain it without an oath because the witness supports him and exempts him from an oath. The latter case must mean that Shimon’s possession was without witnesses, for otherwise his seizure plainly would not help. He is believed only through the migo that he could have denied ever receiving the object, like all Talmudic cases in which a possessor is believed up to the object’s value. Without a migo, how could he acquire another person’s object that he admits is not his? Even a supporting witness cannot enable him to retain another’s money, since such a witness affects only the oath. It follows that his right depends upon the migo that he could have denied receipt, yet the supporting witness still exempts him from an oath. This contradicts the Mordechai in Chezkas HaBatim. It is clear that one must at least distinguish between land and movable property, as indicated by the reason stated there that land remains in its owner’s possession. The reason for the distinction requires explanation, since movable property also involves a migo. Why should one migo differ from another? It appears to me that with land, a person who seeks to retain the land has no right whatsoever except through the migo. When the matter is doubtful, possession does not help, since there is no seizure of land and it remains in the possession of the prior owner. His entire right to retain the land is therefore created by the migo, and he cannot be better than that migo itself. This is the case in Chezkas HaBatim. With movable property, however, possession is effective in a case of doubt. The possessor could simply have said, “I hold nothing of yours,” which would have been sufficient because one need not clarify the details of his defense. He could thus have stated briefly that he holds nothing belonging to the claimant because he retains it for his own debt. His present, longer statement, “I hold your object, but you owe me,” is unnecessary and only creates the need for the migo that he could have denied receiving it. His essential right does not arise from the migo, because he could have pleaded in a manner that required no migo. This is the case in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. By contrast, with land he has no conceivable right except through the migo. We therefore learn that even regarding movable property a supporting witness does not exempt from an oath where the migo is indispensable. An example is one who receives a deposit documented by a note and claims, “I returned it.” The chapter HaMokher Es HaBayis and siman 108 rule that he is believed despite the depositor’s note, because he has a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident. Here the migo is indispensable, just as with land, because the depositor holds a note and the bailee has no defense without the migo. A supporting witness would therefore not exempt him from the oath. This clarifies that the Rama was too brief in seif 6 when he stated that a supporting witness does not exempt from an oath where the defendant relies upon a migo. That rule applies specifically to land, or to movable property where the migo is indispensable, but not to other cases of movable property. We can further explain the Mechaber’s and Tur’s rule here that one who swore to another and was exempt, and afterward the other seized property without witnesses, must swear. The original oath cannot have been imposed because of one witness supporting the claimant. If it had been, then when the oath was later shifted to the claimant by virtue of his seizure, he would not need to swear, since his witness would support and exempt him. The case must therefore concern an oath arising from a partial admission or the like. This entire rule is as clear as the sun in my opinion. The Rama did not discharge his obligation by stating it without qualification, since the Mordechai’s passages plainly contradict one another and require this distinction.
