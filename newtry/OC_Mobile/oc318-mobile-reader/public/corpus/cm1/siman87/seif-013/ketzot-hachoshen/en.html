He erred in an explicit Mishnah. The Shach cites authorities who hold that even hesset is administered with a sacred object in hand. He seeks proof from the shepherd case. The shepherd usually received animals before witnesses, but one day received them without witnesses and later denied the transaction. Witnesses testified that he had eaten two animals. Rabbi Zeira said that under Rabbi Chiya's rule he would swear on the remainder. Abaye objected that he was a robber and therefore disqualified from swearing. Rabbi Zeira answered that the opposing party would swear. The Gemara then asks why, even without Rabbi Chiya, the opposing party should not swear by application of Rav Nachman's hesset enactment, and answers that one rabbinic enactment is not made upon another. The Shach argues that if hesset lacked the sacred object while the shifted Rabbi Chiya oath required one, there would still be a practical difference between the two systems, and the Gemara's question would be weak. I do not recognize this as a conclusive proof. Under the Gemara's initial assumption that one could shift hesset from a suspected defendant to the claimant, the claimant would at least have to take whatever form of oath is required in order to collect. Once witnesses already establish that the shepherd ate two animals, the claimant should not collect the remainder on less than the stronger oath appropriate to his posture. The defendant could say, "Treat me like any ordinary defendant against whom you are claiming additional money." Thus the difference in physical oath form need not create a practical distinction. Perhaps Baal HaIttur's proof is only from the Gemara's phrasing, which treats Rav Nachman's and Rabbi Chiya's mechanisms as parallel. That language could suggest the oath form is identical. But it does not compel the result. Therefore the authorities who say hesset does not require holding a sacred object can still be maintained.
An error in an explicit Mishnah is reversed. The Shach rules that if a judge mistakenly required hesset with a sacred object in hand, and the defendant chose to pay rather than take that oath, the ruling stands once payment was made. Likewise, if the claimant seized property before witnesses, he may say, "I follow the authorities who require a sacred object for hesset and will not return the property until you take that oath." I have already explained above that this is not so. Where hesset is the only oath, beit din does not descend to the defendant's property if he refuses. The claimant therefore has no independent property right upon which to base seizure. In a doubtful case beit din cannot impose a ban, and the claimant must return what he seized. The same applies where the judge mistakenly ruled that the defendant must take hesset with a sacred object and the defendant paid only because he refused that form of oath. Although an error in discretionary judgment may sometimes stand after payment has already left the party's hand, here the mistake directly created the payment. Had the defendant known the court lacked power to compel the stronger form, he would not have surrendered his money. His payment therefore resembles a compromise made under mistake and should be restored.
