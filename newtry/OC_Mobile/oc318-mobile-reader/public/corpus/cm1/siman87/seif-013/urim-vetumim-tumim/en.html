It is an error concerning an explicit Mishnah, etc. The Shach writes at length against Maharik on this point, going so far as to say that Maharik himself erred in calling the contrary ruling an error concerning an explicit Mishnah. The Shach argues that many authorities require holding a sacred object even for hesset. Even if common practice does not follow them, a judge who rules that way cannot be said to have made an error concerning an explicit Mishnah. It is true that the Shach can cite an impressive number of names, but on careful examination one finds essentially only Maharam's view and little else. The Shach's main proof is that Tosafot and the Mordechai infer that a Mishnaic oath requires holding a sacred object. They argue that otherwise the Gemara should have listed that as a distinction between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath. The Shach says that the same proof should extend to hesset, because the Gemara's phrase "rabbinic oath" includes hesset as well. This is the essence of his lengthy argument. There is no proof. When the Gemara asks, "What is the difference between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath?" the term "rabbinic" can encompass both Mishnaic oaths and hesset. The Gemara therefore must answer with a distinction that applies across the relevant rabbinic class it is discussing. If holding a sacred object were required for Mishnaic oaths but not for hesset, the Gemara could not simply answer, "The difference is holding a sacred object," as though that distinction applied uniformly to every rabbinic oath. The Gemara is not a shopkeeper listing every small procedural difference, one for hesset and another for Mishnaic oaths. Tosafot's proof is therefore only that one cannot say all Mishnaic oaths lack the formal object, because then the distinction would apply broadly to the principal rabbinic oath category. This is simple. See Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Shevuot chapter 11, and one will see that this reading is correct. Indeed, Tosafot itself points in the opposite direction. Tosafot discusses whether, when a borrower says to a noteholder "swear to me," the noteholder can respond, "You swear and be exempt." What is the uncertainty? The noteholder could simply say, "I set aside my note and sue you orally. Take hesset." The issue must therefore be whether the very same Mishnaic oath can be shifted, as the Shach himself writes in seif katan 31 when rejecting Mahari. If Tosafot held that hesset also requires holding a sacred object, what practical difference would there be between the borrower swearing because the noteholder shifted the Mishnaic oath and the borrower swearing ordinary hesset? Either both forms require the same object or neither does, depending on how one reads the Shach's authorities. The fact that Tosafot treats reversal as a distinct procedural question suggests that hesset is lighter and does not require holding a sacred object. This also helps explain where the authorities who distinguish hesset from Mishnaic oaths derived their rule, since the Gemara does not state the distinction expressly. The Ran indicates that it rests on the tradition of the Geonim. But there is also an inferential basis. The Gemara says that a rabbinic oath can be shifted, which appears to include at least certain Mishnaic oaths. What practical leniency is created by reversal if the opposing party could in any event be required to take hesset? The meaningful difference is that an oath taken by reversal retains the more formal Mishnaic character and requires holding a sacred object, whereas an ordinary hesset claim does not. Thus the Gemara's law itself supports the distinction. It would be strained to say that the Gemara speaks only about the unusual case where one witness says a note was paid and the same witness then serves as a supporting witness, especially according to Maharam's view or according to those who do not recognize a supporting witness as an exemption. The more natural explanation is precisely that hesset is procedurally lighter. See also what I wrote above resolving Rashi. Rashi too can be understood as holding that hesset does not require holding a sacred object, with no contradiction between his statements. There is another way to reconcile Rashi with his statement in Gittin requiring a sacred object. A claimant who holds a note is entitled by Torah law to collect without any oath. The Sages merely restricted his existing Torah right by requiring an oath before collection. Since the underlying right is already complete, we should not pile additional stringency on top of the rabbinic restriction. It is enough that he swear in the lighter form, without requiring the formal object, unless the Mishnah or established enactment specifically says otherwise. By contrast, where the very underlying right to collect is itself rabbinic, the Sages created both the substantive entitlement and the oath as one package. Without the oath the claimant has no right to the money at all. In such a case there is no reason to minimize the oath. The Sages can require the full formal procedure, including holding a sacred object, because the oath is the condition that creates the claimant's ability to extract money. Thus in Gittin, where Rashi speaks about the basic ketubah whose obligation is rabbinic, one can say that "they said and they said": the Sages created the ketubah and also conditioned collection upon the full oath. But in Ketubot, where the Mishnah speaks of a ketubah of one thousand zuz and therefore necessarily includes a substantial voluntary addition written by the husband, that added obligation has Torah force once undertaken. Rashi can therefore be more lenient regarding the formal manner of the oath imposed merely as a rabbinic safeguard. This is a coherent reading of Rashi. In any event, the great light of the exile can be understood as lenient even in the Mishnaic context, and according to what I have written his rulings need not contradict one another. Who would lightly rule against him? The Shaarei of Rabbi Alfasi likewise indicates that the entire Gemara's question "what is the difference between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath" concerns the Mishnaic oath category rather than hesset. May Hashem forgive the Shach, who surely wrote this passage in his youth, for he speaks far too sharply of the Rif, whether the author there is the Rif himself or another ancient Gaon, saying that his words are confused and that silence would have been better. The Rif's point is that the Gemara's question cannot be directed specifically at Rav Nachman's newly instituted hesset. If it were, the Talmudic formulation would naturally have remained attached to that immediate subject: "What is the difference between this oath and a Torah oath?" Instead it asks in broader language, "What is the difference between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath?" That phrasing indicates a new category under discussion, namely the Mishnaic oaths already taught elsewhere. The Shach objects that the question in fact appears immediately after Rav Nachman's statement. But the Rif is not speaking about physical proximity in the text. He is analyzing conceptual continuity and the natural wording of the question. If the Gemara were still speaking only about Rav Nachman's hesset, it should have said "between this and Torah law," as the Talmud often does when continuing the same matter. By changing to the broader phrase "rabbinic oath," it signals a different category. The Rif's linguistic precision is sound and not strange at all. The Gidulei Terumah also treats the Shaarei's reading as peculiar, but his criticism likewise rests on an impoverished reading of the wording. One need not answer it at length. Consider further that the Shaarei writes regarding those who swear and collect that they can shift the oath, and the defendant cannot prevent them because that was the structure of the rabbinic enactment if the claimant chooses reversal. The Shaarei then says that if the person who should swear and collect is disqualified from taking an oath, we do not create an additional enactment allowing him to shift the oath to the opposing party, because the Sages did not make one enactment upon another. The Gidulei Terumah understands this to mean that the disqualified claimant cannot shift the oath and therefore loses his note, and asks why his position should be weaker merely because he is disqualified if an ordinary claimant can always shift. That is not the Shaarei's meaning. Rather, the disqualified claimant collects without an oath. We do not force the opposing party to swear and thereby become exempt, because that would create a second enactment on top of the original collection enactment. Since the claimant cannot personally perform the prescribed oath, he collects without it, in accordance with the Rif's responsum. There is therefore no difficulty. One problem does remain for me. If we accept the distinction that a Mishnaic oath requires holding a sacred object while hesset does not, how do we understand the question of Tosafot, Ramban, and Rosh in Perek Chezkas concerning Rabbah bar Sharshom? He concealed a mortgage deed so that he would not have to take the heirs' oath and could instead rely on a migo that he had purchased the property. They ask what he gained, because he would still have to take hesset. But perhaps his entire gain was to avoid the heavier oath with a sacred object. He may have been willing to take hesset but unwilling to take the formal Mishnaic oath. That is exactly the type of clever procedural advantage Rav Pappa recognizes here. If one accepted the Bach's suggestion in the rules of migo, siman 115, that where a litigant would still have to take some oath, even a lighter one, we do not use migo merely to exempt him from the heavier oath, this difficulty would disappear. But I already wrote above that the Bach's distinction is strained. It is also difficult to say that Tosafot's entire question follows the Geonic practice that every person who collects must take the stronger oath, and that with land one might have thought no oath was imposed at all, so they cited a proof that even land cases receive hesset. This does not fit the wording of Tosafot, and especially not the Ramban, who raises the same question. The matter therefore still requires study.
