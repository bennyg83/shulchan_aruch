Except. See the Tur, seif 32, who concludes in the name of the Rambam: "It has already become universal practice for a Torah scroll to be held by the synagogue attendant or another person even for a hesset oath, in order to frighten the person swearing." Earlier the Tur writes: "The difference between a hesset oath and a judges' oath is that for a hesset oath the person swearing does not hold a Torah scroll." This proves that for a judges' oath the sacred object must be in the hand of the person swearing himself. It is not enough for the ark to be open with the Torah scroll before him, for that is as though another person were holding it, whereas we require the person swearing to hold the sacred object. If he places his hand upon the Torah scroll while it remains in the ark, however, it appears sufficient, as the Rama writes in seif 15. The Mechaber's statement there that he should hold the Torah scroll in his hand is not literal. He wrote according to the practice of their times, when the Torah scroll was not kept in an ark in the synagogue and they would bring out a Torah scroll specifically for the oath.
And the ruling is reversed. See the Shach, who extensively challenges Maharik and the Rama, from whom this ruling was derived. He cites many authorities who hold that even a hesset oath requires holding a sacred object, and he brings proof from the Gemara. He concludes that in a place where no contrary custom has become established, one should rule that the person taking a hesset oath must hold a sacred object. The Ir Shushan similarly writes that administering a hesset oath without a sacred object is an erroneous ruling that is reversed, but that in his time all the communities in those lands had adopted the practice of administering even hesset oaths while holding a sacred object, with local distinctions between large and small sums. Each place sets its own threshold, according to local custom. He adds that one should not protest this practice, because the generation is lax and treats oaths lightly, and therefore the judges should frighten them greatly. Perhaps that was the custom in his place. Today, however, the practice is not to administer a hesset oath while holding a sacred object, although the judge may impose additional stringency according to the circumstances and the individual. In any event, if a judge actually ruled that a hesset oath must be taken while holding a sacred object and the defendant therefore paid rather than swear, the ruling stands and the payment is not returned. More than this, it appears to me that if the claimant seized property, even in the presence of witnesses and without any migo, although he must ultimately return what he seized, while he remains in possession he can say, "I rely on the authorities who require holding a sacred object for a hesset oath, and I will not return what I seized until this defendant swears while holding one." Although seizure generally does not help where the prevailing practice follows the opposite opinion, that is only where the prevailing law is definitely established. Here, who can say that the usual practice to administer hesset without a sacred object reflects a definitive ruling? Perhaps it is merely because the defendant is ordinarily in possession, and therefore we do not impose the more stringent oath in a case of doubt, as I wrote in seif 3, that where there is doubt whether an oath is required the defendant is exempt. Accordingly, where the claimant is now in possession, we administer the hesset oath with a sacred object. See Responsa Maharashdam, siman 159. Shach.
