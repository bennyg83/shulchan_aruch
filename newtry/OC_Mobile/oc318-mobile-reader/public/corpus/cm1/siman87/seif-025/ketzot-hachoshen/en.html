He says he will give. The Shach writes that if the promised recipient is poor, since the promisor cannot retract, he takes a hesset oath when he denies the promise. At first glance this is difficult according to Maharit, part 1, simanim 39 and 22, who holds that a verbal promise to a poor person is not an actual acquisition like delivery and that until the funds reach the charity collector the promisor may change the specific money, and apparently even redirect the gift from one poor person to another. If so, the original poor person would not have an enforceable monetary claim, because the promisor could say, 'I will give it to another poor person.' The principal law, however, is as I wrote in siman 212: the ability to change applies to the specific coins before they reach the collector, but not to substituting one poor recipient for another. Tumim writes that an oath applies only according to the view that a promise to a poor person creates an acquisition like delivery to an ordinary person, analogous to a declaration to hekdesh. According to the view that the promise operates only as a vow, Tumim compares it to a denial of an oath undertaking, upon which no monetary oath is imposed. He further argues that even according to the acquisition view, this works only with a specified object. If one says, 'I will give you one hundred,' how can that be analogous to delivery? Even with hekdesh, unspecified movable property does not become consecrated. This is incorrect. With hekdesh, one who says, 'I obligate myself to one hundred for the Temple treasury,' binds his property just like one who delivers a promissory note to an ordinary creditor, as explained in the first chapter of Bava Kamma. One who says, 'I undertake an olah,' similarly subjects his property, and the lien is by Torah law, as explained in Kiddushin. The same applies to one who says, 'I undertake one hundred for the poor,' according to the view that a declaration to a poor person is like a declaration to hekdesh and creates acquisition. His property becomes obligated as through a kinyan or formal obligation. Responsa Maimoniyot, Sefer Mishpatim, siman 64, says this explicitly. Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi was asked about Reuven, who promised his son-in-law Shimon two litrin so that Shimon would study with Reuven's grandson, and Reuven later wished to retract because no kinyan had been made. The response was that if Shimon became poor enough to require charity, the mere statement obligates Reuven. With a poor recipient the promise becomes a vow. If Reuven admitted the promise he would have to pay; since he denied it, he must swear hesset that he never made the promise. This implies that even an obligation in an unspecified amount rather than a designated object can create an oath when denied. According to what I wrote in siman 81, that a promise to a poor person may function like a kinyan because of the underlying employment arrangement where there was no jesting, this responsum may not prove the broader point. But in truth, even according to the view that the promise operates only as a vow, a hesset oath can still apply. The reason we do not impose a monetary oath upon one who denies having accepted a Torah oath is the reason explained below in seif katan 28, which does not apply here. Therefore an oath is required. Examine carefully.
For even if he admitted. Knesset HaGedolah raises a doubt about a case where admission would not create a monetary obligation but would subject the person to mi shepara. For example, he sold movable property and received money but no meshichah occurred. If he admitted the sale he would have to accept mi shepara, and could avoid it only by acting improperly. Does this fall under the rule that no oath is imposed where admission itself would not obligate money, or because he cannot escape except through wrongdoing should he have to swear? Knesset HaGedolah leaves it unresolved. The Shach in siman 204 regards it as obvious that no oath is required. In my opinion, because Shulchan Aruch there rules that in mi shepara beit din does not curse him directly by saying, 'He will exact from you,' but says generally, 'He who exacted from those who did not keep their word will exact from one who does not keep his word,' the same formula can be used where he denies the transaction. If his denial is false, the curse naturally applies. This is no more than a general ban. Even according to Rama, who allows a direct formulation in an ordinary mi shepara case, where the alleged sale itself is denied it is enough to use the indirect formula of Shulchan Aruch. This removes the doubt.
With her hire for prohibited relations. See the Shach, who writes that if a man promised something to a married woman as payment for prohibited relations, no oath is imposed because the monetary liability is blocked by the more severe punishment. It appears, however, that if she sues for fifty as such hire and fifty as an ordinary loan, and he admits the fifty of the prohibited hire, he must swear regarding the fifty of the loan. Although beit din cannot force payment of the first fifty because of kim lei bid'rabbah minei, the monetary obligation itself exists fully, as Rashi explains in Bava Metzia 91a. The court lacks power to impose the monetary punishment because a more severe punishment applies, but if she seized we would not remove it. He has therefore admitted a genuine liability. It is analogous to a powerful person whom beit din presently lacks power to compel, but the debt exists. That is sufficient for partial admission. This differs from an ordinary obligation only toward Heaven, which does not count as partial admission because there is no enforceable human liability. In kim lei bid'rabbah minei, the underlying civil obligation remains a full obligation, and this is why seizure is effective, unlike a merely heavenly obligation, where seizure is ineffective, as Maharshal writes in Yam Shel Shlomo and as I cite in siman 28, seif katan 1.
