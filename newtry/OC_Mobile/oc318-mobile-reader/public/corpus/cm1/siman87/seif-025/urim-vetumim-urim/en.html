We do not obligate him to take a hesset oath. All the more so there is no Torah oath based on partial admission or one witness. Likewise, if one demands the partners' oath from him and he says that he was never his partner, while one witness testifies that he was his partner, he does not swear to contradict the witness. Even if he admitted the witness's testimony, he would not thereby become liable for money, but only for an oath.
You said that you would give me one hundred. Shach holds that this applies specifically where the claimant is wealthy. If he is poor, since an admission would obligate the promisor to give it to him as charity, he must swear. See Tumim, where I wrote that this is disputed, and it appears that no oath is required. Rather, he is reminded of the punishment for a vow, particularly where he used language of giving. See there. If he claims, "You said you would sell me the merchandise at such and such a price," it is obvious that there is no oath, contrary to the view of the author of Shiltei Giborim. Knesset HaGedolah is uncertain where one claims, "I purchased objects from you for money," and the defendant denies it. Perhaps there is no oath at all because even if he admitted it he could retract, since money does not effect acquisition by rabbinic law. Or perhaps, since he would have to accept the curse of Mi Shepara upon retracting, he may not accept it, and there is therefore a monetary consequence. I do not understand the basis of the doubt. It is like a handshake oath: since his obligation arises only from the handshake oath, we do not administer another oath to him, but merely remind him of the punishment for violating the handshake oath. So too here, we remind him of the curse of Mi Shepara that applies to one who sold and then retracts, and there is consequently no oath at all.

We do not impose a ban. The same applies where he claims, "You reversed the oath onto me," in circumstances where the other party can no longer retract from the reversal, and one witness supports his claim. If he wishes to swear and the other party denies the reversal, he swears that he has nothing of the claimant's in his possession, but does not swear that he did not reverse the oath. Even if he admitted the reversal, this would not be an admission concerning money, only an admission concerning an oath. This is the view of Baal HaIttur, cited by Shach.
