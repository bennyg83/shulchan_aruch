You said that you would give me one hundred, etc. It appears that when the claimant is poor, since the promisor cannot retract, as explained below in siman 243, seif 2, and in Yoreh Deah, siman 258, seif 12, the promisor must take a hesset oath. I later found that the Mordechai, in the final chapter of Bava Kamma, writes that Maharam ruled that when a poor person claims against a wealthy person, “You vowed to give me such and such,” the wealthy person must swear. Maharshal cites this in the final chapter of Bava Kamma, siman 39. I later found that the Shiltei Gibborim, in the chapter HaMekabel, page 133, writes that a straightforward rule follows from Maharam’s words: one who claims against another, “You agreed to sell me this article and vowed to sell it to me for such and such,” while the owner denies both the valuation and ever agreeing to sell, must swear. He reasons that a wealthy person’s declaration that he will give a gift to a poor person and a declaration that one will sell an article are identical in that, in both cases, he may retract. Since here, regarding a gift, even though he could retract if he admitted it, he must nevertheless swear, the same applies to a sale. In my view, he was imprecise and overlooked the Yerushalmi cited by the Rif and Rosh in the chapter HaZahav, and also by the Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Batra and by all the other authorities, that one cannot retract from a verbal gift to a poor person. The Rif and Rosh further prove this in the chapter concerning an ox that gored four or five oxen from the statement, “By your mouth, this is charity,” showing that speech itself creates liability, because a declaration to the sacred domain is equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person. All the authorities rule accordingly, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 258, and below, siman 243, seif 2. This is also stated in Maharam’s own responsum, Prague edition, siman 726. In contrast, regarding a statement that one will sell, no acquisition is created. A declaration to the sacred domain creates a complete acquisition, equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person. Thus the Gemara says in the first chapter of Kiddushin, at the end of page 28: How is a declaration to the sacred domain equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person? If one says, “This ox is an olah” or “This house is consecrated,” the sacred domain acquires it even at the end of the world, whereas an ordinary person does not acquire until he performs meshichah or chazakah. This shows that a declaration to the sacred domain is a complete acquisition, like meshichah for an ordinary person. But where one merely says that he will sell an article to another in circumstances in which he may retract, it is obvious that he is not made to swear about it. I later found this stated explicitly in Responsa Maharam, Prague edition, siman 996, that one is not made to swear concerning a statement that he would sell. I cited part of it in my responsum below, seif 34. This is clear, contrary to the Shiltei Gibborim.
We do not administer an oath, etc. A responsum states: Reuven produced a document against Shimon recording a kinyan by which Shimon agreed to reverse the oath. The witnesses died, and the signature of one witness was authenticated. Shimon says, “I never agreed.” Shimon must take a hesset oath that Reuven has no claim against him, and he is discharged. There is no oath based upon one witness, because the witness did not testify about money itself, but only about the reversal of the oath, and something that merely causes monetary liability is not treated as money. These are the words of the Baal HaIttur, page 65b. See also Shevuot 32b.

The principal claim concerns a prohibition, etc. The Sema explains that this is so even though the claim also has monetary consequences. An example is where she says, “You betrothed me,” and he says, “I did not betroth you,” or where she says that he had relations with her and fathered this child, who is a mamzer, and that he must support the child, as stated in the rulings of Terumat HaDeshen cited there. But if one says, “You stole from me” or “You robbed me,” the principal claim is ordinarily monetary, as is also evident from those rulings. Nevertheless, the words of Terumat HaDeshen and the Rama are not conclusive, and the law requires study. On the contrary, regarding a prohibition the Gemara says in the first chapter of Kiddushin, 28a, concerning a sotah: we have found the rule regarding a prohibition, but from where do we derive it regarding money? The Gemara derives extension of an oath in monetary law from the law of sotah. Moreover, Rambam writes in chapter 15 of Hilchot Ishut that in all such cases the husband administers a hesset oath to her that she did not miscarry, or that she miscarried a fully formed fetus, because through this claim he would become liable to pay her ketubah. The Tur and Mechaber rule likewise in Even HaEzer, siman 194, seif 15. Rambam also writes in chapter 24 of Hilchot Ishut, and the Semag in positive commandment 48, page 128b, that he administers to her an oath while she holds a sacred object that she did not commit adultery while married to him, and afterward she collects her ketubah. The Hagahot Maimoniyot in chapter 2 of Yevamot likewise rules, as do the Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer, siman 115, seifim 7 and 9. See there. It is difficult to say that those sources discuss a case in which she expressly claims the ketubah, for their language does not imply this. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 15 of Hilchot Ishut also clearly indicates that she is not claiming the ketubah. Furthermore, if she did claim the ketubah, the husband would be believed, as the Tur and Mechaber rule there in siman 154, seif 7. This requires study.
If he vowed to give her something as her hire and denies it, he must swear. The Bach writes that this requires study, for in the chapter HaPoalim, Bava Metzia 91a, Rashi implies that he is not liable under human law if she sues him before us, since the act involves kareit and the more severe punishment exempts him from payment. Since women engaged in promiscuity generally do not immerse and are presumed to be niddot, as the Rosh writes in a responsum cited by the Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 26, their intercourse incurs kareit. The more severe punishment therefore applies, and he is exempt from payment under human law even if he admits. If he denies, he likewise should not be made to swear. The Bach’s argument is imprecise. Rashi there addresses the Torah’s prohibition of the hire of a prostitute even where the man had relations with his mother, which is an offense punishable by death at the hands of beit din. But regarding offenses punishable by kareit, the general conclusion throughout the Gemara rejects the view of Rabbi Nechunya ben HaKanah, as stated in Ketubot 30a, Shevuot 33, and many other places. All the authorities rule accordingly in many contexts, as explained below in siman 350. See also below, siman 380, seif 3, where I challenged the Sema, seif katan 7, on this point.
