You said that you would give me a maneh, etc. The Shach writes that if the claimant is poor and the promisor therefore cannot retract, because the promise constitutes an obligation of tzedakah, the promisor must swear when he denies it. The Mordechai rules this in the name of Maharam, and the Shach cites it against the Shiltei Giborim. I remain very doubtful. The matter depends on the dispute I discussed above in siman 66, seif katan 32, whether a declaration of tzedakah to a poor person is treated like an actual transfer. According to the view that it is like delivery, then if the donor dies his heirs must give it, because the poor person acquired it as completely as an ordinary recipient through meshichah. According to the other view, however, no proprietary acquisition occurred. The donor is personally bound only by the law of a vow, and beit din can compel him to fulfill his vow, but if he dies the heirs are exempt. See what I wrote there at length. The Shach himself concludes later in this siman, seif katan 72, concerning one who obligated himself to give another person money by a handshake oath, that if there is no underlying monetary obligation apart from the handshake oath, the claimant cannot impose an oath upon him. The logic is a dilemma. If the promisor intends to violate the handshake oath, there is no ordinary monetary debt for beit din to adjudicate. One merely reminds him of the severity and punishment of violating the oath. The same should apply here according to the view that a tzedakah declaration creates only a vow. If he violates the vow, there is no independent monetary debt. What role would an additional litigation oath have? Beit din should remind him of his vow and its punishment, no more. The Shach relies on Maharam in the Mordechai, but Maharam follows his own view that a declaration to a poor person is equivalent to complete delivery. That is also why Maharam holds that asmachta does not invalidate such a declaration, as I explained above in siman 66 at length. According to Maharam, the recipient has an acquired monetary right, so an oath can indeed be imposed when the promisor denies it. Even according to Maharam, it appears that this should be limited to a statement concerning a specified object, such as "this maneh I give you" or "this particular object I give you." Only then can the declaration be treated as equivalent to delivery to an ordinary recipient. But if he merely says, "I will give you a maneh," without identifying any particular property, how can that declaration be treated as though the object were physically delivered? Surely all his movable property does not become consecrated. The statement instead creates only a personal duty to fulfill the charitable undertaking. In that case, only the reminder of the vow applies, not an additional oath. Accordingly, the Mordechai and Maharam should be understood as speaking where the donor identified a particular thing and said, "This maneh I give you," and Maharam follows his own view that such a charitable declaration is equivalent to delivery. According to the authorities who disagree and treat the matter only as a vow, no litigation oath should be imposed. This is especially true of hesset, since many Geonim hold that where there is no underlying monetary dispute, Rav Nachman never enacted hesset at all. Mahari Weil regularly ruled this way.

Nevertheless he swears to him. The Taz asks from Kiddushin 28, where the Gemara asks how far gilgul shevuah extends and gives the example that one can say, "Swear to me that you are not my slave." The Gemara challenges this because one who calls another person a slave is placed under nidui. The Taz asks why this differs from a claim such as "you robbed me," where an oath can be imposed. He leaves the matter unresolved. The Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses, gives two answers. First, calling someone a slave damages his status in the Jewish community and therefore is more severe than calling him wicked. Second, with an accusation of theft one can sometimes say that the claimant believes he is owed money from an old loan and has mislabeled the taking as theft. The Chacham Tzvi rejects the Taz's inference from Rabbi Abba's piece of metal. Neither answer is sufficient in light of the ruling of Piskei Mahari, cited by the Shach in seif katan 57, concerning a woman who says that a man impregnated her and thereby fathered a mamzer. Mahari rules that because the essential allegation concerns a prohibited relationship, and only incidentally produces a monetary consequence in the obligation to support the child, no oath is imposed. This implies that if the principal claim were monetary, an oath could exist. Yet an accusation that one fathered a mamzer is a severe family status accusation, no less serious than calling someone a Canaanite slave, and the old loan explanation is irrelevant because the core allegation is one of forbidden relations. It therefore appears that the Gemara's question "how far does gilgul shevuah extend" concerns rolling even a doubtful claim into an existing oath, like the law derived from Sotah. The Gemara is emphasizing the extraordinary breadth of gilgul. Thus its example "swear to me that you are not my slave" is a claim made in doubt, not a definite accusation. The challenge is then straightforward. If the claimant himself is uncertain, how could he even consider publicly raising a suspicion that someone with an established Jewish status is actually a slave? To cast such a stigma on another person based only on doubt is itself a serious offense, for which nidui is appropriate. Therefore such a doubtful claim cannot be used merely as an illustration of gilgul without special basis. The same principle applies to doubtful allegations of theft or forbidden relations. One cannot impose an oath based merely on suspicion, except in a case such as Sotah where there are specific grounds creating a halachically recognized suspicion. Tosafot there, s.v. "it was stated," likewise explains that one cannot roll in the claim "perhaps you stole from me" unless there are grounds for suspicion. If such grounds existed in the slave case, the Gemara's example would prove nothing, because the special factual basis itself could justify the inquiry. This resolves the difficulty.
Because of a prohibited matter we do not impose an oath. The Shach, in seif katan 57, is surprised by this and says that it contradicts the Rambam, Hilchot Ishut chapter 24, and Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer siman 115, where a husband may require his wife to swear that she did not commit adultery while married to him. What prohibition could be greater than adultery, which carries a beit din death penalty? The Shach says it is strained to explain the case as one where she is seeking to collect her ketubah. In truth, what he calls strained is plainly the correct reading. A husband cannot simply compel his wife to swear about adultery in the abstract. The case is where she comes to collect her ketubah and he says, in effect, "Swear that I did not pay you, or that you did not commit adultery while married to me," or raises some other matter that would defeat the ketubah. The Rambam is clearly speaking in that setting. More fundamentally, there is no contradiction at all. The Torah itself expressly establishes an oath in the Sotah context, which is entirely about prohibited relations. Mahari's ruling concerns hesset. His point is that because an accusation centered only on a prohibited matter is uncommon as a monetary litigation claim, Rav Nachman did not include it in the hesset enactment. The underlying rationale of hesset is that a person ordinarily does not sue another unless there is some basis for his monetary claim. That rationale does not fit a purely prohibitory allegation. Mahari says this explicitly and supports it from the Mordechai. A Torah oath, or a Mishnaic oath modeled on Torah law, is different. Those oaths do not depend on Rav Nachman's particular enactment, and there is no general rule excluding them merely because a prohibition is involved. In the ketubah case the woman is swearing a Mishnaic collection oath. The husband's accusation that she committed adultery is one of the factual grounds that would eliminate the ketubah. The oath therefore remains part of the established collection framework, not a new hesset imposed solely on a religious accusation. This also explains why the Gemara does not list an accusation involving a prohibition as a distinction between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath. The Gemara's comparison there is between Torah oaths and the Mishnaic oath category. Mishnaic oaths can likewise encompass prohibited conduct. Mahari's limitation concerns hesset specifically. There is, however, another question. How can the husband require her to swear that she did not commit adultery, if adultery itself is more severe than a false monetary oath? The Gemara often asks: if someone is suspected of one serious transgression, why trust an oath from that person? Here the difficulty seems especially strong. The ordinary answer that a person suspected of monetary wrongdoing is not necessarily suspected of swearing falsely because the severity of the oath deters him is less compelling. Adultery is itself a grave prohibition carrying a death penalty. Likewise, the explanation of Rabbenu Yehudah HeChasid that the oath may frighten a wrongdoer into repentance appears less applicable, because the adultery has already occurred if the suspicion is true. Nor can Abaye's explanation of an old loan or temporary self justification account for adultery. One possible answer is to distinguish between a person who has already been established as wicked by witnesses and a person merely suspected because of the litigant's allegation. Once witnesses establish disqualifying conduct, the person has lost the presumption of fitness and is rabbinically disqualified from an oath. We do not simply assume that he repented. But where there is no proof of wrongdoing and the accusation itself is what creates the concern, we can retain the person's general presumption of fitness. Even if the alleged act happened, perhaps the person has already repented and will not now add a false oath. Accordingly, a mere accusation of adultery does not automatically make the woman legally unfit to swear. This answer, however, raises a difficulty from Bava Metzia, where the Gemara asks how a bailee who may have misappropriated entrusted property can be trusted with the bailee's oath. Tosafot and the Maharsha explain that the sharpest version concerns unauthorized use of the deposit, which resembles theft. Why not answer there too that even if the bailee sinned earlier, he may now have repented and can swear truthfully? The challenge there is stronger because if he is still withholding the property, he has not truly repented, but some formulations of the sugya remain difficult. A better distinction is this. The Gemara's principle concerning one suspected of monetary wrongdoing asks whether love of money itself makes the person willing to violate a monetary prohibition, and therefore whether the same financial desire will drive him to swear falsely for money. If his desire for money is strong enough to make him violate "you shall not steal," perhaps it is also strong enough to make him violate the prohibition against a false oath. The two acts are motivated by the same temptation. But where a woman is suspected of adultery, the motivating temptation is sexual desire, not money. The fact that her desire was strong enough to lead her to violate the prohibition against adultery does not imply that love of money is strong enough to lead her to swear falsely in order to collect a ketubah. A person whose inclination overwhelms him in one domain is not automatically suspected in every unrelated domain. Therefore she can still be required to swear regarding the ketubah. If her adultery has actually been established, and the law treats her as generally disqualified from oaths, that disqualification is itself a rabbinic rule comparable to the disqualification of a robber. But the logical reason is not that the same temptation necessarily extends from one sin to another. This distinction is an important general principle in the laws of suspicion and oaths.
