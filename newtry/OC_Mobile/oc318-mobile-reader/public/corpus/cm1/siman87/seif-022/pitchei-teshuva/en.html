To urge the litigants. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 90, was asked for the source of the common statement that even a truthful oath is a transgression, on account of which people avoid taking even truthful oaths and compromise whenever possible. The Sages also state that a thousand towns belonging to King Yannai were destroyed because of truthful oaths. Where is the principle itself indicated? Chatam Sofer answers that the words of the Sages are of course trustworthy, and refers to Sefer Chasidim simanim 418 and 419, but adds that there is no such teaching without an allusion in Scripture. Temurah 3b does not provide conclusive proof because one could argue that its conclusion removes even a prohibition. He instead finds it explicit in Kohelet 9:2, which contrasts 'one who swears' with 'one who fears an oath.' The straightforward sense is that a person who fears an oath withdraws from swearing even truthfully, both from awe of Hashem's honor and so that he not become accustomed to oaths and eventually, Heaven forbid, violate one. Accordingly, the verse 'and by His Name shall you swear' merely teaches that one does not incur lashes for a truthful oath, contrary to what one might otherwise have thought from Temurah, but a prohibition nevertheless remains. This also explains the Sages' statement that those present at the oath are told, 'Depart, please, from the tents of these wicked men.' How can both litigants automatically be called wicked? If the oath is false, both may indeed be blamed, as Rashi explains, because the claimant was careless in entrusting his money to an unreliable person. But if the oath is truthful, while the claimant may be wicked for imposing an unnecessary oath, what sin did the defendant commit? It must be that the defendant should have compromised and accepted some financial loss rather than swear even truthfully. This also explains why the Sema offers a reason different from Rashi's.
