Whoever, etc. The same applies to one who swears and collects, Sema. Universal Jewish practice is so even with an oath that has been reversed onto the other party. Taz writes that through reversal no ban is required. See Tumim, where I rejected his proof, and Jewish custom has the force of Torah.
In order to make him swear needlessly. See Tumim. The intent of the ban is not that he knows with certainty that the truth is with him, but rather that he does not know explicitly that the other party is speaking truth and is making him swear needlessly. If he is uncertain, he does not violate the ban, even though he pleaded before beit din as a definite claim that the other party owes him. His acceptance of the ban is only that he does not know clearly that the truth is with the other party.
Needlessly. If one swears on an uncertain claim, the claimant likewise accepts the ban that he suspects him and does not know that the truth is with him, Taz. If the defendant is taking an oath and the claimant imposes additional matters upon him by gilgul, and the defendant says that the claimant should also accept a ban concerning the gilgul claims, that he is not imposing them needlessly, the Gaon Maharg writes that this is unnecessary. He supported this from the Gemara, Ketubot 86b: if it is through gilgul, what practical difference is there? Rashi explains that she is already obligated to take an oath to him, so what burden does this additional gilgul place upon her? Moreover, this entire requirement is only an enactment of the Geonim.
And the one administering the oath shall answer Amen. Sema writes that although above, simanim 82 and 71, it is explained that he need not answer Amen, here, since he is ordinarily present at the oath in order that the other party swear according to his understanding, it is proper that he answer Amen. Taz writes that above there is no actual oath, only a ban, and therefore he cannot be compelled to answer Amen. Here, however, since he is making the defendant swear, the defendant may say, "I will not swear until you answer Amen, because perhaps without Amen the matter is lighter in your eyes." I have already written that the practice is to accept the ban directly, saying, "I accept the ban," and no further Amen is necessary.
