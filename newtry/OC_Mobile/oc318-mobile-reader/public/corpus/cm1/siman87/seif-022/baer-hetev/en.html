Who became obligated. The Sema says there is no distinction between one who swears and collects and one who swears and is exempt. In every case the opposing party must first accept a cherem upon himself. The Taz disagrees. In his view, one who swears and collects need not first have the opposing party accept cherem, and likewise a person who reverses a shevuat heset onto his opponent need not first accept cherem himself. The reason for requiring cherem is to discourage frivolous demands made merely to force the other party to pay something in order to avoid a baseless oath. That concern does not apply where the oath was not voluntarily imposed but was reversed as a defensive measure. The Taz nevertheless suggests that even with oaths arising from an uncertain claim, as at the beginning of siman 92, when one swears and is exempt the person imposing the oath may still need to accept a cherem.
Amen. The Sema asks why simanim 71 and 82 say there is no need to answer amen. He answers that those passages address one who wishes to leave the synagogue, where he need not wait merely to answer amen. Here the person imposing the oath is presumably present and wants the oath taken before him according to the authority of Heaven and beit din, so ideally the swearer should answer amen to increase the solemnity. The Taz finds this distinction difficult and says the question does not begin. Here no oath is imposed until the opposing party first accepts the cherem; if he refuses to answer amen, he cannot compel the other party to swear. See also Chacham Tzvi's glosses.
