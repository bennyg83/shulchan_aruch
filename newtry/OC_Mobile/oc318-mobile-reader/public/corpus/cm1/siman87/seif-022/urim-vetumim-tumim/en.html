Anyone who, etc. The Sema writes that the same applies to one who swears and collects. His language implies that even where the oath is shifted to the defendant, the defendant must accept a general cherem concerning a needless oath. I have indeed seen judges rule this way. The Taz, however, proves otherwise from above, siman 82 seif 9. There, if the borrower shifts the oath to the lender and the claimant says, "I will not swear; instead I impose a general cherem," the borrower cannot say, "Either release me from the cherem or swear and collect." The Taz asks: why should the borrower ever be able to demand such a thing? Even if the claimant chose to swear and collect, the borrower would still have to accept the cherem. How then could the borrower's demand affect the claimant's choice? The Taz concludes that no such cherem is required when the oath is shifted. This proof is not decisive. The cherem mentioned here concerning a needless oath does not mean that the litigant must swear that he knows with certainty the other party owes him. Its point is to exclude a person who knows with certainty that the opposing party owes nothing and nevertheless forces him to swear merely for harassment. If the claimant is genuinely uncertain, even though he pleaded certainty in beit din in the ordinary manner of litigants, the cherem does not necessarily condemn him. It is directed at one who knows the truth is entirely with the opposing party and still insists on an oath. The cherem discussed above in siman 82 is different. There the claimant seeks to compel the borrower to state before beit din whether he knows with certainty that he paid or is merely uncertain. If the borrower admits uncertainty, he may already be liable because one who says "I do not know whether I repaid you" must pay, especially where a note exists. Thus the earlier cherem is designed to force a disclosure of the borrower's actual degree of knowledge, whereas the cherem here merely excludes deliberate harassment through a needless oath. The two are not the same. Accordingly, one might have thought in siman 82 that the borrower can condition the shifted oath on the claimant's accepting that more searching form of cherem. The ruling teaches that he cannot. There is therefore no proof against requiring the ordinary cherem described by the Sema in a true swear and collect case.
