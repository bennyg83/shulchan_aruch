(Rama, seif 6) And he has one witness who supports him, he is exempt from the oath. Note: According to Tosafot in Ketubot and Kiddushin 43b, who apply migo even with respect to one witness, it appears at first glance difficult why we do not say here that the supporting witness has a migo. If he were lying in this testimony, he could equally have lied by testifying to some other defense and thereby exempted the defendant from whatever oath he wished to avoid. In the Mordechai, chapter Chezkas HaBatim, ruling 527, cited by the Rama, the defendant sought exemption by claiming that the plaintiff's great-grandfather had given the estate to the defendant's mother. He had one witness supporting this, and the defendant also had a migo that he could have said, "I purchased it from you and possessed it for the required years." That is a migo involving brazenness. Even a brazenness migo can exempt from money. The simple answer is that such a migo is said regarding the litigant, but not regarding a witness. A witness may not be willing to brazenly testify falsely to something his fellow knows to be false; he may prefer to lie about a matter the other person does not know. Therefore this migo existed only for the litigant and not for the witness. This also explains the Ran's ruling that in such a case the defendant must swear and the supporting witness cannot exempt him. The defendant's credibility rests on the migo that he could have claimed, "I bought it from you." Had he made that claim, however, the witness might not have dared testify falsely to it, and the defendant would have been obligated to swear. This also answers the Shach's question in seif katan 21 against the Rosh and Rabbeinu Yonah. There, both the bailee and supporting witness had a migo that the bailee could have claimed unavoidable accident, and the witness could have supported that claim. If the witness is willing to lie by testifying to return, which directly contradicts what the depositor knows, all the more so he could lie that he witnessed an accident, a matter that does not require such brazenness. Although an accident is uncommon and therefore is a weaker claim for a litigant, there is no reason to distrust a witness who says that he saw an accident merely because it is uncommon.
