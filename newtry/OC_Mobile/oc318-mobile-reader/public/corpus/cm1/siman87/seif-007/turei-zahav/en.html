(Seif 15) “A Torah scroll” is not exclusive. The same would seem to apply to sacred books and prayer books, since Divine Names appear in them. This is difficult, however, because the chapter HaDayanim states that a Torah scroll is required, while the Sages were lenient with a Torah scholar and allowed tefillin. If “Torah scroll” is not exclusive, what leniency was granted to a Torah scholar? One cannot answer that tefillin are less sacred than other books. That is certainly incorrect, for tefillin have greater sanctity than prayer books, since the Divine Names written in tefillin were consecrated, unlike those in ordinary Chumashim, and all the more unlike printed books, which bear no comparison to the sanctity of tefillin. Although the Sema writes that other sacred books are preferable to tefillin, this does not appear correct at all in my humble opinion. The glossator adds: I have likewise seen the practice in the cities of Turkey, where litigants are not sworn while holding prayer books, but only with tefillin or a Torah scroll; I followed this practice wherever I sat in judgment. Mahari Weil, the source of this ruling, writes in the name of the Rosh, “a Torah scroll or another sacred object.” It appears that the Rosh meant a Torah scroll for an ordinary person, or another sacred object, namely tefillin, for a Torah scholar. Mahari Weil also cites proof from the Mordechai to Shevuot, but anyone who examines that Mordechai will see that it does not discuss the object used for an oath at all, but the release of an oath. One may defend the custom by noting that later generations became more lenient concerning oaths, as stated below in seif 19, and perhaps they also became lenient in this respect.
