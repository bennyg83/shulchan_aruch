Seif 23, in the gloss: Then he need not swear in the presence of the claimant. Note: See Shach, who raises a difficulty from Even HaEzer siman 96. At first I thought that a claimant who swears and collects is different, and afterward I found that the Urim VeTumim says so. In my opinion there is another possible distinction. Heirs are different. The Sages imposed additional stringencies upon heirs, such as requiring an oath even without the debtor having said, “Swear to me,” and applying the rule that a person does not bequeath an oath to his children. Those rules do not apply to ordinary oath cases. One may therefore say that they also imposed a stricter rule here. At first glance one could have suggested another explanation. I have been asked several times whether, if one litigant does not wish to be present when the other takes the oath, the party taking the oath can compel him to remain present. I answered that he cannot compel him, and I thought there was a proof from Even HaEzer siman 96, which says that the widow swears in the presence of the heirs “if they wish.” This would imply that if the heirs do not wish to attend, she cannot force them. On reflection, however, there is no proof. There the heirs make only an uncertain claim, and perhaps for that reason she cannot compel them. Where the opposing party asserts a definite claim, it may be that the person taking the oath can require him to stand present. According to that approach, the Rama here would teach that where the claimant sued only on the basis of one witness, the person who must swear cannot compel the claimant to stand by the oath unless the claimant wishes to do so. By contrast, when the defendant swears and the claimant wants to be present, the defendant might always be required to swear before him, even where the claim is uncertain. This would reconcile the rulings. Yet the Rama’s wording, “he need not swear in the presence of the claimant,” does not sound like this. We are therefore compelled to the earlier explanations. Examine carefully.
