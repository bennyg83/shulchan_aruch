The Shach explains from Kiddushin that a declaration to sacred property is treated like delivery to an ordinary person: one who says, 'This ox is an olah' or 'This house is hekdesh' effects the acquisition immediately, whereas an ordinary recipient does not acquire until an act of acquisition is made. Dagul Merevavah explains that a declaration to a poor person is stronger than a handshake promise to a wealthy person because the former creates a vow, whereas the latter, though enforceable if true, is not a complete acquisition. Yet in siman 125 the Sema and Shach explain that even a vow to a poor person may be released through regret. This does not prove that the declaration is unlike an acquisition, because even a declaration creating bodily sanctity may be released before the property reaches the treasurer. The Tumim therefore writes that the matter depends on the dispute whether the rule that a declaration to sacred purposes is like delivery to an ordinary person applies also to charity for a poor person. If it does, the promise is treated as an acquisition and an oath is properly imposed. Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat discuss this as well. Teshuvot Mayim Chayim, Yoreh Deah siman 45, similarly explains that according to the Beit Yosef, who limits the rule to actual hekdesh, no oath would be imposed; but since the Sema and Shach rule that it applies to charity, the Shach's ruling here, requiring an oath where the recipient is poor, should be followed. The Tumim, however, distinguishes between a promise of this particular maneh or a specified object, upon which an acquisition can take effect, and a promise of an unspecified maneh. The responsum further discusses a wealthy widow who, during shivah, stated that she would take a Sefer Torah left by her husband and give it to the beit midrash. One witness testified to the statement. She admitted making it but claimed that she had conditioned the gift on continuing to reside in that city, while the witness denied hearing any condition. At first glance she should have to take a Torah oath to contradict the witness, since dedication to a synagogue has the law of charity, and one who denies a charity obligation is at least subject to a heset oath, and when contradicted by one witness ordinarily a Torah oath applies. However, at the time of her statement the Sefer Torah did not yet belong to her. She had only a claim and lien for her ketubah, and the Torah had not been collected for her. Since a creditor acquires property only upon collection, even an explicit dedication of the Torah would not yet have taken effect. At most, her statement created a vow to dedicate it when it came into her possession. Such a vow creates only a religious obligation, analogous to a handshake promise to give a gift. According to the Shach, where there is no underlying monetary obligation but only an oath or vow obligation, even a Torah oath is not imposed to contradict a witness. The widow is therefore not required to swear against the witness; beit din need only warn her concerning the force of her vow. See also Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat on this subject.
