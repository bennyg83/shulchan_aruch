If it appears, etc. This is stated in the later glosses to Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia in the name of Riva and in Hagahot Ashri. Rabbeinu Tam generally ruled according to the latter version of a talmudic discussion, and Riva maintained that in rabbinic matters one follows the lenient view. Since this is also monetary law, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Other authorities reject these arguments because the first version is the anonymous voice of the Gemara and the second a minority view, and Rif, Rosh, and most authorities rule according to the first version. Rabbeinu Tam answered Rif's proof from the shepherd case by noting that there was a concrete monetary basis, since property was regularly entrusted to the shepherd and witnesses saw him consume two animals. He also maintained that the Gemara's proof comes from the latter clause even though it cited the opening words. Nevertheless, even according to Rif's ruling, where the claim appears fraudulent, one should not administer an oath. Ketubot 85b establishes that courts do not facilitate a contrived claim. Thus, where a poor person asserts an implausibly large claim against a wealthy person, or a similarly suspicious disparity appears, the court may combine Rabbeinu Tam's view with the indications that the claim is fabricated and decline to impose hesset.
And likewise if, etc. The marginal reference makes this appear to be a separate rule, but it is not. Mahari Weil's responsa show that the refusal to administer an oath is based on Rabbeinu Tam's position only when joined to another suspicious circumstance. In the cited case, if Reuven's sole claim had stood alone, the court might have followed Rabbeinu Tam because Reuven himself alleged that Shimon had repeatedly robbed him, yet continued to enter partnership with him. He thereby undermined his own accusation, as the Gemara says, 'Until now you suspected him of robbery, and now you associate with him.'
