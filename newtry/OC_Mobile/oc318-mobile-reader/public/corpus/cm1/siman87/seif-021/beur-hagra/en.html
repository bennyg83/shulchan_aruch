They do not frighten, etc. See Sema. The latter clause of the baraita states that if he says, 'Depart,' etc., and the Gemara explains the warning. Rashi understands the warning as illness and punishment. Hagahot Maimoniyot explains that if the oath-taker swears falsely, the punishment rests upon him, while if he swears truthfully after being summoned needlessly, punishment rests upon the one who imposed the vain oath. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi, the Tosefta to Sotah, and Vayikra Rabbah: the consequence does not depart from between the two of them. If the claimant demanded falsely, punishment ultimately comes upon him; if the oath-taker swore falsely, it comes upon him. This explains the ruling above in seif 20 where the other party presses a definite claim. With a doubtful claim, both may be righteous, so the baraita necessarily concerns a definite claim.

The baraita refers to an oath administered by judges, which is less severe than the oath of a widow outside beit din, because the oath-taker is less afraid of punishment, does not swear with the Divine Name, and does not hold a Torah scroll. Rambam therefore does not mention hesset in this context.
