And one of them swears. The Sema writes that if one claimant has a claim against several defendants, one of the defendants, whichever the claimant chooses, can take the oath while the others stand with him and answer amen. One who answers amen is considered as though he took the oath himself, as indicated by the verse concerning the woman's response in the Sotah procedure. The Sema explains that this works where the person administering the oath says, "I place an oath upon whoever is withholding so and so's money," or uses a formulation of curse, and the others answer amen to accept the oath. It need not literally be the word amen. An equivalent expression such as "yes" can suffice, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 236. But if the primary litigant merely says, "I swear that I owe nothing," what would the others' amen accomplish? They are not affirming a proposition framed as an oath upon them. The Shach therefore writes that each can say, "I too am like him," thereby accepting the same oath. The Sema's requirement of amen or another verbal acceptance is modeled on ordinary vows and personal oath expressions. A deposit oath is different. There, the Rambam writes explicitly in Hilchot Shevuot that after another person administers the oath, the defendant need not answer amen at all. Continuing to stand by his denial after the oath has been imposed is itself treated as acceptance. In that category, therefore, a separate amen is not intrinsically necessary. Knesset HaGedolah nevertheless writes that amen should be answered, and perhaps judicial oaths are administered more stringently. The language above in seif 17 also suggests a formal response. The Shach's proposal that another defendant can say "I too am like him" requires study. The source resembles the law of a nazir: one person says "I am a nazir," and another immediately says "and I," becoming a nazir as well. There the second statement must be made within toch kedei dibbur. After that brief interval, the words are no longer grammatically attached to the first speaker's declaration. If the same requirement applies here, beit din would need to determine whether each defendant said "I too am like him" within that very short interval after the relevant oath or claim. Who can reliably measure such moments in ordinary litigation? Perhaps, however, if the later defendant says explicitly, "I too accept upon myself the full severity of the oath that he just took," the statement can operate even after toch kedei dibbur because it is an independent acceptance rather than a mere grammatical continuation. This still requires study.
