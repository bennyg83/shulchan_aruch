The Sema says that when several people are bound by a curse or ban, answering "Amen" can join a person to it, but where one person swore explicitly in Hashem's Name that he owes nothing, another person's mere "Amen" does not suffice. The Shach adds that saying, "I am likewise," should work. In my view even that does not work for a judicial oath concerning a past fact. The Gemara and Rif distinguish between attaching oneself to a vow and attaching oneself to an oath. A vow places a legal status of prohibition on the object, so another can say, "I am like you," or apply that status to another object. An oath rests upon the person, and merely comparing oneself to another swearer does not constitute uttering an oath. Rambam rules similarly. Even according to the Tur, who is more receptive to personal attachment in an oath, the logic works particularly for future conduct: if one hears another swear not to eat something and says, "I am like you," he accepts the same future restriction. A judicial oath concerns a past fact. One cannot transform another person's past factual oath into one's own merely by saying, "I am like him." Therefore the phrase is insufficient for a judicial oath under all major approaches.
