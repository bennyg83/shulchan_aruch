Whoever has become obligated. See Baer Heitev regarding the Taz's ruling that when one party reverses a hesset oath onto his opponent, the party who reversed it need not first accept a ban. The Taz states the same above in siman 82 seif 9 and below in siman 92 seif 11. Netivot HaMishpat cites only the Taz's view, implying agreement. Urim VeTumim, however, writes that common practice is to impose a ban even when an oath has been reversed, and the custom of Israel has the force of Torah. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat siman 71, questions the Taz's proof from siman 82 seif 9 and concludes that a general ban should be required even in a reversed oath, except in a place where the Taz's custom has been accepted. Atarat Tzvi, seif katan 42, and the responsa at the end of that work likewise state that the present custom follows the Sema rather than the Taz. Those responsa add that where a Torah oath is imposed by one witness, the claimant need not first accept a ban. Even a severe oath can be removed by a supporting witness, all the more so a ban. Nor is there concern here that the claimant is imposing a needless oath, since one may initially require an oath from the defendant on the strength of one witness even when the claimant has no personal knowledge of the matter, as the Mechaber and Rama state. Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 13, writes in its case that because the claimant sought to collect from heirs, the heirs could assert kim li in accordance with the Sema, that even when an oath is reversed the opposing party must first accept a ban. They could also reverse the oath concerning the disputed amount back onto the claimant, in which case they themselves would not need to accept a ban, because they could assert kim li in accordance with the Taz.
