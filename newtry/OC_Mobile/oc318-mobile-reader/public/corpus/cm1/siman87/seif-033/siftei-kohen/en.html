One should not administer an oath where there is reason to fear, etc. The Sema understands this language to mean that he should not swear because the matter may later be clarified contrary to his oath, and it would then emerge that he swore falsely. The Levush writes similarly, adding that if the claimant is unwilling to wait, he should nullify all witnesses he has concerning this matter and may not later bring them to disqualify the defendant. But, they say, one should not refrain from administering an oath merely because it may prove unnecessary, for perhaps the matter will never become known and the claimant will lose his claim. In truth, the Levush does write this, but Tosafot at the end of the chapter HaMafkid, Bava Metzia 34b, the source of this law, state the opposite. They challenge Rashi's explanation of the law above, siman 72, seif 15, that the borrower does not swear lest the lender later produce the pledge and disqualify him from testimony and oaths. Rabbeinu Tam objects that, on the contrary, the borrower should swear, for he will take greater care to swear truthfully so that the lender cannot disqualify him. We do not enact safeguards for fraudsters. Rabbeinu Tam therefore accepts Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation that it appears to be an oath taken in vain, and it is degrading to swear where the matter will later become ascertainable. Baal HaTerumot, gate 24, likewise follows Rabbeinu Chananel and not Rashi. It further appears to me that Rashi too agrees that where the concern is that witnesses will later disqualify him, we do not enact a safeguard for a fraudster. Rashi's concern there is only that the pledge may later have deteriorated, as Tosafot explain. If the defendant says, “How can I swear, when you will bring witnesses and disqualify me,” we certainly do not heed him. We tell him: if you know the truth is with you and swear accordingly, how could witnesses truthfully testify against you? His objection itself indicates deceit. The Mechaber's rule, that one does not administer an oath where the matter may later be clarified, therefore applies only where later witnesses would obligate payment without disqualifying him. Examples include where the claimant later produces a document, as above at the end of siman 75; where the defendant says, “I do not know whether you lent me”; or where he claims payment but there are witnesses that he stipulated that payment could be made only before witnesses. In all these cases he is not disqualified, because although the document or condition obligates him to pay, his oath may still have been true, since he may actually have paid. The same applies to comparable cases. Thus the Levush's and Sema's explanation concerning witnesses, that the concern is a false oath, is incorrect. One cannot object that above, at the end of siman 75, we rule that if he says, “Nullify all your witnesses before making me swear,” we do not heed him, for I explained there that this applies only when he does not identify the witnesses. Here the case is where he identifies them. Consider this carefully.
