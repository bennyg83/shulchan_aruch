Penalty. The rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so must pay applies only to a matter where an admission would itself create monetary liability. Ri says this in the name of Tosafot. See siman 399 and elsewhere. Shach.
In judgment. Responsa Mabit II, 318 rules concerning one who claims that his fellow was adjudged liable to him for money arising from partnership dealings, while the fellow denies either that they were ever before beit din or that any liability was imposed. The Mabit says the defendant must swear and cannot be told, "Why not simply relitigate? If you admit the facts you will be liable, and if you deny them you will in any event face an oath." The Shach rejects the Mabit's proofs. Those other cases involve a penalty or similar liability that would not arise now if it had not already been imposed. In an ordinary monetary dispute, why administer an oath about the previous litigation? Let the parties litigate the underlying matter anew, unless the claimant specifically says that the defendant accepted the former beit din's decision even if mistaken and was actually ruled liable, or claims that relitigation is impossible because he forgot key distinctions, or fears that the defendant will not repeat an admission previously made before that beit din.
To swear. This applies in a jurisdiction where monetary penalties are adjudicated. Today that aspect of the law is generally inapplicable. Hagahot Maimoniyot writes that if the defendant denies having paid the penalty, he takes a shevuat heset that he did not pay. Although the monetary duty arose only by enactment, the Sages did not distinguish within their institution of heset. Shach.
