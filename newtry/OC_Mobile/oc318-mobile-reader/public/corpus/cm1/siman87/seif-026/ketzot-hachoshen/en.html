Only a fine. I was uncertain where the claim is for actual monetary damage of a type that our courts do not presently adjudicate because it is uncommon, but the defendant can be placed under ban until he appeases the claimant. Must he take hesset if he denies the claim? On the one hand, if he admitted, beit din would not descend to his assets. On the other hand, admission would still expose him to coercive sanctions until he paid. Semag says that one who claims, "You shamed me," in a place where such monetary penalties are adjudicated can impose hesset upon a defendant who denies. The Tur likewise speaks specifically of a place where the fine is collected. This might imply that no oath applies where the monetary claim cannot presently be adjudicated. Nevertheless, in our practice, where beit din places the wrongdoer under ban until he makes proper payment or appeasement, the situation is functionally like a place where collection occurs. The fact that beit din does not directly seize property does not mean the claim is nonmonetary. Meisharim rules that even certain rabbinic monetary duties that are enforced only by ban can support an oath. If the person refuses the oath, the court can ban him for thirty days and afterward apply further sanctions, though it does not directly seize his property. Responsa Maimoniyot gives a similar example concerning Rabbeinu Tam's enactment for return of dowry property. Even though the liability arises through communal enactment, if the person admits receiving the money he must return it, and if he denies he takes an oath. The oath system was not differentiated merely because the underlying enforceability came through enactment. The Tur's ruling regarding verbal insult or defamation is different. Mere words of shame are not a Torah monetary tort even in the era of ordained judges. Any payment imposed for them is a discretionary fine established by later courts. The defendant is not denying a fixed monetary debt at all. Therefore only a ban may apply, not hesset. But where the underlying act is one that creates true monetary liability under Torah law and we refrain from direct adjudication today only because it is uncommon, the defendant is denying money and must swear. Raavad in Hilchot To'en states this expressly. He notes that although personal injury cases are not ordinarily adjudicated in Babylonia, beit din can still place the defendant under ban until he appeases the claimant. His words show that the lack of direct collection does not remove the monetary character of the claim. Therefore hesset applies in such cases.
