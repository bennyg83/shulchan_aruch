He is believed when he says, 'I swore.' The Shach cites Rav Hai that this applies specifically to a rabbinic oath, because the Sages did not institute an additional enactment merely to verify that another rabbinic enactment was fulfilled. But where the original oath was Torah law, one who says, 'I swore,' must take a hesset oath that he indeed swore. The Shach rules like Rav Hai, and so do the poskim. Accordingly, if one sues a bailee and the defendant answers, 'I never became a bailee; I merely said, "Place it before me,"' the defendant must take a hesset oath. We do not say that one oath cannot be imposed about another oath. Had he admitted becoming a bailee, he would have owed a Torah oath. He now asserts a claim that exempts him from that Torah oath, and therefore he takes hesset, just like one who says, 'I already swore.' See Shulchan Aruch siman 296, seif 7, and what I wrote in siman 91, seif katan 7, explaining Rav Hai's reason. The obligation to swear is itself a matter that can lead to monetary payment if he refuses to swear. In the law of an oath of testimony, one who adjures a single witness may be liable according to the view that something causing money is like money. Since an obligation to swear is only something that can cause money and is not itself a monetary object, no Torah oath is imposed upon its denial, for Torah oaths require the very subject to be money. A hesset oath, however, applies even to claims involving documents, and likewise to something that can cause money.
Until he brings proof. The Shach writes that the defendant is not believed to say, 'You waived the oath for me,' because waiver is an inherently weak claim, and without a migo even an oath does not make such a waiver claim credible. Tumim objects that regarding the oath itself he should certainly be believed to say, 'You waived it,' through a migo that he could simply swear on the underlying claim. The fact that the attendant or communal officer had been designated to administer the oath does not matter; he could say, 'I am prepared to swear on the merits exactly as you demand.' If he would not falsely swear about the substantive claim, neither should we assume he would falsely swear that the oath was waived. In my opinion the Shach is correct. An oath saying, 'You waived the oath,' is not a monetary judicial oath but resembles an ordinary oath of utterance, such as swearing that one did or did not eat. The oath required by beit din on the underlying monetary claim is a judicial monetary oath, which people regard as more severe. Swearing falsely that the creditor waived the oath therefore does not substitute for the required monetary oath. Only in the special law of a widow did the Sages allow a vow to the heirs to replace the ordinary oath. Elsewhere, even if someone swore previously not to eat a loaf, and then a monetary claim arises, the fact that eating would violate his private oath does not satisfy the judicial oath requirement. Thus, if he swears falsely that the creditor waived the oath, he has violated an ordinary oath of utterance but has not fulfilled the more stringent judicial monetary oath. This is clear.
