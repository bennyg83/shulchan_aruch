One who became obligated by beit din to take an oath, etc., until he is established as a denier, etc. The Beit Yosef writes in part 2 that the Tur carefully says “one who became obligated by an oath of beit din,” teaching that only where beit din obligated him does a contradiction by witnesses establish him as a denier when he later says, “I swore.” But where beit din did not obligate him and he voluntarily accepted an oath, saying, “I will swear to you,” and the claimant demanded before witnesses that he swear but he refused, he is nevertheless believed if he later says, “I swore.” Although he put the witnesses off, a person may happen to say, “Beit din did not obligate me, I accepted it myself,” and this is not persistent refusal or retraction but merely a temporary dismissal. The Beit Yosef says that this is explicit in the Gemara. In truth, only the distinction that the obligation was imposed by beit din appears in the Gemara. The stated reason does not appear there, but is Rashi’s explanation, following his view regarding a claim of payment that once a person refused to pay he is no longer believed to say that he paid. The Rosh and Tur already disagree with Rashi above, at the end of siman 79, and the Rama rules likewise there in seif 13. The Tur’s words here also clearly imply this, and the Mechaber himself rules below that the case applies only where the witnesses never left him. According to their view, the Gemara’s statement that where he obligated himself by an oath a person may happen to say something means as follows: where he accepted an oath and said, “I swore,” while witnesses testify that they never left him and that he did not swear, he is not established as a denier, because a person may happen to make an untrue statement in such circumstances when he obligated himself by an oath. This implies that even where he accepted the oath in a manner from which he cannot retract, such as by a kinyan, before beit din, or the like, since he obligated himself and beit din did not impose it, he is not established as a denier. Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 3, part 4, cites the Ramah that even where he was liable to an oath in beit din, if he first volunteered, “I will swear,” before beit din told him to swear, and afterward said, “I swore,” he may have happened to say so and is not established as a denier. The Beit Yosef cites this at the end of part 2.

The Sema, seif katan 83, writes: See the Tur, who disagrees in the name of Rav Hai and writes that specifically concerning a rabbinic oath, if he says, “I swore,” he is discharged, because we do not enact a further safeguard for an existing safeguard. The Mordechai and Rabbeinu Yerucham rule that he is believed and need only accept a general ban, as the Rama concludes and as the Darchei Moshe cites. In my humble opinion, the Sema erred regarding Rabbeinu Yerucham. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes in netiv 3, at the end of part 3: “Some write that when we said above that he is believed to say, ‘I swore,’ this means without an oath, although a general ban is proclaimed.” Rabbeinu Yerucham is in fact referring there to the hesset oath he mentioned previously. Otherwise he should have cited this opinion immediately after Rav Hai’s words above to show that it disagrees with Rav Hai. Rather, his meaning is that when one is believed to say that he already took a hesset oath, he is believed without another oath, although a general ban is proclaimed. The Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe likewise imply that this is Rabbeinu Yerucham’s position. The Mordechai also does not definitively rule otherwise. It cites differing opinions and concludes that he must swear that he already swore. We therefore follow the Tur. This is also the view of the Rosh, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia, and of Ramban, Rashba, and the Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 7 of Hilchot To'en.

Until he brings proof for his words. The Sema writes that if he claims, “You waived the oath for me,” he is believed, and refers to siman 82, seif 11. This requires study. It is not comparable to siman 82, seif 11. Certainly the borrower can require the lender to swear concerning the waiver, but it is unreasonable that the borrower should be believed without an oath. This would also undermine justice, for then everyone would claim, “You waived the oath,” and no oath would ever be administered. Rather, he is not believed to say that the oath was waived except where he has a migo that he could have said, “I swore.” I proved above, siman 75, seif 7, seif katan 20, that a borrower is not believed to say, “You waived it,” unless he has a migo. See there. The same applies here. Ordinarily a person does not waive an oath. Since he cannot claim, “I swore,” because the practice is to administer the oath in the presence of the synagogue attendant, he is likewise not believed to claim that the oath was waived. It is possible, however, that regarding a hesset oath he is believed to say that it was waived, because we do not enact an additional safeguard for an existing safeguard. This too requires study. It is more likely that the Sema means only that he need not bring external proof, but he must still swear that the oath was waived. Even this requires study, for perhaps an oath that it was waived is ineffective, since he is believed to claim waiver only where he has a migo, as I wrote above in siman 75. Here he has no migo. Just as he would not be believed, even with an oath, had he said, “I swore,” so too regarding “You waived it.” One cannot say that he should be believed by a migo that he could have claimed, “I swore in the presence of the synagogue attendant.” If he made that claim, it appears that he would not be believed at all, even with an oath, because by the established practice that oaths are administered specifically in the attendant’s presence, the litigants effectively accepted the attendant’s word as equivalent to two witnesses. Even were one to say that he could be believed with a Torah oath to contradict the attendant, nevertheless, now that he claims waiver, we do not apply a migo of this kind. As I wrote above in siman 75, seif 13, seif katan 39, in the name of Tosafot and the authorities, wherever the alternative claim would require him to contradict a witness and swear against that witness, it does not generate a migo.
