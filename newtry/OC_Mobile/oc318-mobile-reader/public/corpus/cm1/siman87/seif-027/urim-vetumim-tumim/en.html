He is believed. The Shach asks: let the claimant simply say, "You still owe me the oath that beit din imposed. Swear to me now," and require a new oath. The Shach answers that the case concerns an uncertain claim, or else that the defendant is willing to swear again. But one who studies the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia will see that after citing the authorities who hold that the defendant is believed to say "I already swore," the Mordechai says that Raavyah disagrees with the Rif, and that Rashbam likewise writes that the claimant can still say, "Swear to me that you already satisfied your obligation." This clearly shows that the original discussion concerns a case where the claimant could in principle demand another oath on the very matter, and nevertheless the Rif and those who follow him exempt the defendant. Their reasoning is that hesset itself is only a rabbinic enactment. Once beit din already imposed an oath and the defendant says he performed it, the Sages did not require a person to take endless oaths about whether he already took the prior oath. Rav Nachman did not enact hesset in such an extreme recursive form. The claimant could have protected himself by asking that the original oath be taken before witnesses or by stipulating that it not be accepted privately. If he failed to do so, he bears the consequence. I found the same explanation in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 14. It states in substance: when one claims with certainty, "You owe me an oath because of the definite claim of a maneh that I previously made against you," this resembles an ordinary claim of a maneh or "swear and be exempt." The defendant's statement "I already swore" resembles a complete denial. Ordinarily that would justify hesset. Nevertheless, the Gaon ruled that we do not compound hesset upon hesset. Because the matter is uncertain, we apply the lenient rule. The Ramban and Ran say the same. This explicitly confirms that the claimant's allegation can be definite. One should not distinguish between a case where the claimant lacks a written judgment establishing the oath and one where he possesses such a judgment. Above in siman 39 we rule that a debtor generally cannot say "I paid" against a written judgment, because if payment occurred he should have recovered or canceled the judgment. One might similarly argue that if the defendant had already sworn he should have obtained or destroyed the written ruling requiring the oath. Yet I have found no authority who makes such a distinction here. The Gemara likewise does not say that if a written ruling is issued, beit din may no longer allow the defendant to claim that he already swore. The difference is straightforward. A monetary judgment can be used to collect from encumbered property and therefore a debtor has strong reason not to leave it outstanding after payment. A ruling that merely requires an oath does not create collection rights against purchasers. The defendant may therefore be less careful about leaving the paper in the claimant's hands. His failure to recover it does not defeat his statement that he already swore. The Sema writes that Rav Hai Gaon distinguishes between a Torah oath and hesset. Where the original obligation was a Torah oath, the defendant who later says "I swore" must take hesset that he indeed swore. The Mordechai and Mahari Weil, according to the Sema, would extend the leniency even there. The Shach argues that Mahari Weil does not clearly say this. I have not found any authority who truly disputes Rav Hai Gaon. If the claimant now asserts with certainty, "You still owe me the Torah oath," but circumstances no longer permit the original Torah oath to be recreated, for example because the partial admission has already been paid or the witness who originally created the oath is gone, why should the defendant not at least take hesset on the present definite allegation that he still owes performance of that obligation? The special reason discussed above, that the Sages did not create hesset upon an earlier hesset, does not apply. The earlier obligation was Torah level. If, on the other hand, the current claimant is not certain, such as where the original creditor died and the heirs do not know whether the oath was taken, and one does not allow an oath based merely on "I was informed," then there is no basis for hesset. Even an admission by the defendant would not create an ordinary monetary debt, only an oath obligation. The general rule is that where an admission would not create monetary liability, hesset is not imposed. Maharshal already raises this point. Thus the sources should be read consistently. The Rif's broad statement concerns an original hesset obligation, where the Sages did not enact another hesset over whether the first hesset was performed. Rav Hai Gaon is correct that when the original oath was Torah level and the claimant now makes a definite claim that it remains unperformed, hesset can apply. Nimukei Yosef likewise implies that the Rif did not intend to disagree with Rav Hai Gaon on this point. This also affects the Rama's ruling that nowadays, where the custom is to take the oath before the communal attendant, the defendant is not believed to say he swore privately. If he says, "I swore before the attendant," and the attendant says he did not, the defendant cannot contradict that witness by taking another oath. The Shach explains this correctly. Indeed, no additional oath is necessary. If the defendant admitted that he did not swear, he would not owe money directly but would merely still owe the original oath. The attendant is trusted with the force given to the designated official regarding this very matter, and his testimony determines whether the procedural obligation was fulfilled.
He is not believed. But he is believed to say, "You waived the oath for me." The Sema, Shach, and Taz all object to this distinction. A claim of waiver is inherently weak, and if it were accepted freely no oath obligation would ever survive, because every defendant could simply say, "You waived it." Their objection is correct. However, the Shach further writes that even if the defendant is willing to swear that the claimant waived the oath, he is not believed. That does not appear correct. He has a migo that he could simply take the underlying oath on the merits of the original dispute. The fact that, by local practice, he cannot say "I already swore" because the oath had to be taken before the communal attendant does not remove this migo. He can still say, "I am prepared to swear now on the underlying claim exactly as you demand." If he is unwilling to swear falsely on the actual merits, there is no reason to assume he would be willing to swear falsely that the claimant waived the oath. The two are equivalent from the standpoint of the prohibition against false swearing. Therefore, if he takes an oath that the claimant indeed waived the procedural oath, he should be believed by virtue of the migo that he could have sworn directly on the underlying claim. The Shach's statement that there is no migo is therefore difficult. The Taz likewise appears to understand that a sworn claim of waiver can be believed. This seems straightforward.
