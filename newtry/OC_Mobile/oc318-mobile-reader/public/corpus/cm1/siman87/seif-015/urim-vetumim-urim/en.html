Except tefillin. Rav Hai Gaon's *Sefer Shevuot* defines a Torah scholar for this purpose as one who lays tefillin. Shach notes that if so there is no distinction between a Torah scholar and anyone else, because every Jew should lay tefillin. It is more plausible that Rav Hai Gaon meant one who lays tefillin throughout the day, as people commonly did in earlier generations. Merely laying tefillin at the time of Shema cannot define a Torah scholar, for are we dealing with wicked people whose heads are never adorned with tefillin, God forbid? On the contrary, in our times the all-day practice is uncommon, and almost no one falls within that special category.
The same applies to other sacred books. Taz and Shach question this. Why should sacred books be treated as having greater sanctity than tefillin, where this leniency was given only to a Torah scholar? It is strained to answer that ordinary people subjectively regard sacred books as more serious, even though oath procedure may depend partly on popular perception. Nevertheless, the custom in our times has already become lenient, as Taz writes. The matter depends on the judge's assessment of the people and circumstances.
