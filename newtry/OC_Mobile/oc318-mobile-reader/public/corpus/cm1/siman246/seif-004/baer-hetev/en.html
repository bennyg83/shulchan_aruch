One who writes. The Sema writes that according to the Rif and Rambam this rule applies specifically where the father writes all his property to the son. Since he deliberately put it in writing, we say that his intention was merely to honor the son and cause it to become publicly known through the document that he appointed him over all his property, and therefore the son is only an administrator. If, however, a dangerously ill person merely said, "All my property shall belong to my son So-and-so," it is an absolute gift. The Rif and Rambam distinguish in this way in order to reconcile apparently conflicting passages in the Gemara, as the Beit Yosef explains. The Rashbam, Rosh, Tur, and those who follow them do not distinguish between writing and oral declaration. They instead reconcile the passages by distinguishing between a grant expressed as a gift and one expressed as inheritance. This explains the Mechaber and Rama's wording, "Some say that this applies specifically with the language of a gift." One authority says one distinction and the other says another. The Levush writes that some say the rule applies specifically to the language of gift, whereas a dangerously ill person who writes in the language of inheritance transfers ownership, because Scripture says, "on the day that he causes his sons to inherit," showing that a father has authority to cause one of his sons to inherit all he owns. The Sema objects that this reason is irrelevant, since even with gift language any person may give all he owns to others, all the more so to his sons. Rather, the reason is that gift language can be understood as appointment to administration, which itself is called a gift, whereas inheritance language cannot. The Shach agrees with the Levush and writes that his formulation is that of the Rashbam on Bava Batra 130a. See Responsa Rabbi Yosef Beit HaLevi, siman 24.
