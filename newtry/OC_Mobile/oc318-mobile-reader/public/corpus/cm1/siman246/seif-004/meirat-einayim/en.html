One who writes all his property, etc. According to the Rif and Rambam, this law applies specifically when he writes the property to the son. Since he uses a formal document, we assume that his intent is to honor the son publicly by having a document circulate saying that he was appointed over all the property. Therefore the son is merely an administrator. But if a dangerously ill person orally says, "All my property to so and so my son," it is a complete gift. They distinguish writing from oral declaration in order to reconcile apparently conflicting Talmudic rulings. Rashbam, the Rosh, the Tur, and those with them do not distinguish between writing and speech. They reconcile the passages by distinguishing whether the property was given in the language of gift or in the language of inheritance. This explains the Mechaber and Rama. Rama writes, "Some say, specifically in the language of gift," because the authority who says this does not accept the writing versus speech distinction. The Ir Shushan gives the reason that when a dangerously ill person uses inheritance language the son acquires because Scripture authorizes a father to apportion inheritance among his sons. That is not the relevant reason. Even in gift language every person may give all his property to whomever he wishes, certainly to a son. The actual reason is that only gift language can be interpreted as merely appointing an administrator, because administration too can be described through a form of gift. Inheritance language cannot naturally be interpreted that way.
