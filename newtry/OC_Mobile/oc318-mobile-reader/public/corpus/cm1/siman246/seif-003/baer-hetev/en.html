To flee. The Beit Yosef writes that even the authorities who disagree in the previous seif and hold that the presumption of intent was stated specifically regarding a dangerously ill person agree here, because one who was compelled to flee resembles a dangerously ill person. The Sema writes that this is not compelling. On the contrary, the words of the Ramah cited by the Tur imply that one who disagrees concerning a healthy person who heard that his son died likewise disagrees here, and the Bach also writes this. Responsa Knesset HaGedolah concludes that even if a long time passed between hearing of the son's death and writing the gift, we still follow the assessment of intent. He further writes that the case is not limited to hearing that one's son died, but applies similarly to other heirs. He also writes that the baraita necessarily concerns a person who has no other children. If he had other children and nevertheless wrote all his property to outsiders, the gift would plainly be valid, because the presumption of intent is negated. And if he had other children but gave outsiders only the share that would have gone to the son he believed dead, it is equally plain that the gift is void, since his decision to transfer only that son's share reveals his intent. He further writes that the same rule applies even where one consecrated his property. He later found, however, that the Bach in siman 220, seif 4, writes that consecrating one's property presents an unresolved question, analogous to a dangerously ill person who consecrated his property. Bnei Chayei.
