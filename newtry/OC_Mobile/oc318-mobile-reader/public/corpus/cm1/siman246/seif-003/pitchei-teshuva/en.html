See Baer Heitev and Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, end of siman 34, who writes that a claim for reimbursement for supporting an orphan girl at one's table belongs to an obscure area of law, because the authorities appear inconsistent in several places. In his view, absent clear proof that the administrator intended his support as charity, where the administrator is in possession of property belonging to the orphans he may swear that he intended to be repaid and deduct the cost of support from the property in his hands. Everything depends on the beit din's assessment of his intention when he provided the support. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, responsum 147, was likewise asked about one who supported a minor orphan, seized property belonging to the orphan, and demanded reimbursement. He writes that where one supports an ordinary person without specifying whether repayment is expected, the view of the early and later authorities appears to be that the recipient must repay. But where one supports an orphan, Baal HaTerumot cites a responsum of the Rif, codified in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah 253:5, that after the orphan grows up he need not repay when the supporter did not expressly state that the support was a loan. Beit Yosef and Rama there indicate that even where the orphan has assets, the unstated presumption is that the support was a gift. Although Shulchan Aruch speaks of one who supported an orphan intending to perform a mitzvah, Rama indicates that this applies even where no such intention is known. This does not conflict with the Rashba's responsum, cited below in siman 290, seif 25, that orphans who attached themselves to a householder who supported them from his own funds do not leave his money unrecoverable. That case concerns one who undertakes all their needs and therefore has the status of an administrator, so the orphans must repay him. Although Terumat HaDeshen, siman 348, appears to hold that one who supports an orphan can collect at least where the orphan had assets, he did not cite the Rif and evidently had not seen that ruling. Since Rabbeinu Yerucham and Beit Yosef quote the Rif without qualification and Shulchan Aruch establishes it as law, seizure should not help. Beit Yosef, Choshen Mishpat 128, comments on the Rif that where the orphan was a minor, even if he had nothing of his own in the supporter's hands he should be liable because one cannot say the supporter should have stipulated with a child. Rabbi Akiva Eiger explains that Beit Yosef cannot mean every minor, because the Rif expressly concerned a minor. Rather, he means a child who had not yet reached the age at which minors' commercial transactions are effective. Accordingly, in the case before Rabbi Akiva Eiger, where the orphan had already reached that age, the supporter could not collect through his seizure. Indeed, even support provided before the child reached that age may have been lost. Beit Yosef may speak only of very young children where the support later ceased. If the supporter continued after they grew older, then once they reached an age at which he could have stipulated for repayment and did not do so, his present conduct proves that he was giving as a gift, and reveals retrospectively that the earlier support was likewise given as a gift. It is unreasonable to say that while they were very young he supported them as a loan and once somewhat older as a gift. Thus seizure did not help, especially if wealthy people in that city customarily support orphaned relatives gratuitously, comparable to Terumat HaDeshen's discussion of one who supports his daughter and son in law. If, however, he seized without witnesses, there is room to say that he is believed through a migo that the support was a loan, for the presumption of a gift may not be so absolute as to render the migo one against witnesses. Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer 93, seif katan 23, writes concerning a widow who remained two or three years that even seizure without witnesses is ineffective because the migo is against witnesses, but Rabbi Akiva Eiger states that the law in the present case still requires study because there may be a distinction. From his statement that seizure in the presence of witnesses plainly does not help, it appears to me that he means seizure made only after the support had already been provided. If the supporter had the orphan's property in his hands at the time he was supporting him, even possession known to witnesses is effective, as Beit Yosef, Choshen Mishpat 128, expressly writes concerning the Rif: the Rif's ruling applies only when the supporter did not then have property of the orphan corresponding to his claim, but if he did have the orphan's property, he may plainly deduct what he spent, because in such a case we do not say that he supported him as an act of kindness. Rabbi Akiva Eiger did not need to mention this, because in the case before him the supporter seized the orphan's property only after providing the support. See also Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 134, concerning a widow who was supported in the house of one of her sons for several years, while she had money and notes in a chest in a room that the son had designated for her in his home. The son eventually sought reimbursement from her property, while his mother or his brothers claimed that he had supported her as a mitzvah. Chavot Yair develops several distinctions. In substance, if the mother has nothing of her own, although there is no obligation under honoring father and mother to support her from the child's own funds, since the rule is that honoring is funded from the parent's property, nevertheless a son who takes her into his home and supports her plainly acts for the sake of the mitzvah and beyond the strict letter of the law. Even if she later receives an inheritance, he cannot demand payment from her or her heirs for what he did while she was poor. If, however, she had her own property while he supported her, then even without expressly stating that she must pay, she must reimburse him when he demands it, no less than any other person under the Rama at the end of siman 246. This is not contradicted by Yoreh Deah 253, which says that one who supports an orphan in his home need not be repaid even when the orphan has assets, because there is an assessment there that he acted gratuitously so that the orphan's funds would remain intact for his future needs. A son supporting his mother is different. Ordinarily she remains for the rest of her life with the son whose home she entered, and where she has means there is no charity or special kindness involved in using them for her support, particularly after her death as against the other heirs. Why should he waive his claim in their favor? If, however, when she first came to him he knew she intended to stay only temporarily and after a year or two would leave and support herself from her own property, the case is directly comparable to one who supports an orphan, and he cannot demand repayment even after her death. This is clear. All this applies even where the property is generally in the possession of the heirs, and certainly where it is in the hands of the son who supported her. He can say that he did not act gratuitously, whether during her life or after her death; at the least, where there is doubt, the burden of proof rests on the party seeking to extract property. Even if the other brothers produce deeds of gift or debt that their mother gave them, or such documents are held by outsiders, if the supporting brother remains in possession he may retain what is in his hands. He must, however, take a heset oath that he has none of his mother's property other than what is found in her chest and that she paid him nothing toward her support. If the brothers claim that in exchange for her support she acted as his maidservant, even if they prove that from the day she came to him he no longer employed a maid while he had one previously, he may respond that his daughter had by then grown or his wife had become more diligent in the household, and he is not presumed to have subjected his mother to the labor of a servant. Even if she helped raise the children, wash, spin, or prepare produce or the children's clothing, that is not presumed to have been consideration for her support, because elderly women commonly help with such household tasks in the home where they live even when they pay for their own food.
