Because there is room for doubt. Shevut Yaakov, part II, siman 146, discusses a wealthy man who directed before his death that if one of his sons delivered a public derashah in the synagogue he should receive an additional sum from the estate. The son did so. His brothers argued that the derashah was not good and, further, that he had learned it from others or from written material, which their father had not intended. Shevut Yaakov rules that the first contention is no contention, since standards of a derashah differ and the father did not necessarily require an exceptional discourse. The second contention, however, is valid if established by witnesses, for the father's purpose was plainly to induce his son to persevere in learning until he could compose and deliver a discourse himself, thereby bringing merit to his father, not to wrap himself in another's tallit, which would itself be improper. One might suggest that the father cared only that he be capable of speaking before scholars without embarrassment, even if another composed the discourse, especially if everyone knew that he was not capable of composing it himself. That is insufficient. At best it is a doubt, and wherever the wording of a gift is doubtful the recipient bears the burden of proof. Even a definite assessment of presumed intent is not used to extract property from heirs, as the Rosh rules in the responsum cited in Ramah at the end of siman 281. If the brothers cannot prove their allegation, however, they cannot nullify their father's testament merely by asserting it. They may impose a general cherem concerning the truth, or have a scholar test whether the son can himself develop an exposition fit for public delivery; if he can, he receives the gift. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 9, discusses a benefactor who established a permanent fund whose income was to be used for meritorious acts for the elevation of his soul. One provision directed that a gifted youth, between ten and fifteen years old, capable of becoming a Torah authority, receive two hundred gold coins annually until age twenty-five, on the repeated condition that he travel away and study specifically in a yeshivah outside the country. The testator explained that he saw no suitable yeshivah in his own country. The administrators were to select the youth, supervise that he devote himself to Torah and fear of Hashem, and replace him if he strayed or failed to persevere in Talmud study. They found only the son of a leading rabbi, sent him to an out-of-country yeshivah, and another orphan sent by a Torah-study society joined him. After a year and a half the yeshivah declined and both youths returned home. The rabbi then refused to send his son elsewhere, arguing that the year and a half had already taught him the method of yeshivah study and analytic halachic reasoning. He undertook to train both youths himself in the methods of the poskim over five years and asked that the annual two hundred be divided between them. The question was whether this could be done, perhaps increasing the donor's merit by supporting two students, or whether the administrators lacked authority to depart from the express condition that the student learn outside the country. Chatam Sofer answers at length. The donor was correct to insist on study away from home. Even when a father is a great scholar and maintains a yeshivah at home, there is substantial value in going elsewhere to learn, as is evident from Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi's arrangement for his son, from Rabbi Abbahu's sending his son to Tiberias, and from students who traveled even from Eretz Yisrael abroad for Torah study. Nor does a year and a half automatically fulfill the testament. Although no duration was stated, the evident meaning is that the youth remain in yeshivah as long as he needs it. If he still requires yeshivah study, the fund may not be diverted merely because two students would now be supported. Were the change genuinely an elevation in holiness, a direction to perform a particular mitzvah could include a superior form of that mitzvah. Here there is no such added merit. The rabbi's son would study with his father in any event, and the orphan likewise may not benefit from this fund if he no longer studies outside the country as required. Nevertheless, seeking a basis to uphold support of Torah scholars, Chatam Sofer first explains that no other potential student is being robbed. The donor dedicated only future income, analogous to fruits not yet grown, and had not designated a particular alternate student; selection remained in the administrators' discretion. Regarding the mitzvah to fulfill the wishes of the deceased, the evident intent was not that the student remain away until age twenty-five no matter what, but that he stay until he had acquired the methods of true Talmudic analysis and the structure of Shas and the Rishonim, after which he could fill himself with Shas and poskim even at home. If the administrators, in their judgment, determined that the selected youth had acquired enough from a year and a half of yeshivah to begin studying poskim, then although it would certainly be preferable for him to continue outside his home, he has already been selected and acquired his status, and should not be removed unless it is clear that he has failed the testator's conditions. This is supported by the testament's express provision allowing removal only if the selected youth strays from the proper path or fails to persevere, language that would be unnecessary if the administrators could freely replace him at any time. Since he continues to study Torah day and night and they do not know that the yeshivah period was insufficient, he should not be displaced. He may divide his stipend with the orphan so that he continues to learn together with a worthy companion. Chatam Sofer stresses, however, that this was a ruling for that particular circumstance. For the future the administrators should select no one unless he agrees at once to remain away in yeshivah for a specified period and not return home before its completion; no general precedent should be drawn from this exceptional ruling.
