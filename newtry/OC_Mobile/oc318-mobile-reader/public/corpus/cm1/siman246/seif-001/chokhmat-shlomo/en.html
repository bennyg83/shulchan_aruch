Seif 17, gloss. One who says to another, "Eat with me," must pay him, etc. Note: See Shach, seif katan 10, regarding one who feeds another and regarding an invited guest. A case came before me concerning an orphaned minor who had been maintained free of charge by a certain person. The child did not wish to remain there, and his mother took him into her home and fed him. She later demanded reimbursement for his maintenance from money held in trust for the orphan. The first caretaker argued that he had been prepared to continue feeding the child without charge, so the orphan should not have to pay the mother. I answered that the orphan appears exempt. The Shach, citing Maharshal, rules that when a guest expected to eat free of charge because he could have eaten elsewhere for free, he is exempt. A minor's consent is legally like lack of consent, since his understanding is not treated as a binding legal decision. Beit Yosef, siman 128, similarly explains that the rule that one should have notified the orphan does not apply where the orphan lacks legal capacity. Conversely, even if the child agreed to pay his mother, that agreement is not a binding undertaking. Since there was no valid obligation created by the child's consent, the mother has no monetary claim where he could have been fed elsewhere without charge. The child's assertion that he was unhappy with the first caretaker does not change this. She should not have removed him without permission from beit din or from the guardian, who could determine whether it was truly in his interest to live with her even at the cost of his own funds. Without such authorization, she forfeited a monetary claim against him. One might question this because above, in siman 235, a minor from age six may effect transactions in movable property. If so, perhaps his decision concerning maintenance, where he preferred to live with his mother and pay her, should be effective, especially since a loan for his necessities may be collected from him. The Beit Yosef might then refer only to a child below six. Nevertheless, that argument also fails here because siman 235 states that where a minor has a guardian, his transactions are ineffective until majority. In this case beit din had appointed as guardian the person who was feeding him free of charge. The child's act therefore had no effect without the guardian. In addition, the mother never stipulated at the outset that she was taking him only on condition that he pay her. She simply fed him. It is therefore possible that she initially intended to do so gratuitously, as feeding an orphan is a mitzvah, especially when the orphan is her own son. Accordingly, he should not be charged. Since I have not found the precise case stated explicitly, however, the proper course is a compromise according to the judgment of the dayanim. This appears correct to me.
