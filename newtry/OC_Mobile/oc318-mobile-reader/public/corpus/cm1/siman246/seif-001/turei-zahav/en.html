(Seif 1: any amount of his property, and so forth.) It appears to me that according to the Rosh, who holds in siman 250 that a deathly ill person who retains any amount has not made a gift of all his property only if he retained enough for his support, the same applies where one wrote all his property to others but left any amount of land for his son. The retained amount must likewise suffice for the son's support. The primary rule requiring enough for support is stated in the Gemara concerning a deathly ill person, which is the core case. There the Gemara asks who follows presumptive intent in the case of a deathly ill person who wrote all his property, and answers that it is Rabbi Shimon ben Menasya, who rules similarly where one heard that his son died. Thus both cases share one law. Indeed, one opinion says the presumption is even stronger where he heard his son died than in the deathly ill person's case. Since the presumption regarding a deathly ill person is not overcome unless he retained enough for support, all the more so the presumption in the case of one who heard his son died is not overcome unless enough was retained. The Rosh writes in chapter Mi Shemet that the reason a deathly ill person must retain enough for support is logical. The whole rule rests upon the presumption that a person would not give away everything he owns and die of hunger, but gives only because he expects to die. That presumption is not overcome unless he retained enough for support. The same reasoning applies where he heard his son died. The presumption there is that a person does not abandon his child and give his estate to others. It is not overcome until he leaves enough for the son's support. The Tur states the rule where it is mentioned in the Gemara. I find further proof in the Maggid Mishneh, chapter 6 of Hilchot Zechiya, who writes explicitly that the rule where one heard his son died is included within the rule of a deathly ill person, and therefore the Talmud did not need to count it separately. This shows there is no distinction between them, for had there been one, the Gemara would have listed the case separately. The Sema writes otherwise, but what I have written appears primary. From this point until the end of the work, the Turei Zahav was not previously printed within the early editions of Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat, but only in a separate volume in the year 5526. We have now restored it to its proper place.
