For there is room for doubt. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 2, siman 146, concerning a wealthy man who directed before his death that one of his sons should receive a certain sum from his estate, in addition to his share, if he delivered a public discourse in the synagogue. The son delivered the discourse, but his brothers argued that he did not deliver it well and that he had learned it from others or from written material, and that their father had not intended to make the gift on that basis. He answered that the first claim is no claim at all, because opinions differ concerning the quality of a discourse, and their father's intention was not necessarily that he deliver an outstanding discourse. The second claim, however, that he did not learn the material himself, is a valid claim if established by witnesses. Their father presumably intended that he apply himself diligently to his studies so that he could deliver a discourse of his own, thereby bringing merit to his father, rather than clothing himself in a garment that was not his, which would involve wrongdoing. One might argue that the father's assessment was only that the son be capable of speaking and delivering a discourse before the sages without embarrassment, so that he should receive the reward even if the material was not his own, particularly where it was known that he was incapable of composing such a discourse and therefore there would be no wrongdoing. This is not so. First, this is not a clear assessment but only a doubt, and whenever the language of a gift is doubtful, the recipient bears the burden of proof. Second, even if there were a strong assessment of intent, we do not follow such an assessment to extract property from heirs, as the Rosh writes in a responsum cited by the Rama at the end of siman 281. If, however, their claim is not established, they cannot nullify their father's will without evidence. They may impose a general cherem concerning the matter, or have one of the scholars examine him to determine whether he can independently present a proper explanation in a discourse fit to be delivered publicly. If he can, he acquires his gift. See there. He also cites Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 9, concerning a wealthy man who appointed administrators before his death and set aside a specified principal whose income was to be used for good deeds for the benefit of his soul. One provision directed that a young and wise student, fit to stand in the palace of the King of the universe, blessed be He, should diligently study Torah until qualified to render halachic rulings. The student was to be at least ten and no more than fifteen years old, and was to receive two hundred gold coins annually for support and books until age twenty five, on the repeatedly stated condition that he travel away and study specifically in a yeshiva outside the country, because the testator did not regard any place in his country as suitable for a yeshiva. The administrators were empowered to select the student and supervise him so that he devote himself throughout the day only to Torah and fear of Hashem. If they saw that he departed from the proper path or failed to persist in Talmud study, they could dismiss him and choose another in his place. The administrators found no suitable candidate except the son of a prominent rabbi of that country, and sent him to a yeshiva outside the country. Another orphan, sent there by a Torah study society, joined him. They studied there for a year and a half, after which the yeshiva diminished and both returned home. The rabbi was unwilling to send his son to another yeshiva. He argued that the will had already been fulfilled by his son's year and a half in yeshiva, during which he learned the yeshiva method and dialectical analysis, and he undertook to teach both young men the methods of the halachic authorities himself, so that within five years he would prepare them for examination in practical rulings in Orach Chaim and Yoreh Deah. He asked that the administrators give the annual two hundred gold coins jointly to his son and the orphan. The question was whether they could do so, on the theory that supporting two was a greater merit than supporting one, or whether they lacked authority to deviate from the testator's insistence that the student study outside the country. Chatam Sofer answered at length. In substance, he writes that the testator was certainly correct to insist that the student leave the country to study, for there is significant value in doing so even where the father is a great scholar and maintains a yeshiva in his own home. We find that Rabbeinu HaKadosh arranged for his son Rabbi Shimon to go study with his teacher for twelve years, and that Rabbi Abbahu sent his son Rabbi Chanina to Tiberias. Sages even traveled from Eretz Yisrael to outside the Land to study Torah despite the presence of yeshivot in Eretz Yisrael. As to whether the young man had already fulfilled the will by studying in yeshiva for a year and a half, although the will did not specify how long he must remain there, it plainly meant for as long as necessary. See what I wrote above, siman 60, seif 3, seif katan 6. Thus, if the young man still required yeshiva study, he could not be supported from this fund merely because they proposed to support two young men. Had this truly constituted an elevation in sanctity, it would have fallen within a direction to perform a particular mitzvah, where doing something of a higher degree is generally included. Here, however, there was no additional merit. The administrators had initially hesitated to give the testator's charity to the rabbi's son, who would in any event have studied with his father, and selected him only because he was especially promising and his father would not send him away without the specified support. Once he returned home, if a year and a half in yeshiva had not supplied all that he needed, he did not need the fund for studying with his father, since his father would teach him regardless. Likewise, the orphan would have been entitled to the money had he remained outside the country for the necessary period, but if he too was no longer studying outside the country, he had no right to benefit from the fund. Accordingly, there was no increased merit for the donor in the proposed arrangement. Nevertheless, seeking grounds in favor of Torah scholars, Chatam Sofer first explains that this change would not constitute theft of a gift from other poor students who might otherwise have received it. There are two reasons. First, the donor consecrated only the fruits of money, analogous to the fruits of a date palm or the use of a house, as explained in Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 7, cited above in siman 212, seif 9, seif katan 10. Thus, one who redirects these fruits does not steal from an intended recipient who never acquired them. Second, no particular young man was designated; selection was left to the administrators, as discussed in Responsa Maharit, also cited there. As for the mitzvah of fulfilling the words of the deceased, in circumstances where that rule applies, see siman 252, seif 2. Here there is a compelling assessment that the testator did not intend the student to remain in an out of country yeshiva until age twenty five. His intention was that he remain there until trained to analyze halachah truthfully and to learn the structure of the Talmud and early authorities, after which he could fill himself with Talmud and halachic authorities even at home. Therefore, if in the administrators' judgment the student acquired in that year and a half sufficient training to begin studying the halachic authorities, then although it would certainly be preferable for him to continue that study in a yeshiva away from home, once he had been selected and had thereby acquired the benefit, it should not readily be taken from him. This may be inferred from the will itself, which stated that if the administrators saw the selected student turn from the proper path or cease diligent study they could dismiss him. At first glance this clause seems unnecessary, since the whole object was to increase Torah and fear of Hashem, and there would be no basis to support one who abandoned them. Rather, it indicates that although such a person should not initially be selected, once selected and vested with the benefit he should not quickly be removed unless he departs from the path of Hashem and His Torah. Here the student was still engaged in Torah day and night, had already learned the yeshiva method, and in the administrators' judgment that sufficed. Since it was at least uncertain whether he had already received the necessary measure of yeshiva training, they should not remove him. The arrangement proposed in the question could therefore be followed, with the rabbi's son giving part of his share to the orphan so that he would at least continue to study together with a worthy companion. Chatam Sofer concludes, however, that this was an exceptional ruling for that case alone. From then onward the administrators were to record that they should not select anyone unless he immediately undertook to remain away from home for a fixed number of years and not return before that period ended. No rule should be derived from this exceptional ruling for another case. See there.
