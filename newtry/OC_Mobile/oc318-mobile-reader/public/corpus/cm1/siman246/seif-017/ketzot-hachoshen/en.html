Eat with me, he must pay. Shach questions this ruling from the rule of one who says, "Tear my garment." Unless the garment had previously come to the other person's hands as a deposit, such permission ordinarily exempts him even without the express words "and be exempt." The distinction is conceptual. Saying "tear my garment" does not itself contain express language of forgiveness. Rather, when the owner authorizes the destructive act, the resulting loss is not treated as a tort at all. The owner caused the loss to himself by authorizing it. Shitah Mekubetzet uses this to explain why a seduced orphan can lack a fine even though the fine had not yet come into existence and could not be waived: she gave permission to the act itself. If the item had already entered the person's hands as a custodian, he already bears an existing obligation. Later permission to damage does not automatically alter that existing custodial liability unless the owner expressly says, "and be exempt." This is why the same phrase has a different legal result without changing its linguistic meaning. Eating another person's food is different because liability is not only the law of a tortfeasor. It is liability for benefit received. Even when the owner voluntarily allows the benefit, one who receives measurable benefit can owe its value, just like one who descends into another's field and improves it without authorization. The phrase "eat with me" contains no express waiver of payment. Mahari Chayut cites the garment case only to prove that the words themselves do not mean "and be exempt." Since the eater received benefit and was not expressly released, he must pay.
He must pay him. Maharit asks from the law of priestly gifts. If the government took a person's grain in payment of his tax, he must tithe because the grain saved other property and he became enriched. Tosafot asks why one who ate priestly gifts is not similarly liable for the benefit, and answers that he can say he might have fasted. Maharit therefore suggests that one who is told "eat with me" should likewise say he could have fasted, and concludes the eater is liable as a tortfeasor. That cannot be the basis, because Rama elsewhere says that one who is told "live in my courtyard" can be exempt where he himself would not have rented, even though the courtyard owner lost rental value. The distinction is whether the recipient actually benefited. Thus the liability for "eat with me" is liability for benefit. Food produces real bodily benefit. Ketubot proves this in the case where another person forces terumah into someone's throat: even though he did not voluntarily damage anything, he can be liable for the benefit to his body. One cannot answer that he would otherwise have fasted. Eating itself is a benefit. Priestly gifts are exceptional because they are money without a particular claimant. Just as one who destroys them is not liable to an individual kohen, ordinary benefit from consuming them is likewise not collected. Liability arises only where the person's existing assets were directly preserved, such as when the king took the priestly gifts instead of taking other grain. That is a concrete enrichment remaining in his property. Hence Tosafot's "I might have fasted" answers only the claim of preserved assets, not the existence of bodily benefit. In an ordinary person's food, where a claimant exists, the eater must pay for the benefit.
