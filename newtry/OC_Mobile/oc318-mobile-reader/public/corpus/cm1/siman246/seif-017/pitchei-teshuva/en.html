Noda BiYehudah, second edition, end of siman 34, writes that a claim for reimbursement of food provided to an orphan is among the obscure areas of law, with sources that appear to conflict. He therefore rules that where there is no clear proof that the administrator intended the support as charity, and the administrator is in possession of the orphan's assets, he may swear that he intended to be repaid and deduct the cost of the food, the matter depending on the judge's assessment of his intent when he provided it. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, responsum 147, likewise discusses one who supported a minor orphan, later seized the orphan's property, and demanded reimbursement. Ordinarily, one who feeds another person without stipulation is entitled to payment, as appears from the early and later authorities. But regarding an orphan, Baal HaTerumot, citing Rif, rules, and Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah siman 253 seif 5, codifies, that when the orphan grows up he need not repay unless the provider expressly said it was a loan. Beit Yosef and Ramah indicate that even if the orphan owned assets, the presumption is that the support was a gift. The statement in Choshen Mishpat siman 290 seif 25 that children who attached themselves to a householder and were supported from his money must reimburse him concerns one who undertook all their needs and thus had the status of an administrator. Although Terumat HaDeshen appears to hold that one who supports an orphan may collect at least where the orphan had assets, he apparently had not seen Rif's responsum, which Rabbeinu Yerucham and Beit Yosef quote without qualification and which Shulchan Aruch establishes as law. Accordingly, later seizure does not ordinarily help. Beit Yosef, Choshen Mishpat siman 128, says that if the orphan was a small child, even if he then had nothing of his own, he should be liable because one could not expect the provider to stipulate with a child. Rabbi Akiva Eiger explains that this cannot mean every minor, since Rif himself discusses a minor. It means a child below the age at which commercial acts have legal effect. In his case the orphan had already reached that age, so the provider could not collect by seizure. Indeed, even support given before that age may have been lost if the provider continued supporting him afterward without making any condition, because the later gratuitous support reveals that the earlier support was likewise intended as a gift. This is especially so where wealthy people in that city customarily support orphaned relatives gratuitously. If, however, the provider seized without witnesses, he may perhaps be believed by migo that the support was a loan; the presumption of gift may not be so absolute as to constitute a migo against witnesses. Rabbi Akiva Eiger leaves this point requiring study. Pitchei Teshuva adds that Rabbi Akiva Eiger's statement that seizure in the presence of witnesses does not help refers to seizure after the support was already provided. If, while supporting the orphan, the provider already held the orphan's property, even openly, he may deduct his expenditures. Beit Yosef, siman 128, expressly says that Rif's ruling applies only when the provider did not then hold the orphan's property against his claim; if he did hold it, he may plainly deduct what he spent, for we do not presume in such a case that he supported him as an act of kindness. Rabbi Akiva Eiger did not mention this because his case involved seizure only after the support. Chavot Yair, siman 134, discusses a widow who lived for years with one of her sons and had cash and securities in a chest in the room he provided. The son later demanded reimbursement for her support, while his mother or siblings claimed he had maintained her as a mitzvah. He distinguishes several cases. If the mother had no property, a son is not technically obligated to support her from his own funds, since the law is that honoring parents is paid from the parent's money, but where one son nevertheless took her into his home and maintained her, he plainly acted as a mitzvah beyond the strict law. Even if she later inherited property, he cannot charge her or her heirs for what he did while she was poor. If, however, she possessed her own funds while he supported her, she must pay when he demands it even without an express agreement, no worse than any other person who benefits from another's support. The rule that one who supports an orphan is presumed to intend a gift is different, because one naturally wants to preserve the orphan's funds until adulthood. A mother ordinarily remains indefinitely with the child she joins, and where she has means there is neither charity nor special kindness in paying her expenses from his pocket, especially as against other heirs. If, however, when she came he knew she intended to stay only temporarily and would later support herself, the case resembles supporting an orphan and she need not reimburse him even after death. Where the assets remain in the supporting son's possession, his claim is stronger; at minimum, doubt is resolved in favor of the possessor. Even if siblings or outsiders produce gift or debt instruments from the mother, the son may retain what is in his hands, subject to a heseit oath that he holds none of her property beyond the contents of her chest and that she did not repay his food expenses. Claims that the mother served as a maid in exchange for support ordinarily fail. Even if she helped rear children, wash, spin, prepare produce, or mend clothing, elderly women commonly help in the household where they live even when they pay for their own food, and such assistance is not presumed to be compensation for maintenance.
