All those who give away all their property, when the reason disappears, etc. This refers, for example, to the cases at the beginning of the siman: one who heard that his son died, one who needed to flee from enemies, or a dangerously ill person who gave away all his property and afterward recovered. If the son returns or proves alive, if he makes peace with his enemies, or if the dangerously ill person recovers, the gift is revoked. Nevertheless, the recipient need not return produce already consumed. The Tur, quoting the Rambam, says specifically "the produce they ate." The Mechaber omits "they ate," which implies that even produce still intact in the recipient's possession need not be returned once it was detached and taken into his possession. The Rambam explains the reason: even one who gives an explicit gift on condition that it be returned after a fixed period may eat the produce until that time. The rule requiring return of produce applies where the original gift was made in error and the donor regrets it retroactively. Here the donor does not regret the arrangement during the period in which the triggering circumstance remained. On the contrary, while he remained ill, while the son was presumed dead, or while he remained in danger, he wanted the recipient to hold the property and enjoy its produce. Therefore this is no worse than an express temporary gift.
Eat with me, etc. All of this is from Terumat HaDeshen siman 317. The first rule is cited there in the name of a Gaon, who proves it from the law of one who says, "Break my jug," or "Tear my garment," where permission to do the act does not necessarily waive payment for the resulting loss. Regarding the second rule, that a son in law must pay his father in law for his own food, Terumat HaDeshen writes that this applies even if the father in law is wealthy. He proves it from the rule that one who lives in another's courtyard can owe rent even where the owner did not ordinarily rent it, if the case is one where the resident benefited and the owner suffered a loss. Although where the occupant lived there with the owner's knowledge the rule differs if the owner suffered no loss, here the father in law actually expended food and therefore suffered a loss. Terumat HaDeshen further writes that even if the father in law had customarily supported earlier sons in law at his table for two years or more, that does not prove a gift to this one. A person may say, "I wished to give to your brother, but I do not wish to give to you," even if the later relative is in some respect more deserving. Where circumstances clearly prove that the support was intended as a gift, however, the recipient is exempt, because once it initially came to him through waiver the giver cannot later retract. Regarding support of the recipient's wife, he is exempt from reimbursing the father in law. This follows the rule that one who supports another man's wife while the husband is overseas loses his money. Mordechai in Maharam's name writes that this applies whether the supporter is her father or another person. See Yoreh Deah siman 253, where some distinguish whether the husband went overseas or remained locally with his wife.
