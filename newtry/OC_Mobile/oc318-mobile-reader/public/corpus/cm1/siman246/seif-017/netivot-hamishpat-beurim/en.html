Eat with me, he must pay. See Shach, seif katan 9, who questions this because the law is learned from the case "tear my garment," and there, if the object never came into the person's hands as a custodian, he is exempt. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that a damager is exempt where the owner authorized the damage and the object never came to him as a custodian because the Torah did not impose tort liability for damage occurring with the owner's permission, where the owner himself caused the loss to come about. Shitah Mekubetzet similarly explains the exemption from a fine in the case of a seduced woman even though the fine cannot simply be waived: the damage arose through her participation. If, however, the object had already come into his hands as a custodian, the existing liability cannot be removed by mere words, as Mordechai writes concerning a loan converted into an iska, cited at the beginning of siman 176. Here, by contrast, "eat with me" creates liability not as a damager but because he received benefit. Benefit liability applies even when the owner authorized the act. This is why in siman 363, "live with me," where the person is not one who ordinarily rents, he is exempt because he received no compensable benefit. Ketzot's answer is correct. Maharit asks why the eater must pay, since he can say, "I would have fasted," as Tosafot say in Chullin 137 concerning one who consumed gifts due to kohanim. Ketzot's answer there is also entirely correct. For benefit, eating unquestionably counts as benefit. He cannot say he would have fasted, because he nevertheless received nourishment. Rashi in Ketubot 30 says that even if another person forced food into his throat, so that he is not liable as a damager because the act was against his will, he is still liable for bodily benefit. He cannot say, "I would have fasted." Priestly gifts are different because they are property with no specific claimant. Therefore one who merely damages them is exempt, and even one who benefits from them is exempt from ordinary benefit liability. He is liable only where the value remains presently embedded in his estate, as in the case where the king took his threshing floor in payment of a debt. Had the tithe not been there, the king would have taken something else, and the alternative property he saved remains in his possession. Tosafot therefore ask that eating too should produce retained value, because otherwise he would have had to spend money on food, and that money remains in his house. They answer that he can say, "I would have fasted," meaning there is no presently retained monetary saving in his estate. But there certainly was benefit. Accordingly, with priestly gifts benefit alone is insufficient because there is no claimant, while with another person's ordinary property benefit alone creates liability.
