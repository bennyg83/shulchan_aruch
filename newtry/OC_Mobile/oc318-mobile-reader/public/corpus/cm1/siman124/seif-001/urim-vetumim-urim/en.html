The defendant cannot appoint an authorized representative. Tur explains the reason: the judges should not hear false arguments through an interpreter. The defendant himself is presumed not to have the brazenness to state a false claim in the presence of the person to whom he is liable. This is why one who denies everything is exempt from a Torah oath. That restraint is weaker when he speaks through an agent. A claimant, however, may appoint an authorized representative, and moreover he transfers his monetary claim to the representative, who then stands in his place. Sema's final answer is correct; the Shach agrees, and I agreed in Tumim. Rambam too must be understood this way.
And he remains at home. Sema writes that even a claimant was permitted to appoint a representative only when the claimant remains at home and sends the representative to beit din with his authority. If the claimant himself is also present in beit din, he may not argue through an advocate but must speak personally, as Tur and Mechaber state above in siman 17. Sema there explains that our present practice of hearing claims through interpreters is based on the parties' acceptance of that procedure; once accepted, no objection remains. The Shach writes that from this arose the widespread custom to allow both claimants and defendants to appear through representatives even without a formal authorization document, because it is treated as accepted by the parties. This is correct. Knesset HaGedolah cites Mishpetei Shmuel that even if the claimant agrees, the court should not permit it because hearing through an interpreter is itself prohibited. Nevertheless the principal law follows Sema. See Tumim, where I brought a clear proof from Ran. I also wrote above in siman 17 against Knesset HaGedolah, and at the end of siman 75 I collected many laws concerning authorized representatives. The Shach further cites Rivash that if the defendant's representative makes an admission, it is a complete admission, unlike Maharashdam. This is correct, although in Tumim I explained that Rivash's words themselves require careful analysis. The end of siman 75 also supports the same point. I have seen communities where both claimant and defendant appear through representatives and sometimes neither litigant is personally before beit din. When the matter reaches direct factual contradiction, however, the court does not rely on the representatives and requires the litigants themselves to appear. This is a proper custom.

Sema writes that this special accommodation was stated only for those whose Torah is truly their occupation, because concern for their honor justifies arranging that they need not personally litigate with an unlearned person. See above, siman 7:6. The Shach questions the current practice. Yoreh Deah 243 states that one who conducts business only as needed for livelihood and immediately returns to learning is still considered one whose Torah is his occupation. Why then do we now require Torah scholars to come personally to beit din? He answers that this is like communal taxation, which depends on accepted custom. This is strained.
