The defendant. Darkei Moshe, Sema, and Bach write that even a plaintiff may argue through an agent only when he remains at home and sends the agent to beit din. If the plaintiff himself is present, he must argue personally, as Rambam and Tur state in siman 17. Current practice nevertheless allows a plaintiff to appear together with another who argues for him, even over the defendant's objection, while the defendant is generally not allowed the same arrangement. The correct explanation is that there is a difference between a mere interpreter and one whom the litigant grants legal power and authority. The latter is treated as a litigant and may speak. A plaintiff can ordinarily grant such authority to the person standing with him. A defendant cannot appoint an authorized agent even when absent, and therefore cannot do so merely by standing beside another in court. The earlier statements prohibiting a plaintiff present in court from appointing an agent apply where it is known that he has not actually granted the representative power and authority.

Whose Torah is his occupation. As explained in Yoreh Deah, beginning of siman 243, this means that he has another occupation only enough to support himself, while the remainder of his time is devoted to Torah. Such a Torah scholar may appoint an agent because compelling him to suspend his learning is a burden. The same applies to a person of high standing whose personal appearance would be degrading, according to the circumstances and the judgment of beit din.
