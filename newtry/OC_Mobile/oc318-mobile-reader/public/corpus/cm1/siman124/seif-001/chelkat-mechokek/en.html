However, regarding something that must be destroyed from the world, etc. I do not know why he writes categorically that the get is void. Ran stated this rule only according to the opinion that anything destined to be burned is considered already burned. Rabbi Shimon is the one who holds this, as stated in Menachot 102, following his general view that anything destined to be thrown is considered as though already thrown. We do not rule like Rabbi Shimon. Rambam, Hilchot Pesulei HaMukdashin 4, rules regarding an asham talui that if the person learned of his status only after the blood was sprinkled, the meat is eaten by the Kohanim. According to the view that what is destined to be sprinkled is treated as already sprinkled, even blood still in the cup would suffice. See Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah, Keritot chapter 6, although Rashi's explanation in Menachot there sounds otherwise. The Bach writes that although the Gemara says that a get written on prohibited-benefit material is valid, which sounds only after the fact, and Tosafot explicitly write that one may not do so initially because he benefits from something prohibited for benefit, nevertheless he thinks the main law is that one may do so initially, since his intention is not to derive benefit but only to write a get on it. His words are astonishing. What greater benefit could there be than using an object from which benefit is prohibited for one's own need, while also saving the money he would otherwise spend on parchment? This is no better than slaughtering a dangerously ill animal with a knife of idolatry, which is forbidden. There is also no greater benefit than removing a troublesome wife from one's house and thereby curing oneself of that affliction. The implication of Tur and Rambam that one may "write" on such material initially means only that there is no intrinsic prohibition affecting the validity of the get itself. This resembles what the Rosh writes at the beginning of Shabbat concerning the phrase "exempt and permitted": although there may be a separate prohibition of placing a stumbling block before the blind, there is no Shabbat prohibition. The matter is clear.
