According to the Rambam, however, the rule that the Sanhedrin may not hear testimony through an interpreter applies even where only one litigant requires interpretation, and both claimant and defendant are included. The truth is as stated afterward and as the Shach explains: a claimant can transfer part of the claim to an authorized agent, thereby making him a litigant in his own right, since his claim can even require witnesses to bring a korban shevuah. For a defendant, however, there is no comparable authorization mechanism. If the opposing party accepts the representative, the Sema above in siman 17 and the Shach here in seif katan 1 rule that it is permitted. From this developed the widespread custom that both claimant and defendant may appear through representatives even without a formal authorization document, because this is treated as an acceptance by the parties. The Knesset HaGedolah cites Mishpat Shmuel as holding that even if the claimant agrees, we do not listen to him because the prohibition against hearing through an interpreter and judging on that basis remains. The Sema's view is primary. Otherwise, the Gemara's statement regarding the Kohen Gadol, that he should appoint an antelar, is difficult. The Ran answers that this is permitted because of the honor of the Kohen Gadol, but how could honor permit an actual prohibition? We do not honor a person at the expense of Torah law. According to the explanation above, however, once the litigant accepts the arrangement it is permitted. The claimant certainly agrees that the Kohen Gadol should not need to stand personally, because the Torah commands all Israel to honor him through "you shall sanctify him." We can therefore compel the claimant to waive his objection, rendering it an accepted arrangement and permitted. The Shach in seif katan 4 writes that if the defendant's representative admits the claim, the admission is fully binding, as the Rivash rules in siman 392, contrary to Maharshadam. The Shach is correct that we should follow the Rivash, but the Rivash's own words require clarification. The Rivash writes that because the Yerushalmi, when asking why the Kohen Gadol cannot appoint a representative, does not answer that an admission by the representative would be ineffective, but instead answers regarding the representative's oath in place of the Kohen Gadol, it follows that the agent's admission binds the principal. Yet the Rivash qualifies this as applying only where the principal appointed the representative before witnesses and accepted before witnesses that the representative's admission should be equivalent to his own. Presumably "before two" is not literal. One witness, together with the representative, could suffice because the representative can become a witness, giving two witnesses total. If the representative is a relative, however, two independent witnesses are needed. Even so, the matter requires clarification where the representative admits a definite sum and it is known that the principal did not instruct him to admit, but the representative acted on his own because he believed the claimant was right. The principal then protests: "Who authorized you to admit? I sent you to improve my position, not to impair it. Why did you admit matters I never placed in your mouth?" Why can he not make that claim? If we say that by the original appointment he accepted every possible admission the representative might make, regardless of its content, such acceptance should require a kinyan, because he undertakes liability for something he presently does not owe merely on the representative's future statement. Even with a kinyan I am uncertain whether this would not be an asmachta, since he never contemplated that the representative would admit something the principal had not authorized. If there is a factual dispute, with the representative saying, "You instructed me to admit this," and the principal denying it, the representative is only one witness. Yet the principal accepted him as reliable, which is equivalent to accepting one witness as two, as explained above in siman 22, and he is believed. But according to this, the representative must be qualified as a witness. If he is a relative or otherwise disqualified, we already explained above in siman 22 in the Rama that where there are two defects, namely accepting a disqualified witness and accepting him as two, a kinyan is required. See also the Shach there, who notes that even accepting one qualified witness as two, many poskim require the acceptance to be before beit din; otherwise a kinyan is necessary. The same would apply here. This is not a mere kinyan devarim, as Maharshadam thought, because the principal is validating the representative, who is otherwise only a single witness, to be believed like two when he says that every admission he made was actually authorized by the principal. The case of the Kohen Gadol proves nothing, because the Gemara may well be asking why he cannot appoint an antelar and make a kinyan accepting him as credible regarding every matter. These laws therefore require clarification. Here in our community the custom is for both claimant and defendant to appear through representatives, and sometimes neither litigant personally stands before beit din. But when the matter reaches a direct factual contradiction, we do not rely on the representatives. The litigants themselves must appear before beit din so that the denial can be made in the other's presence. This is a proper custom.
