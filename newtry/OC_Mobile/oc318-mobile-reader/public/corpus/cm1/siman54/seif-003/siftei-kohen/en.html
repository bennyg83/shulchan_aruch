[" The law favors the borrower. He need not deposit the money even into the hands of beit din. So writes Responsa Rashba there:"]
[" He must immediately pay what he admits and receive a receipt. Agudat Ezov, p. 79d, questions the Rama’s ruling, arguing that while the original document still exists one should not write a receipt, and that even Rashba, in the printed responsa, siman 930, did not state the rule with certainty. His objections are not compelling. Ir Shushan also writes that the Rama’s ruling is reasonable. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if the payment can be recorded on the document itself or between its lines, it should be written there, and the creditor cannot object:"]
[" Even so, a receipt is effective. The clause does not literally mean that the receipt must be written upon the document itself, as is clear below in siman 71, seif 5, gloss, and from Maharik himself. This is contrary to Ir Shushan, who explains that a receipt is effective because all such clauses merely strengthen documents, and therefore concludes that if the document explicitly says no receipt is effective unless written upon the document itself, an external receipt would fail. Ir Shushan himself below in siman 71 writes in accordance with my explanation. See there:"]
[" When a receipt is written, etc. Responsa Maharan Sasson, siman 145, writes that an antedated receipt is valid, even when intentionally dated early and even without the creditor’s consent. This is obvious, and there was no need for his lengthy dialectic and concluding that the matter still required study. How could a receipt intended for the borrower’s benefit be invalid merely because its early date weakens the borrower rather than the creditor? See below in siman 65, seif 18:"]
[" If they remember the date of the document, etc. This implies that initially the receipt should identify the date of the document, stating that it applies to the document dated such and such. Although after the fact a receipt remains valid even if it does not mention the document’s date, as above in siman 43, seif 24, when the date is remembered it should initially be written in the receipt. This is also implied by the Gemara and the poskim. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 31, end of part 1, and Shaar 56, part 1, has difficult wording on this point. It further appears that if it is known that the witnesses knew the document’s date but nevertheless wrote an undated receipt, without the creditor’s knowledge, the receipt is invalid because they improperly weakened the creditor’s rights. This resembles a postdated receipt, which I explain below in seif katan 11 is invalid:"]
[" They should write it without specification, etc., and should not write a date in the receipt, etc. This implies that they need not notify the creditor that they are writing an undated receipt, even though it weakens his position, because the creditor himself knows that this is what they will do when they do not know the date of the original document. Accordingly, if he knew the document’s date at the time of payment but the witnesses afterward forgot it, they may not write an undated receipt without the creditor’s knowledge. We must be concerned that he may later lend the borrower the same amount, relying on the fact that the receipt will refer to the earlier document of a known date, while the borrower will then produce the broadly worded undated receipt and improperly defeat the later loan. Nor should the witnesses solve this by writing the current date and saying the payment related to a document dated before that date. There may be another debt document whose date also precedes that day, and the creditor may have omitted mentioning it because he expected the receipt to identify the specific document whose date was known when payment occurred. There is therefore no remedy until they inform the creditor that they intend to write an undated receipt:"]
[" And they should not write a date in the receipt, etc. Then the document is defeated regardless of whether its date is earlier or later than the receipt, because the receipt itself is undated. The creditor must therefore be careful not later to lend this borrower an amount equal to the debt reflected in the receipt, because the receipt will undermine that later document; if he nevertheless lends the same amount, he has caused his own loss. Rashbam and Baal HaTerumot write this. It appears that when Rashbam says the creditor should not lend again ‘the amount written in the receipt,’ he means the amount written in the document, because in the case discussed the amount of the receipt equals the amount of the document. There is a further reason for his wording: it appears from Rashbam that a receipt need not state that it refers to a document of a particular principal amount at all; it may simply say that a certain sum was paid. If that sum equals the amount of the document, the entire document is paid. The Tur also agrees that language such as ‘So and so received payment from so and so in the amount of such and such’ is sufficient, as is implied above in siman 43, seif 24, and below at the end of seif 5. The present practice of writing that the receipt applies to a document of a certain amount exists only to allow the creditor safely to lend again in a different amount or where the creditor holds other documents of different amounts. Thus, if the debtor paid half the debt, for example the document is for one hundred and he paid fifty, and the receipt says he paid fifty on a document for one hundred, the creditor must avoid later lending him another one hundred, the amount stated in the document; he need not avoid a later loan of fifty merely because fifty is the amount stated in the receipt. Responsa Maharan Sasson, siman 145, writes that a postdated receipt is invalid, comparing it to a predated document because perhaps the creditor later extended another loan after the actual forgiveness. The analogy is not exact. A predated debt document is invalid even when made with the debtor’s consent because it may improperly seize property from purchasers. Here, if the receipt was postdated with the creditor’s consent it is valid, for otherwise an undated receipt would also have to be invalid. Nevertheless, his ruling is correct that a receipt intentionally postdated without the creditor’s knowledge is invalid. It resembles postdated sale documents discussed above in siman 43, seif 13, and below at the end of siman 239, which are invalid. There is also a direct concern that the creditor may hold another similar document dated after the original debt but before the falsely late date on the receipt. He may not have disclosed that second document to the receipt witnesses because he expected them to write the receipt at its true date for the specifically known original document. Accordingly, the receipt is invalid only when it is established that the witnesses knew the date of the original debt document and deliberately postdated the receipt without the creditor’s knowledge. Otherwise we do not invalidate it merely because its date is later. We presume that either the creditor and witnesses did not know the relevant date, or that the later date was written with the creditor’s consent:"]
