There is one who says that we do not heed him. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 38, section 1, derives this rule from Ketubot 16: where a woman says her ketubah was lost, the Gemara treats it like a case where she concealed it in our presence. This suggests that if the instrument is actually being concealed and could be returned, she cannot demand payment merely by offering a receipt. Gidulei Terumah objects that the sugya appears to prove the opposite. The baraita says that if the ketubah was burned, lost, or concealed, and she has proof, she receives two hundred. The Gemara initially derives from this that receipts are written. Rav Pappa answers that in truth receipts are not written, and the case is where ketubot are ordinarily not written but this woman had one. The Gemara asks: what does it mean that it was "lost"? It answers: it was lost in a fire. It then asks from the clause "concealed" and explains that an ordinary loss is treated like concealment before us. According to the opinion that receipts are written, however, the clause of concealment posed no difficulty at all. This appears to show that even if the instrument is still in her hands, she can collect by giving a receipt. There is nevertheless no contradiction to Baal HaTerumot. The Gemara's phrase, "a lost ketubah is like one concealed before us," would be pointless if concealment and loss were literally identical in every respect. According to the view that receipts are not written, the loss must be one that genuinely destroyed the possibility of returning the instrument, such as loss in a fire. But if she merely concealed it and now cannot remember where, a receipt may still be written according to the opinion that ordinarily writes receipts. Raah and Ritva to Ketubot state explicitly that if the instrument is intentionally concealed and available, everyone agrees it must be returned. The baraita can therefore be discussing a form of "concealment" where she herself no longer knows where she placed it. Such concealment still prevents return in practice and can justify a receipt. Gidulei Terumah apparently did not have Raah and Ritva before him. There is, however, a significant contrary view in Tosafot Bava Metzia 17b. Tosafot say that even if the ketubah document remains in the woman's hands and the husband demands its return because he fears losing his receipt, she is not required to return it. They reason that even without the ketubah she can later collect under the rule that one who pleads payment against an obligation established by beit din is not believed. The husband therefore must keep the receipt in any event. Although possession of the ketubah may remove a possible migo such as "I never betrothed you," Tosafot do not require her to surrender it merely because of that remote concern. Tosafot appear to infer this from the very point raised by Gidulei Terumah: the Gemara did not challenge the view that receipts are written from the clause of concealment. I found that Raah himself raises a related difficulty from Bava Metzia concerning a found receipt. The Gemara worries that perhaps the woman wrote the receipt in Nissan but did not deliver it until Tishrei, and in the interim sold her ketubah. If she now returns a receipt dated Nissan, purchasers could be harmed because the receipt would appear to predate their acquisition. The Gemara answers that this proves Shmuel's rule that the assignor of a debt can later forgive it, or, according to Abaye, establishes the case where the ketubah remains in the woman's possession. But if the ketubah did not remain with her, Abaye would indeed fear that it had been sold. This creates a problem with the Mishnah that says a woman who produces a get but no ketubah can collect: if she does not possess the ketubah, perhaps she already sold it and purchasers will later collect from the husband. Tosafot therefore understand "she has no ketubah with her" to mean she does not present it to beit din, but it is known that the document remains in her possession. That removes concern that she sold it, yet she still collects by receipt without returning it. This strongly supports Tosafot's view. Rashba in responsa 1010 and 1013 cites Baal HaTerumot's rule but says he would state it as halachah and not necessarily as practical ruling, indicating some uncertainty. It would be strained to say Tosafot discuss only R. Yochanan's personal reason for writing receipts, while our final halachah follows the Gemara's policy rationale that otherwise "if the lender's document is lost, shall the debtor rejoice?" The latter rationale would indeed be weaker where the note remains available. But R. Yochanan surely also accepts the general logic, and Abaye ultimately accepts the receipt regime after Rava's answer. Tosafot's position therefore cannot easily be dismissed. The better distinction appears to be this. Where returning the instrument would completely protect the debtor and no receipt is otherwise needed, the debtor may insist on return. If his receipt were later lost, he would otherwise be exposed to a repeat claim, while the creditor can simply surrender the already-paid document. This is the situation of an ordinary loan note. The rationale, "should the debtor rejoice because the creditor's note was lost," does not apply where the creditor can readily return the note. A ketubah is different according to Tosafot because return of the document does not fully protect the husband. Even if she gives back the ketubah, she may later sue based on witnesses to the marriage and the rule that an obligation established by beit din cannot be defeated by a payment claim. The husband therefore must take a receipt in any event. Once he must safeguard a receipt regardless, Chazal did not burden the woman with both returning the ketubah and issuing a receipt merely to guard against the remote circumstance that the receipt is lost and she then finds witnesses enabling a second claim. The rule that the borrower is servant to the lender applies. According to our halachah, however, we do not follow Tosafot on this point. We follow Rif that in a place where ketubot are normally written, if she does not possess the ketubah she cannot collect merely by relying on the underlying marriage. Thus a ketubah returns to the same legal category as an ordinary note: once she returns the document, no separate receipt is necessary. Accordingly, she should be required to return it. Mechaber in Even HaEzer also does not bring Tosafot's contrary opinion. Still, if the note is in another city and bringing it would impose significant hardship on the creditor, while delay creates a real risk to collection, all should agree that beit din may write a receipt. The creditor should then undertake before beit din to return the original note to the debtor when it comes into his possession. Responsa Rashba 930 implies this, and it is the proper practical rule.
When writing a receipt, and so forth. Responsa Mahara Sasson rules that an antedated receipt is valid, even if written without the debtor's request and even deliberately antedated. Shach calls this obvious: how could one invalidate a receipt merely because it was dated earlier, when that only weakens the creditor and benefits the debtor? To me this is astonishing. Bava Metzia asks concerning a found receipt: why return it? Perhaps it was written in Nissan for delivery then, but was not delivered until later, and in the meantime the creditor sold the debt. The Gemara answers that this proves Shmuel's rule that an assignor can forgive a debt even after assignment, or Abaye answers that the ketubah remains in the woman's hands. Tosafot ask that perhaps she sold the ketubah through ma'amad sheloshtan, where later forgiveness would not work. Maharshal similarly asks that perhaps she later became indebted to others under R. Natan's rule, where she could no longer forgive. Their answers depend on special features of ketubah law. Those answers do not apply to an ordinary loan note. Suppose the creditor sells the note in Iyar through a mode that prevents later forgiveness, or becomes indebted in Iyar to another creditor whose claim attaches under R. Natan. He is actually paid by the borrower only in Sivan. If the witnesses deliberately write the receipt with a Nissan date, they destroy the purchaser's or later creditor's rights, making it appear that the debt was already discharged before the intervening assignment or lien arose. This is precisely the sort of harm Chazal feared in refusing to return a found early-dated receipt. What difference is there between returning such a receipt and witnesses intentionally creating one? Therefore the rule that a deliberately antedated receipt is always valid requires study. Its principal place is below in siman 65, seif 18, where I discuss it at length.
A receipt, and so forth. The Gemara says that at first they instituted that when a document was postdated, the note should state explicitly, "this document was postdated." Later, when scribes were no longer accustomed to add that phrase, they instituted instead that receipts be written without a date. Why did Chazal not simply enact that loan documents may never be postdated? Why all the elaborate arrangements of special wording in the note or undated receipts? The answer appears to be that sometimes a kinyan is made in Nissan and the creditor expects the borrower to repay within a few days. He does not wish immediately to trouble witnesses and a scribe or burden the borrower with the expense of drafting a full note. When he later sees that payment is not forthcoming, he returns to the witnesses and asks them to write the document, but they may no longer remember the exact date of the original kinyan. They must then write from the current date, creating a valid postdated instrument. Chazal therefore could not prohibit all postdating. They instead regulated the issue through the wording of notes and receipts. This resolves Gidulei Terumah's question: if Chazal instructed scribes to state in a postdated loan note that it was postdated, why did they not make the same enactment for sale deeds and thereby eliminate the concern that perhaps the seller later repurchased the property? The answer is that in sales there is no parallel practical need to postdate. A purchaser does not ordinarily acquire land on the understanding that perhaps a deed will be written only much later if needed. Even where there is a conditional arrangement, undoing or renewing a sale generally requires a new transaction; mere cancellation of the earlier kinyan does not recreate the same situation. Chazal could therefore simply prohibit postdating sale deeds, and no special notation system was required.
If they remember, and so forth. Shach writes that if the witnesses know the date of the original note, they should not write an undated receipt. They should state both the date of the receipt and the date of the note, so that the creditor will not later be unable to make a new loan to the same borrower in the amount appearing in the receipt unless he gives additional notice. Shach says that if they fail to do so, the receipt is invalid. The first part is correct: it is preferable to identify the note's date when known, as the Gemara says, "If you know the date of the note, write it." But I see no basis for making this essential after the fact. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 56, section 1, law 3, writes explicitly at the end: "Every receipt should be written without specification, and whoever does otherwise causes his own loss." Shach calls this language awkward, but I see nothing awkward in it. It says plainly that the normal form of a receipt is undated. Tosafot Bava Batra 169b provide a decisive proof against Shach. They ask why, when a loan note is lost, we do not write a second note and protect the borrower by giving him a receipt. With a loan there is no concern that the receipt will harm a purchaser, because purchasers inquire of the borrower. Tosafot answer that if this were done, the creditor would lose the ability to make future loans, because later he might forget the old receipt and the borrower could use it against the new debt. That would discourage lending. Tosafot then ask why the receipt cannot say, "This applies only up to such-and-such date," and answer that perhaps we decree because scribes may mistakenly write an undated receipt. This is directly contrary to Shach's distinction between cases where the date is known and those where it is not. If knowing the note's date made it legally mandatory to specify the date in the receipt, there would be no reason for the decree. A scribe who mistakenly wrote an undated receipt would simply have produced an invalid receipt, and there would be no danger to the creditor. Nor can Shach say the concern exists only where the creditor was not informed. Tosafot's entire worry is that the creditor may forget; the danger remains even if he was once informed. The baraita there clearly concerns a note whose date is known, since the witnesses come and say, "We wrote this document," and the entire question is whether to recreate the instrument from its original date. The Gemara itself assumes knowledge of the date. Yet Tosafot still fear that an undated receipt could be written and be effective. This is a clear proof that the normal law of a receipt is to be undated even where the original note's date is known. At first glance one might support Shach from Bava Metzia. The Mishnah says that if a found receipt is acknowledged by the woman it may be returned to the husband. The Gemara asks: perhaps she wrote it in Nissan and delivered it only in Tishrei, while in the meantime she sold her ketubah. This concern yields the rule that an assignor can forgive after sale. If a person can arrange fraud by asking witnesses to write an undated receipt and then hand it over later, why does the Gemara focus on the dated found receipt at all? She could simply have told the witnesses that her ketubah was lost, obtained an undated receipt, and later used it to defeat purchasers. One might answer in Shach's favor that an undated receipt may be written only where there is genuine concern that the original note itself was postdated. If there is no such concern, the witnesses must write a dated receipt. A ketubah is not normally postdated. Indeed, according to Shach below in siman 115, seif katan 29, the standard after-acquired-property clause is not effective in a ketubah, and without such a clause a postdated ketubah would be invalid. Even apart from that view, postdating a ketubah is highly problematic because the marital lien does not begin until the written date. If a woman married in Nissan but the ketubah were dated Tishrei, she would spend the intervening months effectively without the required ketubah security, and marital relations would be treated as prohibited under the enactment of Shimon ben Shetach. Thus witnesses would know there is no legitimate reason to suspect a postdated ketubah and could require a dated receipt. This would also resolve Maharsha's question why the Gemara did not raise the same "perhaps it was written in Nissan and delivered in Tishrei" concern on the earlier Mishnah concerning found receipts generally. With ordinary documents there is a valid possibility of postdating, so the receipt regime is different; with a ketubah there is not. But this support for Shach is not decisive. As I explained above in siman 48, the chronology of the enactments matters. Bava Batra says that until the time of Rav Safra, receipts were written with dates, and postdated documents were valid only if the instrument explicitly stated that it had been postdated. Rav Safra later instructed scribes to write receipts without dates because scribes were no longer consistently writing the postdating notation. The baraita underlying the Bava Metzia discussion predates that later enactment. At that time a bare postdated note without explicit notation was invalid, and receipts ordinarily bore dates. Therefore, the Gemara's concern naturally focused on a dated receipt written earlier and delivered later. There is no proof that where the note's date is known, an undated receipt is invalid under our later practice. This historical point also explains Tosafot's question in Bava Batra. Their question is not necessarily directed against the old baraita's practice; it asks why, under the law as ultimately practiced, Chazal do not allow replacement loan notes protected by receipts. Their answer assumes our present system where receipts may be undated. The implication is again that even if the date of the note is known, the receipt can be written without date. Rosh in responsum klal 76, law 1, gives another strong proof. He rules that a receipt need not mention the date of the note or its witnesses. He proves this from the case where a person finds among his papers a receipt concerning a note of Yosef ben Shimon: both same-named notes are treated as paid. If receipts ordinarily specified the note's date and witnesses whenever known, such uncertainty would rarely arise; the receipt could simply be matched to the correct note. Gidulei Terumah asks why Rosh needed proof for omission of the date, since the Gemara expressly says, "If you know the note's date, write it; if not, write the receipt without specification." The answer is that Rosh's doubt is precisely Shach's doubt. The Gemara's phrase can be read as speaking only where the note's date is unknown, because perhaps the note itself was postdated. Rosh wanted to know whether, when the date is known and the note was not postdated, it becomes mandatory to state the date. He therefore brings independent proof from the two Yosef ben Shimons that the practice remains not to specify the date. A further way to understand Rosh is through Ramah's rule that the solution for two same-named people is to distinguish the original notes. If receipts likewise had to identify the note's date, the debtor could ordinarily be told which date to put in the receipt, and the problem of ambiguity would disappear. The fact that the Mishnah still treats a general receipt as affecting both notes shows that the standard receipt need not include the note's date even when those details are available. Chazal did not make distinctions and allowed the ordinary undated form. Rosh's proof therefore directly answers Shach. The rule is uniform: a receipt may be written without date even where the original document's date is known. It may be better practice to state the date, but omission does not invalidate the receipt. This also sheds light on Rif's position concerning one who pleads payment against an obligation established by beit din. Rif holds that in a place where ketubot are normally written, if the woman does not have the ketubah she cannot collect. Rosh questions where Rif derived this distinction. I believe it follows from the same receipt framework. In Ketubot the Gemara asks from the Mishnah, "She produced a get but no ketubah," and says this proves receipts are written. Tosafot ask: even if she returns the ketubah, the husband might still need to keep a receipt because she could later sue through witnesses to the marriage under the rule that one cannot plead payment against a beit din obligation. They answer through various migo distinctions, but the explanation is strained. Rif instead reads the Gemara more simply. If ketubot are not normally written, of course the husband must rely on a receipt because there is no document to return. If ketubot are normally written, however, returning the document should ordinarily suffice, and the husband should not have to bear permanent receipt risk. The Mishnah's statement that she can collect without the ketubah therefore must concern a place where ketubot are not written, unless some special explanation applies. This yields Rif's distinction. The same analysis explains the historical baraita of found receipts. In the period when receipts were dated, an antedated or delayed receipt created a real danger in a world of postdated documents. Later Chazal changed the form to undated receipts precisely to avoid that problem. Once that enactment was made, the undated form became standard, even where the note's date happened to be known. We do not fragment the institution case by case. One can still distinguish ketubah. Because a ketubah is never legitimately postdated in the ordinary way, perhaps its receipt should always bear a date. But even this is not certain, for Chazal may have standardized the receipt form across documents to avoid confusion. Rosh's rule and Tosafot's concern strongly indicate a uniform practice. There is also the question whether an undated receipt can defeat a later note whose due date has not yet arrived. If one insists on Shach's approach, perhaps not, because the creditor can say the receipt necessarily referred to an earlier debt. But once we establish that undated receipts are valid regardless of knowledge of the date, the borrower can use such a receipt against any note falling within its language unless the creditor proves it referred to another debt. This is the practical force of the enactment. For all these reasons, Shach's ruling that omission of the date invalidates the receipt when the witnesses know the note's date has no sufficient basis. The words of Tosafot and Rosh point clearly in the opposite direction.
A receipt that was written, and so forth. Gidulei Terumah, page 94, infers from a responsum of Rosh that if the borrower holds a general receipt and the creditor produces a note whose due date has not yet arrived, the receipt does not cancel that note because we have the presumption that a person does not pay within the term. Gidulei Terumah notes that most authorities do not appear to hold this way and that ordinarily the receipt cancels the note. He suggests a distinction: if the receipt contains language of waiver, it certainly cancels even a debt still within its term; but if it merely records payment, perhaps it does not, because we do not assume early payment. The correct view, however, is the simple implication of Shulchan Aruch and of Gidulei Terumah's own broader conclusion: a valid general receipt cancels the note even though the note is still within its contractual term. What Rosh meant in the cited responsum can be understood differently, as I explain below in siman 65. There is a clear proof from the enactment to write receipts without dates because perhaps the original note was postdated. Suppose the borrower actually borrowed in Nissan and the debt was due three months later. The witnesses then postdated the note to Tishrei. The borrower, unaware of that fact, pays in Tishrei and receives an undated receipt. In Cheshvan the creditor produces the note, which is dated Tishrei and says payment is due three months later. He even warns the borrower before beit din not to pay again except in court. If an undated receipt could never defeat a debt still within its stated term, then the borrower would be trapped. His genuine Tishrei receipt would not cancel the note because, on the face of the postdated document, the debt was not due until Tevet. Chazal's entire enactment of undated receipts would fail to protect against precisely the danger for which it was created. Therefore, a general receipt necessarily applies even to a note that appears still to be within its term. One could perhaps distinguish a case where the creditor proves there was another debt between them. If the receipt says generally that all debts were paid, but one of the creditor's notes is still within its term, the creditor might argue that the receipt referred to the other debt and that the within-term presumption creates an opening in his favor. But this distinction is strained and lacks a clear basis. Certainly where the creditor proves no separate debt, there is no foundation for excluding the within-term note from the receipt. The general rule should stand: the receipt cancels it.
