He should write him a receipt and pay him the entire debt. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 10, concerning Reuven, who lent Shimon money under a document in the borrower's own handwriting of the commercial form commonly called a mamram. The borrower signs his name in the middle of a sheet, and opposite the signature there is wording stating that the bearer of the document is entitled to a specified sum, payable on a specified date. Such a document has all the force of ordinary commercial documents, except that it is not a full debt document for collecting from encumbered property. It does collect from unencumbered property, even from heirs, and the borrower cannot claim payment if his signature can be authenticated. A person can transfer the document to another without formal written assignment or authorization, and whoever produces it may collect, all according to commercial custom. The document was later lost by Reuven, the lender, who sought payment from Shimon. Shimon replied that he feared another person might find it and later collect from him. The questioner related that this had happened many times, and judges customarily announced the identifying details of such a lost document, including its amount and due date, requiring anyone possessing it to appear before beit din within thirty days or lose his rights. They would then give the borrower a signed record of the announcement and require him to pay the original lender. See also Ateret Tzvi above, siman 50. Here, however, the borrower added that perhaps someone might later find the document and claim that he had not known or heard of the announcement because he was abroad or under compulsion, deny the original lender's account, and say that he himself had originally lent the borrower the money on this document, or that the original lender had transferred the document to him in payment of a debt, and then collect from the borrower. He therefore refused to pay Reuven unless Reuven provided security against any loss if he were forced to pay a second time. The lender argued that he was willing to give a receipt, as in every case of a lost debt document, and should have no further obligation. Tzemach Tzedek first analyzes whether a receipt is effective for a bearer document of this kind. Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Get Pashut, cited by the Rama in siman 50, writes that a receipt is effective for a document that does not name the lender. This is true, however, only where it is known that the document had previously been in the hands of the person giving the receipt. Since that person was then able to collect with it, he was its legal holder and could waive its lien, so his receipt is effective against anyone who later produces it. If it is not known that the document had ever been in his possession, and he merely comes to beit din with the borrower and says that he had such a document and lost it, then his receipt is certainly ineffective. Otherwise the borrower could collude with someone and fabricate such a claim. This is also implied by the wording of Nimukei Yosef. Accordingly, if it is known that the document was in the lender's possession, his receipt is effective and the borrower must pay him upon receiving the receipt, which will bind anyone who later produces the document. This is like any lost debt document, where the lender writes a receipt and the borrower pays. If it is not known that the document was ever in his possession, however, the receipt is ineffective, and the borrower need not pay until the lender accepts responsibility for any loss caused by the lost document, meaning that if another person later produces it and collects, the lender will reimburse the borrower. Although beit din presently announces that anyone possessing such a document must produce it and no one appears, there is still concern that someone may later produce it and prove that he was prevented from appearing, deny the borrower's account, claim that he originally lent the money, and collect. Therefore, the lender must accept responsibility. Tzemach Tzedek discusses this at length. See there. Responsa Givat Shaul, siman 25, addresses a similar case. There, after the announcement had already been made and beit din had given the borrower a signed record of it, while he was about to pay the lender, another person appeared with the mamram and sued the borrower, claiming that the borrower had borrowed from him on that document and that he had been away during the announcement. The borrower then refused to pay the original lender unless the latter gave security against a second payment. The lender argued that he need only give an ordinary receipt, especially since the borrower himself knew that this was the document the lender had lost, and the fact that an unscrupulous person found it and now sought improper payment should not create a further obligation. Givat Shaul rules that the borrower must pay the lender, who gives him a receipt identifying the mamram. Such a receipt is effective against anyone who later produces it, as follows from the Rama in siman 50. He disagrees with Tzemach Tzedek's limitation that a receipt is effective only if it is known that the lender had possessed the document. If we were concerned for collusion, the same concern could arise even where it is known that he once had such a document. Necessarily, we do not entertain such concerns. Even if one assumes that a receipt in this circumstance is effective only against the original lender and not against another holder, so that the borrower could suffer loss from a third party, all the more so in Givat Shaul's case where another person had already produced the document, the borrower should nevertheless pay and take a receipt. The reason is that we are more concerned for the lender's certain loss than for the borrower's possible loss, as Bava Batra 171 says: Abaye challenged the practice of writing receipts, asking whether if the borrower's receipt is lost the borrower should consume the lender's money and rejoice. Rava answered, 'A borrower is servant to the lender.' Tzemach Tzedek's distinction, that there the borrower's loss is uncertain while here it is certain, is rejected, because here too the borrower's loss is not certain. He can claim that he already repaid, and it is uncertain whether the holder of the document will swear and collect, as discussed in Shevuos 32b. Even if the document contains a credibility clause, the borrower can still require a hesset oath after payment. Since the lender's loss is certain and the borrower's is not, there is no reason for the borrower to consume the lender's money and rejoice. This is the substance of Givat Shaul's ruling. See there. Netivot HaMishpat at the end of siman 50 also disagrees with Tzemach Tzedek, but in the opposite direction from Givat Shaul. Tzemach Tzedek implied that once it is known that the bearer document had been in the lender's possession, his receipt remains effective even after the document is lost. Netivot HaMishpat holds that a receipt is effective only if witnesses testify that the document was still in the lender's possession at the time he wrote the receipt. Otherwise it is ineffective, because perhaps he had already sold the document, unless the witnesses saw it in his possession after the relevant time before payment. This is also implied by Nimukei Yosef. As to Tzemach Tzedek's further ruling that when such a bearer document is lost the borrower must pay if the lender gives security against any future loss from the document, Netivot HaMishpat questions the source. The Gemara provides only the remedy of writing a receipt. If no effective receipt can be written for this document, how do we know that a security arrangement can substitute? The borrower may say that the proposed security does not protect him sufficiently. See there. I found in Responsa Radbaz, part I, end of siman 193, a ruling like Tzemach Tzedek and unlike Givat Shaul. He adds that if the lender is not financially reliable, he must provide a guarantor or collateral. Radbaz writes that a debt of this form, where the document obligates payment to whoever produces it even without authorization, cannot be collected by a person who does not have the document in hand, even if he writes a receipt. The borrower can say: Perhaps you transferred the document to someone else, or perhaps it will fall and be found by another, and I will be unable to deflect him because the document expressly contains this bearer condition. Therefore, if the document is lost, the lender has no ordinary remedy. Nevertheless, if the lender is financially reliable, it is reasonable to compel the borrower to pay him while the lender accepts responsibility for any claim, damage, or loss that later results from this payment. If the lender is not financially reliable, he must provide a guarantor or collateral so that the borrower not lose his money while the lender consumes his funds and rejoices. This is the proper ruling. Shiltei Gibborim, seif katan 2, briefly cites Tzemach Tzedek and Radbaz and writes that although their proofs are not fully compelling, elder authorities have already ruled and we follow them. Responsa Nachalat David, siman 21, also discusses this matter at length, challenges all of Givat Shaul's arguments, and concludes that Tzemach Tzedek's ruling is correct without reservation. He further investigates where it is known that the mamram was lost by unavoidable accident, where the lender claims it was lost by accident and the borrower does not know and says perhaps not, and where the borrower claims with certainty that it was lost without accident. He concludes that if another person later finds this mamram and collects from the borrower, an ordinary receipt from the present lender will not protect the borrower, because we can say that perhaps it is another mamram or there was collusion, unless circumstances eliminate that concern, such as where the witnesses to the receipt recognize the document itself. Therefore, the borrower need not pay now until the lender writes that if the borrower is later forced to pay another person, the borrower may take a hesset oath that this was the same mamram and recover from the lender the amount he now paid. The lender need not reimburse more than the amount received now, even if the lost document was written for a larger amount and the borrower later pays the larger amount. If the lender presently claims that the document was lost by unavoidable accident, he need not give such an undertaking where witnesses establish the accident. He should merely give an ordinary receipt in case he himself later finds the document. If another person finds it and collects, the lender need not reimburse, because that loss is mere grama, no worse than a third party who improperly returns a document or the law of collateral in siman 72, seif 2. If there are no witnesses to the accident and the borrower says perhaps it was not lost by accident, the lender swears that it was and the borrower pays. If the borrower positively denies the accident, the borrower swears and is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed repayment, since the lender does not hold the document. See there at length. Responsa Chemdat Shlomo, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, was likewise asked about a person who gave his fellow a handwritten commercial obligation, with the amount written at the top of the sheet and the signature far below, as was common among merchants. During wartime disturbances the lender hid this debt document together with a bundle of documents in a sack in his cellar. Raiders later conquered the city, and afterward the lender searched the cellar and could not find the sack. The lender sought payment. The borrower admitted the debt but feared that someone might find the document and collect from him, and argued that a receipt from the lender would not help because no lender's name appeared on the document. Even a reimbursement undertaking, as suggested by Tzemach Tzedek, was inadequate because a finder could alter the amount, cut off the original text, or create other uncertainties as to whether the produced document was the same one. Chemdat Shlomo discusses the matter at length and challenges Tzemach Tzedek both regarding the effectiveness of a reimbursement undertaking, in light of Bava Batra 169b's concern that the borrower may meanwhile consume produce and the difficulty of restitution once consumed, and regarding Tzemach Tzedek's rejection of the local judges' custom of public announcement followed by requiring payment. That custom indicates that no concern is entertained that another person may later find the instrument, for otherwise beit din itself should raise the concern even if the borrower did not. Chemdat Shlomo explains that the entire dispute over whether receipts are written concerns fear that the same creditor will collect a second time and directly cause the borrower's loss. A mere concern that a third person might later find the instrument and collect does not provide a basis to burden the lender, because the lender does not even cause the loss by grama and has no duty of safekeeping toward the borrower. Therefore, the judges' custom is sound, and no reimbursement undertaking is required. The lender gives a receipt preventing himself from collecting again, while beit din's written record of the public announcement serves as the borrower's protective record. He concludes that in the case before him as well the borrower must pay without a reimbursement undertaking. He nevertheless raises a further concern that perhaps the lender had already sold the handwritten instrument. In ordinary cases where a receipt is written, we do not fear prior sale of the debt document because, as Shitah Mekubetzes in chapter 2 of Kesubos writes in the name of the Ra'ah, we rule that a seller of a debt document who later waives it effects a waiver. With a mamram, however, if the original lender's receipt does not bind another holder, perhaps prior sale must be feared. He suggests that the public announcement resolves this, because a legitimate purchaser would have appeared before beit din. In any event, in his case this concern was unnecessary, both because the borrower did not suspect the lender of having sold it and because the reason to suspect sale ordinarily is the defect that the lender no longer has the document. Here there had been wartime upheaval, so the lender's lack of possession was not itself suspicious. He concludes that if the parties would listen to him, it would be proper to compromise, because the borrower can rely upon the major authority Tzemach Tzedek. The borrower had told him that he was willing to pay in three or four installments, which he considered very appropriate. See there. In siman 2 of that work appears the response of Rabbi Akiva Eiger concerning the same matter. He initially writes that, simply understood, the Sages strengthened the borrower's protection to the extent that concern for his potential loss may cause the lender to lose, for according to the opinion that receipts are not written, the lender loses because of possible loss to the borrower. One could therefore argue that even Rabbi Yose, whose view we follow that receipts are written, disagrees only because a receipt provides a remedy and the borrower may be required to guard it on the principle that a borrower is servant to the lender. Where a receipt provides no remedy, as in this case, perhaps all agree that the lender bears the loss, even if the document was lost through unavoidable accident and the lender was not negligent, as seems implied at the beginning of chapter 2 of Kesubos. Rabbi Akiva Eiger then answers the author's objection to Tzemach Tzedek from Bava Batra, and discusses the author's novel distinction between concern that the lender himself will collect twice and concern that another person will find the document. In the end, he agrees with that distinction as practical law and rules that the borrower must pay the lender even without a reimbursement undertaking. Although the distinction is not compelled, since the source cases concern only fear that the original lender himself will collect again, and there is at least room for the distinction, we have no basis to extend the law to a new case involving only the possibility that a stranger will find the document. This is especially so because the questioner in Tzemach Tzedek testified that local judges customarily collected through public announcement. Although Tzemach Tzedek ruled that the lender must give a reimbursement undertaking, which would not fully protect the borrower in the present case because a finder could alter the instrument, nevertheless the very logic of requiring such an undertaking is difficult. Why should the borrower have to trust that an impoverished lender will later be able to reimburse him? Necessarily, Tzemach Tzedek meant only that beit din requires the lender to provide whatever protection is practically available, but not that inability to provide absolute protection prevents collection. Therefore, Rabbi Akiva Eiger likewise agrees that the lender should collect after public announcement where the borrower trusts that he did not already sell the document. End of the substance of his words. See there. The entire matter still requires careful study.
