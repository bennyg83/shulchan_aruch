If the receipt says "dinars," and so forth. See what I wrote at length above in siman 42. I have difficulty understanding Rosh's responsum, klal 108, siman 22. A creditor held a loan note. The borrower held the creditor's handwriting stating that he had received from him, as interest, all the produce from the date of the loan in a certain year until a specified date. Rosh ruled that these payments constituted fixed biblical interest and must be deducted from the note. If the borrower claims the produce was worth a large amount and the creditor says it was worth little, then if there are witnesses to the interest transaction, both parties are suspect concerning an oath and the creditor collects his note without swearing. If there are no witnesses and the only proof is the creditor's handwriting, so that the creditor is not formally disqualified, the creditor swears how much produce he received and then collects the balance. Why should the creditor collect? Above, where a receipt merely says "dinars" without stating the number, the receipt can cancel even a note for one thousand dinars; the creditor does not swear and collect. Here too the writing says simply that he received all the produce as interest. Why not say that the produce received equaled the entire debt and thus cancel the note? One must distinguish. Where the document is a receipt written against a specific note, the receipt directly weakens that note. Its ambiguous language is therefore interpreted against the creditor who issued it, up to the amount of the note. But where the creditor's handwriting is a separate document concerning an independent matter, such as a general acknowledgment that he owes Reuven money from another source, and the amount is not stated, we do not use it automatically to cancel a separate loan note. The independent writing is construed narrowly. So too in Rosh's case. The handwriting did not say that the produce was received as repayment of the principal note. It recorded a separate stream of produce received as interest. We now require the creditor to return that forbidden interest and set it off against the debt under the rules of fixed interest. This is conceptually like a separate debt the creditor owes the borrower, not a receipt discharging the note itself. Still, a difficulty remains. Even if it is an independent writing, above in siman 42 I explained that where the possessor has seized property, ambiguous language may sometimes be interpreted in his favor. Here the borrower already holds the entire amount claimed in the sense that he is withholding payment under the outstanding note. Why should he not be treated as the possessor and have the writing construed broadly? In the ordinary case of a separate acknowledgment saying merely, "I owe him money," one can answer through the rule, "if you seize too much, you have seized nothing." Since no upper bound appears in the writing, construing it expansively would have no natural limit; perhaps it means a whole treasure of dinars. Therefore we restrict it to the minimum. A receipt tied to a particular note has a natural maximum, namely the amount of that note. But that answer is not fully sufficient in Rosh's case. The quantity of annual produce is naturally bounded by reasonable agricultural value. No one assumes the borrower paid two hundred in interest on a principal of one hundred merely because the writing is broad. The ambiguity is therefore limited enough that the ordinary possession principles could arguably apply. Perhaps Rosh holds that the holder of a formal note is considered the possessor for these purposes. Even though the borrower physically retains the money, the creditor's validated document gives him the stronger legal status. Therefore the borrower cannot invoke ordinary seizure rules merely because he has not yet paid. I discuss this question in the general rules of possession. The matter requires further investigation in the responsa literature to see whether later authorities addressed this precise tension.
