He shall write him a receipt, and he shall pay him his entire debt. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 10, discusses a merchant's bearer note, known as a memrani, signed by the borrower and payable to whoever produces it. Such a note can be collected from the borrower and even from heirs, and when the signature is authenticated the borrower cannot claim payment. It may also be transferred without a formal deed of assignment or power of attorney, all according to commercial custom. The note was lost by the creditor, who sought payment. The borrower feared that another person might later find and collect it. The local judges had a practice of publicly announcing the identifying details of a lost note for thirty days, after which anyone who failed to appear would lose his rights, and they would give the borrower a signed certificate of the announcement. The borrower nevertheless argued that a later holder might prove that he had been outside the country or otherwise prevented from hearing the announcement, deny the first creditor's ownership, claim that he had originally lent the money on that very note, and collect. He therefore demanded security that the creditor reimburse him if he were forced to pay a second time. The creditor maintained that, as in every lost-document case, a receipt should suffice. Tzemach Tzedek first distinguishes whether it is known that this very bearer note had previously been in the creditor's possession. If that fact is known, his receipt is effective against every later producer, because he was then the holder entitled to collect and therefore could waive the lien. If it is not known that the note was ever in his possession, a receipt issued merely on his assertion that such a note was lost cannot bind an unknown later holder, for otherwise collusion would be possible. Accordingly, when prior possession is known, the borrower must pay upon receiving the creditor's receipt. When it is not known, the borrower need not pay until the creditor accepts responsibility for any loss caused by the lost note, namely that if another later produces it and collects, the creditor will reimburse the borrower. The public announcement does not remove every concern, because a later holder may prove that he was unavoidably absent and then assert independent ownership of the note. Givat Shaul, siman 25, was asked a similar case in which, after the announcement and after beit din gave the borrower a signed certificate, another person actually appeared with the bearer note and claimed that the borrower had borrowed from him and that he had been away during the announcement. Givat Shaul ruled that the borrower must nevertheless pay the original creditor upon receiving a receipt specifying the bearer note. He argues that such a receipt is effective against any later producer under the Rama in siman 50, and he rejects Tzemach Tzedek's limitation. He further reasons that even if a later holder could still cause loss, the creditor's certain loss is more immediate than the borrower's possible loss, paralleling Bava Batra 171, where Abaye objects that if receipts are written the borrower might lose his receipt, and Rava answers, 'A borrower is servant to the lender.' Moreover, the borrower's loss is not certain because he may later claim payment and require the holder to swear. Netivot HaMishpat at the end of siman 50 also disagrees with Tzemach Tzedek, but in the opposite direction. He holds that a receipt on a bearer note is effective only when witnesses saw that the note was actually in the creditor's possession at the time the receipt was written, or under circumstances eliminating the possibility that it had meanwhile been transferred. He also questions Tzemach Tzedek's ruling that an indemnity undertaking can compel payment, since the Gemara established only the remedy of a receipt, and if a receipt cannot be written for such a note the borrower may say that the offered security is not reliable enough for him. Radbaz, part 1, end of siman 193, agrees with Tzemach Tzedek against Givat Shaul. He writes that where a note expressly obligates payment to whoever produces it, the original creditor cannot collect without possession of the note, even if he offers a receipt, because the borrower may say that perhaps the creditor transferred it, or perhaps another will find it and the borrower will be unable to resist that holder. Nevertheless, if the creditor is financially sound, beit din should compel the borrower to pay provided the creditor accepts responsibility for every claim, loss, and damage arising from that payment. If the creditor is not financially sound, he must provide a guarantor or collateral so that the borrower will not suffer loss. Shaarei Mishpat cites Tzemach Tzedek and Radbaz and observes that although their proofs are not entirely compelling, earlier authorities have already ruled so and should be followed. Responsa Netivot David, siman 21, also strongly defends Tzemach Tzedek and concludes that if the bearer note is later produced, the borrower may indeed have to pay it, because the original creditor's receipt may not identify it conclusively. Therefore, the borrower need not pay now unless he receives a written undertaking that if he later pays another holder, he may swear that this was the same note and recover from the first creditor what he paid him now. The first creditor need not reimburse more than the amount presently received. If the creditor proves by witnesses that the note was lost through unavoidable accident, he need not assume this additional responsibility, although he should give an ordinary receipt against the possibility that he himself later finds the note. If there are no witnesses and the borrower merely says, 'Perhaps it was not lost by accident,' the creditor must swear that it was. If the borrower positively asserts that it was not lost by accident, the borrower can swear and is believed through the migo that he could have claimed payment, since the note is absent. Responsa Chemdat Shlomo, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, discusses a similar commercial note lost amid wartime looting. He challenges Tzemach Tzedek both as to the usefulness of an indemnity document and as to the need for one at all. He argues from the reported judicial custom that the public announcement itself was intended to permit collection, and that the ordinary dispute whether a receipt may be written concerns fear that the same creditor will collect twice, whereas mere fear that an unrelated finder will later cause loss is too remote to impose liability on the creditor, who is not a custodian of the borrower. The beit din certificate of the announcement therefore functions in place of a receipt. He notes one remaining concern, namely that perhaps the creditor already sold the note. That concern can be answered by the announcement, since a lawful purchaser should have appeared, and in the particular case the borrower did not suspect the creditor of such a sale and the wartime loss itself explained the absence of the note. He therefore recommends a reasonable compromise if possible. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, in a responsum printed there as siman 2, initially reasons that Chazal strengthened the borrower's position when a real danger of double payment exists, since according to the view that no receipt is written the creditor loses because of the borrower's possible loss. Yet after analyzing Chemdat Shlomo's distinction, he agrees in practice that where the only concern is that some third party may later find and collect the note, the borrower should pay the creditor after the public announcement, even without an indemnity instrument, at least where the borrower trusts the creditor that he did not sell the note. He explains that Tzemach Tzedek's indemnity requirement can be understood as requiring the creditor to do what is reasonably possible to protect the borrower, not as making such security an absolute condition of collection. See all these responsa, and the matter still requires study.
