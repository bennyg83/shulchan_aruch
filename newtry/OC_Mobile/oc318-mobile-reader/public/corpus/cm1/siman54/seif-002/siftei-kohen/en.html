The lender takes a heset oath. Although the paper itself is not worth a perutah, the evidentiary value of the deed remains worth more than a perutah to the borrower even after a receipt is written. Therefore, when the borrower makes a definite claim that the deed is still in the lender’s possession, the lender takes a heset oath that it was lost. The Giddulei Terumah asks why this valuation is needed, since without an oath the borrower could refuse payment until the deed is returned. In my view, the borrower may delay payment only where return of the deed will eventually permit safe payment. Once the lender writes a receipt, the borrower may not retain the money indefinitely. The oath concerns a separate claim for the value of the instrument. It is not a condition precedent to payment, because we do not find a heset oath imposed before recovery. Accordingly, the borrower must first pay the debt against a receipt, and the lender then swears concerning the lost deed. The Rambam and Mechaber’s order is therefore not literal, as in several other places where the language places the oath before taking or returning property although the property is transferred first.
