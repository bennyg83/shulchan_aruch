And the lender says, 'I lost the document.' The Gemara records a dispute. According to the view that receipts are not written, the creditor can collect only if he brings witnesses that the debt document was burned; otherwise he does not collect. This is difficult. Why should a document be stronger than a pledge? If the creditor holds a pledge and says it was lost through unavoidable accident, he swears and collects the debt, because the borrower is like one who says, 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' Why should a creditor who says his debt document was burned not likewise swear and collect? Nor can the difference be merely purchaser liability, because the same issue exists with a document lacking responsibility, and even a creditor who loses a pledge can still collect from purchasers through a separate valid document. The distinction is that a pledge is ordinarily taken only as security or a reminder of the debt. It is not itself payment. A pledge is comparable to an ordinary apotiki, and the debtor can still satisfy the debt with money. Therefore creditor and debtor effectively have reciprocal claims: the creditor claims the money with certainty, while the debtor has only an uncertain claim for return of the pledged item if it was perhaps lost. Even while the pledge remains physically in the creditor's hands, it does not count as payment in the ordinary sense. A debt document is different. So long as a fully enforceable document remains in the creditor's hands, the borrower need not pay until the document is returned or otherwise neutralized. He did not give the document to create a reciprocal debt requiring the creditor to return paper; rather, his monetary obligation was undertaken on the basis that payment would be made against return of the enforceable document. A clear document in the lender's hands is in a sense treated as already collected because the assets are encumbered by it. Therefore, while the document remains outstanding, the borrower's position resembles one who says, 'I do not know whether I ever became obligated to make another payment without receiving the document back.' The creditor must therefore prove the document was destroyed before he can demand payment under the no-receipt view. The pledge cases fit this distinction. Ran cites Ramban's question concerning a pledge whose value is disputed. If the borrower would be wholly exempt while the pledge remains in the creditor's hands, then uncertainty whether the pledge still exists should look like, 'I do not know whether I became obligated.' Ran explains that where the pledge is worth less than the debt, even if the creditor wrongfully refuses to return it, the borrower must still pay the excess. The continued possession of an under-value pledge is therefore not itself equivalent to payment. Where the pledge equals the entire debt, however, its continued possession can count as payment, and uncertainty whether it still exists can function like uncertainty whether the borrower repaid. This reconciles Rabbi Eliezer's rule that if a full-value pledge was lost the creditor swears and collects. A debt document has no independent monetary value comparable to a full-value pledge. Its significance lies in the condition of the obligation itself: the borrower need not make another payment while the enforceable document remains outstanding. Therefore the question whether the document still exists goes to whether the borrower's present duty to pay has arisen at all. Even Ramban can agree that the case is one of, 'I do not know whether I became obligated,' because no concept of actual payment through the paper is needed. Under the view that receipts are written, the Sages created an enactment to protect the creditor from losing his debt merely because the document was lost. The borrower then receives a receipt as protection against later presentation of the document. That is a special remedial enactment, as the Gemara says, so that the debtor should not 'eat and rejoice' merely because the creditor lost his instrument.
The lender takes a hesset oath. See Shach, who writes that the document has the value of a perutah as evidence. One should not ask that, since after payment the document belongs to the borrower and is worth a perutah as evidence, even a borrower who claims uncertainty should be able to require the lender to swear that it is no longer in his possession, as one may swear concerning documents. The oath that an item is not in one's possession applies only to one who became a custodian. A creditor holding his own debt document did not become a custodian for the borrower's evidence. He may destroy his own proof if he wishes. He is a custodian only of the paper itself once it belongs to the borrower, and the paper as mere material is not worth a perutah. Custodial oaths require a perutah of value.
