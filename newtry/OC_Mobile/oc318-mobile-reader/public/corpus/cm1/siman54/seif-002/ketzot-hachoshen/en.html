A hesset oath. Although the paper of the document itself may not be worth a perutah, the evidence contained in it has value. Even after a receipt is written, the borrower would pay more than a perutah to obtain the original document so that he need not continue safeguarding the receipt. Therefore, when the borrower asserts with certainty that the creditor still has the instrument, the creditor must take a hesset oath. Maggid Mishneh explains this way. Gidulei Terumah asks why this reasoning is needed. If the creditor does not swear that the document is lost, the borrower need not pay anything until the creditor returns the document, as stated in seif 3. There can be no greater monetary consequence than that. The Shach answers that seif 3 prevents payment only because the creditor will ultimately be paid once he returns the instrument. The borrower cannot permanently keep the money merely because the creditor has not yet returned the paper. Therefore Maggid Mishneh needed to show that the document itself has monetary value. However, Baal HaTerumot states explicitly that when the document is known still to exist, even if the creditor himself cannot retrieve it, the creditor loses the right to collect until the document is returned. He discusses a creditor who transferred a debt instrument without the necessary writing so that the purchaser acquired only the paper itself. If the borrower says, "I will not pay until you return my document," and it is known to be in the purchaser's hands, beit din does not compel payment through a receipt. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes that where it is known where the document is, the creditor must exert himself to retrieve it. The original proposal in Ketubot that a lost ketubah resembles one intentionally hidden shows that if the document is intentionally hidden before us, no receipt is written and no collection occurs. Although we do not rule that a merely lost document has the same status, Baal HaTerumot proves that where the document is definitely extant, even beyond the creditor's immediate control, the borrower can withhold payment. Rashi, however, explains "as though she hid it before us" to mean that she hid it specifically in order to produce it and collect again. According to that reading, an ordinary document merely hidden or inaccessible may not prevent collection through a receipt. Tosafot in Bava Metzia likewise implies that even if the creditor admits the ketubah remains in her possession, a receipt can sometimes be written. Rashba also indicates that the rule barring collection while the creditor holds the document is more a practical protection than an absolute legal nullification. Rambam appears to hold that where the document definitely exists and can be returned, payment is delayed until it is returned, because the borrower paid the scribe's fee and the document has value to him in avoiding the burden of safeguarding a receipt. But where the creditor denies that he has it, the borrower cannot stop payment merely because he has a migo of having paid. Returning the document is not itself part of the debt payment. It is a protective order that beit din can impose when the document's existence is known. Ran gives a similar rule concerning a creditor who holds collateral: if it is known that the pledge remains with him, beit din can delay payment until he returns it, even if the pledge is worth less than the debt. But if its continued possession is not known, payment is not blocked beyond the value of the pledge. The same applies here. Where the instrument's existence is known, beit din can delay payment. Where it is disputed and not proven, the borrower cannot create that delay merely through his own assertion.
