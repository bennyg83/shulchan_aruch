(See there: “only so that he would have property from which to collect”) In the Gemara the Sages hold this view. Admon asks: if so, he should have delivered a notice that he was selling only in order to be able to seize the field. The Sages answer that one friend has another friend: word of the notice would reach the buyer, who would then refuse to purchase the field. This is difficult. In every case where a notice is required, why do we not say that the coerced party feared making one lest it become known and cause him a great loss? The Sema answers that here the creditor relied upon the note in his possession, whereas elsewhere he has nothing to rely upon but his own allegation. This too is difficult, because the note is itself weakened by this sale, which appears to prove that it was paid. If the Sages hold that the sale does not weaken the note, then no notice is needed at all, and there would be no reason to answer that word travels from friend to friend. It appears that Admon and the Sages dispute how to weigh the two implications of the sale. The sale supports the borrower, because it resembles an admission that no debt remains, as Tosafot explain. It also supports the seller-creditor, because he may have sold only to make the property available for seizure. Admon considers the borrower’s implication primary: if the sale was for the creditor’s benefit, he should have secretly prepared a notice so that the sale would not injure him and the borrower would have no later argument. The Sages hold that since the sale was truly for the seller’s benefit, he was unwilling to protect himself against the borrower’s challenge at the cost of defeating the sale, for a buyer who learned that the seller intended to retake the property would refuse to buy. He therefore relied on the court to recognize his purpose from the circumstances. This differs from an ordinary notice, where the court cannot otherwise know what occurred. [Editor’s note: This is insufficient, because in the ordinary case there must also be witnesses to the coercion, since a notice that does not state that the witnesses knew of the coercion is invalid. It appears that the original difficulty is no difficulty. Ordinarily, the presumption is that under coercion a person nevertheless resolves to transfer title, and without a notice there is no proof that he did not. Here, by contrast, the creditor holds a validated note and we seek to nullify it on the basis of the sale. Once the sale is not proof because word of a notice would reach the buyer, there is no ground to void the note. This is correct, and perhaps this was the Sema’s intent.]
