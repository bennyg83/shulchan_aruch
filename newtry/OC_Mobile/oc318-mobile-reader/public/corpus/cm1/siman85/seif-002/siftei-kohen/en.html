How can the borrower say, “You should have seized the field for your debt,” when he could have discharged the creditor with money? The creditor may answer: “Had I pressed you, you might have paid me in cash, perhaps because you had money or because you would have sold the field to someone else. You would not necessarily have sold it to me. I therefore had to persuade you to sell it to me, and I had to pay the purchase money first because local practice is to pay first and write the deed afterward.” I asked several scholars, and they agreed that reason supports the creditor’s answer. A valid note should not be invalidated by the inference that he should have seized the field. There is proof from Ritzba’s responsum in Teshuvot Maimoniyot, Sefer Mishpatim, siman 34. A claimant alleged a debt, and the defendant said, “I paid you, and afterward you bought a coat from me. I refused to extend you credit without a guarantor. Had I owed you, you should have said, ‘Give it to me for my debt.’” Ritzba ruled that even if witnesses establish the sale and establish that the purchaser did not say, “Give it to me for my debt,” the creditor’s position is not weakened. The case does not resemble the second chapter of Ketubot, where in a place that pays before writing the deed the borrower can say, “Had I owed you, you should have collected your debt when you sold me the field.” There the seller had no need to sell his field after receiving money equal to the debt. In the coat case, if the creditor had demanded that it be transferred for the debt, the seller might have refused to sell at all. Beit Yosef cites this briefly. Rama appears to understand the responsum as limited to movables because the creditor feared they would be hidden. I do not think so. Had that been the reason, Ritzba should have said the creditor feared concealment. His wording, that the creditor feared the seller would not sell at all, means that even without concealment the seller may simply refuse to sell his goods. The same reasoning applies to land. Bach also distinguishes movables from land, saying the creditor may fear the movables will be hidden. He claims clear proof from the Mishnah at the end of Ketubot: where a person identifies his own field as belonging to another in a boundary description, he loses his claim, but if he described it that way in a deed concerning himself, he does not, because he may say, “Had I not done so, the other would not have sold it to me.” Bach’s proof is surprising, for that Mishnah concerns land. On his own distinction it disproves him. The proof can in any event be rejected, but the practical ruling remains as I have stated. Where local practice is to write the deed first and pay afterward, however, Tur and Mechaber are correct. The borrower can say, “Had I owed you, you should have withheld the purchase money as payment of your debt,” since the deed of sale was already in the creditor’s hands.
