If the borrower sold, etc. The Shach wonders why this differs from movable property. Regarding movables, the Rama writes in the gloss that the creditor's rights are not impaired, because we say that had he intended to collect those items he would not have sold them to the debtor. Why should land be different? Surely there too we can say that had the creditor disclosed his intention to collect that land, the debtor would not have sold it to him. The Bach writes that this reasoning has support from the Gemara in Ketubot concerning one who used property as a boundary marker for another, where we say that when he himself is the purchaser the rule is different because otherwise the seller would not have sold to him. That case concerns land, making the question even stronger. The Bach sought support for the Tur but found evidence against him. It appears that there is no difficulty for one who examines the Tur carefully. The Tur writes, "How could he purchase land upon which his lien already rests?" This is the point. With movable property, even while it remains in the borrower's hands the creditor never truly relies upon a lien over those particular items. He can therefore say, "What would I gain by refusing to purchase them? My debt remains precarious in any event. It is better that I purchase them myself than allow someone else to do so. If I disclose that I intend to collect them, the debtor may simply sell them to another person." But land already bears the creditor's lien. If the debtor sells it to someone else, the creditor's earlier lien allows him to seize it from that purchaser. If the creditor himself buys it, however, from whom will he later collect? There may be some special reason he prefers this particular land to other parcels, but land is generally available for purchase. We do not say that merely because he especially preferred this parcel he intended to forfeit his lien entirely. This resolves the matter, and the Tur's wording is precise. Accordingly, the Bach's proof does not contradict the Tur. In the boundary marker case there is no existing lien at all, since the purchaser owes the claimant nothing. We can therefore say that otherwise he would not have sold it to him. That is the same reasoning as with movables. Here, by contrast, land already bears the lien, and by purchasing it himself the creditor would lose that lien and leave his money exposed. The cases are different. The Giddulei Terumah holds that if the borrower has other land from which the creditor can collect the debt, the seller does not lose his rights. This requires study, because one can always be concerned that the borrower has many creditors and the other land may not suffice. The Tur states the rule without qualification. Nevertheless, this distinction is not found or even hinted at by other authorities apart from the Tur himself. Therefore, where the borrower has other property, we should not extend the Tur's innovation beyond its necessary scope. The words of the Giddulei Terumah are therefore correct.
