(Sema, seif katan 5) At the end of the passage. From this we infer that he fully resolved and transferred it to him. Note: This requires study. In a forced sale, even where it is known that there was no practical way for him to deliver a protest and witnesses saw and recognized his coercion, nevertheless the sale is valid because we say that due to the coercion he fully resolved and transferred ownership. Here, however, the seller did not intend that his sale be invalid. He truly resolved and transferred ownership because he knew that he would later be able to return and collect the field from the purchaser in payment of his debt. The entire purpose of the protest here is not to void the sale, but to prevent the purchaser from arguing, 'If you truly owed me money, why did you sell me this field?' On this point the Rabbis disagree with Admon and hold that the seller can say: because a friend has a friend, I refrained from delivering a protest and relied on this explanation being accepted by beit din, so that my opponent would not be believed against the document I hold against him. I never intended to void the sale itself, only to collect my debt from that very field. See the Taz and the glosses of Chacham Tzvi. If the purchaser pays me the debt, I am content that the field remain his. Although a person does not ordinarily sell his field willingly except under financial pressure, in a case such as this, where the sale itself enabled him to secure repayment of his debt, he fully resolves and transfers it, either willingly or because of the pressure created by his need to secure repayment. This can be explained further: in every forced sale, because there is reason to say that perhaps he fully resolved and transferred ownership, even if he claims that he did not, his claim is only an unexpressed intention and does not undo the act of sale. Here we are not discussing voiding the sale at all, because the sale truly remains valid, and the purchaser may pay the debt in cash and retain the property. The only issue is the weakness in the debt claim, because the debtor says, 'If I owed you, why did you sell me the field?' It is enough for the creditor to offer a plausible explanation that he sold it so that he would be secure in collecting his debt. Admon holds this is not a sufficient explanation because he should have delivered a protest stating that this was his reason. The Rabbis hold that he may say that because a friend has a friend, he feared disclosing his intention to witnesses. He therefore sold it, knowing he would in any event obtain repayment, either in money or through collection from that field.
