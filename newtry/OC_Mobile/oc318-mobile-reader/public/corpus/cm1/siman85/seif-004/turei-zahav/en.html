(Seif 6) “He returns and collects from him.” The Sema limits this to where the earlier creditor sold his note to a purchaser, and the purchaser collected from the later creditor. The earlier creditor then returns and collects from the purchaser the same field he had transferred, because that field was subject to his own prior debt against the first creditor. If, however, the later creditor sold his note and the purchaser collected from the earlier creditor, the earlier creditor cannot take that field back from the purchaser on account of the debt owed him by the later creditor. The Sema reasons that the earlier creditor’s field had already become subject to and acquired by the later creditor, but when the earlier creditor later lent to the later creditor, the later creditor had not yet made the earlier loan. It is therefore as though the later creditor acquired the field after borrowing, and without an after-acquired-property clause it was not subject to the earlier creditor. This is his position, with additional explanation. In my humble opinion the plain meaning is that the same rule applies even where the later creditor sold his note. The earlier creditor may afterward collect his own field from the purchaser. Ordinarily, property acquired by the borrower after the loan is not encumbered where he acquired it independently and without the creditor’s knowledge. Here, however, the earlier creditor later borrowed from the later creditor, and the circumstances attest that he did so to secure the debt the later creditor already owed him. [Editor’s note: I already answered the Taz above, and write here as well that his position is unpersuasive. The Shach’s agreement with the Bach is also unpersuasive. Their proof from heirs who collected land for their father’s debt, from whom the father’s creditor then collects, is not comparable. There the result is as though the father himself collected during his lifetime and bequeathed the land, from which his creditor may collect. Here, even if the later creditor himself collected land and then sold it, the earlier creditor certainly could not collect from the purchaser. Why should the purchaser of the note, who acquired the field before the debtor himself collected and sold it, be worse? The Shach’s further proof is no proof. Before the purchaser acquired it, the lien indeed rested upon the field, just as all fields later acquired by a debtor are subject to his existing creditors even without an after-acquired-property clause while still in his possession. Yet when he sells them, their lien falls away. See Tosafot in Pesachim, s.v. “I become subject to him.” I later saw that the Ran, cited by the Beit Yosef in seif 7, adduces the same proof from heirs as the Shach, and the Bach himself also cites it. The difficulty nevertheless remains. The Ran’s language could perhaps be reconciled with the Sema by explaining “when his loan is earlier” to mean that it has priority in collection, but this is not the place to elaborate.] This resembles what I wrote at the beginning of this siman. Although there the proof lies in his selling the field and not taking the purchase money toward his debt, while here that proof is absent, there is nevertheless proof here as well. He made himself a servant-borrower to one who was already his servant-borrower, although he could have borrowed elsewhere, comparable to the Tur’s reasoning in seif 5. He therefore did so only to secure his own debt, as though expressly stipulating that result. The earlier creditor may thus collect his field from the purchaser. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, also applies the ruling where the later creditor sold his note, contrary to the Sema, but reasons that this is not a case of a borrower who acquired and sold after the loan, because the later creditor sold only his lien and not the field itself; the field is treated as free property. These words are difficult. The purchaser removed the field from the earlier creditor, and there can be no greater sale than that. Moreover, if only a lien was sold, by what right did the later creditor acquire the earlier creditor’s field? He too had only a lien against the earlier creditor. Even had the earlier creditor made a complete sale to the later creditor, it would have occurred after the earlier creditor’s loan to him, and without an after-acquired-property clause it would not have become subject to that debt. I have written what appears to me the correct conclusion.
