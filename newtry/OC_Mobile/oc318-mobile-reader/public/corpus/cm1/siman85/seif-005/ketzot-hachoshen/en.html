Ramban explains that in the reciprocal debt case, where one party acquires land from the heirs and then the opposite creditor seeks to collect it under Rabbi Natan's lien, the possession can be treated like a seizure made during the decedent's lifetime. This answers the difficulty that beit din does not ordinarily collect from minor heirs. The Taz asks: if it is really like lifetime seizure, why does the land not become ordinary movable property of heirs when what was collected was movable property? Actual lifetime seizure can help even with movables. He leaves the matter very difficult. Chacham Tzvi distinguishes between the enactment not to proceed against minor orphans and the enactment excluding heirs' movables from ordinary creditor collection, calling the former a lighter enactment. It is difficult to know by what measure one calls one enactment light and the other severe. Maharit gives a better explanation. The reason beit din ordinarily does not collect from minor orphans is concern that the father may have handed the creditor bundles of money for repayment. Here the opposing creditor already holds a reciprocal claim equal to the debt. That existing claim itself functions like the separate security that the father would otherwise have deposited. There is therefore no reason to suspect some additional hidden bundle. This resembles the rule that where the decedent designated a particular parcel on all four boundaries, collection can proceed without the ordinary oath because that designation itself serves as the security. A further question remains where the first borrower rather than the second lender dies. Perhaps the deceased first borrower had handed security to the creditor before the reciprocal loan was later made. We could then still have a case of minor heirs. The answer follows the Gemara's establishment of the dispute on the very day the first debt becomes due. If the supposed security had been handed before the reciprocal loan, it would have been within the original term. We rule that beit din can proceed even against minor heirs where the debt remained within its term, because early repayment is not ordinarily suspected. If the security was handed on the due date itself before the reciprocal loan later that same day, Raavad, cited by Rama in siman 78, holds that although the debtor himself can plead certain payment on that day, we do not advance that claim for heirs. After the reciprocal loan occurs, there is no reason to suspect additional security because each side already holds a matching claim against the other. Thus the concern for hidden security never creates a barrier in the precise due date setting of the sugya, and Ramban's result can be sustained.
