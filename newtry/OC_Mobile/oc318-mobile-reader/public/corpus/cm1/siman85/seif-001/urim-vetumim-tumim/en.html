And some say that he is believed. The Shach wonders how he can be believed to say that the deed is forged when witnesses authenticate it. He therefore strains to say that although Rashi and the Ran explicitly state that the borrower pleads forgery, this is not literal, and the true plea is payment. This is strained in their words. It appears that they do not speak of authentication by witnesses who testify with certainty that these are the witnesses' signatures, but rather authentication by comparison. The Shach himself wrote above in siman 46 and siman 69, in explaining the Gaon's view concerning a handwritten obligation and similar cases, that where authentication is required by Torah law one cannot rely upon this sort of comparison, which is only a rabbinic form of authentication. Tosafot in Ketubot 92, s.v. "the law," likewise write that where a deed was lost and found, we do not rely on such authentication because the deed has suffered an impairment. There can be no greater impairment than the present case, where instead of collecting on his deed the creditor sold his land. This is an even greater defect than a deed that was lost. In the presence of such an impairment, authentication is certainly required by Torah law, so comparison is ineffective, as the Shach writes in several places. Therefore, the Tur, who above in siman 69 disagrees with the Gaon and holds that even a handwritten obligation may be authenticated by comparison, likewise disagrees here and limits the rule to a plea of payment rather than forgery. Everything is thus consistent and there is no difficulty.
I did not sell, etc. The Taz asks that this works where the debtor wrote a clause subjecting future acquisitions to the debt. But where he did not write such a clause, he can still sell the property to someone else and place it beyond the creditor's reach, just as with movable property, since property acquired after the loan and then sold cannot be collected without such a clause. Because of this difficulty the Taz wished to innovate that, since there is a clear presumption that he sold the property in order to make it available for collection, it is treated as though he had expressly written a future acquisitions clause. The Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses, writes that we have never heard of extracting money on the basis of such an assessment. See there. I wonder why there is any need for their discussion. In Bava Batra 156 the Gemara seeks to prove from this very ruling, that the debtor was clever in selling the field so that he could seize it back, that future acquisitions are subject to a lien. The Gemara rejects the proof and says that its question concerns even the shirt on one's back, namely whether property acquired and then sold is subject to collection. If the Taz's question had force, the difficulty would remain: what good is the debtor's acquisition of the field, since he could still evade collection by selling it? One cannot answer with the Taz that it is as though a future acquisitions clause were written, because then the Gemara would have proved that such a clause is effective. An unspoken presumption cannot be stronger than an express clause. It is therefore clear that the Gemara was not concerned with the Taz's objection. The reason is that the creditor's deed is collectible at every moment. A sale of land becomes publicly known. When the creditor learns that the debtor intends to sell, he can come to beit din, obtain authorization to seize, and prevent the sale from defeating him. Once the Taz's question falls, the answer he built upon it likewise has no foundation, and his innovation should not be relied upon.
