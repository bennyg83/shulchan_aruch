(Shach, seif katan 3) At the end of the passage: regarding another matter he did not render him credible. Note: This reason was unnecessary according to what I wrote in the Rules of Migo, letter 5, that we do not say a migo based on the ability to contradict witnesses who are disqualified. There is a slight difficulty from siman 75:1, where credibility by stipulation is stronger than a migo. If credibility does not help here, certainly a migo should not help. One can answer that credibility is stronger than a migo because all monetary stipulations are valid. Once he rendered him as credible as two witnesses, he certainly rendered him credible against a migo. Here, by contrast, the entire weakness is circumstantial: why did you sell this to him, when that person will then be able to say to you, "if I owed you, why did you sell it?" This creates an assumption that a person would not act in a way that gives the other side such an obvious response. Therefore, where the seller has a strong migo, anyone hearing the case can judge that he is telling the truth and the circumstantial argument falls away. The migo creates a compelling assessment that the facts are as he says. If so, however, only a strong migo should help here. A weak migo cannot create such a compelling assessment. Thus, even if a person could rely on a migo that he could have brazenly contradicted relatives, that would still be only a weak migo and would not help here. Furthermore, even if the borrower had granted the lender credibility to say that the witnesses on the sale document signed falsely, since those witnesses are in truth qualified and were disqualified only against the borrower because he rendered the lender as credible as two, the borrower still has a valid circumstantial argument against the lender. We then have an assumption that the document was certainly repaid and that the borrower's account is true.
