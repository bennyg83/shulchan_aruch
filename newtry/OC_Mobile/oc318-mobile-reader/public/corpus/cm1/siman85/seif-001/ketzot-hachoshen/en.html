He first gives money and afterward they write the deed. Urim writes that in a place where land is acquired through money alone, without need of a deed, the creditor has not lost his existing debt by accepting the purchase money. He can say that the buyer acquired the field immediately upon payment and the sale could no longer be retracted. But in a place where the sale is completed only through the deed, the seller could have retained the purchase money on account of the old debt before the deed was completed. Urim cites Hagahot Ashri, Mordechai, and Rav Hai Gaon. The cited sources do not support this reading. They say that if Reuven owes Shimon one hundred and, after the due date, Shimon agrees to sell Reuven an object for one hundred, once Reuven hands him the money Shimon may say, "I take this money for my old debt and will not give you the object," without incurring the "Who exacted payment" curse. If Reuven then gives another one hundred and demands the object, Shimon must either deliver it or incur the curse. With land the law differs. If the parties agreed on sale for one hundred in a place where money itself acquires land and Reuven paid, the land is immediately acquired. Shimon cannot reassign the money to the earlier debt so as to undo the sale. He must treat the land as sold and then separately pursue the old debt. But this does not mean that the seller has necessarily lost all leverage regarding the written deed. Even where the land itself is acquired through money, the witnesses do not write a deed over the seller's protest. The seller can therefore withhold the sale document until his existing debt is paid. The Mordechai's language, "from here it follows," fits this. Just as a seller of movables need not hand over the object until the prior debt is satisfied, so too in land he need not hand over the deed until the prior debt is addressed, even though the land itself has already been acquired by money. The general language of the decisors, that where the buyer pays first and the seller later writes the deed the seller can be faulted for not retaining the money on his debt, therefore applies even in a locale where money effects acquisition. The seller could at least have withheld the deed until payment of the old obligation. Urim's distinction is therefore not established by the cited sources.
From what could I collect my debt? This is based on the Mishnah at the end of Ketubot. Rashi explains that the debtor had been hiding movables, leaving the creditor no practical property from which to collect, so the creditor sold him land in order to create reachable assets. The Taz asks what is gained. If the debtor later sells that land, the creditor still cannot seize it unless the original debt contained an after acquired property clause. Taz therefore concludes that no express after acquired clause is needed here. Since the creditor had no reason to sell except to secure his own debt, we treat the sale as though the parties expressly stipulated that the creditor's lien would remain on the field, and he can seize it from later purchasers. This is difficult against the Gemara in Perek Mi SheMet. Shmuel asks whether an after acquired property clause is effective and attempts to resolve the question from this very Mishnah, which says the creditor was clever because he could seize the field. The Gemara rejects the proof by saying perhaps he can seize even the cloak on the debtor's shoulders. If the sale itself automatically included an understood condition preserving the prior lien, there would be no after acquired issue at all. The lien would arise when the land entered the debtor's hands. The entire Gemara therefore proves that we do not infer such an automatic sale condition. The actual rationale is simply that hidden movables can easily be concealed from collection, whereas land cannot. Even if the land could later be sold and even if after acquired property were not generally encumbered, sale of land is less immediate and less hidden than disappearance of movables. The creditor improves his practical ability to collect by converting concealed movables into visible land. Tumim also objects from this Gemara but concludes that the passage proves after acquired property must be encumbered, because otherwise the unstated arrangement could not be stronger than an express clause. That does not follow. If there truly were an implied condition made at the very moment of sale, the subject matter would then exist and there would be no future property issue. The stronger point is that the Gemara does not accept such an implied condition at all. The question therefore remains exactly an ordinary after acquired property question.
