(Seif 1) “For he will say, ‘I sold you the field only so that I would have property from which to collect my debt.’” This is the Mishna in the second chapter of Ketubot. Rashi explains that the debtor had been concealing movable property, leaving the creditor nothing to seize; now the creditor will take the land. This is difficult. What has he gained? Even now, if the borrower sells this land, the creditor cannot collect it from the purchaser, because the borrower did not own it when the loan was made. It is property acquired and then sold, which a creditor cannot collect unless the note contains an after-acquired-property clause, as stated in siman 112. The authorities should therefore have specified here, as they commonly do elsewhere, that the note contains such a clause. In my humble opinion no such clause is needed here, according to any view. The creditor could have collected from the debtor without selling him his own land. He sold it only to secure the preexisting debt. It is therefore as though he expressly stipulated that the sale was on condition that his lien remain upon the land forever. Accordingly, if the debtor later sells the land to another, the creditor may collect it, since the circumstances themselves attest to this understanding. I wrote further on this in seif 6, with God’s help. [Editor’s note: This ruling is exceedingly difficult. How can property be extracted from a purchaser on the basis of such an assessment, something never otherwise heard of? The Taz’s original question is not compelling. Presumably the borrower does not intend to sell the field, for otherwise he would not have bought it. Even if he later wishes to sell, who says that a purchaser will be immediately available? Before he can arrange a sale, the creditor may summon him to court and collect the land, unlike money, which can easily be hidden. Even according to the Taz, there is no necessary inference that the creditor may collect the land from a purchaser. His statement that the creditor could have collected without selling his field is incorrect, because the case concerns a place where the deed is written before the purchase money is delivered; he could not collect until he first sold the land. Moreover, even if he could have collected without selling, the opposite inference is possible: since he sold the land rather than collect his debt, his note evidently has no substance and the buyer owes him nothing, as the Gemara says in a place where money is first delivered and the deed is then written. It therefore appears that if the borrower sells the field to another, the creditor cannot collect it from the purchaser, contrary to the Taz.]
