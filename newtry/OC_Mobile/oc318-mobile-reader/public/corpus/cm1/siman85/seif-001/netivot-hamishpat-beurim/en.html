The same question arises in the case of two people who each produce a promissory note against the other, where the holder of the later note says to the holder of the earlier note, "If I owed you, how could you have borrowed from me?" We invalidate the earlier note and uphold the later one, obligating the holder of the earlier note to pay the later creditor. Why assume that the earlier note is forged? Perhaps the later note is forged. At the very least, both documents should remain in doubt. It appears that this can be resolved as follows. If two groups of witnesses contradict one another, one group saying that a loan occurred and the other saying that no loan occurred, both groups are placed in doubt, and seizure can be effective. But if one group says that a loan occurred and another group says that the lender admitted before them that no loan occurred, seizure does not help, because both groups can be telling the truth. The later admission of the litigant is legally equivalent to a waiver. So too here, the witnesses to the second document are like witnesses to an admission, as I wrote above in seif katan 21, and we therefore follow them.
I did not sell you the field. See Sema, seif katan 4, through the words that he should have sold it to someone else. His words imply that were it not for the reason that the seller wanted to be able to seize the field for his debt, the earlier promissory note would be void. This is difficult. If he sold movable property, then even where the buyer drew the movables into his possession before paying, the note would be void, because the rationale of later seizing the property for the debt would not apply. More fundamentally, what proof is there from the sale to invalidate the promissory note? Merely because one person owes another money, must the creditor stop doing business with him? In a locale where payment precedes the writing of the deed, there is indeed a proof, because the creditor could have retained the purchase money. But where the deed is written first and the money paid afterward, there is no such proof. At times he needs the money from the sale more than he needs immediate collection of the old debt, and therefore must sell the land despite the debt. The words of Tosafot in Ketubot 110, s.v. veRabbanan, are also difficult in this regard, as Maharsha notes. According to Rashi, however, the matter is clear. It therefore appears that the essential proof always comes from the fact that he should have retained the money for his debt. Even where the deed was written first and the money paid afterward, the acquisition occurs as one transaction, and land is not acquired by deed without money, as explained in siman 191. Siman 83 further explains that where one has two promissory notes against another and the debtor pays expressly toward one debt while the creditor remains silent, the creditor can say, "I remained silent so that you would give me the money." So too here, even if the buyer expressly handed over the money as the purchase price and the seller remained silent, the seller could afterward have said that he accepted it on account of the old debt. The buyer would then still have needed to pay the purchase price in order to complete the sale. Although below, siman 190 seif 6 in the Rama, it is stated that Shimon cannot say, "I am taking this maneh toward my debt," in order to undo the sale, that rule itself requires explanation in light of siman 83. We must say that acquisition is different, because unexpressed thoughts do not undo a clear kinyan performed before us. According to this, however, within toch kedei dibbur after receiving the money he certainly can undo the sale and say that he takes the money for the debt, because one can retract from a kinyan within toch kedei dibbur, as explained below in siman 195 seif 9. Thus the proof that the promissory note is void even where the deed was written before payment is that the seller did not say within toch kedei dibbur after receiving the money that he was retaining it for the debt. This reasoning also explains Tosafot in Bava Kamma 35b, s.v. leima tehevei tiyuvta. Tosafot write that even Rabban Gamliel, who obligates one who was sued for wheat and admitted barley, nevertheless agrees that the defendant is exempt from paying for the barley. This seems difficult, because if he is exempt from the barley, why should he be obligated in an oath? The admitted barley should be no worse than "here it is." We must say that the proof that the claimant waived the barley arises only after the defendant's admission. At the moment of admission, the claimant could still say, "Certainly, as you say, you owe me barley, but I am not presently demanding the barley," as explained in siman 88 seif 14. Thus at the moment of the admission the barley obligation still exists and can generate an oath. Likewise here, the proof that the earlier note was waived comes from the seller's failure to say within toch kedei dibbur after receiving the money that he was applying it to the old debt. To this the Sages respond that the seller acted shrewdly in selling the field because he could later seize it for his debt. In other words, he deliberately accepted the money as purchase money because he was afraid that if he applied it to the debt the buyer would cancel the sale. He needed the proceeds of the sale, so he took the money as the purchase price and intended to pursue the old debt separately. He was not afraid of losing the debt, because he could later seize the field. Why did he not silently make a declaration before witnesses within toch kedei dibbur that he was applying the money to the debt, conditioned on the debtor's failure to pay, so that he would preserve both options? Because "your friend has a friend," and he feared that the declaration would become known to the purchaser and cause the sale to be canceled. With this, Tosafot's words are properly understood. Accordingly, in a place where the money is paid first and the deed written afterward, even if money alone effects the acquisition there, the debtor can say, "If I owed you, you should not have given me a deed." Still, the acquisition itself would not necessarily be void, because one can retract from the document without retracting from the field. The creditor could then demand payment of the old debt in cash if the debtor had cash. With movable property, if the buyer first drew the item into his possession and paid only afterward, the seller could not have retained the money as a condition of completing the acquisition, because the item was already in the buyer's possession. There is therefore no proof that the promissory note is void. But if payment preceded the act of drawing the movable property, the old debt can certainly be treated as waived, because the rationale of later seizing the property is unavailable, and it would have been preferable simply to retain the money for the debt rather than complete the sale.
