And the borrower is believed, even if the document contains a clause of credibility. See Baer Heitev, seif katan 2. Netivot HaMishpat writes that it appears the borrower does not even require an oath, because the circumstances amount to the equivalent of witnesses. At first glance, however, Beit Yosef appears to imply otherwise. He cites Baal HaTerumot, gate 34, who compares this case to one in which the creditor says that a payment was applied to a different debt. Rav Nachman rules there that the document has been impaired, and the authorities explain that even its credibility clause is nullified, while the borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. Beit Yosef says that our case is the same. This wording suggests that here too the borrower must take a hesset oath. Drishah also appears to imply this, since in the following discussion he questions only the law of seizure. Responsa Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 86, discusses judges who were uncertain in a practical case. A person held communal funds and returned them one by one until nothing remained in his possession. He later produced several written acknowledgments signed by communal officials stating that the community owed him money. The community argued that if the debt were genuine, he should have retained payment from the funds already in his hands. The Rama in siman 86 seif 3 indicates that where the claimant has no migo, such an argument is effective. Here there was no migo because the communal trustees knew that he had repaid the funds after his own notes had already fallen due. The judges nevertheless questioned whether he could at least impose a hesset oath on the community that they did not owe him, because Baal HaTerumot's comparison to the case of 'another debt' might apply fully, including the requirement that the borrower swear hesset, or might refer only to invalidating the credibility clause because the document had been impaired. Panim Meirot concludes at length that the community can be required to swear hesset. Although we weaken the document because, if the community truly owed him, he should have collected from the money he already held, the claimant can answer that he relied upon their credibility or had some other reason. Even where one has been established as a denier and therefore cannot claim payment after first denying the debt, the Shach rules in siman 79 seif katan 18 and siman 82 seif katan 14 that after payment he may still impose a hesset oath that he was not made to pay twice. Here too the claimant is not extracting money; the other side swears and is exempt, so the case resembles one after payment. There is further proof from Shach, siman 78 seif katan 15, regarding payment within the due date. The presumption that a person does not pay before a debt falls due is strong enough to treat the creditor as if he possessed the money, yet after payment the creditor still must swear hesset. Likewise, a three-year presumption of ownership does not eliminate the hesset oath. Thus a presumption is not strong enough to remove hesset liability. Shaar Mishpat, seif katan 2, cites part of this discussion and states that Baal HaTerumot explicitly requires the borrower to swear hesset. He also proves this from the Rama in seif 3, who allows the creditor's migo where there are no witnesses to the sale of the field. If the presumption here were overwhelmingly strong, we would not say that a migo can overcome it to extract money by means of a document. Responsa Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 34, however, implies exemption even from hesset. Shaar Mishpat reconciles the sources: where the creditor holds a document, we invalidate the document because of the contrary circumstances, but those circumstances do not exempt the debtor from hesset. Where the claim is merely oral, however, the same presumption assists the defendant and exempts him even from hesset. He rules that this is the correct practical distinction.
