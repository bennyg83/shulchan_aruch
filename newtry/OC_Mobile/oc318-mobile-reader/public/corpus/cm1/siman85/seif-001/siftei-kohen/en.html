Sema writes that even if the borrower granted the creditor credibility equal to two witnesses, it appears ineffective against this presumption, because such credibility does not apply to every matter, as explained above at the beginning of siman 71. Although I wrote above in seif katan 1 that a claimant who has a migo is believed, there is no migo here. Had he said, “I never sold it to you,” he would contradict the witnesses to the deed of sale and would not be believed. The borrower’s grant of credibility equal to two witnesses applies if witnesses testify that he paid and the creditor contradicts them. It does not empower the creditor to contradict witnesses testifying to an unrelated fact. This is clear.
Some say that he is believed. It appears to me that no authority disagrees that one cannot invalidate an authenticated note merely by alleging forgery on the basis of an inference from circumstances that we visibly know is untrue. When Rashi and Ran describe the purchaser as producing a deed of sale and the creditor saying, “Your note is forged or paid, for had I owed you, you should have collected your debt when you sold me the field,” they mean that he says, “I do not know what sort of note yours is, but I certainly owe you nothing. It must therefore be forged or paid, because had I truly owed you, you would have collected then.” If he alleges only forgery, however, he is plainly not believed against an authenticated note. Ir Shushan explains the dissenting opinion as believing him to allege forgery through a migo that he could have claimed payment. This too is incorrect, for once the note is authenticated, such a migo is against witnesses. Gedulei Terumah, gate 34, beginning of part 3, writes that according to Rashi the note is not authenticated. He argues that a weak inference from the sale could not defeat a note whose signatures were authenticated, but could justify tearing an unauthenticated note when the borrower definitely alleges forgery or payment. This also does not appear correct. Rashi nowhere says the borrower asks to tear the note. Moreover, Rashi appears to agree that payment may be claimed even against an authenticated note. Nor is Gedulei Terumah’s proposed law correct. If the creditor stands ready to authenticate the note, we leave it in his hands; when he later authenticates it, the borrower’s allegation of forgery will be shown worthless.
