And one of them sold, etc. In Baal HaTerumot, Gate 34, beginning of part 2, he writes in the name of some authorities that if one sold the debt instrument after he had already borrowed from the second party, then there was already a presumption that this instrument had effectively been satisfied. Shimon lacked nothing from Reuven except return of the instrument itself, since Reuven was already indebted to Shimon for an equal amount. It follows that when he sold the instrument he sold an already satisfied instrument, and the purchaser caused his own loss and cannot collect anything from Shimon. The Baal HaTerumot argues against them that although we say each party remains with his own property, this does not mean that the debt is actually paid, but only that beit din does not intervene, etc. The view of those authorities requires study. If they treat the debt as presumptively paid because each party remains with his own property, what does Rami bar Chama say according to Rav Sheshet when he establishes the case as involving orphans, such that the orphans collect from the living creditor while the living creditor cannot collect from the orphans? As soon as the second party borrowed, the instruments should have become satisfied and nothing should remain except their return. If a purchaser bought such an instrument, he lost because it was already paid. How, then, could the orphans collect with it? The instrument was already satisfied and its force terminated immediately when their father borrowed from the other party. I am surprised that the Gidulei Terumah did not note this difficulty. What appears to me in explaining their reasoning is based on what the authorities write concerning why one who sells a debt instrument can afterward waive the debt: there are two liens, the personal obligation and the lien on property. The personal obligation is not sold, only the lien on property. Here, when we say that each party remains with his own property, there was never an effective lien on property, because each remains with his own. Nevertheless, there must certainly be a personal obligation. We say according to Admon that a person would not make himself "a borrower who is servant to the lender," etc. If there were no personal obligation at all when they owe one another, in what sense would he make himself a servant by borrowing? We must therefore say that the personal obligation does take effect. Only the property lien does not, for here there is no principle that a person's property serves as guarantor for him, since each party remains with his own property. Accordingly, the orphans can collect from him because his person was obligated to their father, and he may be compelled until he pays. But a purchaser of the instrument did not acquire the borrower's personal obligation, as explained, only the property lien, and there never was a property lien because each party remains with his own. This is satisfactory. On this basis I found a very satisfactory answer for the Rif and Rambam, who rule without qualification that each party collects and do not distinguish at all whether the second loan was made within the first term or after it, or whether the money was first given or the document was first written. They hold that the Ramban's rejection of those authorities applies because we rule like Rav Nachman, according to whom there can indeed be a practical case where this one collects and that one collects. Consequently, when each instrument was first written, it was not written merely as a receipt nor did its lien terminate, because it was possible that one debtor would have intermediate and superior quality land while the other had inferior land, in which case actual collection would occur. Thus from the outset each instrument was written with full legal force. The Tur explicitly gives this reason for rejecting those authorities, writing in substance: proof is that if afterward one party acquires superior land, each one collects, even though at the time of the loans their property positions were equal. This differs according to Rav Sheshet, who holds that each party always remains with his own property and there is never a case of actual mutual collection. According to him it is obvious that the property lien terminated, in accordance with those authorities. See the Yerushalmi and what I wrote above in seif katan 4. It is explicit there that according to Rav Sheshet we say that the instrument is merely in the nature of a receipt. That Yerushalmi therefore provides some support for those authorities, and their basis appears to come from there. If so, there is no difficulty in asking, if the second loan was made after the first debt became due, what is the reason of the Sages? It may be that he deliberately borrowed from the second party as a stratagem because he owed earlier creditors. He could borrow from the second party, and afterward an earlier creditor of the second party would come and collect from him through Rav Nachman's rule of subrogation. If the second party then argued that the first creditor himself owed him money, that claim would be later in priority than the second party's earlier creditor, and he would emerge empty handed. Chazal were concerned for collusion in many places. What, then, is difficult about the question of how he could have borrowed from him? At the time he may have intended by this device to deprive the second party and emerge free of the debt. If now, when collection is attempted, no earlier creditor appears, that proves nothing. Perhaps he later became wealthy, perhaps he regretted the scheme, and circumstances change over time. But then this may have been his intent in borrowing. Why should we invalidate an instrument when there is a plausible explanation that preserves its validity? Nor is Admon's reason difficult. This answer works according to the view that valuation is made from each debtor's own property. Then the reciprocal debts are not regarded as paid, because each side may actually collect, as explained, and earlier creditors can therefore come and collect. Admon, however, may hold that valuation is made according to general market categories, like the baraita in Bava Kamma and as I explained above in the intent of the Baal HaMaor. In that view, each party always remains with his own property, so the instruments function like receipts, and earlier creditors cannot collect from the second party at all. There is therefore no possible collusion, because the debt is treated as paid and Rav Nachman's rule does not apply once payment has already occurred. This is all the more so according to those who say that even an obligation arising through Rav Nachman's rule can be waived, so that only a property lien exists. There is therefore no difficulty. All this works according to Rav Nachman. Rav Sheshet, however, holds that even the Sages say valuation is made according to general market categories. The difficulty then remains whichever way one turns: if the reciprocal instruments are treated as receipts, what is the reason of the Sages; and if they are not, what is Admon's reason? Therefore the Gemara needed a different answer according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman, however, the explanation above works well as the respective reasoning of the Sages and Admon. Since the halachah follows the Sages, the Rif and Rambam rule that he always collects on his instrument without making any distinction. Consider this carefully.
And he returns and collects from him, etc. The Sema and Shach disagree in this seif about two rules that are major foundations in monetary law. It is therefore necessary to investigate the Gemara and poskim carefully in order to understand the matter thoroughly and determine which view is correct. To avoid confusing the reader by discussing two people who borrowed from one another, I will present the case in a different form, although for halachic purposes it is exactly equivalent, without any difference. Suppose Yaakov lent to Reuven, Reuven lent to Shimon, Reuven sold his debt instrument against Shimon to Yehudah, and Yehudah collected his debt from Shimon. Yaakov then comes to seize what Yehudah collected, arguing that Reuven owes him and Yehudah collected property that was subject to his lien. If Yehudah collected land from Shimon, the Sema holds as follows. If Reuven's loan to Shimon preceded Reuven's borrowing from Yaakov, then when Reuven later borrowed from Yaakov, his claim against Shimon was already included within the property subject to Yaakov's lien. Yaakov therefore may seize from Yehudah even if Reuven did not write to Yaakov an after-acquired property clause. But if Reuven lent to Shimon only after Reuven had borrowed from Yaakov, Yaakov cannot seize from Yehudah the land that Yehudah collected from Shimon unless Reuven wrote to Yaakov an after-acquired property clause. This is the Sema's view. The Shach disagrees and holds that in every case no after-acquired property clause is required, and Yaakov may seize from Yehudah because Shimon became obligated to Yaakov through Rabbi Natan's rule. If Yehudah collected movable property from Shimon, the Sema holds that if Reuven wrote to Yaakov a clause encumbering movables together with land, Yaakov may seize the movables from Yehudah even if Shimon did not write such a clause to Reuven. The Shach holds the reverse: Reuven's writing a movables-with-land clause to Yaakov is entirely irrelevant. Everything depends on whether Shimon wrote such a clause to Reuven. If he did, Yaakov can collect from Yehudah; if not, Yaakov cannot seize from him. This is the root of their dispute, and it is stated again below in siman 111. See there.
Now, the foundation upon which the Shach rests his position is Tosafot in the second chapter of Pesachim. The Gemara challenges Rava, who holds that a creditor acquires the debtor's property only from the time of actual collection, from Rav Nachman's rule that if orphans collected land in payment of a debt owed to their father, their father's creditor may in turn collect that land from them. Why? Let the orphans say, "Our father left us movable property, and we are purchasers, and movables are not subject to the creditor's lien." The Gemara answers that the property is subject through Rabbi Natan's rule, etc. Tosafot ask that if so, when the orphans collected movable property we should say the same thing, namely that the movables are subject through Rabbi Natan. They answer that this applies specifically to land, which is naturally subject to a lien. When that land is subject to Reuven's claim, we regard it as though it were already in Reuven's possession, because if the debtor sold or mortgaged it, Reuven could still return and collect that land. It is therefore also subject to Reuven's creditor. Movable property, by contrast, cannot be regarded as though it were already in Reuven's possession, since if the debtor sold or mortgaged it the creditor could not collect it from the purchaser. These are the words of Tosafot. On this the Shach erected the foundation of his position and holds that everything depends on whether the last borrower can sell or mortgage the property. Accordingly, if the final borrower encumbered movable property together with land, the movables are no longer within his unrestricted power to sell or mortgage, because his creditor can seize them. On this theory movables and land are equivalent. If orphans collected such movables, their father's creditor can collect them from the orphans, and similarly, if the intermediate creditor sold the debt instrument, his creditor may collect from the purchaser just as from land. In truth, these conclusions have no sound basis, for several reasons and from several clear proofs. First, consider Tosafot themselves. If their intention were as the Shach says, namely that everything depends solely on whether the final borrower can sell the property, why did Tosafot need to say in their answer that the land is regarded as though it were already in the second creditor's possession? They write explicitly that "we regard it as though it were in Reuven's possession, and therefore it is also subject to his creditor." See the Maharsha, who explains explicitly that Tosafot introduced a new point in their answer. They did not say that the final borrower is directly indebted to the first creditor. Rather, they treat the land subject to the second creditor as though it were actually in the second creditor's possession. The wording of Tosafot itself indicates this. What need was there for that formulation? They could have retained the assumption of Tosafot's question, that the final borrower is directly indebted to the first creditor, but limited it to land, which cannot be diverted, as opposed to movables, which can be diverted. More strongly, why did Tosafot need to distinguish between property that can be sold and property that cannot? Once they answer that the property is merely regarded as being in the intermediate creditor's possession, as the Maharsha explains, the distinction should be simple. With land, if the intermediate creditor died leaving land, would his own creditor not collect it from the heirs? With movables, however, even if they are treated as already in the intermediate creditor's possession, they become movables of the heirs, from which his creditor cannot collect because movables of heirs were not subject to a creditor under the law of the Gemara. One who studies the Maharsha carefully will see that in truth this is how he understands Tosafot. It therefore requires explanation why Tosafot further distinguish between property that can be diverted and property that cannot. The words of Tosafot themselves are very difficult to reconcile. There is another difficulty according to the Shach. He holds that everything depends on whether the final borrower wrote to the second creditor a clause encumbering movables together with land. This indicates that, in his view, the second creditor functions as the agent of the first, as though the final borrower had written that clause directly in favor of the first creditor. If so, consider the Mishnah in Ketubot, chapter 2, concerning one who was a creditor of his brother and the brother died. He may not say, "Since I am the heir, I will retain the property," etc. The Gemara says that this is not an accepted Mishnah because we do not combine two stringencies in a ketubah, Rabbi Meir's view that movables are subject to a ketubah and Rabbi Natan's rule that one creditor may collect directly from a debtor of his debtor. According to the Shach, why reject the baraita from halachah? We could establish it where the yavam wrote to his brother a movables-with-land clause. Then the woman should collect from him just as, according to the Shach, a first creditor collects movables from heirs that otherwise would not be subject. See also what I will write as another proof against the Shach. But first we must clarify Tosafot's intent, because it requires analysis and seems as though Tosafot are riding two different horses. Initially it occurred to me to resolve their language with some difficulty. Although Tosafot write that we regard the property as though it were in the intermediate creditor's possession, they may not mean literally in his possession, since actual collection has not yet occurred. Rather, the meaning may be that it is regarded as though he had collected the property and then deposited it with the final borrower. If so, these movables ultimately are not physically in the intermediate creditor's possession, but in another person's hands. This would answer the earlier difficulty of why Tosafot needed the distinction between property that can be diverted and property that cannot, instead of answering simply that even if movables are regarded as collected they become movables of heirs. On this reading they are regarded as already collected and then redeposited with another person, so they are not in the heirs' possession. Tosafot hold in Ketubot and in the chapter Yesh Nochalin that movable property held by a third party is not included in the rule that movables of heirs are not subject, even according to Rabbi Akiva. Rabbi Akiva's reason there is only that the creditor requires an oath. See there. Where the creditor was granted trust and does not require an oath, he indeed collects. Thus here as well the first creditor would be able to collect directly from the final borrower. Tosafot therefore had to distinguish that with movables the fiction of prior collection does not apply because they can be diverted. This would also answer why the baraita concerning one who was a creditor of his brother was not established as a case where he had written a movables-with-land clause. Even if the movables were regarded as previously collected, they would still be movables of heirs, as above. Nor can we say that they are considered deposited with another person. That works where the debtor is a third person, but here the debtor himself is the heir, and the property cannot be treated as deposited with another person. Tosafot explicitly write this there, s.v. lo ashkachan. If this interpretation were correct, it would itself refute the Shach's ruling. Certainly where a purchaser bought from the intermediate creditor, the first creditor would have no right to seize from the purchaser. The whole concept is only that the movables are treated as collected into the intermediate creditor's possession, and then he himself sold them. What difference does it make whether they were physically in his possession or deposited with another person, as against a purchaser? Moreover, even regarding heirs Tosafot's ruling would not remain halachah. Tosafot follow their own view that, according to Rabbi Akiva, if the creditor was granted trust he can collect from property held by another, which means deposited property is not considered movables of heirs. But we do not rule like Tosafot on that point. Even if the creditor was exempted from an oath, he has no claim. We must therefore say that such property is still treated as movables of heirs. Consequently, the first creditor has no right in the movables even if they are notionally treated as already collected by the second creditor and redeposited, because they still have not escaped the category of movables of heirs. Yet the very explanation just offered does not appear correct. Apart from being strained, the earlier problem remains: why was Tosafot compelled to say that the property is regarded as collected by the intermediate creditor rather than simply retaining their original assumption that the final borrower is directly obligated to the first creditor, but only with land and not with movables? More importantly, there is a decisive difficulty. If, as the Shach says, wherever diversion is impossible, such as when movables were encumbered together with land, the first creditor can collect, then consider the Mishnah concerning a deposit held by another person. Rabbi Akiva says it should be given to the heirs. What is accomplished by giving it to them? The father's creditor should then come and seize it, just as he seizes land that the heirs collected. The bailee is subject under Rabbi Natan's rule, because Rabbi Natan applies to a deposit as well, as is clear in the chapter Shor Shenagach Arbaah VaChamishah and as the Rashba states explicitly in his novellae to the first chapter of Kiddushin. And can the bailee divert the deposit? How could one sell someone else's property? Are we dealing with wicked people? The depositor recovers his property wherever it is, apart from protections such as market regulation. But certainly there is no reasonable assumption that a bailee may sell another's deposit. A deposit is stronger than a movables-with-land lien. The deposited objects actually belong to the depositor, and if the depositor consecrates or sells them his act takes effect because they remain fully within his legal possession. This directly refutes the Shach's rule that if the second debtor gave the intermediate creditor a movables-with-land lien the first creditor collects, whereas if the second debtor's deposit is in another's hands the first creditor does not collect. Surely an actual deposit is no weaker than a contractual movables-with-land lien. Therefore it is clear that Tosafot required both rationales together and that each is needed. Consider the ruling stated there, and likewise at the end of Ketubot, where Reuven lent to Shimon, Shimon lent to Reuven, and Reuven died. If Shimon has only movable property with which to pay, Reuven's heirs collect from him and Shimon loses. Why can Shimon not say: "These movables of mine that you seek to collect, which were subject to your father, are in turn subject to me through Rabbi Natan because your father owed me"? If the answer is that the movables are regarded as already collected into Reuven's possession and thus became movables of his heirs, that still does not solve the case when the movables remain in Shimon's own hands. If they are treated as having been collected into Reuven's possession, Shimon should be regarded as having seized his debtor's movables during Reuven's lifetime, and such seizure is effective according to everyone. It is clear that Shimon's possession during Reuven's lifetime should count as seizure, since Reuven owed him the same amount that he owed Reuven. See the Taz and the gloss of the Chacham Tzvi in seif 6 on this point. Tosafot therefore had to answer that no lien under Rabbi Natan takes effect on movables because they can be diverted. Thus they are not regarded as already collected into Reuven's possession. Since actual collection remains necessary, there can be no seizure by Shimon, because what is he seizing if Rabbi Natan's lien never attached to his movables? The movables of Shimon that were subject to Reuven never became subject to Reuven's creditor through Rabbi Natan. This is satisfactory. Tosafot therefore needed both principles. Their first answer, that the property is only regarded as collected by the intermediate creditor and that we do not say the final borrower is directly obligated to the first creditor, resolves the difficulty from the Mishnah concerning a deposit, where the property cannot be diverted. There, because it is only treated as collected into the intermediate creditor's possession, when he dies it becomes movables of his heirs. But that answer alone would not suffice, because the case of two mutual borrowers would remain difficult, as explained: Shimon's possession would count as seizure, and it would not matter that the movables became movables of heirs. Tosafot therefore add that with movables Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply because they can be diverted. Both principles are thus necessary together. These points are simple, straight, and sound.
The conclusion from this is that even if the final borrower wrote a movables-with-land clause to the intermediate creditor, the first creditor nevertheless has no right in movables collected from the final borrower as against the intermediate creditor's heirs, under the law of the Gemara. The reason is that the movables are only regarded as though they had been collected into the intermediate creditor's possession, after which they become movables of his heirs. All the more so where a purchaser bought the debt instrument from the intermediate creditor and then collected movables from the final borrower. It is as though the purchaser acquired those movables from the intermediate creditor. What right does the intermediate creditor's creditor have in them? We do not say that the second borrower became directly obligated to the first creditor. On this point there should be no doubt or dispute, as shown by the many proofs above, and further proof will be given. But if the movables-with-land clause was written in favor of the first creditor, while the final borrower did not write such a clause to his own creditor, it appears from Tosafot discussed above that this still does not help. Even if the movables were considered already collected, so that they would not become free movables of the heirs because the first creditor has his own movables-with-land lien, nevertheless the final borrower can still divert them, since he did not write such a clause to his creditor. Accordingly, they never become subject through Rabbi Natan at all. This is contrary to the Sema, who holds that if the first creditor has a movables-with-land lien he may seize the movables. The Rashba writes similarly in his novellae, in the later supplement at the end of Kiddushin, that if one expressly encumbered movables the creditor can collect movables from heirs, yet he still cannot collect a debt owed to their father, because such a debt is not subject at all even where the father expressly encumbered movables. This is explicit against the Sema. If a movables-with-land clause allows seizure from purchasers, how could it fail to allow collection from heirs? The matter is explained even more clearly in Tosafot to Bekhorot 52, s.v. velo bera'ui. Tosafot write that after the enactment of the Geonim allowing collection from movables of heirs, a creditor may collect even from a loan owed to the deceased, and even if the heirs collected cash. Since the Geonim treated movables like land, the obligation becomes subject through Rabbi Natan. Just as with land the law treats the land collected by the heirs as though their father had collected it during his lifetime, so too with movables after the enactment of the Geonim. Tosafot then write at the end that one can still distinguish between movables and land because the lien on land is stronger with respect to seizure from purchasers, unlike the lien on movables. These are their words. This is exactly what we have written. With land, because it is naturally subject to a lien, the lien attaches. With movables it does not. This precisely matches Tosafot's answer in Pesachim discussed above. Tosafot require the two rationales in combination. Thus the Sema's rule does not follow this Tosafot either. Under this approach, both creditors would need movables-with-land clauses. One must then explain Rabbi Akiva's ruling regarding a loan and a deposit held by others, where he says they are given to the heirs who do not require an oath. The implication is that if the creditor had been granted trust he could collect. Why should that be so? Even if such property is not treated as movables of heirs, Rabbi Natan should still not apply because the property can supposedly be diverted, and the third party could say, "You are not my litigant." The debt should also be considered merely prospective property because Rabbi Natan does not apply to it. One can answer either that Tosafot hold, as they write there in Bekhorot, that even the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Natan admit this particular rule because the property never came into the heirs' possession at all, or that Tosafot accept the view of most authorities that in truth according to Rabbi Akiva even where the creditor was granted trust he still cannot collect, and the property always goes to the heirs. See the Rashba's novellae to Kiddushin. Nor can one challenge this from the fact that the baraita concerning one who was a creditor of his brother is established as following Rabbi Meir, who holds that movables are subject to a ketubah, together with Rabbi Natan. What does Rabbi Meir add? Even if the property is treated as collected, why would movables be subject through Rabbi Natan if they can be diverted? We can answer, as above, that the case is where the brother encumbered movables together with land. Still, even though the movables are treated as though collected into the husband's possession, ordinarily movables would not be subject to the ketubah. The Gemara therefore needs Rabbi Meir. This is the approach of Tosafot in Pesachim and Bekhorot. Tosafot in Bava Batra 125b, s.v. ve'ein habekhor, however, do not appear to agree. They prove that a woman collects her ketubah from a debt owed to her husband from Rabbi Akiva's statement that all claimants require an oath, implying that one who was granted trust may collect. Those same Tosafot in Bava Batra 159, s.v. velimru, write that Rav Nachman's rule concerning orphans who collected land applies even though the property would otherwise be considered prospective inheritance, because it is subject through Rabbi Natan. It follows that when they rule definitively that a woman collects her ketubah from a debt owed to her husband, they do so because of Rabbi Natan even where the debtor pays movables. This shows that in their view Rabbi Natan applies even to movables, and they do not say that the debtor's ability to divert them prevents the lien. Moreover, they write explicitly that after the enactment of the Geonim allowing collection from movables, movables are equivalent to land. Just as with land, Rabbi Natan causes the property to be regarded as though the father collected it during his lifetime, so too with movables. These are their words. They do not give the simpler reason that even without Rabbi Natan, since a creditor can now collect movables left in the heirs' hands, why should a debt owed to the deceased be any worse? We must say that a debt is indeed worse because it is prospective property, as the Rashba explicitly writes at the end of Kiddushin. Tosafot therefore needed Rabbi Natan to treat it as though the father had already collected it. If these Tosafot agreed with Tosafot in Pesachim, the comparison between land and movables would fail: the land cannot be diverted and therefore a lien attaches, whereas movables can be diverted and therefore no lien attaches. This is exactly the distinction Tosafot themselves make in Bekhorot. It follows that Tosafot in Bava Batra do not accept that distinction. They hold that everything depends on whether the property is regarded as though the father already collected it. This is the difference between land and movables under the original law, because even if the father had collected movables, they would become movables of the heirs. After the enactment of the Geonim, that concern no longer applies. Regarding the earlier difficulty from two people who borrowed from one another, another answer must therefore be given, namely that the debtor's possession is not considered a valid seizure, as many authorities explain. The Ramban likewise writes in his novellae to Bava Batra in the sugya of a firstborn taking prospective property that after the enactment of the Geonim a woman collects her ketubah from a debt owed to her husband, because this is like heirs collecting land, from which the creditor collects through Rabbi Natan. The same applies now after the enactment of the Geonim, even though the lien on land is not comparable to the lien on movables because even after the enactment, if the debtor sold movables the creditor cannot seize them from the purchaser, etc. The Ramban thus rejects with both hands the distinction based on whether the property can be sold. He then writes explicitly that some disagree and hold that even after the enactment of the Geonim a woman still cannot collect her ketubah from a debt owed to her husband, because the Geonim did not enact so far as to permit collection from debts. But if the husband granted her a movables-with-land lien, then in every respect those movables are treated like land. The Ramban thus writes explicitly in accordance with the Sema that a movables-with-land clause in favor of the first creditor is effective. This source escaped the Shach's attention. Moreover, the rationale of Tosafot in Pesachim is not accepted by all authorities who rule that after the enactment of the Geonim a woman collects her ketubah from debts. The authorities also rule concerning one who was a creditor of his brother, as do the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 168, and their ruling rests on Rabbi Natan together with the enactment of the Geonim, as is clear from the Rif and Rosh. They do not require the second debtor to have written a movables-with-land clause. This proves that Rabbi Natan applies even to movables that the debtor can divert, contrary to Tosafot in Pesachim and Bekhorot. The Rashba likewise writes explicitly in responsum 914 that although we say specifically that if orphans collected land their father's creditor may collect it from them through Rabbi Natan, but not if they collected movables, nevertheless where the deceased expressly encumbered movables to the first creditor, the first creditor can collect even movables through Rabbi Natan. This makes clear that the Rashba is not concerned at all with Tosafot's rationale in Pesachim that Rabbi Natan's lien does not attach to movables because the debtor can sell them. Even in the Rashba's case the debtor could sell them. The essential reason is simply that although the movables are regarded as though collected into the intermediate creditor's possession, they would ordinarily become movables of heirs. The Sema's words are therefore clear, while all the Shach's arguments stem from insufficient examination of Tosafot and the authorities. The Ritva likewise writes in Ketubot, chapter 2, that if a movables-with-land clause was written in favor of the first creditor, he may seize from the second creditor even according to the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Natan, because the money that the first creditor lent to the intermediate debtor became subject to him. See there. The Shach does, however, raise one good argument. According to the explanation that the movables-with-land clause was written in favor of the creditor who now seeks to seize property, namely the earlier creditor Reuven as explained in Urim, and the later creditor Shimon comes to seize, why is an after-acquired property clause needed? Reuven's debt instrument against Shimon already existed before Reuven later borrowed from Shimon, and we rule that debt instruments themselves can be encumbered together with land. If so, the purchaser took property already subject to Shimon's lien. Why should an after-acquired property clause be necessary? This is indeed a good argument. But the language of the Ramban and Tur indicates that they did not approach the matter through the theory that the debt instrument itself was directly encumbered. Rather, they proceed through Rabbi Natan's rule, as explained above. We must therefore say that the case involves no existing written instrument, but only witnesses to a kinyan, or else the instrument was lost and it is known what had been written in it. If the instrument itself no longer exists, the underlying loan cannot be encumbered merely by an agav arrangement, because the money itself is not physically present and there is no instrument. What exactly would be encumbered? Even regarding an existing instrument, the Rashba and Ritva strongly dispute whether such an agav transfer works, as seen in Ketubot 82. All the more so when no instrument exists, the loan itself cannot be encumbered through agav. We must therefore rely on Rabbi Natan, and the matter is correct. From here the first dispute between the Shach and Sema also depends on the same issue. Yaakov lent to Reuven, afterward Reuven lent to Shimon, and Yehudah, who purchased Reuven's instrument against Shimon, collected land from Shimon. According to the Shach's theory, everything depends on whether Shimon, the final borrower, could divert the property. If the field Yehudah collected from Shimon had already belonged to Shimon when he borrowed from Reuven, then Shimon could not effectively sell it free of Reuven's lien because Reuven could seize it from a purchaser. It should therefore also be subject to Yaakov through Rabbi Natan. But according to what I established above, the controlling principle is not whether Shimon could divert the property. Rather, we ask: if the property is treated as though already collected into Reuven's possession, and Reuven then sold it, could Yaakov seize it from Reuven's purchaser? Rabbi Natan only treats the property as if it had been collected by Reuven. If Reuven then sold it, what right does Yaakov have? Since Yaakov's instrument predated Reuven's loan to Shimon, Shimon's land is after-acquired property with respect to Yaakov. Had Reuven himself acquired that land and then sold it, Yaakov could not seize it from the purchaser without an after-acquired property clause. It is therefore obvious that Yaakov needs such a clause in order to seize the land Yehudah collected. For this reason the two rulings depend on one another.
But the Sema's position is explicit in Baal HaTerumot, Gate 43, part 4, rule 18. He writes that where a borrower has no assets other than debts owed to him by others, the borrower's creditor may collect those debts through Rabbi Natan even if the borrower did not write a movables-with-land clause or an after-acquired property clause at all. This is true even from heirs according to the view that property acquired by the debtor and then inherited by his heirs is subject even without an after-acquired property clause. There is, however, an authority who disagrees where the borrower died and had not written such a clause. All this is straightforward. See there. Thus Baal HaTerumot states explicitly that when property is collected from the second borrower and the intermediate creditor has died, the result depends on the dispute whether an after-acquired property clause is needed to collect from heirs. According to the view that it is needed, the first creditor cannot collect without it because the case is treated as though the intermediate creditor himself had collected the property and then left it to his children. If so, all the more so where the intermediate creditor sold the property, everyone agrees that an after-acquired property clause is required and the first creditor cannot seize from the purchaser without it. This source escaped the Shach's attention at the time. Baal HaTerumot later likewise writes that if Reuven's loan to Shimon postdates Yaakov's loan to Reuven, collection from heirs depends on the same issue, whether an after-acquired property clause is required. I am nevertheless uncertain about the Sema's further statement that if Reuven first lent to Shimon, and only afterward Yaakov lent to Reuven, and Reuven later sold his instrument to Yehudah, Yaakov may seize from Yehudah even if Yaakov's instrument contains no after-acquired property clause, because when Yaakov lent to Reuven, Reuven's instrument against Shimon already existed. I have a doubt about this. What difference does it make that the debt instrument already existed? We rule that a creditor acquires the debtor's property only from the time of actual collection. The field Yehudah collected from Shimon therefore became Reuven's only at the time of collection. Even if Reuven had held an instrument against Shimon for a long time, before actual collection the particular field was not Reuven's. If Shimon had consecrated or sold it earlier, Reuven had no ownership in it as such until collection. By the time of collection Yaakov's instrument against Reuven already existed. That field should therefore still be treated as after-acquired property. Why should the existence of the debt instrument at the earlier date matter when we follow the time of collection for ownership? Yet I saw that Baal HaTerumot in the cited place writes explicitly like the Sema, that if the instrument the debtor made to Reuven predates the instrument Reuven made to Shimon, the property is treated like property already acquired and then borrowed against, so it is subject without any after-acquired property clause. However, the Rashba in the cited responsum 914 writes explicitly otherwise. If Reuven borrowed from Shimon in the first year of the Shemittah cycle and from Levi in the second year, while Reuven himself had debts owed to him, even though those debts predated both Shimon and Levi, neither creditor takes priority in what is later collected. Rather, it is treated like a case where one borrowed from two creditors and then acquired property, so they divide it. This is explicit like what I wrote. Although Shimon's lien attached to those debts a year before Levi's lien, it is still treated as "borrowed from one, borrowed from another, and then acquired property," because we follow the time of actual collection, which came later than both loans, since we rule that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. One should not emend that responsum by changing its statement that the debts were earlier to say they were later. The Beit Yosef in siman 104 also quotes it as written. Moreover, the responsum carefully explains all the possible cases. Had preexisting debts given Shimon priority because his claim arose in the first year, the Rashba should have explained that case as well. It is therefore clear that no emendation is appropriate. I later saw that Tosafot themselves were uncertain about this reasoning. In Bava Batra 157, s.v. amar Rav Huna, concerning the question whether an after-acquired property clause is effective, Tosafot write that proof could not have been brought from Rav Nachman's rule that if orphans collected land their father's creditor may collect from them, because that is based on Rabbi Natan. They add that even if proof could be brought from there, a Mishnah would be preferable. We must understand their additional answer. It introduces a strained explanation, especially according to the Maharsha, who says that perhaps Rav Nachman's ruling follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that a person can transfer something not yet in existence. Yet Tosafot's first answer appears independently sufficient. Rav Nachman's rule that a creditor may collect land from orphans who collected it on their father's debt may rest either on Rabbi Natan or on Abaye's theory that the creditor acquires retroactively. According to Abaye it is obvious that the case would not involve after-acquired property. We must therefore say that Tosafot in their second answer did not accept the first answer as complete. They reasoned that saying the property is subject through Rabbi Natan does not itself solve the after-acquired property problem. Since a creditor acquires only from the time of collection, when the orphans collect the land it is only then treated as having been collected by the intermediate creditor, not earlier. If an after-acquired property clause is ineffective, how can the first creditor seize it? Rabbi Natan does not mean that the property was directly subject to the first creditor from the outset, but only that it is treated as if it were in the intermediate creditor's possession. If so, it remains after-acquired property in relation to the first creditor. Tosafot therefore offer the alternative answer that a Mishnah would be preferable. Under their first answer, however, we must say that they establish Rav Nachman's case as one where the debt owed to the father preceded the father's own debt. Because it was already in existence when the father borrowed, and it becomes subject through Rabbi Natan, no after-acquired property clause is needed, as Baal HaTerumot and the Sema hold. Tosafot's two answers therefore reflect the two possible theories. But if the heirs collected on a debt that arose only after their father's own debt, it is obvious that an after-acquired property clause is required, because Rabbi Natan merely treats the property as though collected by the intermediate creditor, as explained above. In particular, Tosafot's second answer in Bava Batra just discussed shows that the only reason Rav Nachman's ruling could not prove the efficacy of an after-acquired property clause is that his case may involve a debt that predates the father's own borrowing, so Rabbi Natan could explain the result. This is straightforward. Tosafot themselves are thus uncertain between the two approaches, and who can decisively resolve their doubt? At first glance, according to the Ramban and those who agree with him, who hold that where no after-acquired property clause was written even heirs are not bound by later-acquired property, as I wrote at length below in siman 111, there is proof for the Sema. Rami bar Chama establishes the Mishnah's rule that "this one collects and that one collects" as a case involving orphans, etc. Rava responds that there are two objections, and one of them is that the living creditor should pay them land and then collect that same land back. What is the difficulty? Perhaps the Mishnah concerns a case where no after-acquired property clause was written. Then the living creditor could not collect from the heirs because the land they received would be after-acquired property, exactly as Baal HaTerumot states explicitly according to this view. We must therefore say that Shimon, whose instrument is later, does not require an after-acquired property clause. In Rami bar Chama's establishment of the Mishnah as involving orphans, it must be Reuven, the earlier creditor, who died and whose orphans collect. If the reverse were true and Reuven were the living party, then Reuven would lose and could not collect from the orphans. In that case there would be no practical dispute between the Sages and Admon. According to Admon the earlier creditor Reuven would not collect, and according to the Sages he also would not collect, whatever the reason. There would be no difference in law. It must therefore be that Reuven died and his heirs collect according to the Sages but not according to Admon. Shimon, whose instrument is later, then can seize from the heirs without an after-acquired property clause. The Sema's ruling would follow necessarily. However, there is an independent difficulty according to Tosafot in Pesachim. They hold that wherever the debtor can divert the property by selling or mortgaging it, Rabbi Natan does not apply. The Shach accordingly writes that everyone agrees that if the final borrower paid with a field he acquired only after borrowing from his creditor, Rabbi Natan does not apply, because he could have sold that field just like movables. If so, when Rava asks that the living creditor should give them land and then collect it back, what is the difficulty? Perhaps the Mishnah concerns a person who owns no land, only cash, and teaches that the orphans collect while he loses, just as we established it as a case involving orphans. One can answer that since he has cash he should buy land in the market, for land of some quality is surely available, and then give that land to the orphans so that he will not lose his debt. Who would foolishly forgo this opportunity? This is straightforward. Yet according to the Shach's own premise there remains a difficulty. If he now purchases land, it is after-acquired property and Rabbi Natan does not apply to it because it remained within his power to divert it. The law of that land would then be like movables, and he could not recover anything from the orphans because it would be treated as though they had collected movables. The Mishnah could concern a case with no after-acquired property clause. One might answer that the Gemara assumes otherwise from Admon's position. Why does Admon say, "You should not have borrowed," etc.? Perhaps the debtor's intent was collusion and diversion from the second creditor. He intended to sell the debt instrument, and the Mishnah speaks without qualification even where he owns no land. In particular, if we say Shimon owns no land, he can claim that Reuven likewise owned no land. The purpose might have been to divert the cash, so that when Shimon later came with his instrument he would emerge empty handed. It must therefore be that Shimon's lien predates the purchaser's rights. This in turn would prove either that land was available or that an after-acquired property clause had been written. But all this is strained. It therefore appears that Rami bar Chama did not prefer the answer that one loan was for ten years and one for five because that answer requires the forced assumption that the case is specifically in a place where money is given before the document is written and specifically on the very day the term expires. The entire Mishnah would then revolve around one particular day. If instead the practice were to write the document first, the Ramban notes that this would be identical to the first clause. Rami bar Chama therefore establishes the Mishnah as involving orphans. Then it may concern a place where the document is written first, and there is a true novelty: one party collects while the other does not because of the orphans. It teaches that the orphans collect, whereas against them the debt is treated as movables of heirs even if the living creditor has possession. That is a substantial novelty. Still, even in a place where the document is written first, the reason of the Sages remains difficult. We must say, as the Ramban writes in Sefer HaMilchamot, that he borrowed from the other party in order to obtain a pledge. See there. But what kind of pledge is meant? Perhaps the debtor will sell the debt instrument. With land, as I wrote at the beginning of the siman, there is publicity and the creditor can prevent transfer. But a debt instrument can be transferred by writing and delivery, or by a kinyan through agav land and similar methods. What publicity exists? We must say that even if he sells it, the earlier creditor can still seize it by virtue of his prior instrument. Yet when Shimon, whose later instrument is sold, seeks to defeat the earlier Reuven, this necessarily requires Reuven's earlier instrument to include an after-acquired property clause. It follows that such a clause must have been written. Presumably both instruments contain it, because it would be strained to say one contains it and the other does not. The Gemara's argument therefore works because the case necessarily involves an after-acquired property clause. This is satisfactory. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch, who above rule without qualification concerning a place where documents are written first and do not state a condition that an after-acquired property clause be present, omit the Ramban's rationale that he borrowed in order to obtain a pledge. The Tur evidently does not accept Tosafot's Pesachim rationale as stated above, nor the Ramban's separate view that even heirs require an after-acquired property clause. Without those premises there is no difficulty.
