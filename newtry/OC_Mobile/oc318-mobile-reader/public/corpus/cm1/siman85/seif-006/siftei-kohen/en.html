He then collects from him again, etc. Sema writes that this appears to apply even without an after-acquired-property clause. In his view, the case is specifically where the earlier creditor sold his note. If the later creditor sold his note, however, the earlier creditor cannot return and collect the land from the purchaser unless his own note includes a lien on after-acquired property. Bach objects that since the collection rests on Rabbi Natan’s lien, no after-acquired-property clause is needed. Bach’s conclusion appears primary. This follows from Pesachim 31a. Rava holds that a creditor acquires the debtor’s property only at the time of actual collection, so if the creditor consecrated or sold it before collecting, his act is ineffective. Yet Rava also holds that where heirs collected land in satisfaction of debts owed to their father, their father’s creditor may collect that land from them. The reason is that just as the second debtor was obligated to their father, he was directly obligated to their father’s creditor through Rabbi Natan’s rule. Otherwise, the heirs would be treated as having acquired entirely new property that was never subject to the creditor’s lien. It follows that the second debtor is regarded as though he borrowed directly from the first creditor. His land, specifically land and not movables, as the Gemara and Tosafot show, became subject immediately to the first creditor through Rabbi Natan’s lien. No after-acquired-property clause is therefore required. The first creditor collects by saying that this land was directly subject to him through the second debtor’s obligation under Rabbi Natan, and there is no difference whether the intermediate creditor is himself or another person, as the sugya indicates. Otherwise, another difficulty would arise. The purchaser acquired only a promissory note, which is movable property. How could the original creditor seize that note? The answer must be that when the purchaser later collected land, that land had already been subject through Rabbi Natan. The second debtor is regarded as having borrowed directly from the first creditor, and the land became subject to him immediately. For this same reason no after-acquired-property clause is necessary. Study the sugya carefully and this will be clear. See also what I write below in siman 111, seifim 22 and 23.
If he obligated movables together with land, etc. Ir Shushan writes, “If the seller obligated movables together with land,” and Sema, seif katan 22, likewise explains that even where the earlier creditor sold his note, and even if an after-acquired-property clause is ordinarily presumed in a lien on movables together with land, the later creditor can collect from the purchaser only because the earlier creditor obligated movables together with land. The purchaser may therefore say that this clause covered only movables already acquired by the earlier creditor and not property that had not yet come into his hands, unless the earlier creditor expressly included after-acquired property. Their words are astonishing. What help is it that the seller obligated movables together with land? The movables later collected by the purchaser remain movables. Rather, the case plainly concerns the person from whom the movables are being collected. He obligated his movables together with land, thereby giving them the law of land. This is the simple meaning of Tur, who discusses the obligation of the party whose movables are now being seized. Whether the seller himself obligated movables together with land makes no difference. Ran states this expressly in the second chapter of Dayanei Gezeirot: if the debtor paid the intermediate creditor with movables, they are not subject to the latter’s creditor unless the debtor had obligated those movables together with land, in which case their law is like land, as is shown in Chazakat HaBatim in the discussion of one who sold a cow and cloak. The same follows from Pesachim and Tosafot cited below in seif katan 24. The decisive clause must have been given by the debtor whose movables are being collected. Sema’s interpretation raises a further difficulty. If the earlier seller obligated movables together with land, Tur holds that promissory notes themselves may be subjected through such a clause, as I explain above in siman 66, seif 10, seif katan 25, contrary to Rashba’s responsum 914. The seller’s creditor would then seize the note itself even if the second debtor never obligated his own movables to the seller. The note has the law of land. This is also explicit below in siman 111, seif 22. Why, then, would an after-acquired-property clause be needed? The earlier creditor’s note already existed when the later creditor’s lien arose. The correct explanation is therefore that the second debtor, the debtor of the seller, obligated his own movables together with land, while the seller did not need to give such a clause. This is clear. It is nevertheless true that if the seller himself obligated movables together with land, his creditor may seize the promissory notes even where the second debtor paid with movables and had not subjected his own movables together with land. In that separate case, if the earlier creditor sold the note, the later creditor may seize it without an after-acquired-property clause, because the note already existed. If the later creditor sold his note, however, the earlier creditor may seize only if his note contains an after-acquired-property clause. Understand this distinction carefully.
That he acquired and will acquire. Without both terms, the debtor can say, “I acquired these movables only afterward, and they were never subjected together with land.” With land, however, it is ordinarily known when he acquired it. A similar rule appears below in siman 104, seif 5. Nevertheless, where it is known that the second debtor acquired the land only after the loan and his note lacked an after-acquired-property clause, the first creditor cannot collect it. Tosafot in Pesachim explain that land collected by the heirs is subject to their father’s creditor through Rabbi Natan. They ask why the same should not apply to movables and answer that only land is inherently subject to liens. When such land is subject to Reuven, it is treated as though it were in his sphere, meaning not literally in his possession, for otherwise Rava would allow him to consecrate or sell it, but as though the first creditor had lent directly to the second debtor. If the land is sold or mortgaged, Reuven can still collect it, and it is therefore also subject to Reuven’s creditor. Movables are not so treated, because if sold or mortgaged they cannot be collected. Accordingly, land acquired by the second debtor only after the loan, where his creditor lacked an after-acquired-property clause, could have been sold free of that creditor’s lien. It is then like movables and is not inherently subject. The first creditor cannot have greater rights than the second creditor. Rabbi Natan’s lien therefore does not attach to it either. This is the primary ruling.
