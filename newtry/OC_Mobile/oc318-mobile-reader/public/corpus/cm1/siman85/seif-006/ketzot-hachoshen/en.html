He goes back and collects from him. The Sema explains that this applies specifically where the earlier creditor sold his document. Suppose Reuven lent Shimon in Nisan, and Shimon lent Reuven in Tishrei. If Reuven sells his Nisan document, Shimon can collect from the purchaser because the field collected by the purchaser was already encumbered to Reuven from Nisan and later became encumbered to Shimon through the Tishrei loan. But if Shimon sells the later document and the purchaser collects land from Reuven, Sema says Reuven cannot retake that land on account of his earlier debt unless Shimon had written an after acquired property clause in Reuven's favor. Similarly, if movables were encumbered together with land, Sema allows recovery from the purchaser only where the seller himself had subjected those movables in the necessary manner. The Taz generally agrees, though he argues that an express after acquired clause may be unnecessary because the entire reciprocal transaction was clearly structured to preserve collection rights. I have already rejected that reasoning above. The Shach, following Bach, takes a different approach. He says that no after acquired clause is needed here even when the later creditor sold his document, because Rabbi Natan's lien gives the earlier creditor a direct right in the later debtor's claim. Chacham Tzvi attacks the Bach and Shach. He argues that the proof from heirs who collect land on their father's debt is unlike our case. There the heirs' collection can be treated as though the father himself collected the land during his lifetime and then left it to them, so the father's creditor reaches it. But if a later creditor himself collected land and then sold it to another purchaser, an earlier creditor could not ordinarily seize from that purchaser absent the necessary lien. Why should a purchaser of the later debt be in a worse position than the later creditor himself? In my view the Bach and Shach are correct, based on Tosafot in Perek Kol Sha'ah. The Gemara asks, according to the view that a creditor acquires only from collection onward, why a creditor can later recover land collected by heirs on their father's debt. It answers that Rabbi Natan's lien applies: just as the land was encumbered to the father, it is encumbered to the father's creditor. Tosafot asks why the same should not apply to movables. They answer that land is inherently lienable. When land is already encumbered to Reuven, we treat it as though it is in Reuven's legal hand, because if the debtor sells or mortgages it Reuven can seize it. The right is therefore strong enough to pass through Rabbi Natan's lien to Reuven's creditor. Movables are different because Reuven could not follow them after sale or mortgage. This shows that Rabbi Natan's lien is not merely an ordinary property lien upon the debtor's claim. Rather, the Torah gives the first creditor the very substantive debt owed to his debtor, as though the intermediate debtor had lent directly to the first creditor. If it were only an ordinary lien, no special verse would be needed because all of the debtor's property is already encumbered. And ordinary debts are otherwise intangible and not subject to standard liens in the same way, as Rashba and Ran explain. Therefore when the intermediate debtor has a strong right in land, a right that would survive sale, Rabbi Natan transfers that very strength to the first creditor. The fact that the first creditor's own original claim would regard the land as after acquired does not matter in the ordinary way, because he is not receiving a newly created property lien. He is stepping into the intermediate creditor's existing substantive right. By contrast, if the intermediate creditor's own right is weak, as with movables that can be sold free of lien or with land that itself arose only as after acquired property not covered by the original lien, Rabbi Natan does not strengthen it beyond what the intermediate creditor possessed. Tosafot's distinction between land and movables therefore rests precisely on the strength of the intermediate right. Accordingly, the entire matter depends upon the nature of the intermediate creditor's right. Where it is strong and surviving, Rabbi Natan places that right directly in the hands of the first creditor, and no additional after acquired clause is needed. Where the intermediate right is itself weak or defeasible, Rabbi Natan does not cure it. This is the Shach's approach and it is correct. See further what I write in siman 86, seif katan 1.
If he subjected movables together with land. The Sema explains the clause as referring to the seller having subjected his movables together with land. The Shach rejects that reading and explains that the debtor had subjected movables together with land to the seller, so that the seller's lien over those movables had the strength of a land lien. According to the Shach's own approach, this is coherent, for, as I wrote above, everything depends on how strong the intermediate creditor's lien against his debtor is. If that lien is strong, Rabbi Natan's lien carries the whole underlying right over to the first creditor. Nevertheless, the Shulchan Aruch cannot be explained according to the Shach in our times. Today, even where movables were subjected together with land, one ordinarily does not collect them from a purchaser because of the market overt enactment. The debtor can therefore still sell them, and the lien over the movables is no longer comparable to a land lien. We must therefore return to the Sema's reading, that the seller himself subjected the debt instrument together with land. The market overt enactment does not apply to such a documentary lien, as the Rosh and Tur explain in siman 60. The Sema's reading, however, raises a different question. Why is an after acquired property clause needed once the body of the debt instrument itself was already subjected? His explanation, that the instrument had not yet reached the seller's hands, is difficult, and in siman 111 the Sema appears to rule otherwise. According to the Shach's theory, another consequence follows. If a debtor held only an oral loan against another person and transferred that debt through a three party arrangement, then even if the purchaser later collected land on that debt, the first creditor cannot retake it through Rabbi Natan's lien merely because he held a written loan against the transferor. The decisive point is the strength of the intermediate creditor's right against his own debtor. An oral loan does not collect from purchasers. Its lien is therefore weak, like a movable lien, and Rabbi Natan's rule does not operate with the same force. This also clarifies Rashi in the sugyot concerning Reuven who sold land to Shimon with responsibility and then converted the purchase price into a loan. Rashi explains that Shimon did not pay cash but wrote a debt instrument for the purchase price. Why insist upon a written instrument? Because the later collection and recapture there proceed through Rabbi Natan's lien. Without a written debt instrument, the intermediate right would be too weak for that mechanism. This likewise explains the language that the purchase price was "converted into a loan." The same point resolves the Taz's question on Rambam. Rambam rules that where two people hold debt instruments against one another and one dies leaving minor heirs, the surviving creditor's position can be treated as though he had seized during the other's lifetime. Yet if the heirs collect movables from him, he cannot then recover those movables, because heirs' movables are not encumbered to creditors. The Taz asks: if the position is treated as lifetime seizure, why should movables be different? The answer is that there was never an ordinary physical seizure. With land, Rabbi Natan's lien gives the first creditor the very underlying right during the debtor's lifetime, so it is treated as prior acquisition. With movables, Rabbi Natan's lien does not operate with that strength. The surviving creditor is only attempting ordinary debt collection after death, and heirs' movables are not encumbered. The same reasoning explains the distinctions between medium and inferior quality land. Where Rabbi Natan's lien applies, the underlying debt is regarded as already belonging to the first creditor during the debtor's lifetime.
