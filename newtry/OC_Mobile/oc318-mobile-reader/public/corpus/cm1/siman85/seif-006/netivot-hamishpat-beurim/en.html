And one of them sold his debt to another. See Sema and Shach. Their dispute essentially turns on how Rabbi Natan's lien is understood. According to Sema, the first creditor's right to seize from the purchaser is an ordinary right of collecting from encumbered property. When one person lends to another and the borrower encumbers his property to the lender, the lender is treated as though he acquired an interest in that property. If the lender later borrows from the first borrower, the first borrower is treated as though he reacquired the property he had previously transferred. Accordingly, when the second borrower becomes obligated to the second lender, the second lender acquires the lien upon the second borrower's assets. If the second lender then sells that lien to a third party, this is like selling his property, and the first lender may seize it. Therefore, where the relevant asset is after acquired property not covered by the original lien, the first lender cannot seize it. On this approach, if the first lender sold his own promissory note before he later became the second borrower, the second creditor likewise cannot seize from the purchaser, because that purchaser's acquisition preceded his. The same analysis applies to movable property encumbered together with land. Everything depends on whether the relevant transferor wrote a clause encumbering movables together with land. When Reuven lends to Shimon, it is as though Reuven acquires Shimon's movables, for a creditor may collect even from the cloak on the debtor's shoulders. When Reuven later borrows from Shimon, it is as though he sells back to Shimon the movable property interest he previously acquired. When Reuven then sells his note against Shimon to Yehudah, it is as though he again sells to Yehudah the movable property interest that he had acquired from Shimon at the time of the first loan. Thus, if Reuven, who is the second borrower, wrote to Shimon at the time of the second loan that movable property is encumbered together with land, the movables of Shimon held by Reuven from the first loan take on the status of land. Selling the note against Shimon to Yehudah is then like selling land to Yehudah. Shimon can return and seize it because his acquisition preceded Yehudah's. In short, Sema treats lender and borrower as successive purchaser and seller. Shach, however, makes the matter depend on whether the second borrower is able to remove the relevant asset from the reach of the second lender. Where he cannot remove it, Rabbi Natan's lien attaches not only to the personal obligation but also to the second borrower's property lien, as though the second borrower were directly the first lender's debtor with respect to both the personal lien and those assets that he cannot remove. The second lender's later sale of the promissory note is therefore ineffective as against the first lender, who can seize even where ordinary purchaser collection rules would not suffice. But where the second borrower could have removed the asset from the second lender's reach, for example after acquired property not covered by the second lender's lien, or ordinary movables where no movables together with land clause was written, Rabbi Natan's lien does not attach to the property lien, as Tosafot in Pesachim explain and Shach cites. The first lender therefore cannot seize such property even by ordinary purchaser rules, because the second borrower's property was never subjected to him. Chacham Tzvi asks in his gloss to Taz that even if Shimon had already collected the field and then sold it, Reuven could not seize it unless Shimon had written an after acquired property clause, so all the more so before Shimon collected it. In my view, once Shimon actually collected the field and it became his, his sale is a Torah law sale, and the creditor cannot seize it absent an after acquired property clause. A sale of a promissory note is different. That sale is rabbinic, and the original creditor can waive the debt, because, as the authorities explain and Shach cites in siman 66, the personal lien remains with the original lender. When he waives that personal lien, the property lien falls away as well. Here, however, the personal obligation of the second borrower, together with the lien on those assets that he cannot remove, belongs under Rabbi Natan's law to the first creditor. The second lender's sale therefore does not defeat the first creditor, and the purchaser's collection is not valid against him. Tumim asks from a deposit held by another person, which likewise cannot be transferred away by the custodian, yet when the depositor dies it is treated as movable property of heirs. There is no difficulty. Where the property belongs to the second borrower and is merely subject to the second lender's property lien, and it cannot be removed from that lien, Rabbi Natan's lien transfers that property lien to the first lender while the second borrower remains alive. It is therefore not movable property of heirs. A deposit is different because it is not encumbered as property of the custodian at all. It remains legally in the depositor's domain, so when the depositor, who is the debtor, dies, it becomes movable property of his heirs. Shach further writes that if the second lender wrote a movables together with land clause, the first lender can seize the promissory note itself. At first glance this is difficult from Ketubot 82, concerning one who was owed money by his brother and then inherited the brother, where the Gemara says we do not combine two stringencies for a ketubah, Rabbi Meir's view that movables are encumbered to a ketubah and Rabbi Natan's lien. According to Shach, even without Rabbi Natan, once movables are encumbered to the ketubah she should be able to seize the promissory note itself. The answer is that without Rabbi Natan there is no personal lien against the second borrower at all. Even if she seizes the document, which represents only a property lien, the second borrower can say, "You are not my litigant." Here, however, we rule like Rabbi Natan, so the first creditor already holds the personal lien against the second borrower. The only gap, as to assets that can be removed, is the property lien. If he can seize the promissory note itself, he thereby acquires the property lien as well, while his personal lien against the second borrower already exists under Rabbi Natan. He then stands in the place of the second lender and can properly collect. Nor should one infer from Tosafot in Pesachim, cited by Shach, that if the second lender's claim is an oral loan then even if the heirs collected land on their father's debt, the creditor cannot collect from them because the second borrower could have removed the land by selling it and an oral creditor does not collect from purchasers. Tosafot's limitation concerns movable property, which is not subject to Torah law liens. An oral loan differs because land is subject to the lien by Torah law. According to the opinion that liens are Torah law, even an oral creditor collects from purchasers on the Torah level. The Sages' limitation of oral loans did not destroy Rabbi Natan's lien in this respect. Rabbi Natan's lien therefore applies even to an oral loan. This appears correct. Also see Sema, seif katan 22, where there is a textual error. The correct reading is: "which had not yet come into the earlier creditor's possession, but had merely become encumbered to him," and so forth.
