If he collected land. The Sema holds that when the earlier creditor sold his promissory note, the clause covering after acquired property is unnecessary. But when the later creditor sold his note, the earlier creditor can recover it only if the earlier note contains such a clause, or in a place where omission of that clause is treated as a scribal error. Where after acquired property is not automatically encumbered, the earlier creditor cannot seize the note. See the Beurim. The same applies if the first lender sold his note before the debtor borrowed from the second lender: according to the Sema, the first lender cannot seize, because the purchaser can say, "My acquisition preceded your acquisition." The Shach disagrees. Since the debt becomes encumbered under the law of Rabbi Natan, no after acquired property clause is needed. It is as though the second lender had borrowed the first lender's money and as though the second debtor had directly obligated himself to the first lender. However, even if the first note contains an after acquired property clause, according to the Shach the property is not encumbered against the second lender if the second debtor could have removed that property from the second lender's reach, for example by selling it. Since the second lender could not have seized such property from a purchaser, the lien of Rabbi Natan does not attach to it in favor of the first lender:
He encumbered movables together with land. The Shach holds that a clause encumbering movables together with land must appear specifically in the note that was sold. Since the person from whom collection is sought could not have removed those movables from the second lender, they become encumbered to the first lender as well. But if the second debtor did not encumber movables together with land, so that he could have sold or otherwise removed them from the second lender's reach, they do not become encumbered to the first lender, even if the first lender's own note contains a movables together with land clause, and he cannot seize them. This applies where the second debt was oral but supported by a kinyan. If it was evidenced by a promissory note, however, then even if the sold note lacks an agav clause and only the collecting creditor's note contains one, he may nevertheless seize the sold note itself, because documents can be encumbered through agav. The Sema, however, holds that the essential factor is whether the collecting creditor's note contains both an agav clause and an after acquired property clause. Regarding movables encumbered together with land, omission of an after acquired property clause is not treated as a scribal error, because it is reasonable that the debtor intended to encumber only movables he owned at the time. Even where it is uncertain whether the movables were after acquired, the creditor cannot seize them. But if collection has not yet occurred and the purchaser seeks to retain them, the purchaser is the one relying on the after acquired status, and the burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to extract:
He returns and collects. This applies specifically where it is unknown whether the seller has other property. If it is known that he has no other property, there is no point in making assets circle pointlessly, and the purchaser does not collect at all. Sema. See the Beurim to siman 86, where I explain that if Yehudah nevertheless paid after it had already become established that Reuven had no property, Yehudah must pay a second time.
