Collects. The point is that one cannot argue that because the law is that each party simply retains his own position, the document is treated as though already paid, and therefore a purchaser who derives his rights from the seller should have no stronger claim than the seller himself. The Tur teaches otherwise. If the second party later acquires additional fields so that he possesses superior and medium-quality land while the other has only inferior land, each creditor may then collect his debt. The debt document therefore was never considered paid, and a purchaser who acquired it may likewise collect. Sema.
Returns. The Sema understands this to apply even without an after-acquired-property clause, and specifically where the earlier creditor sold his debt document. If the later creditor sold his document, the earlier creditor cannot later collect the field from the purchaser unless his own document contains an after-acquired-property lien. The Bach disagrees, arguing that because collection arises through Rabbi Natan's lien, no after-acquired-property clause is needed. The Shach regards the Bach's view as primary based on Pesachim, Perek Kol Shaah, and discusses the point further. See siman 111, seifim 22 and 23. The Taz likewise challenges the Sema, while Chacham Tzvi responds and also challenges the Shach at length.
Encumbered. The Ir Shushan and Sema explain the case as one where the seller encumbered movable property together with land. The Sema reasons that even if an after-acquired-property clause is normally implied for movables attached to land, where the later creditor can reach the purchaser only through the earlier creditor's encumbrance, the purchaser may say that the earlier creditor encumbered only movables already in his possession, not movables that would come to him later, unless he expressly included after-acquired property. The Shach finds this difficult. Even if the seller encumbered movables with land, the movables later collected by the purchaser remain movables. The case must instead be where the person from whom the movables are being collected had himself encumbered them together with land, thereby giving them the status of land for collection purposes. The plain words of the Tur indicate this, and the Shach brings further proofs.
And that he will acquire. Otherwise the possessor can say, "I acquired these movables later, after the lien, so they were never included in the encumbrance together with land." With real property, by contrast, it is generally known when the property was acquired, as in siman 104, seif 5. Nevertheless, land known to have been acquired later is not collectible without an express after-acquired-property clause. Tosafot in Kol Shaah proves this. Therefore, if the second borrower acquired land only after the loan and did not grant his creditor an after-acquired-property lien, he could have sold that land free of the creditor's claim. Such land is then functionally like movables, outside the lien. The first creditor should have no stronger right than the second creditor, and Rabbi Natan's lien likewise does not apply. The Shach regards this as the principal law.
