If the purchaser collected land, etc. Sema holds as follows. Reuven lent Shimon in Nisan, and Shimon lent Reuven in Iyar. If Shimon sold his document and Reuven seeks to recover from the purchaser on the ground that Shimon owes him and that the land the purchaser collected from Reuven was encumbered to him, Reuven's document must contain an after-acquired-property clause; otherwise he cannot collect. This depends on whether omission of such a clause is considered a scribal error. But if Reuven sold his own document and the purchaser collected land from Shimon, Shimon may use his later Iyar document to collect that same land even if his own document lacks an after-acquired-property clause. Shach disagrees and says no such clause is required in either case because Rabbi Natan's lien applies. See Tumim. I explained at length that if Shimon sold his document and Reuven seeks to foreclose, Sema is certainly correct that Reuven needs an after-acquired-property clause. Even if Reuven sold his document and Shimon seeks to foreclose, there is a major dispute whether such a clause is required. Everyone agrees that if the land collected by the purchaser was land Shimon acquired only after both loans, Shimon cannot take it from the purchaser unless his document contains an after-acquired-property clause.
If he encumbered movables together with land, etc. Shach explains that if Reuven sold his document and Shimon seeks to foreclose, then if the sold document contains a clause encumbering movables together with land, Shimon may collect even if his own document lacks that clause. But if only Shimon's foreclosing document contains it, then because Shimon's loan arose in Iyar he can collect, since a document can be subject to a lien through incidental acquisition and no after-acquired-property clause is required. In the reverse configuration, such a clause would be needed. According to Shach, if no document presently exists and there was only a loan with kinyan, writing an incidental-acquisition clause for the foreclosing creditor does not help. In Tumim I concluded like Sema. The incidental-acquisition clause in the sold document is irrelevant; what matters is the foreclosing creditor's own document. Even if no formal document exists and there was only a loan with kinyan, if the foreclosing creditor's document or kinyan included movables together with land, he may collect from the purchaser. There is, however, a distinction. If Reuven sold his document, Shimon does not need an after-acquired-property clause, because a debt instrument can itself be encumbered through incidental acquisition and Reuven's document already existed when Shimon lent him. But if Shimon sold his document and Reuven seeks to foreclose, then since Shimon's document did not yet exist at the time of Reuven's loan, Reuven needs an after-acquired-property clause. If there is no formal document at all and recovery is sought only through Rabbi Natan's lien, Sema holds that whether Reuven or Shimon sold the debt, the foreclosing party's instrument must contain both an incidental-acquisition clause and an after-acquired-property clause. One point still requires explanation. When Reuven sold his document and Shimon seeks to foreclose, why should an after-acquired-property clause be required for movables when, regarding land, we said that because Shimon's document is later, the lien already existed at that time? Perishah answers that the purchaser can say: who says these movables were in your possession when Reuven encumbered his property to you? If they were not, they are after-acquired property. According to this, the requirement applies when the purchaser has already collected. But if Shimon is only coming to collect and the purchaser resists, Shimon need not prove the after-acquired-property clause, because the burden of proof is on the possessor. This is straightforward.
He then collects again. This refers to a case where it was not initially known that the seller would be left without assets. Beit din therefore permits the purchaser to collect, after which the original obligor may seek reimbursement from the seller; if the seller has nothing, he may in turn foreclose from the purchaser. But if beit din already knows that the seller has no property, there is no purpose in making the parties perform a circular collection. The purchaser is not allowed to collect only to have Shimon immediately take it back. Sema. This is the situation in Rosh, responsum rule 70: Reuven sold a debt document he held against Shimon. When the purchaser came to collect, Shimon produced a document that he had acquired from Yehudah against Reuven. Rosh ruled that each should remain with what he already has. This is exactly Sema's case. Yehudah's document against Reuven predated the sale of Reuven's document to the purchaser, as is evident because Reuven's heirs made the sale. Shimon therefore had a right to foreclose from the purchaser. Since beit din already knew that if the purchaser first collected Shimon would immediately take it back, Rosh avoided the pointless cycle. Knesset HaGedolah says this responsum contradicts what Tur cites here in Ramban's name, which is the rule stated in this seif. It does not.
