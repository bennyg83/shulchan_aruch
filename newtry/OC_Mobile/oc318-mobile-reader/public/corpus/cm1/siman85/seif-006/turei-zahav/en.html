(Ibid., seif 4) The Tur writes, “Under the strict law of the Gemara, the heirs...” This is difficult. Shortly afterward the Tur cites the Ramban that even with heirs the creditor prevails because he seized during their father’s lifetime. Why, then, does he write here that under the Talmudic law the creditor loses? We hold that seizure during the father’s lifetime is effective against the heirs’ movable property. The creditor should be able to say to the heirs, “I seized during his lifetime.” In the final chapter of Ketubot we likewise find that seizure is effective where the heirs possess inferior land and the competing party possesses superior or medium land. Even according to Rav Sheshet, who assesses according to the debtor’s estate, the heirs benefit because they will later give only inferior land, since collection from heirs is only from inferior land. Yet the Gemara concludes that since the creditor seized, his seizure stands and he need not surrender medium land and accept inferior land. That proof alone would not trouble me, because perhaps the seizure is regarded as occurring only after death. It is nevertheless effective because the heirs currently possess land and are themselves debtors; the creditor may say, “I now retain my own land, even though it is medium quality, since you are in any event obligated to pay.” But the Ramban expressly states that he seized during the father’s lifetime, and that should also be effective regarding the heirs’ movable property. Upon analysis, even the Gemara just cited shows that this is seizure during the father’s lifetime. The rule in HaKotev that possession does not count unless demand was made during the decedent’s lifetime applies specifically to a bailee, as Rashi explains, because a deposit remains in the owner’s legal possession. A loan, however, is given for expenditure, so possession counts even without a demand during life. Likewise, in siman 107 the Tur requires only possession during life and mentions a demand during life solely for a deposit; and in siman 108, seif 38, he explicitly writes that such a case is possession during life. It is therefore difficult why Rami bar Hama thought the heirs would profit, since he speaks of movable property, where possession during life certainly helps. One might say that Rami bar Hama stated this according to Rav Sheshet, who may not accept Rav Nachman’s rule that possession during life helps against heirs’ movable property. But the Tur’s words require a master craftsman to resolve them. [Editor’s note: I am no master craftsman, yet I can resolve them. Notes are certainly not regarded as already collected and paid, as the Ramban expressly writes and as the Gemara clearly proves. Regarding collection from inferior land, however, the Sages were lenient and treated this as possession during life. From there the Ramban derives that the same constructive possession helps against concern for hidden payment, which is only a rabbinic enactment based on a slight concern. But for the strict Talmudic rule that heirs’ movable property is not subject to a creditor unless he actually seized during life, this is plainly not possession. Thus the Gemara states in Pesachim and in Mi SheHaya Nasui that where Reuven sold land to Shimon with a warranty, converted the price into a loan, died, and a creditor came, the heirs can say, “Our father left movable property in your hands, and heirs’ movable property is not subject to a creditor.” This is settled law, and even Rava agrees. The Taz’s suggestion that Rami bar Hama spoke only according to Rav Sheshet, who disagrees with Rav Nachman, therefore falls away. His further statement that Rami bar Hama erred in thinking the heirs would profit even though possession during life certainly helps with movable property cannot be sustained. The cited passages expressly show that Rava too agrees that the heirs may say, “Our father left movable property in your hands,” even though the creditor held it during the father’s lifetime in a case exactly like this. Rava’s statement here, “One collects and the other collects,” does not mean that collection occurs even from heirs. Rather, he proves that the Mishna cannot concern heirs, since it states that each party collects. This is clear.]
