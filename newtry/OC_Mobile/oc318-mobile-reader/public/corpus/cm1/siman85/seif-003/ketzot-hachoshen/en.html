Reuven produced a document. At the end of Ketubot Admon and the Sages dispute a case of reciprocal obligations. The Gemara asks whether the original debt had already matured or not and ultimately establishes the dispute on the very day the term ends. Baal HaMaor explains that the Gemara's difficulty is especially according to Rav Sheshet, who says there is no reason to make the parties exchange payments when the obligations can simply be offset. According to Rav Nachman, who says each party collects from the other, the structure can be useful because the legal act of separate collection itself matters. Ramban in Milchamot explains that according to Rav Nachman the dispute can be established where the later document was written before the money was handed over. The separate teaching is still needed to establish the principle that each collects. A difficulty remains. Even regarding debt instruments there is a difference between first writing and then giving money, or first giving and then writing, just as in the sale case. If the later lender first gave money, the original creditor could have retained it against the old debt. If he first wrote the document, perhaps there was no opportunity to withhold payment. On the exact day the first debt becomes due, however, the original creditor may not be able to demand payment until the end of the day. Shitah Mekubetzet in Perek HaShoel says the due date runs through the whole day, and the sugya itself indicates this from the dispute whether a person borrows for one day. If the creditor had the right to retain money at the beginning of that day, he should simply have done so and never written the later loan document. It follows that when the Gemara establishes the dispute on the due date, it can be speaking even where the document was first written and afterward the money was given. According to Admon, the creditor still should have protected himself, perhaps through a protest declaration, unless one assumes he genuinely needed the new loan for that day. According to the Sages, people do borrow for a single day, so his conduct is not suspicious. This also explains why Rif did not separately codify a special rule for the day of maturity. Even if the later money was handed over before the document, the creditor could not necessarily retain it at the beginning of the day because the old debt was not yet enforceable until the day's end. The situation is therefore no worse than one where the document was written first. The main practical distinction is already encompassed by the general rules. The Shach rejects Ramban's explanation because the Mishnah says the creditor was clever in the sale case because he could seize the field, and according to him that rationale is absent in the reciprocal loan case. I do not see the force of the objection. The second clause was not necessary to repeat all the reasoning of the first. Its primary novelty is the law that each creditor collects from the other. The relevant comparison remains fully intelligible under Ramban's reading.
