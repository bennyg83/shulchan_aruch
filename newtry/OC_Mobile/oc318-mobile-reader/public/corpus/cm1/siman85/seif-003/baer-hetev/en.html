To collect. The Shach argues at great length against the Tur and Shulchan Aruch on this law and brings many proofs. He concludes that where two people each produce a debt document against the other, in every case each collects his own debt. He says this follows from the Gemara and is the view of Rif, Rambam, Rosh, Rashi, Baal HaMaor, Ramah, and Ritva, contrary to Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and those who follow them.
Clear. The Shach likewise challenges the Rama here. Responsa Rosh did not state the rule so categorically but relied on additional considerations. The Rama's ruling therefore requires study. According to the Shach's own conclusion that in every case each creditor collects, however, the point is largely irrelevant here, though it matters in other contexts such as the field sale discussed in seif 1.
In a place. The Sema gives two reasons for this distinction. The Shach questions the Tur and Shulchan Aruch because on their reasoning Reuven's document should always be nullified, whereas the Mishnah says that the Sages hold each party collects his debt document. The Gemara limited one opposing view to the very due date only according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman, whose view is accepted, even when the due date arrived each may collect. The Shach therefore says the Ramah's language was intended only to explain the Gemara's discussion, not to establish the Tur's broader rule.
Superior land. The Tur explains that even if Reuven had not gone back and borrowed from the other party, he could have collected from the debtor's superior land. One cannot say that he borrowed merely out of fear that the other would pay him with cash already in hand, because that concern would remain: the other could transfer the land to someone else, pay him cash, and later redeem the land through the rule that an appraisal is reversible upon repayment. Nor is it normal for a person to borrow merely so that he can transfer his own inferior land. If he says he intended to sell that land and use the proceeds to repay, he could just as well have borrowed from someone else and transferred the inferior land to that lender. Sema.
With his own. This means, for example, that he never possessed superior land. If he had superior land when he borrowed and later sold it, he must pay with medium-quality land. The Bach says this, and the Shach agrees. See siman 102, seif 4. The same interpretation applies in the earlier case where one party has medium land and the other inferior land: if the first had superior land and sold it, he must give medium land.
Its time. Meaning that the date of the loan evidenced by Shimon's document against Reuven was before the due date of Reuven's document. In such a case, regardless of whether local practice is to write documents, each remains with his own claim where there is no practical gain from cross-collection. But if one has superior and medium land and the other only inferior land, each actually collects.
Still. Responsa Mahara Sasson asks how a migo is possible when one can simply examine the dates in the documents. He answers that the document must be undated, as the Tur says at the beginning of siman 43 that an undated document may still collect from unencumbered property. The Shach says this misses the point. The migo is that the party could have claimed, "The payment I made to you occurred before my debt was due." Gidulei Terumah explains the same. Mahara Sasson's citation of the Tur concerns the date of the loan, whereas the present issue is the due date. See also the cited responsa and later authorities.
