You should have paid your debt. See Shach, seif katan 7, who discusses this at length. Before responding to him, I will explain how the Gemara appears to me. The poskim ask why the Gemara challenges Rav Sheshet from the Mishnah and establishes the case, according to him, as one debt due in five years and the other in ten, yet does not give the same answer according to Rav Nachman. Maharsha also questions Rashi's explanation that it was advantageous for the first creditor to borrow now and collect his own debt after five years while the second debt remained outstanding. Why was that explanation necessary? Even if both debts matured together, Admon's argument seems difficult where neither due date had yet arrived, since the first creditor could not have demanded payment at that time.
In my view Rashi can be explained as follows. If both debts had the same due date, the Mishnah could indeed have been established as a case where neither had yet matured and where the money was first delivered and the document written afterward. Suppose Shimon first gives Reuven the loan money and then demands that Reuven execute a note. According to Rav Nachman, who holds that where two people owe one another each collects independently, Reuven cannot discharge himself merely by handing back Shimon's existing note. Shimon is presently lending money before the due date of Reuven's earlier claim, so Reuven must execute the new note. According to Rav Sheshet, however, who holds that each party stands on his own property, once Reuven receives Shimon's money and is asked to sign a new obligation, Reuven can return Shimon's existing note and need not execute a new note. We rule in siman 74 that a borrower may compel a lender to accept early payment. Every time Reuven elects to repay, it is therefore as though the due date arrived, and he can use Shimon's note against him in exchange for his own. Admon can thus argue, "If I truly owed you, why did you borrow from me?" Meaning: because the local practice was to deliver money before writing the note, you should not have executed a note against yourself at all; you should simply have returned my earlier note. Rashi therefore needed to explain why it might nevertheless have been advantageous to borrow. The Gemara then says that if the first debt was for five years and the second for ten, and the first had already matured, what is the Sages' reason? One cannot answer that the borrower gained by receiving his money immediately while his own loan remained outstanding until the tenth year. Once the first debt matured, a normal creditor would demand payment before making a new loan. Sema, seif katan 4, writes that one would not even engage in a sale or purchase with the debtor before collecting a matured debt, all the more so one would not lend him additional money. The creditor would first demand payment and only afterward discuss a new loan. The poskim cited by Shach similarly rely on the argument that he should have demanded his debt first. Moreover, a ten year loan is effectively a time limited benefit, and there is a chazakah that a person does not grant such a benefit before collecting what is already owed to him. If the debtor refused to pay, the creditor would certainly not advance a new loan. The Gemara therefore properly asks the Sages' reason where the first debt had already matured. Conversely, where the first debt had not matured, Admon cannot simply argue that the creditor should have returned his earlier note instead of executing a new one, because the borrower may have needed the new money to remain available to him for the full ten year period. This is why, according to Rav Sheshet, the Gemara establishes the case specifically on the very day the five year term ended. According to Rav Nachman, however, it can be established where both due dates are the same and neither had yet arrived. The dispute can then be understood as follows. Admon holds that valuation is made by reference to ordinary property, so when the debts mature each side will stand on his own claim. He can therefore say, "If I owed you, why did you borrow from me? You should have returned my note rather than creating a new one." The Sages hold that valuation is made according to the debtor's own property, so each debt may be collected separately and that argument fails. Tumim likewise writes that Admon and the Sages dispute whether valuation is according to the debtor's property or according to general standards, a tannaitic dispute in Bava Kamma. This is the position of Rif and Rambam, who rule like the Sages and Rav Nachman and understand the Mishnah where the first due date had not yet arrived. Once the first debt has matured, however, the debtor can say, "If I truly owed you, you should have collected first," which the commentators correctly explain to mean that the creditor should have made a demand before extending a new loan. As explained above, matured debts are ordinarily collected before new loans are made. It therefore makes no difference whether local practice is to deliver the money and then write the document or to write first and then deliver the money. If the earlier debt was already due when the new loan was made, the creditor should first have demanded payment. Admon and the Sages disagree only where the due date had not arrived at the time of the second loan. In that case the halachah follows the Sages. When the due date had arrived, everyone agrees that the debtor can raise Admon's argument. This also explains the view of Ramah, cited by the Mechaber as "there is one who says," that whether the note was written before the money was given or afterward, the debtor can say, "If I owed you, why did you lend me money?" One cannot answer that the creditor acted this way because each party can collect independently. Since the earlier debt had matured, he should have demanded it first. The Gemara says this even where the first debt was due after five years and the new loan after ten, despite the substantial advantage of leaving the new money outstanding longer. All the more so one would not deliberately create the arrangement merely to gain a difference between intermediate and inferior land. Rif, Rambam, and Ramah therefore stand in one line. Baal HaMaor may also fit this approach when he writes that the first due date arrived before the later one, meaning before the due date of the later debt, while the second loan itself had been made before the first debt matured. This answers Shach's objection in seif katan 10 as to how any authority could hold such a view. Rif, Rambam, and Baal HaMaor may all do so. We can now respond to Shach's arguments. His entire reconciliation of Ramban's challenge to Baal HaMaor is strained. Shach also infers that Rif follows Baal HaMaor rather than Ramban because Rif does not record the special case of the exact day the term expires. This proof is difficult. On Ramban's understanding, Rav Nachman cannot rely, according to the Sages, on the argument that the creditor should have demanded payment first, because if that were the reason the same argument would apply even where the note was written before money was given. Rav Nachman's reason is instead that the creditor should have withheld the money, which applies specifically where money is given first. On the exact day the term expires, however, the creditor cannot yet force immediate repayment in the same manner, and the argument that he should have withheld the money is weaker. That case can therefore resemble one where the note was written first, in which the debtor cannot make Admon's argument according to Ramban. It is strained to say that the creditor should simply have waited until the next day, because in a place where money is given before writing he could answer that he was afraid to delay. Rav Sheshet, who allows Admon's argument even on the due date itself, must therefore rely on the broader reasoning that the creditor should have demanded payment, and for him there is indeed no distinction between the two local practices. Ketzot HaChoshen writes something similar. Shach's proof from Ramah that Ramah follows Baal HaMaor is therefore likewise not compelling. The primary understanding of Ramah is as explained above. If the second document expressly states that the creditor stipulated a right to collect from superior land, even Shach would agree that this changes the analysis, and that is straightforward.
