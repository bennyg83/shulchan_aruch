But if there is a claim, etc. The Shach questions this because the Rosh mentioned the consideration only as an additional supporting factor and did not establish it as an independent rule. It appears that the case is in a place where documents are written before the money is actually handed over. If the money were first delivered, how could the debtor say, "I needed to borrow because I had no time to litigate my counterclaim"? The money was still in his own hands, and the other party would have needed to pursue him if he wished to enforce the document. We must therefore be speaking of a place where the document is written first. The ruling then follows the Ramah, who holds that after the maturity date a party ordinarily loses the evidentiary force of a counterdocument in every circumstance. Against that baseline the Rama writes that in this case he does not lose his document. Thus the Rosh's consideration remains only an additional factor, because according to most authorities even without it he would not lose the document. Joined with this reason, however, the ruling certainly follows that view. These are precisely the Rosh's words, so the Shach's objection and his conclusion that the point requires study do not stand.
There is no distinction, etc. This refers to a claim made after the maturity date, as the Shulchan Aruch states at the end. See Tumim for how I reconciled the Ramah's language.
Where they write the document, etc. The reason is that he should have pursued his own claim rather than place himself in the subordinate position of a borrower toward his lender. By contrast, one who sells his field does not thereby become the lender's subordinate debtor.
This applies specifically where they first give the money, etc. The Shach objects. See Tumim for my lengthy discussion.
From you, the superior quality property. Meaning now, when the maturity date of my claim has arrived. If you later come with your own document, you will not collect from superior quality land but only from inferior property. The Shach emends the text to "intermediate quality": if both collect at the same time, one creditor collects intermediate land and the other inferior. See Tumim. I wrote that no emendation is necessary.
Each remains with his own property. There is no purpose in merely exchanging the same assets. Certainly where their properties are of equal quality nothing is gained. Even where one has intermediate land and the other inferior land, one might have thought that the first should collect intermediate land, after which it becomes superior relative to what he then owns, so that when the second creditor later collects from him he could take only inferior property. The ruling teaches otherwise, because there is no reason to choose who collects first. Perhaps the other creditor should collect the inferior land first, whereupon the first person's intermediate land becomes superior relative to his remaining holdings, and the second creditor likewise can take only inferior. But if one party owns superior and intermediate property while the other owns only inferior property, whichever collection occurs first, one party gains. If the owner of intermediate land collects first, he can take only the other's inferior land, and he then owns superior, intermediate, and inferior property. When the other creditor later collects from him, he takes intermediate property. In the reverse order, if the owner of inferior land collects first, he collects intermediate property, and when the other later collects from him, he takes inferior property, because valuation is made relative to the debtor's own holdings and that intermediate property is now his superior quality.
Then in every case each shall remain, etc. Meaning that the first creditor does not lose his document and may still collect it. Nevertheless, where one owns superior and intermediate property and the other owns only inferior property, each actually collects from the other as described above. Shach, and this is clear.
Even on the last day. Although people sometimes pay on the day a debt matures, the creditor cannot compel payment earlier that day. Sema. See Tumim. I wrote that "the last day" appears to mean the day immediately preceding the day on which people ordinarily pay, because by the end of the actual due day the borrower is already obligated to pay. I also wrote that perhaps this distinction applies specifically where a due date was explicitly fixed. If the document states no date and the loan is simply subject to the ordinary thirty day term, then even if the counterloan was made within those thirty days, in a place where money is handed over before the document is written it is possible that he loses the force of his counterdocument. See there. This requires study.
For ten years, etc. Such a long term is chosen to teach that we do not say, "Why would he subordinate himself for ten years merely because he needed a one day extension? Tomorrow the other creditor could compel him to pay and he would become the servant of the borrower to the lender." The ruling teaches that if the lender would not lend on any other terms and the borrower urgently needed the money, he does not thereby lose his existing claim. Sema. See Tumim. One must say that the parties expressly stipulated that the long term debt could not be prepaid, because otherwise the time term is ordinarily for the borrower's benefit and he may pay early. See there.
Reuven who claims, etc. This refers to a documented claim. With an oral claim, even if the circumstances prevent an ordinary plea of payment, such as where the loan was witnessed and the lender stipulated that repayment must be before witnesses, a migo cannot be used to extract money. In conjunction with a formal document, however, such a migo may be accepted.
My time had not yet arrived, etc. For example, if the debt was due on the first of Nisan and the claimant admits that payment was made on the eighth of Nisan, he has a migo that he could instead have said that payment occurred on the twenty eighth of Adar, before the due date. Shach and Gidulei Terumah.
