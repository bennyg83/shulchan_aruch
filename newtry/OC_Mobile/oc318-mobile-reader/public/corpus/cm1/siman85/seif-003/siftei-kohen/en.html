Reuven, etc. Since the Tur and the Mechaber followed in these laws the words of the Baal HaTerumot and the Ramban, whose position appears in his novellae and in Sefer HaMilchamot, and their words do not appear correct to me in light of the Talmudic sugya, I must elaborate, present the sugya, show that many of the great authorities disagree with them, and clarify the matter properly. We learned in the last chapter of Kesubos, page 110a: One who produces a promissory note against another, and the latter produces a deed showing that the first sold him a field, Admon says that the purchaser may argue, "Had I truly owed you, you should have collected your debt when you sold me the field." The Sages say that the creditor acted shrewdly in selling him the land, because he can now seize that land for his debt. The Gemara asks that Admon's argument appears sound. It answers that in a place where money is given first and the deed is written afterward, all agree that the purchaser can say, "You should have applied the money toward your debt when you sold me the field." Their dispute is in a place where the deed is written first and payment is made afterward. Admon holds that the creditor should have issued a protest, while the Sages hold that he could fear that a protest would become known through the principle that one friend tells another, and the friend of your friend is also your friend. The Mishnah then states: If two people each produce a promissory note against the other, Admon says, "Had I owed you, how could you have borrowed from me?" The Sages say that each collects on his promissory note. The Gemara records a dispute. Rav Nachman says each collects from the other, while Rav Sheshet says, "Why reverse the burden?" Rather, each remains with what he has. If both have superior land, both intermediate land, or both inferior land, all agree that reversing the collections serves no practical purpose. Their dispute concerns a case where one has intermediate land and the other inferior land. Rav Nachman says each collects from the other because he holds that the quality of land is assessed relative to the debtor's own holdings. Thus the owner of inferior land first collects the other's intermediate land, which is superior relative to the debtor, and the other then collects the inferior land. Rav Sheshet says there is no reason to reverse the burden, because he holds that land is assessed relative to the general public, so the second creditor would ultimately receive back his own intermediate land. The Gemara then refines the case and concludes that the dispute is where one debtor owns superior and intermediate land while the other owns only inferior land. One authority holds that land is assessed relative to the debtor's own holdings, and the other relative to the holdings of the public. The Gemara challenges Rav Sheshet from the Mishnah, where the Sages say, "Each collects." Rav Nachman explains the Mishnah according to Rav Sheshet as a case where one borrowed for ten years and the other for five. If the first loan was for ten and the second for five, Admon could not say, "Had I owed you, how could you have borrowed from me?" because the first debt had not yet become due. If the first was for five and the second for ten, then if the first debt had already become due, why would the Sages validate it, and if it had not yet become due, why would Admon invalidate it? The Gemara therefore explains that the second loan was made on the very day the first five year term ended. One authority holds that a person may borrow even for a single day, while the other holds that people do not ordinarily borrow for a single day. Rami bar Chama offers another explanation involving orphans, because one may collect for orphans but does not collect from them. This is the substance of the sugya. The Baal HaMaor explains that a person may borrow for a day and collect the first debt the next day, while the other debt remains collectible when its later due date arrives. The reference to ten years is not literal, for the same would apply if both loans were for five years in a similar arrangement. He adds that this resolution is only according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman, even if the first debt became due before the later loan was made, the first creditor did not lose his note, because he can say, "I borrowed from you precisely so that I could collect your intermediate land while you would collect my inferior land." He thus acted intelligently and benefited from the transaction. It is clear that the Gemara's question, "If the due date had arrived, what is the reason of the Sages?" and its answer that a person may borrow for a day are required only according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman there is no difficulty and no need for that answer. This cannot be a question raised independently from the Mishnah itself, for if so the Gemara should have raised it immediately after the Mishnah. Moreover, the wording, "If the first was for ten and the second for five ... rather, the first for five and the second for ten, how is the case? If the due date arrived, what is the reason of the Sages?" plainly shows that the question arises specifically within Rav Sheshet's interpretation that the loans had differing terms. Likewise, Rami bar Chama's alternative explanation comes afterward, which also shows that this particular question was directed to Rav Sheshet. Thus, according to Rav Nachman we must explain why the case is satisfactory even after the first due date, and that is why the Baal HaMaor says that the creditor can claim that he borrowed in order to preserve the advantage of collecting intermediate land and paying inferior land. I saw in Gidulei Terumah, at the beginning of gate 34, page 146a, that he assumed as obvious that the Gemara's question about the due date was directed both to Rav Sheshet and Rav Nachman. He therefore raised a lengthy difficulty against the Rambam for not limiting the rule to the day the first debt became due. He was not precise. He overlooked all the indications just stated, which make clear that the question is asked only according to Rav Sheshet. This is plain both according to the Baal HaMaor and according to the Ramban, whose words I will cite immediately, and it is likewise explicit in the Ritva and in the novellae of Maharsha. Therefore there is no difficulty at all with the Rambam, whose ruling is correct and clear as I will explain below. The Ramban writes in Sefer HaMilchamot that the Gemara's resolution is according to Rav Sheshet, but that the Baal HaMaor's explanation according to Rav Nachman, that the creditor borrowed so he could collect intermediate land and pay inferior land, is weak. The Ramban argues that even without borrowing he could already have collected the other's intermediate land, and people do not ordinarily borrow merely in order to dispose of their own inferior or intermediate property. If such a claim were accepted, then in the Mishnah about the creditor who sold a field in a place where money is paid before the deed is written, why could the purchaser say, "You should have collected your debt"? The seller could answer that he wanted to sell him that particular inferior field and later collect payment in cash or from intermediate land. The Ramban further argues that the creditor could have borrowed from someone else and paid that other lender his inferior land, and that even according to Rav Sheshet one might say that from the outset he intended to collect intermediate land, sell it for more money before the later loan became due, and then pay his own inferior land. Likewise, according to Rav Sheshet one could construct a case where the second debtor has superior land and the first inferior land, so that the first creditor would say that he borrowed in order to collect the other's superior land immediately and repay his own inferior land only later. If that were a valid explanation, Rav Nachman would not have needed to establish the case as one loan for ten years and one for five. The Ramban therefore concludes that this reasoning is insufficient. According to him, Rav Nachman understands the latter Mishnah like the former one, in a place where the deed is written before money is given. Whether or not the first debt has matured, the first creditor can say that he borrowed from the other in order to have property of the other available for seizure. Nevertheless, each note remains valid because the transaction was truly a loan, just as the sale in the first Mishnah remains valid even if the seller's motive was to create property that he could later seize, since he did not issue a protest. According to Rav Sheshet, however, the Mishnah cannot be teaching that same point again, and therefore the distinction regarding the day of maturity is needed. The Ramban explains on this basis why the Rif omitted Rav Sheshet's particular interpretation and stated the Mishnah without qualification. The Ran, Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Mechaber followed the Ramban. Parenthetically, the Ran's phrase "as the Baal HaMaor wrote" refers to the preceding point, namely that the creditor cannot claim that this was his intention as the Baal HaMaor suggests, and not, as the Beit Yosef understood, to the later statement. The Beit Yosef's attribution there is therefore inaccurate, for the Baal HaMaor's actual position is the opposite. In my view, however, the Baal HaMaor's words are compelled by the sugya and are primary, while the Ramban's objections can all be answered. First, even the Ramban must concede that the question, "If the due date arrived, what is the reason of the Sages?" is directed specifically to Rav Sheshet and not to Rav Nachman, as I demonstrated above and as the Ramban himself effectively states when he says that these resolutions are according to Rav Sheshet. The Ramban then explains why Rav Nachman does not face the question by saying that the latter Mishnah resembles the first and concerns a place where the deed is written before the money is given, and that it teaches the rule of assessing land relative to the debtor's own holdings. This explanation does not appear correct for several reasons. It is difficult to say that the entire latter Mishnah exists merely to teach the collateral rule of how land quality is assessed, since that rule is not inherently connected to the dispute between Admon and the Sages. If that were its sole purpose, the Mishnah should have stated the land assessment rule directly rather than framing it as a dispute between Admon and the Sages. Moreover, the Mishnah does not expressly mention that central point at all. If the principal novelty were that land is assessed relative to the debtor's holdings, the Tanna should have stated it explicitly rather than hinting at it through the words "each collects." Further, according to the Ramban the Gemara's question about a matured debt means only that because the money was handed over first, the creditor should have withheld the money as repayment. But the straightforward meaning is broader: even if he never physically received money from the other, once his earlier debt had matured he should have demanded payment rather than borrow and place himself under the rule that "the borrower is servant to the lender." Rashi explicitly explains the question this way, writing that since the first debt had already matured, the first creditor should have demanded it instead of borrowing. The Ramah cited by the Tur likewise understands it this way. If so, the difficulty returns even according to Rav Nachman, for even in a place where the deed is written first, why did the first creditor not demand his matured debt? We are therefore forced to explain as the Baal HaMaor does: according to Rav Nachman the first creditor can say that he did not collect because he wished to preserve his original lien on intermediate land while giving the other creditor his inferior land. The plain flow of the sugya also supports this. According to the Ramban, a lengthy unstated analysis would be missing: Rav Nachman's case would have to be limited to a place where the deed is written first, and the Mishnah would be teaching that land is assessed relative to the debtor, while according to Rav Sheshet the Mishnah must instead concern a place where money is given first, leading to the question why the Sages disagree with Admon. None of this appears in the Gemara. The Baal HaMaor's reading, by contrast, follows the plain text. The Ritva in the chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot likewise writes that according to Rav Nachman, because he holds that each collects, the Sages' reasoning is that the first creditor can say he borrowed precisely so that he could collect intermediate land from the other while paying only his own inferior land. Once it is established that the Baal HaMaor's reading is compelled, the Ramban's objections are really objections against the Gemara's reasoning itself. They can, however, be answered. The Ramban first says that the argument is weak because the first creditor could have collected intermediate land even without borrowing. That misses the point. We are not saying that he borrowed in order to create a right to collect intermediate land that he otherwise lacked. Rather, we answer the contention that his earlier note must have been paid because he later borrowed from the same person. He can explain why he did not collect the earlier note: until now he had a lien on the other's intermediate land. If he had collected, that lien would have disappeared. He therefore left the earlier lien in place and, because he needed money, accepted a new loan, intending that the other creditor's claim against him would be satisfied from his inferior land. He does not lose money by doing so, because the loan proceeds remain in his hand. He merely gave the other creditor a promissory note in exchange for the money, while preserving his own earlier lien on intermediate land. This also answers the Ramban's comparison to the sale of a field. One cannot say that every creditor will eternally refrain from collecting merely to preserve a lien on intermediate land. Ordinarily a creditor must demand and collect his money. The explanation works only where, in practical terms, he still has the equivalent of his money in hand because he has just borrowed that amount and given a promissory note in exchange. In the field sale, by contrast, he received money and transferred a field in return. He no longer holds the equivalent of his debt as money in hand, since the purchaser has received land for the payment. There it is reasonable to say that he should have applied the purchase money directly to the debt. In the mutual loan case, he can say that he retained the earlier lien and merely exchanged paper obligations while keeping the loan money in his possession. This distinction is clear. The Ramban also says that he could have borrowed from someone else. That is no difficulty. What difference is there between borrowing from another person and borrowing from this creditor? Indeed, it is more natural that the person to whom he previously extended a favor by lending would now return the favor and lend to him. Moreover, if he borrowed from a third party, when that third party demanded payment he would have to pay or surrender his inferior land. By borrowing from his own debtor, he retains the option of setting the two notes against one another when advantageous. Even on the Ramban's own approach, one would otherwise ask according to Rav Nachman why, after the first debt matured, the creditor borrowed from this very person rather than from someone else. The Ramban himself answers that according to Rav Nachman the first creditor can say that he borrowed from the debtor so that he could later seize from him. The same reasoning supports the Baal HaMaor: he borrowed from this person and not another so that, if he chose, he could set his own debt against the other's obligation. This alone would not be sufficient, because one could still ask why he did not simply demand his matured debt instead of becoming a borrower at all. That is why the additional reason of Rav Nachman is necessary: each party collects, so he preserved his prior lien on intermediate land while accepting a new obligation that could be paid from inferior land. The Ramban's further arguments based on Rav Sheshet also do not trouble me. We are saying that the creditor does not wish to extinguish his existing lien on intermediate land. Even if he now needs cash and therefore borrows, he may preserve the earlier lien so that if the debts are ultimately collected at the same time he can profit from taking intermediate land while giving inferior land. This is entirely different from positing an elaborate fraudulent scheme in which he immediately forces the other party to surrender intermediate or superior land, sells it, and leaves the other creditor unable to collect until a much later date. We do not presume such obvious trickery. Would a sensible person knowingly lend for ten years to someone who could immediately use the arrangement to seize his superior land while preventing reciprocal collection for years? In such a case one would indeed infer that the first note had already been paid. According to Rav Nachman, however, no fraud is necessary. The original lien remains exactly as it was; the creditor simply chooses not to extinguish it and accepts a reciprocal debt, hoping that at maturity the land quality situation may favor him. The other borrower can rationally accept this too, because circumstances may equally reverse and benefit him. This is clear. The Ramban's statement that according to Rav Nachman the Mishnah comes to teach that land is assessed relative to the debtor's holdings has already been refuted by several arguments. His explanation that the Rif omitted Rav Sheshet's interpretation because the earlier Mishnah already supplied the relevant qualification also does not appear correct. In my view the Rif follows the Baal HaMaor. First, it is strained to say that the Rif silently relies on the first Mishnah. Second, it is implausible to say that the second Mishnah merely teaches the land assessment rule, especially since the Rif immediately afterward expressly records the dispute about whether land is assessed relative to the debtor or to the public. If that were all the second Mishnah taught, why cite it at all? Further, even if one accepted the Ramban's reading of the Mishnah, the Rif should still have cited the rule that a person may borrow on the very day a debt comes due, because although that statement arose in Rav Sheshet's explanation, Rav Nachman is nowhere shown to dispute it. The better explanation is that the Rif follows the Baal HaMaor. The second Mishnah therefore applies even where money is given first and the note written afterward, because the creditor can explain that he borrowed in order to preserve the practical result that each collects from the other. The special rule about borrowing on the due date becomes unnecessary according to Rav Nachman, because if even after maturity the first creditor does not lose his note, certainly he does not lose it merely by borrowing on the day of maturity. Since the Rif's view is therefore that of the Baal HaMaor, the same must be true of the Rosh, who follows the Rif's formulation. I have already shown that this is the primary interpretation, and the Ritva and Rashi likewise support it. Rashi explains that the Gemara asks why the Sages validate the first creditor's note after its due date, since he should have demanded payment instead of borrowing. This explanation applies even where the document is written before money is given. Why then is the same question not asked against Rav Nachman? Necessarily because of the Baal HaMaor's explanation. The position of the Ramah cited by the Tur also proves this. He writes that whether the local practice is to give money and then write the note, or write the note and then give money, the later creditor can say to the earlier creditor, "You should have collected your debt," unlike the case where a creditor sold land to his debtor, because there, when the deed was written first, the creditor can say that he feared the purchaser would spend the money and therefore sold him the field in order to create property he could seize. In the mutual loan case, however, even when the document is written first, the first creditor should have collected. This is the "some say" opinion cited by the Mechaber. But according to that opinion too one must ask why the Gemara raises the due date difficulty only according to Rav Sheshet and not Rav Nachman. The answer must again be the Baal HaMaor's reasoning. Plainly, the Ramah's statement was intended only as an explanation of the sugya according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman, even if the creditor cannot say he borrowed in order to create seizable property, his note is not invalid, because he can say he acted so that each would collect from the other. This makes the Tur and Mechaber difficult, for they present the Ramah as though he ruled that in every circumstance the first creditor can be told, "You should have collected your debt." Indeed, the Gemara itself compels the conclusion that without the reason that each collects, one could say, "You should have collected your debt," as the Ramah maintains, rather than the contrary opinion of the Ramban and those who follow him. In the first Mishnah the Sages justify the creditor's conduct by saying that he acted shrewdly in selling the field because he could later seize it. This implies that absent that reason he should have collected his debt instead. Although one could force an alternative explanation that the point is only that he might otherwise have sold to someone else, this is strained and not the plain meaning. Moreover, the Gemara's straightforward question against Rav Sheshet, "If the due date arrived, what is the reason of the Sages?" appears to apply whether money was given before the document or the document before the money, just as Rashi explains. This supports the Ramah's understanding. The Rambam likewise clearly follows the Baal HaMaor rather than the Ramban. At the end of chapter 24 of Hilchot Malveh he rules: If two people each produce a promissory note against the other, the later borrower cannot say to the earlier, "Had I owed you, how could you have borrowed from me?" Rather, each collects his debt. If the debts are equal and both have the same class of land, each remains with what he has. If one has superior and intermediate land while the other has inferior land, one collects from the intermediate and the other from the inferior. He then separately rules that where a creditor produces a note and the debtor produces a deed showing that the creditor sold him a field, if local practice is that the purchaser gives money and the seller then writes the deed, the creditor's note is invalid because the purchaser can say, "Had I owed you, you should have applied the purchase money to your debt." If local practice is to write the deed first and pay later, the creditor's note remains valid because he can say that he sold the field so there would be known property from which he could collect. The Maggid Mishneh writes that the Ramban and Rashba limit the first rule where the earlier debt had matured before the second loan, and suggests that the Rambam did not mention this because loans are ordinarily documented before money is delivered. I do not find this persuasive. The Rambam's silence indicates the opposite, especially because immediately afterward, in the field sale case, he expressly distinguishes the two local practices while making no such distinction in the mutual loan case. He even reverses the order of the Mishnayot, discussing mutual loans first and the field sale second. Had the mutual loan rule depended on the same local practice, he should not have altered the order in a way that removes any possible implication that he relied on the immediately preceding qualification. Nor is it clear why loans should, as a rule, be documented before the money is delivered more than sales. On the Ramban's own approach the Mishnah according to Rav Sheshet can concern a place where money is given first, despite its unqualified language. The Rambam therefore plainly holds like the Baal HaMaor and intentionally teaches that in mutual promissory notes there is no distinction between the two local practices. Each collects from the other. Only afterward does he distinguish the sale of a field, where the practical reason differs. The distinction is sound. In a mutual loan, the first creditor still has in hand the money he borrowed from the other. His loss is only that he gave a paper obligation in exchange. We can therefore say that although he accepted the other's money, he intentionally did not extinguish his prior lien on intermediate land. In a field sale, by contrast, he received money but transferred land in exchange. He no longer retains value corresponding to the debt in the same manner, and if money was paid before the deed was written he should have applied it directly to his debt. This is, in my view, the clear meaning of the Rambam. The lengthy discussion in Gidulei Terumah at the beginning of gate 34, page 146, which raises various difficulties against the Rambam and other authorities, is based on a failure to understand the sugya. The truth of the sugya and the Rambam's position is as I have explained. According to the Baal HaMaor and those who agree with him, even if neither party owned land at the time of the second loan, or both had equal quality property, such as both intermediate or both inferior, the later loan does not invalidate the earlier note. The reason is not that the creditor saw an existing advantage in land quality. Rather, since an ordinary creditor's right is to intermediate land, he wished to preserve his existing lien, because perhaps by the time of collection his debtor would acquire intermediate land while he himself would own only inferior land. Since the law is that each collects from the other, preserving the first note can have practical value. This is why the Mishnah states without qualification that the Sages say each collects his promissory note. The Rambam likewise states the rule without qualification. This appears primary. The conclusion is therefore that whenever two people each produce a promissory note against the other, each collects his debt. This is evident from the Gemara and is the view of the Rif, Rosh, Rambam, Rashi, Baal HaMaor, Ramah, and Ritva, unlike the Tur, Mechaber, and those who follow them. I will now address the wording of the Tur and Mechaber point by point.
What they wrote, that Shimon can say to Reuven, "Had I owed you, etc." This is not correct. Even in a place where money is given first and the document written afterward, Shimon cannot make that argument, because Reuven can answer: I did not wish to extinguish my lien on your intermediate land while giving you a lien on my inferior land. Even if neither owns land now, or both own equal classes of land, such as both superior, both intermediate, or both inferior, Reuven may say: Since the law is that each collects from the other, I left my note in force because perhaps at the time of collection you will have intermediate land and I will have only inferior land.
And specifically when the note is clear, etc. This ruling requires study, because the Rosh in responsa did not state it as a simple rule but only together with additional considerations. He writes in kelal 72, siman 2, that he does not fully understand the proposed argument if it is based on the last chapter of Kesubos concerning two people who each hold a promissory note against the other. There too Shimon seeks to invalidate Reuven's note because Reuven later borrowed from him without first collecting, yet we rule like the Sages. Moreover, perhaps when Reuven borrowed from Shimon the third party had not yet returned Shimon's note to Reuven. Even if the note was written after that note was returned, the case is not comparable to Admon's field sale, because a promissory note is not such certain, immediate property that collection is assured, and Reuven may meanwhile have needed money and borrowed from Shimon because he did not have time to press collection on the note. Thus the Rosh did not rule on the basis of this consideration alone, and the Rama's ruling therefore requires study. According to what I established above, that each collects in all circumstances, this point makes no difference here, though it can matter in the separate field sale rule in seif 1.
And what they wrote, "There is an authority who says there is no distinction, etc." The Tur writes this in the name of the Ramah. I am astonished at the Tur and Mechaber. How can this authority mean that there is literally no distinction, such that Reuven's note is always invalid? Our Mishnah says that according to the Sages each collects his note. The Gemara's limitation to a loan made on the day of maturity was only according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman, whom we follow, the first creditor collects even after the first due date. Even if one says that Rav Nachman's Mishnah is limited to a place where the note is written before money is given, as the Ramban understands, it still cannot be true that there is no distinction at all. Plainly the Ramah wrote his statement only to explain the sugya according to Rav Sheshet. The Gemara asks that if the first debt had matured, why the Sages validate it, and one might answer that the practice was to write the note first and give money afterward. The Ramah therefore explains that in the mutual loan case, unlike the field sale case, even this local practice does not supply the reason that the creditor wished to create property he could seize. That is only according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman the point does not matter, because even if the creditor cannot say that he borrowed in order to seize property, the first note remains valid on the ground that each collects from the other. To force the Tur's words into a narrower meaning is very strained. The Mechaber, Ir Shushan, Sema, and other later authorities also appear to understand the cited view as invalidating the first note in all circumstances, and that is astonishing.
And what they wrote, "Some say this applies only in a place where they give the money, etc." It is true that this is the Ramban's view, followed by the Ran, Baal HaTerumot at the beginning of gate 34, Tur, and Mechaber. But I have already proved that this is not correct. Even in a place where money is given first and the note written afterward, each collects his promissory note in all circumstances. This is evident from the Gemara and is the view of the Rif, Rosh, Rambam, Rashi, Baal HaMaor, Ramah, and Ritva. This is clear and primary.
And what they wrote, "Even if Reuven has inferior land and Shimon has superior land, etc." "Superior" here means property better than Reuven's, namely intermediate land. Likewise, when they later say, "so that I may collect your superior land," they mean property superior to his own, namely intermediate land. The wording is abbreviated in this sense, as the Bach also explains. Alternatively, "superior" is a scribal error and should read "intermediate." This is unlike the strained explanations of the Ir Shushan and Sema, seif katan 8.
And what they wrote, "If he is in a place where they write the document, etc." I have already explained that this is unnecessary. The same rule applies even in a place where money is given first.
Likewise, if one has intermediate and inferior land, etc., each remains with his own. This means that he never owned superior land. If he owned superior land at the time of the loan and later sold it, he must give intermediate land, as the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Kamma reconciles the apparently conflicting baraitot. The Bach writes this correctly, and the same is explained below in siman 102 seif 4. It appears that the same qualification is required in the earlier clause that discusses one party having intermediate land and the other inferior land. If the first had superior land and sold it, he must give intermediate land. The Bach therefore could even more appropriately have stated his qualification there.
And if the time, etc. Meaning, the date on which Shimon lent Reuven the loan evidenced by Shimon's note was before Reuven's earlier note had reached its due date.
Then in every circumstance, etc. "In every circumstance" means whether local practice is to write the note first or to give the money first. In either case both notes remain valid. If there is no practical gain, each remains with what he has. If one has superior and intermediate land and the other inferior land, then each actually collects from the other. This is clearly the implication of the Ir Shushan. Gidulei Terumah, page 146d, objects to the Tur and Mechaber and also to the Ir Shushan for writing that in every circumstance collection proceeds in the manner described. There is no difficulty. The intent of the Tur and Mechaber is clearly as I have explained, and the Ir Shushan merely expressed it more precisely.
Even if it was on the final day, etc. The Tur and Mechaber imply that this is true even where money is given before the note is written, and this follows even on the Ramban's approach. Although I disagreed with the Ramban above, on this point his conclusion is correct. According to Rav Sheshet, the Mishnah presumably concerns both local practices, just as according to Rav Nachman it does. Thus Rav Sheshet's ruling regarding a loan made on the very day the first debt becomes due applies even where money is given first, and we have no indication that Rav Nachman disagrees with him. Nevertheless, according to the conclusion I reached above, that even after the first debt has matured the creditor cannot be told, "You should have collected," because he can say that he acted so that each would collect from the other, the special rule about borrowing on the day of maturity has no independent significance in the mutual loan case. If the note remains valid even after maturity, certainly it remains valid on the day of maturity, without needing the additional argument that a person may obligate himself for a single day. This is why the Rif, Rosh, and Rambam do not mention that special rule. It does, however, matter in the field sale case in seif 1, where the reason that each collects is inapplicable. If the creditor sells the debtor a field on the very day the debt matures, even in a place where money is given first and the deed written afterward, he does not thereby lose his note, as explained above in seif katan 5.

He answers that the case concerns a note without a date, which the Tur at the beginning of siman 43 rules is still valid for collection from unencumbered assets. This is not precise.
