And some say that there is no distinction, etc. The Shach wonders at the Ramah's ruling, since there is an explicit Mishnah stating that each creditor collects from the other. The Gemara's establishment of the case as occurring on the very day payment becomes due is needed according to Rav Sheshet, but according to Rav Nachman, whose view we follow, the Gemara appears not to require that limitation. One must therefore answer either like the Baal HaMaor or like the Ramban as to why Rav Nachman does not face the same difficulty. In any event, the law remains that the creditor does not lose his deed merely because he borrowed from the debtor. The Shach leaves the matter requiring study. It appears that there is no difficulty. The Ramah certainly confronted the question raised by the Baal HaMaor, Ramban, and other authorities, namely that the Gemara's question against Rav Sheshet, "if it is within the term, what is Admon's reason, and if it is after the term, what is the Sages' reason?" appears equally applicable to Rav Nachman. The Ramah did not accept the Baal HaMaor's answer because of the Ramban's objections, as explained above. Nor did he accept the Ramban's answer that the case concerns a place where deeds are written first and money is then delivered, so that the plea, "You should have collected your debt," is unavailable. The Shach discusses this at length. The Ramah therefore understood the matter as follows. The Baal HaTerumot was uncertain whether the second person's deed is legally treated as a receipt against the first deed. The Yerushalmi at the end of Ketubot indicates that this depends on the dispute between Rav Sheshet and Rav Nachman. According to Rav Sheshet, who holds that each remains with his own claim, if one party forgives his debt the other still does not collect, because from the outset each deed functions as a receipt against the other. Likewise, if one debt is evidenced by deed and the other is oral, the holder of the deed cannot collect from encumbered property, because the other claim is effectively a receipt beside it. According to Rav Nachman, however, who holds that each collects from the other, the party who did not forgive his deed may collect, while the party who forgave cannot. Similarly, the creditor with the written deed may collect from encumbered property, whereas the oral creditor cannot. See there. Thus the Yerushalmi understands Rav Sheshet as treating each deed as a receipt against the other. But the Bavli does not appear to understand Rav Sheshet this way. Rami bar Chama establishes the Mishnah according to Rav Sheshet in a case of heirs, where the living creditor cannot collect from the orphans but the orphans can collect from the living creditor. If each deed were treated as a receipt against the other, how could the orphans collect from the living creditor? Their father's deed would already have functioned as a receipt canceling the deed they now hold. Necessarily, even according to Rav Sheshet, one deed is not literally treated as a receipt canceling the other. The Gemara says that according to Admon, "a person does not borrow for one day," meaning that he would not voluntarily make himself a servant to the lender for such a brief interval, as Rashi explains. That reasoning applies only if the second deed is not itself treated as a receipt. If it were treated as a receipt, the borrower would not truly make himself a servant to the lender at all, since he already holds an offsetting deed. What practical difference would there be whether the document says, "so and so borrowed," or "so and so paid," if the second deed has the legal effect of a receipt? Accordingly, at the Gemara's initial stage it followed the Yerushalmi's premise. With Rav Nachman, the answer was already known: the case occurs on the day payment falls due, and the parties disagree as explained in the Gemara. According to Rav Nachman, all agree that the second deed is not a receipt. But against Rav Sheshet the Gemara asks properly that one cannot explain the dispute as concerning that day. What is Admon's reason? If Rav Sheshet treats the second deed as a receipt, the borrower has not truly made himself a servant at all. The Gemara then answers that even according to Rav Sheshet the Bavli's approach works, namely that the second deed is not treated as a receipt. The dispute can therefore concern the day payment falls due, whether borrowing for that one day reflects a genuine debtor relationship. According to this, as a matter of law even Rav Nachman agrees that the dispute between the Sages and Admon concerns the day payment falls due. After that day, everyone agrees that the creditor has lost his deed. These are precisely the words of the Ramah. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch make clear that his case concerns after the due date, whereas on the due date itself each remains with his own claim. According to all opinions, after the due date he loses the deed. There is therefore no difficulty. In my humble opinion this is a straightforward and spacious explanation.
And Shimon has superior land, etc. This is the Sema's reading. He explains that Reuven can collect Shimon's superior land before Shimon's own debt reaches its due date. But if both debts come for collection at the same time, that explanation cannot stand, as the Sema himself writes. The Shach therefore proposes reading "intermediate land" instead of "superior land," meaning at the time of collection, because we rule that valuation is made according to the debtor's own property. The Shach is consistent with his general approach. The Baal HaMaor explains why the Gemara above did not ask Rav Nachman as well what the reasons of the Sages and Admon are. According to him, the borrower can say, "I borrowed from you so that I could collect your intermediate land, while you would collect only my inferior land." But according to Rav Sheshet, who holds that each remains with his own property, the question remains. The Ramban objects that even without borrowing from him the creditor could have collected intermediate land on his own debt. If his purpose was to sell his inferior land, he could have borrowed from someone else. Further, even according to Rav Sheshet, he could immediately collect the intermediate land and sell it to another at a high price, while the other creditor would then not find it in his possession. Moreover, even according to Rav Sheshet, where the second creditor owns only superior land and the first only inferior land, the first can say that he borrowed specifically so that he could immediately collect the other's superior land, while the other, when his debt later matures, would be limited to the inferior land. These are the Ramban's objections. The Shach labors at length to answer them. Especially regarding the final objection, that one creditor could collect superior land immediately while the other later receives only inferior land, he answers with difficulty that we do not suspect such a stratagem. According to his approach, it is indeed necessary to emend the Shulchan Aruch here. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch seek to reject the Baal HaMaor's view and follow the Ramban, that such considerations are not accepted as explanations for why the person borrowed and do not preserve the deed. How then could they themselves give the argument that he intended immediately to collect superior land, an argument that even the Baal HaMaor would have to reject, since otherwise the Ramban's question would answer Rav Sheshet as well? It is therefore better, according to the Shach's approach, to emend the text to "intermediate land." That is an argument recognized by the Baal HaMaor, yet the Shulchan Aruch teaches that unlike the Baal HaMaor, even this is not a sufficient reason to say that the creditor would deliberately become a borrower. However, in truth all of the Shach's resolutions on behalf of the Baal HaMaor are exceedingly strained and insufficient to reject the Ramban's words. Anyone who examines them will see the difficulty. Yet I did not understand the Ramban's first objection, that the creditor could in any event collect intermediate land on his own debt. We are saying that he set his eye upon this particular intermediate parcel and desired it. If he simply tried to collect his existing debt, the debtor had cash available and could discharge the debt with money. How then could he force collection of the land? He therefore contrived to remove the debtor's available cash through a long term loan, ensuring that when the original debt later came for collection he would necessarily receive the land. This also answers the Ramban's second objection, that even according to Rav Sheshet the creditor could collect the intermediate land and sell it. First, we are saying that he desired the other person's specific land and wanted to retain it. But even if he intended to collect and sell it, either buyers are available who will pay a high price, in which case the debtor himself can sell it and discharge the debt in cash while retaining the profit, or buyers are not available, in which case the creditor will end up retaining the land. That is precisely Rav Nachman's issue of whether valuation is according to the debtor's property or the general market. Moreover, if the creditor sells land he collected, what will he do with the proceeds? He will then have cash, and if he holds cash he cannot insist upon collecting land. These objections are therefore weak, and the Shach labored unnecessarily to answer them. The Ramban's final objection, however, where the second creditor owns only superior land, is powerful. One can plausibly say that the first creditor borrowed for a fixed term so that he could immediately collect the superior land, while the second creditor would later receive only inferior land. The Shach's answer that we do not suspect trickery is strained. The entire proposed reason for the loan is that the first creditor sought to profit. How can we cancel the other creditor's deed when he cries out that this profit was exactly his intent and the reason he borrowed? There is no need to lengthen the discussion, since the difficulty is evident. It therefore appears to me, on the contrary, that the Baal HaMaor considers this last argument compelling. Where the second creditor owns only superior land, the first can plainly say, "That is what I intended. I set my eye upon the superior land." Indeed, this argument is so strong that it creates the question: what is Admon's reason? Surely this is an excellent explanation for why the creditor borrowed. The Gemara therefore assumes that in such a case all agree that a person may make himself a borrower, because what difference does it make to him when he already holds the money and his existing deed remains effective, as the Shach himself observes. Rav Sheshet's view therefore cannot be established in such a case, because Admon's position would be inexplicable. Rav Nachman, however, can be understood in a case where one party has superior and intermediate land while the other has only inferior land. According to the Sages, each collects from the other because the creditor can say that he borrowed in order to gain the advantage in land quality. Admon's position is also understandable, because the Tannaim may disagree whether valuation is made according to the general market or according to the debtor's own property. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 8 presents this as a Tannaitic dispute according to one answer; see the Nimukei Yosef and Ran in the chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot on this sugya. Admon can follow the Tanna who values according to the general market, in which case there is no advantage of the kind described by the Baal HaMaor, and the creditor's deed is therefore impaired. The Sages can follow the accepted halachah that valuation is according to the debtor's own property, so each collects. Thus Rav Nachman's position is entirely consistent. But Rav Sheshet cannot be established where the second creditor owns superior land, for the Ramban's objection remains: what is Admon's reason? This answer is broad and straightforward, and resolves all of the Ramban's objections. Even so, the Ramban did not accept this approach. The Baal HaTerumot, Tur, Ran, and Shach write that the Rambam agrees with the Baal HaMaor because he does not distinguish between a place where money is first delivered and a deed is then written, and a place where the deed is written first and the money later given. This is not correct. The Rambam explicitly writes concerning the plea, "If I owed you, why did you borrow from me?" that if both parties own superior land or both own inferior land, each remains with his own property. According to the Baal HaMaor, once one creditor lent to the other after the due date, the first should lose his deed where their properties are equal, because there is no profit to explain why he borrowed. One cannot say that perhaps he borrowed in order to sell or purchase, for then the same answer would apply even according to Rav Sheshet. It is therefore clear that when both own equivalent property, even according to the Baal HaMaor there is no explanation preserving the creditor's deed, contrary to the Shach's suggestion. The Rambam's ruling thus shows that he does not follow the Baal HaMaor. The Rif's unqualified language likewise gives no distinction that where both have only inferior property the creditor loses his deed, which also indicates that the Rif does not follow the Baal HaMaor, contrary to the Shach's attribution of that view to the Rif and Rambam. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch therefore wrote their rule precisely to reject the Baal HaMaor. They state the stronger case that even where one party owns only superior land, the creditor does not thereby lose his deed. In such a case there is certainly a plausible argument that he set his eye upon the superior land, which is not commonly available for purchase, and intended to collect it, as the Sema explains. The Baal HaMaor considers that such a strong argument that, as I explained, it would make Admon's position impossible. Nevertheless, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch teach that a person does not make himself a servant to the lender merely for that purpose, following the Ramban. There is therefore no need to emend the text at all. This is precise.
And if it is in a place where they write the document first, etc. This view is that of the Ramban, who holds that in a place where they first write the document, even if he borrowed after the due date he did not forfeit his document, because it was difficult for him to press the debtor for payment and he needed money. The Mishnah is thus established according to Rav Nachman, and therefore the Sages hold that the document is valid. Nor is it difficult that this is identical to the first clause, for the novelty there is Rav Nachman's rule. According to Rav Sheshet, however, the Gemara properly asks that this is identical to the first clause, and it establishes the case where he first gave the money and where it was on the very day the term expired. The Baal HaTerumot, Ran, Tur, and this view agree with this. The Shach argued against the Ramban: how could the entire Mishnah have been taught merely for the rule that valuation is made from his own property? It should have taught that rule separately. What then is taught by the dispute between Admon and the Sages? This objection can be answered. Certainly the words and reasoning of the Baal HaMaor, although not accepted as halachah according to the Ramban, contain enough basis for an erroneous assumption. Thus the latter clause teaches that one might otherwise have thought like the Baal HaMaor, in which case even Admon would agree that he did not forfeit his document, since he could say, "I intended to collect intermediate quality land." The Mishnah therefore teaches otherwise. On this reasoning the Sages and Admon would not disagree. This also answers why it could not teach only the latter clause and omit the first, because one might have thought that the Sages rule as they do only because the reasoning of the Baal HaMaor is also present.
However, what the Shach wrote, that Rashi does not accept the Ramban's reasoning because Rashi writes, "If it was at the due date, what is the reason of the Sages? He could have demanded payment," and this indicates otherwise than the Ramban, since according to the Ramban, where the practice is to write the document first, we say that he did not forfeit his document even though he could have demanded payment, is well stated. Yet in my opinion Rashi had another intention that resolves the entire difficulty and is almost consistent with the Ramban's rule. The Gemara answers that one loan was for five years and the other for ten years. It certainly could have said that one loan had no fixed due date at all and the other was for ten years. But the ordinary implication of the Mishnah, which states simply that two people produced debt instruments against one another, is that one document is of the same general character as the other. If one had a fixed term, so did the other. Since the second loan necessarily had a fixed due date, as is explicit in the Gemara, presumably the first did as well. The commentators likewise explain that this must be so. Nor is there a difficulty why both need fixed terms. Since the second borrower borrowed when the ordinary thirty day term of the first loan expired, and his own loan was also unspecified and therefore had thirty days, collection of the first debt would seem to precede collection of the second. This is not so, because the first borrower, against whom his creditor now seeks collection, is entitled to the beit din extension of thirty days, while the second borrower is not entitled to such an extension, since the other can say to him, "You already have what to pay me with, namely what I am paying you." Consequently, their collection times coincide. This is straightforward. Accordingly, below in siman 100 seif 2 the view of Rabbenu Tam is stated that where a term was fixed, no beit din extension is granted when payment becomes due. Thus according to Rav Nachman there is no difficulty in the Gemara's question, "If it was after the due date, what is the reason of the Sages?" Rashi explains, "He could have demanded payment." What follows from the fact that he could have demanded it? Beit din would still have given the debtor thirty days, while the creditor needed money and could not wait, so he necessarily had to borrow. There is therefore no difficulty at all. Nor can one object that this works only if no term was fixed in the instruments, whereas if a term was fixed there is no beit din extension and he should indeed have demanded payment. The Mishnah simply says that two people produced debt instruments and can therefore be understood as speaking of ordinary instruments without a fixed term. There is no difficulty. But according to Rav Sheshet, who is troubled by the Mishnah's statement that "this one collects and that one collects," and must say that the second loan had a term fixed in the instrument for five years, it follows that presumably the first also had a fixed term, as explained above. Moreover, how could the Mishnah speak without qualification? We cannot say, as above, that it discusses ordinary instruments without a fixed due date, because the second instrument necessarily did contain a term. This is clear. Therefore, since the case includes an instrument with a fixed term, the question is properly asked: what is the reason of the Sages? As Rashi explains, he should have demanded payment. There is no concern for a beit din extension, because no such extension applies. This is an exceedingly satisfactory explanation. All this is where the practice is to write the document and then give the money. In the reverse order, the beit din extension is irrelevant, because the creditor already had his own money in hand. He should not then have written a new document. What beit din extension could he invoke when he himself already held the money? This also resolves the Ran's difficulty. The Ran asks that according to Rav Sheshet the Gemara could have explained "this one collects and that one collects" as a case involving minor orphans, who collect from him immediately while he must wait to collect from them until they reach majority. At first glance his question is difficult to understand, for this is the very same answer as the Gemara's answer that he lent for ten years and must wait until the term ends. What difference is there whether "this one collects" means that he must wait until the term expires or that he must wait until the orphans reach majority? What is gained by this answer? According to what I have written, it is well understood. If so, even according to Rav Sheshet we can say, as according to Rav Nachman, that the ordinary instruments mentioned in the Mishnah have no fixed due date, and therefore he was afraid to demand payment lest beit din grant the debtor an extension. The statement "this one collects and that one collects" would mean that meanwhile one of them died, leaving minor orphans, so he must wait while they collect immediately. This is satisfactory, and the Ran asked a powerful question. Indeed, this answer would have been especially satisfactory in the Rambam, who omitted the rule concerning a place where they first give the money and then write the document, namely that if he lent after the due date he forfeited his document. The Maggid Mishneh explains that the ordinary course of a loan is always to write the document before giving the money. According to the Ramban this is difficult, because according to Rav Sheshet the Mishnah was established precisely in the reverse setting, even though it speaks without qualification. How then can we determine that documents always precede delivery of the money? According to what I have written, this would appear resolved, because even according to Rav Sheshet the Mishnah can concern a place where the document is written first, while nevertheless he should have demanded payment, as Rashi says. Still, it remains difficult why the Rambam did not mention that if a due date was fixed in the document and the creditor lent after that due date, he forfeited his document, as above. Therefore it appears more correct that with loan documents, almost all are written before the money is advanced, or at least both occur simultaneously. With a sale it is different. A seller who transfers his field in the presence of witnesses allows collection from encumbered property, and the seller will also trust the purchaser, for why should one be more trustworthy than the other? But with a loan, before there is a document there is no lien, and "the borrower is servant to the lender". Certainly they therefore first write the document. Yet Admon's reasoning, that he should have issued a protest in a sale, can also be applied in a loan document in the following way. In a sale he issues a protest because there was no genuine sale of his field at all, only a device to extract his money. In a loan, however, the loan itself is genuine. If the purpose of the document is a lien on property, and he were to issue a protest that he did not truly encumber his property but wrote it only to obtain the money, that works according to Rav Nachman, because his property became encumbered. According to Rav Sheshet, however, each party remains with his own property, so his property was never effectively encumbered, since the other cannot collect from it if he chooses not to pay. When the creditor comes to seize property he can tell him, "Here is your own document; collect what is yours." What role, then, could a protest have here? See what I wrote above in seif katan 4 in the name of the Yerushalmi, see there. Accordingly, one may say that the Gemara assumes that Admon and the Sages would not disagree over the reasoning expressed by Rashi, that once the term arrived he could have demanded payment. Thus according to Rav Nachman one can say that the Sages hold that he did not forfeit his document because we do not say that he should necessarily have demanded payment, as the Ramban explains. If one asks why Admon disagrees, it can be answered as in the preceding Mishnah, that Admon holds he should have issued a protest. According to Rav Sheshet, by contrast, such a protest is inapplicable. Therefore, if we were to say that the argument "he should have demanded payment" is not accepted, what would be Admon's reason? The parties would not disagree over that point. It follows that we do say he should have demanded payment, as Rashi writes. The Gemara can therefore ask: whichever way you take it, if it was at the due date, etc., and it answers that it was on the day itself, etc. All this is according to Rav Sheshet. According to Rav Nachman, however, we say that the argument "he should have demanded payment" is not controlling, and the dispute is as explained above. There is thus no contradiction in Rashi, because Rashi wrote his comment according to Rav Sheshet, not according to Rav Nachman. This is satisfactory and should be carefully considered. In my novellae I offered many approaches to resolve this difficulty of the Ran, but because most of them are lengthy dialectical arguments far from the central path of the poskim, I refrained from recording them here. See also what I write in the next seif katan on this matter.
And on the day that the five years were completed. The Sema and the Perishah write that although we said above that a person may pay on the day the term expires, nevertheless the creditor cannot compel him to do so. I am astonished. The entire discussion concerns a case where the money was given first and only afterward was the document written. If so, on the day the term expires, can it be imagined that if the creditor seized the money in the morning the borrower could recover it from him? Today is the final day of the term, and by the end of the term he is obligated to pay. Would a creditor who seized at the beginning of the day have to return it to the borrower until the end of the day? This cannot be. If so, why should he make himself "a borrower who is servant to the lender"? When the other gives him the money, he can say, "I am retaining it in payment, because today is the end of the term." Who compels him to execute a new document? It therefore appears that "on the day the five years were completed" means the day before the actual expiration, because a person sometimes pays on the day preceding the end of the term. The obligation of payment takes effect only at the end of that day. The case therefore concerns an earlier day, when there is no obligation at all to pay, even at day's end. The creditor therefore cannot seize anything, and if he does seize it, the borrower recovers it from him. This is satisfactory. From here I found support for the Ramban's explanation that according to Rav Sheshet the Mishnah concerns a place where they first give the money and afterward write the document. We must understand why Rav Nachman, according to Rav Sheshet, is forced to say that the first loan was for five years. Why not say that the first loan was an ordinary unspecified loan, and that when its term expired the creditor borrowed from his own borrower? Then "this one collects" immediately and "that one collects" at the end of the fixed day. Why is there any need to say that the first loan was for five years? As I wrote above in siman 67 in Tumim seif katan 16, with proof, the rule that an unspecified loan is for thirty days means only that the lender cannot compel repayment before then, but if he seizes payment we do not remove it from him. Accordingly, we cannot say that the first loan was unspecified, because then the reason of the Sages would again be difficult. Even if the second loan was made on the day the first term expired, since the money was given first the creditor should have retained it toward his debt, for if he seized it we would not remove it from him. Why should he execute a document? We must therefore say that the first loan had a fixed term of five years or the like. If he seized before the term was fully completed, the money would unquestionably be taken from him. He therefore needed to make a document for ten years, as explained above. This is satisfactory. All this follows the Ramban's view that according to Rav Sheshet the case can be established only where the money was given first. If, however, we say that even according to Rav Sheshet the case concerns a place where the document is written first, and that he should have demanded payment as the Shach writes, the difficulty remains: why not establish it as an unspecified loan? What compels us to say that a term of five years was fixed? Consider this carefully.
For ten years, etc. The Sema writes in seif katan 15 that the Tur and Mechaber state this for a greater novelty. Even if he was unwilling to lend to him except for a long term of ten years, one might have thought that for the sake of one day he would not subject himself and become "a borrower who is servant to the lender" for such a long period. The ruling therefore teaches that he can still claim, "I was compelled to do so because I needed the money that day and he was unwilling to lend it to me except for such a long term." This is the substance of his words, with slight changes in formulation, and he writes similarly in the Perishah. This requires explanation. We rule above in siman 74 that a fixed term benefits the borrower, and if the borrower wishes to pay within the term the lender must accept payment. What difference, then, does it make to the borrower whether he makes himself a servant for one day or for ten years? If he wishes, he can pay early; and if he does not, the longer term benefits him because he is not obligated to pay and the lender cannot press him. Likewise, what does it mean that the lender compelled him and would not lend for a shorter term? What benefit does the lender receive? Even if he lengthens the term, the borrower can still pay within it, so the term ordinarily benefits only the borrower. We must therefore say that here the case is that he lent for ten years on condition that the borrower would have no right to repay within the term. The Tur thus interprets the Gemara when Rav Nachman, according to Rav Sheshet, says that one loan was for five years and the other for ten years: the meaning is that the borrower had no right to pay before the term. See Rashi there in that sugya, s.v. beha, etc., which likewise implies that this term benefits the lender and that he prefers its length. There must therefore have been such a stipulation. What compelled the Tur to interpret the Gemara this way appears as follows. According to what I wrote above, and below in siman 100, when a term is fixed in a document the debtor is not granted an additional beit din extension. Why, then, did Rav Nachman need to say that the first loan was for five years and the second for ten years? Let him say that the second loan was simply unspecified, without any fixed term. Its period would nevertheless be thirty days. The first loan's term had already ended when the second loan was made, as is explicit in the Gemara, and no beit din extension would apply. It would then be obvious that each collects from the other, with collection of the first debt preceding collection of the second in time. See what I wrote above in seif katan 6 resolving this. The Tur therefore holds that Rav Nachman is explaining Rav Sheshet's statement that "this one collects and that one collects" in the Mishnah to mean that the time for this collection precedes the other. Yet a difficulty remains: what novelty does the Mishnah teach by that? The entire subject of the Mishnah is to teach whether the holder of the first document retains his rights under it. It could simply have stated, "The Sages say he collects his document." Do we not already know that if his collection date is earlier, he collects before the second creditor? According to Rav Nachman the Mishnah teaches the rule that valuation is made from each debtor's own property, but according to Rav Sheshet what does it teach at all? Therefore Rav Nachman explains that one loan was for five years and one for ten, meaning that the borrower could not repay within the term and thus made himself a servant to the lender for that entire period. The phrase "this one collects and that one collects" means at the respective fixed times, and even if he wishes to satisfy the debt earlier the lender can prevent him. The Mishnah therefore teaches a substantial novelty: even in this circumstance the Sages hold that he collects his document, and we do not say that for the sake of one day he would not have made himself a servant to the lender for so long, as the Sema explains. This is indeed a major novelty and fits the Mishnah well. Rav Nachman therefore needed a fixed term, because with an ordinary unspecified loan the borrower is not constrained and can repay at any moment, leaving the earlier difficulty about what the Mishnah teaches by saying that this one collects. This is satisfactory. On this basis I found a very satisfactory answer for the Rif and Rambam, who rule without qualification that this one collects and that one collects, and do not distinguish between one local practice and another, or between borrowing at the due date and after it. One may answer differently. I already wrote above in siman 43, regarding why a postdated document is valid, that we should seemingly be concerned here as well that Reuven borrowed from Shimon while the term stated in Reuven's own document had not yet expired, and therefore did not forfeit his document, but afterward the witnesses postdated the document. The date of the second loan would then appear later than the due date of Reuven's document against Shimon, causing Reuven's document to be invalidated improperly. I wrote there that if he makes such a claim he is indeed believed. The case under discussion is only where he admits that he borrowed after the due date of his own document. Yet one might say that he has a migo: he could have claimed that the second document was postdated and would have been believed, so when he says truthfully that he borrowed only after his document became due, but nevertheless his first document is genuine, he should also be believed. This is not a migo to extract money, because it operates in support of an existing document. We must therefore say that since there is a chazakah that a person does not make himself "a borrower who is servant to the lender" when he is able to collect his own document, the migo stands against a chazakah. According to this, however, the Mishnah may always concern a loan made after the due date. The reason of the Sages is not difficult: the Sages hold that this chazakah is not sufficiently strong, and therefore a migo is effective against it, whereas Admon holds that it is a strong chazakah and the migo is ineffective. See Tosafot to Ketubot 19, s.v. chazakah, who write that Rabbi Meir and the Sages dispute whether a migo is accepted against a chazakah. Thus one can say that Admon and the Sages dispute this point as well. But according to Rav Sheshet, where the second document must be for ten years, meaning, as explained, that the borrower is compelled not to repay within ten years, a postdated document of this kind is certainly invalid. If its date is delayed, the borrower becomes obligated to retain the money for a longer period. For example, if he actually borrowed in Nisan, then in Nisan of the eleventh year he would be permitted to repay. If the witnesses delay the date until Tishrei, however, the ten years would not end until the following Tishrei. This is plainly detrimental to the borrower and invalid. We therefore have a chazakah that witnesses act properly and did not postdate it. The Gemara can then properly ask: what is the reason of the Sages? We cannot answer that he is believed through a migo that the document was postdated, because the witnesses were not permitted to write such a postdated document. This is satisfactory. According to this, for us who rule like Rav Nachman, even if he borrowed after the due date he still collects on his first document because he has the migo that the second instrument was postdated. This explains why the Rif and Rambam state the rule without qualification. Consider this carefully.
