If he claims, "I am a partner in this wall," Kesef Mishneh explains Rambam as speaking of a wall known originally to belong to one party. If the other claims only a waiver allowing him to insert a beam, the waiver gives him no more than that particular beam. But if he claims he paid his share of the value of half the entire wall, he is believed once he has already used the wall for even one beam. In that situation there is no room for the concern, "Who says the Sages will obligate me?" because one who actually placed even one beam upon the wall is clearly obligated to participate in the applicable cost. His claim of payment therefore resembles ordinary payment after the due date. According to this, Rambam is best understood where the wall was built upon land belonging to both neighbors. In that case the second neighbor automatically acquires half of the wall through his courtyard. Nimukei Yosef explains similarly regarding a courtyard wall that fell and was rebuilt above four amot: once the wall stands on both parties' land, each courtyard acquires its half, because from the outset the builder intended that when the other agrees to pay he will own half. The second neighbor's only remaining obligation is payment of his share, and regarding that debt he is believed to say, "I paid," once the legal obligation is clear. But if the entire wall was built on Reuven's land alone and Shimon claims he paid and purchased half of it, the wall remains under Reuven's original ownership presumption. Shimon cannot establish purchase of half merely by claiming payment. He must prove acquisition by the ordinary modes for buying property, just as with any other purchase claim. Sema explains Rambam differently, as speaking where it is not known which neighbor owns the wall. Rivash already considered that reading and objected: if the wall stands between them and neither has a stronger possession than the other, why should one beam prove anything? Even if the wall were jointly owned, either partner could place beams on it whenever he wished. Rivash therefore left that explanation difficult. Kesef Mishneh's interpretation is preferable.
The Shulchan Aruch says the presumption arises only if he made a ceiling. Sema explains that according to Rambam and those who follow him no three year period is needed, while according to Rabbeinu Tam and his school three years of such use are required. He adds that Rama's later opinion saying there is no distinction follows Rosh and therefore means three years of inserting beams. In my view, here everyone agrees that three years and a supporting ownership claim are required. Rambam's rule that nuisance uses can acquire a presumption immediately applies only to rights of use. Here the possessor is claiming ownership in the body of the wall itself, and for that ordinary three year possession with a claim is required. Even according to what I wrote above, that Rambam believes one who placed even a single beam can say, "I paid my share," without three years, that was where the wall had been built on both parties' land and the second owner had already automatically acquired half of it. His only claim was that he had paid the monetary debt. Here, by contrast, the wall stands entirely on one person's property and the other claims that he purchased half of the wall. That requires ordinary possession and a claim. Indeed, the very fact that Rosh is cited as saying the possession is effective as to the stones of the wall but not the soil beneath it indicates that the wall was built entirely on the first owner's land. In such a case, according to all opinions, three years and a proper purchase claim are necessary.
