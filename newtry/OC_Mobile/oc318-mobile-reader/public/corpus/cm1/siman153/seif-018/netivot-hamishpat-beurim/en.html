He waived only one. This implies that if he established use of all of them and placed a roof upon them, everything is waived and he need not pay half the wall. One might ask why this differs from the case of one who surrounds another's field on three sides and the surrounded party later fences the fourth, where the entire benefit is assessed against him because he benefited. Likewise, when one builds a wall above four cubits and the neighbor later joins another wall to it, the neighbor must contribute because he benefited. Here too the user benefits and causes a burden on the wall, so even if the owner cannot remove the beams because he remained silent and thereby consented, why should payment be waived? The distinction is that here the owner could have prevented the use altogether even if the other party was willing to pay, unless they reached an agreement. The wall is his, and another person has no right to insert beams into it. The other party's conduct visibly showed that he intended to use it gratuitously. When the owner remained silent, he agreed to the use on those terms. Since the waiver permitted the use, we presume that the waiver extended to the entire burden of the use, including payment. By contrast, where one surrounds another's field, the inner owner could not prevent the outer owner from building on his own land. The only issue is monetary contribution. Failure to demand payment immediately therefore does not prove waiver of the money. The same applies to a wall above four cubits. If the second owner wishes to join a wall or insert beams on condition that he pays his share, the original builder cannot prevent him. His failure to demand immediate payment therefore does not prove that he waived the monetary obligation.
But if he claims, "I am a partner in this wall." See Sema, seif katan 55, who explains that even if he claims purchase, insertion of beams proves only that he purchased a general right to use the wall. Ordinarily, one who acquires a right to rest beams on another's wall thereby acquires a share in the wall, but not in the underlying land unless that was expressly stated. Since the insertion of beams proves only a general purchase, it does not prove purchase of the site itself. This is how the Rosh appears to understand the matter. Nevertheless, Sema's reading of Mechaber is strained. If the claimant says simply that he is already a partner, why are the inserted beams needed at all? Beit Yosef himself raises this difficulty. The primary explanation is therefore Taz's: he claims partnership through a purchase claim. There remains another difficulty. This seif comes from Rambam. The first clause necessarily concerns immediate chazakah, because according to Rambam insertion of beams creates chazakah immediately. The latter clause, if it concerns a purchase claim, might appear to require three years, making the latter clause unlike the former. It therefore appears that here too chazakah is immediate even with a purchase claim. As I wrote above in siman 149, seif 9, digging pits can create immediate chazakah where the act benefits the possessor while damaging the owner. Where an act is destructive to everyone, like planting continuously in a place normally left fallow, it does not create chazakah. Where the act benefits everyone, like improving another's property, the owner can say, "I did not protest because I assumed you were improving it for me and would claim ordinary compensation." But where one alters the property in a way that disadvantages the owner while directly benefiting himself, that act can establish chazakah immediately. Rashba expressly rules in responsum 941 that one who digs a pit in another's property and opens it toward his own property establishes chazakah immediately. Inserting beams into the wall from one's own side is analogous to digging into it and opening the excavation toward one's own property. It should therefore establish chazakah immediately. It is even possible that Rambam, who may disagree above concerning ordinary pits, would agree here. Rashba writes in the cited responsum concerning one who dug an underground chamber beneath another's house that perhaps the depth beneath the house never belonged to the upper owner. The same can be said here. Who says that the far side of the wall ever belonged exclusively to the other owner when this person openly uses it and the other remains silent? Moreover, this case is stronger than ordinary excavation because at least regarding the inserted beams the owner can no longer revoke the use. Since even a protest cannot defeat the existing beam right, the silence more readily demonstrates that the wall itself is jointly owned.
I am a partner in it. See Sema, seif katan 35, who says that they live side by side and that we do not know to whom the intervening wall belongs. This cannot mean an ordinary jointly owned courtyard wall. If so, Rivash's question would apply: why are inserted beams needed at all, when the wall is already presumed jointly owned? Even if one says the case concerns a wall above four cubits or a parapet, the difficulty remains. If it is not known who built the wall, it is presumed to belong to both. If it is known that one person alone built it, then according to the opinion that insertion of beams does not prove ownership of the wall itself, the result should be straightforward: if the user is presumed to have paid, the stones are his; if not, even the stones are not his. It therefore appears that the case concerns Shimon's courtyard adjoining the wall of Reuven's house, with Shimon's property line reaching the outer face of Reuven's house wall. Without Shimon's beams inserted into the house wall, Shimon would not be believed to claim partnership in the wall, because the wall's principal use is for Reuven's house and Reuven would be regarded as the possessor while Shimon seeks to extract. But once Shimon's beams are inserted into the wall, and their arrangement shows that the beams are intended to support a house, Shimon is visibly using the wall as Reuven does. He is therefore deemed a possessor equally with Reuven. This resembles two people disputing a roof above their houses, as explained in siman 138, where they divide it. This is why Rama writes that this applies specifically when he says that he is a partner in the wall. But if he admits that originally the wall belonged entirely to Reuven and claims only a later purchase right, insertion of beams does not by itself prove the broader purchase claim. One can then speak of two houses on opposite sides of a public way, with no courtyard between them, as Sema was forced to explain. Sema's statement in seif katan 36 that according to Rambam a roof creates immediate chazakah is very difficult, as Ketzot HaChoshen objects. If the claimant comes through a purchase claim, one would ordinarily expect three years. The answer is what I wrote above: this is analogous to digging a pit for one's own use in another's property and opening it toward one's own domain, which Rashba says creates immediate chazakah.
