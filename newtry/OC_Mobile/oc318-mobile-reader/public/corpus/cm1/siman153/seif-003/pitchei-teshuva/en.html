See Baer Heitev and Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 160, seif 4, concerning one who sold his house but retained several rooms and the upper story for his own residence throughout his lifetime, and held a deed to that effect. The purchaser later altered the part of the upper story used by the seller, reduced its size, and incorporated some of it into his own portion. The seller protested privately, but the purchaser ignored him. When the seller later summoned the purchaser to beit din, the purchaser denied that any protest had been made and argued that because the seller saw the alteration and remained silent he waived his right. Shevut Yaakov ruled for the seller for several reasons. First, as explained in siman 153 in Sema, seif katan 52, where one comes to use another person's property, seeing and remaining silent does not suffice; a three year chazakah is required. Second, because the seller has a deed obligating the purchaser to provide the residence together with use of the entire upper story, waiver may not be effective, as Sema writes in siman 12, seif katan 21. Although the later authorities contain several contradictions on this issue, it remains at least a dispute, and the possessor can say that he follows the view favorable to him. Here land also remains in the chazakah of its original owner, as noted above in siman 154, seif 12, at the end of the gloss. More fundamentally, none of this is necessary. Even according to the view that waiver can work where a person holds a deed, that is only when the waiver is certain and clear from his own admission or from witnesses. Where the litigant denies that he waived and says that he did not, he is believed because the deed in his possession supports him. Proof for this distinction comes from Ketubot 104a, in the discussion beginning with the testimony of Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose before Rabbi. Accordingly, the law clearly favors the seller, and the purchaser must dismantle the construction he made in the upper story. See there.
