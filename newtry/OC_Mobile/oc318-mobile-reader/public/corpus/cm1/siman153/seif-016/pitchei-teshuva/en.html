A seller conveyed his house but retained several rooms and the upper story for his lifetime, documenting that right in a deed that remained in his possession. The buyer later altered and reduced the portion of the upper story used by the seller, incorporating some of it into his own area. The seller protested privately, but the buyer ignored him; when the seller later sued, the buyer denied any protest and argued that by seeing the alteration and remaining silent the seller had waived his right. Shevut Yaakov rules for the seller. First, where one begins using another's property, mere silence does not establish a right; ordinarily three years of possession are required. Second, because the seller holds a deed granting residence and use of the entire upper story, waiver is not readily inferred. Although later authorities differ on whether waiver can defeat a written instrument, the matter remains a dispute and the possessor may assert the opinion favorable to him; moreover, land may remain in the presumptive possession of its original owner. Most importantly, even those who recognize waiver despite a deed speak of a waiver that is certain, whether by admission or witnesses. Where the party expressly denies waiving and the deed in his hand supports him, he is believed. The Gemara in Ketubot 104a supports this distinction. The buyer must therefore dismantle the encroaching construction and restore the seller's use.
