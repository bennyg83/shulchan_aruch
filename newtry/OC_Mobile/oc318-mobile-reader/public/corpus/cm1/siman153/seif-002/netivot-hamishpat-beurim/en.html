And he did not protest. See Sema, seif katan 5, who understands Rambam to hold that even a projection smaller than a handbreadth can establish chazakah. According to this, Rambam must explain the Mishnah's phrase that the owner "cannot protest," and the dispute of Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah, in accordance with Rashbam's second interpretation: that one cannot initially prevent the projection from being fixed in the wall. Rambam takes the same approach, cited by Mechaber in seif 13 concerning a ladder, where one may initially place it in another's courtyard. We cannot explain Rambam as saying that Rav Huna's statement concerns use of the projection after it has already been acquired, because according to Rambam the owner of the projection already has a chazakah in it and could certainly object to the courtyard owner using it. Therefore the dispute must concern the initial placement. One may ask why Mechaber did not state that law here. It appears that he understood the matter like Shach in seif katan 3, that the projection may be placed only adjacent to one's own wall, a self evident rule. He therefore did not need to state it here, having already stated the parallel rule regarding a small ladder in seif 13. There is also no visual privacy damage here from the courtyard owner to the roof owner, so it is exactly comparable to the small ladder.
Immediately. See Sema, seif katan 3, meaning immediately once the damage became clear to him and he remained silent. At first glance it might appear that mere sight is enough, once he saw the window opened or saw that the required distance was not maintained. Ketzot HaChoshen appears to understand it this way, and asks what difference there is between sealing a window and other damages, since in all cases the one causing the damage must show that the neighbor saw the damaging condition. When they dispute that point, the one causing the damage is believed, as explained in siman 155, seif 43. Ketzot answers that with other damages it is enough to wait the amount of time in which the damage could become known, while with a window it is immediate. This is difficult. What amount of time is needed merely to see something? A glance takes a moment. If the one causing the damage is believed to say that he acted in the neighbor's presence and the neighbor saw him, then in every case the damage should be considered known at once. One possible distinction is that with ordinary damages the possessor must make a definite claim that the neighbor knew of the damage, as indicated by Mechaber's wording there, while with sealing a window perhaps even an uncertain claim would suffice. But the underlying proposition that mere sight always suffices is itself difficult. In siman 142, seif 3, Shach cites Bedek HaBayit, based on Maggid Mishneh, to reconcile Rambam with the ruling of R. Chiyya in Bava Batra 59 that one who labored to open a window must labor to close it when the neighbor protested immediately. If "immediately" meant the very instant he saw the window and protested, then the opinion of R. Yishmael son of R. Yose that an act done in another's presence immediately establishes chazakah would become inexplicable. His entire position is that presence and silence create immediate chazakah. If there was an immediate protest, everyone would agree there was no chazakah. Rambam's wording likewise indicates that sight and silence alone do not always suffice. In chapter 11 of Hilchot Shekhenim he writes that where the required distances were not maintained and the neighbor saw and remained silent, he is deemed to have waived, but only when conduct demonstrating waiver was seen, such as assisting him. Later Rambam says that if he saw him doing the act immediately beside him without any required distance and remained silent, the other acquired the right. This seems contradictory if sight alone always suffices. It therefore appears that mere sight is not enough when the neighbor can plausibly say, "I did not know this would harm me." Maggid Mishneh explains these laws through the concept of toleration. So long as no harm has yet become apparent, the neighbor has not necessarily tolerated anything. If, however, the matter is one that beit din judges to be obviously damaging to everyone, mere observation is enough. Rambam's phrase that he saw him act "beside him without distancing" means that the act was so close to the wall, with no distancing at all, that the neighbor could not plausibly claim ignorance of the damage. Thus every case depends on beit din's assessment whether enough time and circumstances existed for the damage to become clear, because a definite claim that he knew is required. With a window, the possessor must therefore say that the neighbor repeatedly saw him actually looking through and using the window, yet did not object, until beit din can conclude that he waived the privacy damage. This resolves R. Chiyya. R. Yishmael son of R. Yose holds that presence and silence create immediate chazakah even before the damage became fully clear. R. Chiyya requires that the damage itself first become evident according to beit din's assessment. Sealing a window is different, because the damage caused by sealing is obvious to everyone, so no special period of observation is needed. One should not ask why silence exempts the one causing the damage from payment where the damage has a measurable monetary component, as in siman 155, seif 33, where some hold that a person who caused damage against the owner's will must pay. Why should silence alone waive payment? This differs from Bava Kamma 27, where one places a coal on another's garment in the owner's presence and the owner remains silent, yet the damager must still pay, because the owner may think, "Tomorrow I will sue him." The difference is that here the damage is not fixed and complete at the moment of the act. Rather, a small harm accumulates day by day in a way that cannot easily be quantified separately for each day. It is therefore unreasonable to say that the owner remained silent only because he intended to sue later. With the coal, by contrast, the damage is immediate, definite, and measurable at the time it occurs, so later suit is entirely plausible. Nor should one ask that even if silence proves consent to placement of a gutter or projection, siman 363 rules that one who says, "Live in my courtyard," can still demand the ordinary rental value. Likewise, in siman 142, seif 2, if Reuven built his wall into Shimon's boundary and Shimon assisted while knowing the facts, the encroachment becomes established. Why is this less than one who explicitly says, "Build your house on my land," who should seemingly be entitled to payment, no worse than "Live in my courtyard" or "Eat with me," where payment is due even though the owner actively assists? The distinction is that when the owner himself initiates and says, "Eat" or "Live with me," we presume that his intent was for ordinary payment. But when the other party initiates the taking or use in circumstances showing that he intends it gratuitously, and the owner knowingly remains silent, the owner's consent follows the same gratuitous understanding. The consent is therefore to free use. With consumption of produce in the owner's presence, however, we do not say that silence waives payment, because produce is ordinarily sold. We therefore assume that the possessor too intended ordinary payment, for we do not presume people to be robbers, and the owner's silence is likewise understood as consent on that paid basis. In siman 142, by contrast, the one causing the encroachment did not even know that he had entered the neighbor's land, and the property was not ordinarily offered for such use. His intent was clearly not to pay, so the neighbor's silence is understood on the same gratuitous basis.
This is a strong fish that does not pay for the rest of the powerful honeyforth, and eat the fruits of the groceries, even though they did not use it at all, and did not care for any stronger and less powerful than a cultivator.
And if he did not protest. See Shach, seif katan 2, where he writes that the language of the Rosh also supports this and not Bach's interpretation. His intent is to answer the difficulty of Bach and Darkei Moshe in the Rosh. The Rosh writes that with a projection of one handbreadth, the courtyard owner can protest against the roof owner and the roof owner can protest against the courtyard owner. This seems inconsistent with the end of the Rosh's discussion. Bach and Darkei Moshe answer that the beginning speaks after three years. Shach instead explains that when the Rosh says the roof owner can object to the courtyard owner, he means that he can remove the projection, whereas with a projection smaller than a handbreadth the owner of the projection cannot object to removal. There is also a scribal error at the end of Shach and it should read that the words do not fit the first interpretation of Tosafot. Even so, Shach's words require study. The Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber necessarily disagree with Tosafot's first explanation. According to that explanation, the wall owner may protest against the courtyard owner even within three years, and even in a courtyard that already has doors and windows one can protest against a projection, because of concern that it may fall on his head. This also resolves Maharsha's question on Tosafot. Maharsha asks why Tosafot, who invoke the requirement of a parapet because of privacy, did not ask that question on the Mishnah itself. According to what I have explained, the Mishnah can concern a place where the parties waived privacy damage between them. In such a courtyard a four cubit parapet is unnecessary, yet one can still protest against a full handbreadth projection because of the danger of falling that Tosafot mention. Tosafot therefore direct their privacy question specifically against the dispute of Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah concerning a projection smaller than a handbreadth, where the danger of falling does not apply and the Gemara itself says their dispute concerns privacy damage. Thus Tosafot can properly ask that where there is privacy damage a parapet should be required. In any event, Tosafot's first explanation clearly holds that a projection of one handbreadth can be objected to even in a courtyard with existing doors and windows because of the danger of falling. Tur and Mechaber explicitly rule in seif 8 that in such a case one cannot protest. They therefore plainly reject Tosafot's first explanation. Shach's statement that only Bach's interpretation fails to fit that first explanation is therefore difficult.
