Sema explains the phrase "and the owner of the courtyard did not protest immediately" to mean not literally at the first instant, but immediately once the damage became apparent to him and he remained silent. Sema distinguishes the case of blocking a window, where the presumption arises literally at once because the harm is immediately obvious, from other nuisances, where some time may be needed before the harm becomes clear. The basis for this distinction is not entirely clear. Rambam, at the end of the laws of neighbors, groups together opening a window, opening a water channel, and failing to keep the required distance. If the possessor says, "You told me to do this," or, "You sold or waived the right after seeing it and remaining silent," while the injured party says, "I only now discovered it," or, "I protested when I saw it and you promised to remove it," Rambam places the burden of proof on the injured party. Maggid Mishneh explains that because Rambam holds that a nuisance presumption arises immediately, it is treated like a three year land presumption in placing the burden of proof on the challenger. Rambam's language therefore suggests that the same basic rule applies to windows and other nuisances. The Tur likewise writes in siman 154 that presumptions concerning windows, projections, and similar matters do not require three years but arise once the damage has become apparent and the injured party remains silent. "Once the damage has become apparent" does not mean that witnesses must prove actual subjective knowledge, for the injured party is not believed simply to say, "I did not know." Rather, it means after enough time has passed that the damage reasonably could have become apparent. If the interval was so short that discovery was impossible, there is no presumption. Rambam says similarly in his commentary to the Mishnah: nuisance presumptions do not require three years, but neither do they arise at the first instant. The nuisance must remain long enough that one can fairly say to the injured party, "You must already have accepted it, since you did not protest." Accordingly, there is no firm basis to distinguish between different nuisances. In each case the required time is the amount needed for the particular harm reasonably to become apparent. Rivash, however, does support Sema. He writes that the Geonim agreed that windows, projections, and other nonpossessory nuisances acquire a presumption once the harm becomes apparent and the injured party remains silent. The Gemara's phrase that blocking a window creates a presumption "immediately" does not mean that other nuisances require three years. Rather, other nuisances require enough time for the harm to become known, while when one's light is blocked the harm is necessarily apparent at once because no one ordinarily remains silent while his light is shut off. Thus Rivash understands "immediately" as the time needed for clear awareness, which is instantaneous in the window case and longer elsewhere. The matter still requires study. The Tur implies that a projection can acquire a presumption even where its use creates some invasion of privacy into the courtyard. In siman 154, by contrast, he cites Ramban that the Mishnah's law of a window with a presumption does not apply where the window creates a serious invasion of privacy, because such harm is comparable to smoke and a privy and is not waived by ordinary presumption. The Tur does not make the same qualification here regarding a projection. Ramban's own novellae, however, state that the rule of a projection having a presumption likewise applies only where it does not create an invasion of privacy into the courtyard. Nimukei Yosef draws a distinction between projections and windows. He explains that the Mishnah can discuss projections even in a courtyard where there is some visual intrusion, because the intrusion is not constant. It arises mainly when the person actually uses the projection. The Gemara's discussion of whether the parties dispute whether visual intrusion counts as damage implies that the harm here is less severe than ordinary privacy damage. Such a minor intrusion can be acquired by presumption. A fixed window is different. It creates constant visual intrusion and is therefore comparable to smoke or a privy, where ordinary presumption does not help. Nimukei Yosef therefore limits the Mishnah's window presumption to a case where there is no serious privacy damage. This appears to be the Tur's position as well.
