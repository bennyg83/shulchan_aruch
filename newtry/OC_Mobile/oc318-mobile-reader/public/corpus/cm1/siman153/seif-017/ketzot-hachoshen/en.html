I later found this difficulty addressed by Knesset HaGedolah in siman 155. He explains that Rambam's rule placing the burden of proof on the injured party applies specifically to matters in which the act itself ordinarily creates an immediate presumption, such as opening the relevant opening or fixing a beam. By contrast, in the first chapter of the laws of neighbors, attaching a temporary sukkah beam to another's wall does not create a presumption merely through the initial placement. The user can say, "I did not waive anything; I allowed you because the structure was temporary." The presumption arises only from the beam being fixed permanently with plaster. Because the owner may not have noticed that specific permanent attachment, the possessor must prove the waiver. Alternatively, Rambam's rule applies only where it is highly likely that the injured party immediately saw and understood the nuisance. Where a beam is plastered into a wall, the owner may have seen the beam but not realized it had been permanently fixed. His failure to protest the mere resting of the beam is not significant because he could assume the user would remove it within thirty days. According to this, however, if the beam remained more than thirty days, or a sukkah beam remained beyond the seven days of the festival, the user should no longer need to prove payment or consent. Tosafot in the first chapter of Bava Batra, citing Rabbeinu Tam, relates the thirty day rule to the Mishnah's presumption of nonpayment. For the first thirty days the person is presumed not to have paid because he can think, "Who says the Sages will obligate me?" But once he leaves the beam there for the full thirty days, or fixes it permanently with plaster, his legal obligation becomes clear and he is then presumed to have paid. Nimukei Yosef similarly says that where one erects a permanent structure above four amot, he is presumed to have paid, and Rama rules so in siman 157. Accordingly, even if the other owner did not actually know that the beam was plastered in, once the user himself knows that his legal obligation has become clear, he is like any other person who knows that he owes money, such as a thief, robber, or tortfeasor. Even if the creditor does not yet know the debt exists, one who knows himself to be liable must pay. The same applies here, and the user need not prove the other's awareness.
