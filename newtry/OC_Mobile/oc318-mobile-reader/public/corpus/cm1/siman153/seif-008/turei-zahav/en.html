This is Rabbeinu Yonah's view in Derishah, that as long as he has not placed a ceiling upon the beams, he acquires no right in the substance of the wall, but only in its stones. Rabbeinu Yonah also discusses one who advances a claim of ownership, as Rivash states explicitly in responsum 211, cited nearby by Beit Yosef. It follows that there are four opinions where the claimant advances a claim. Rivash holds that insertion of beams establishes no right beyond those particular beams, even if a ceiling was placed upon them. Rabbeinu Yonah agrees regarding insertion of beams alone, but holds that placing the ceiling proves that the claimant acquired half the stones of the wall. Rambam holds that even insertion alone establishes a prescriptive right to the place occupied by the wall when accompanied by a claim, because this is comparable to consuming the produce of a field. The Rosh holds that even without a ceiling and plasterwork, he acquires what Rabbeinu Yonah grants when a ceiling is placed, namely half the stones but not half the ground beneath the wall. Rama's words are difficult. He writes, “Some say specifically,” which is Rabbeinu Yonah's view concerning one who advances a claim. How can he then speak of one who admits that the wall originally belonged to his fellow and claims that he paid him half? This is the same as the first clause, which speaks of one who says that the wall was originally jointly owned. What difference is there between claiming that both built it originally and claiming that he later purchased half? Moreover, even if there were a distinction, he should be believed by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed original joint ownership. The Sema's explanation of Rama here is astonishing. I cannot reconcile Rama's words unless he interpreted Rabbeinu Yonah as also discussing a case without a contested ownership claim. The claimant says that when he purchased the right to use the wall, he acquired half of the wall as part of the transaction. In that case we say that his use is not proof unless he placed a ceiling upon the beams. The subsequent “some say that there is no distinction,” representing the Rosh, would likewise concern a case without contradictory claims. Yet one who examines the Rosh will see explicitly that he discusses a prescriptive right accompanied by a claim and bases the ruling on credibility, as explained nearby. As a matter of law, it appears proper to rule like Rivash, who follows Rabbeinu Yonah, that where one advances a claim, a ceiling and plasterwork are specifically required, and only then does he acquire half the stones of the wall. Beit Yosef writes in the name of the Rosh that in this matter everything follows local custom.
