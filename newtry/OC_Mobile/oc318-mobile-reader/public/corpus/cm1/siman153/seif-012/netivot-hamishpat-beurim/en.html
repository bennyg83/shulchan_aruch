He pours wastewater in his own property through a drain. See Sema, seif katan 28. His apparent reasoning is that when a person physically enters another's property and pours there, the owner sees what he is pouring and may say, "I remained silent only because the amount was small, but I never agreed to a larger quantity." By contrast, if one made a drain from his own property into the other's property, once he established the drain itself the owner can no longer remove it from his property, just as with a water spout. Concerning the act of pouring, the owner cannot prevent him because he pours while standing in his own property. This does not contradict Nimukei Yosef, who writes regarding a water spout that one may not increase the damage, and similarly concerning one who acquired a right to pour wastewater. There the person increases the damage within the other person's courtyard beyond what was established, such as by raising the outlet there. Here, however, the pouring begins in his own property. There remains a difficulty. The first clause appears to deal with one who had no drain of his own, but merely became accustomed to pouring wastewater by hand into a drain located in his neighbor's courtyard, at least for the amount of one laundry. Why should this differ from a right of passage, which all the poskim cited above in siman 140, seif 19, hold does not create chazakah because no permanent structure was made, unlike a fixed water spout? We do not ordinarily find a chazakah merely from repeated acts of use. Moreover, according to the opinion that these chazakot do not require three years, a single act of pouring would then create a perpetual right to pour, which is unreasonable. It is also almost impossible to prove by witnesses a three year chazakah of this type. They would need to know that during all three years every quantity of wastewater was poured into this drain and nowhere else. Even neighbors could not know this with certainty. Merely seeing repeated pouring during the three years should not create chazakah. It therefore appears that the first clause too concerns one who established a permanent drain within his neighbor's courtyard for his wastewater, or placed a rainwater pipe from his property onto the neighbor's drain. Even then, if the original established use was for one laundry, he may not pour the amount generated by several laundries. Similarly, if he was a small scale launderer when the right was established and later became a large scale launderer with five washings every week, the neighbor can object. Without some permanent installation, I do not find it reasonable that there should be chazakah at all. The language of Nimukei Yosef and Rashba supports this. They explain the Tosefta, which says that a drain made for laundry water may also be used for rainwater, by saying that it concerns rows of upper stories whose waters flow into a common drain. The Tosefta in Bava Metzia, chapter HaBayit VeHaAliyah, discusses five courtyards sharing one drain, with all of them contributing to its repair. In that context the rule is not based on chazakah at all. The drain is jointly owned. Even if at the outset they said that they were constructing it for rainwater, there is an obvious assumption that they did not mean to exclude ordinary household wastewater, because everyone knows that each household normally pours all its wastewater into the common drain. Since each person is pouring into property in which he has a share, he may use it for all ordinary wastewater. This is the meaning of Rama's language as well. If one acquired a right in another's courtyard for the amount of one laundry, he may not increase beyond that. But one who pours wastewater in his own property through a drain that passes through the neighbor's courtyard means that the drain begins in his property and was originally constructed for both courtyards. Because he is obligated to participate in maintaining the drain, he has a share in it. He is therefore pouring into his own jointly owned drain, and his right to pour all ordinary wastewater does not arise from chazakah at all. This reading also best fits the language of Nimukei Yosef and Rashba, who speak of rows of courtyards or channels flowing into a drain designated for that purpose.
