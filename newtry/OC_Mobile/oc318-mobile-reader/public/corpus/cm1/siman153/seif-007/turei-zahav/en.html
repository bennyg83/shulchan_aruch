(Seif 18) Unless he claims, and so forth. Rivash, cited by Beit Yosef, was troubled by what proof the insertion of beams provides that half the wall belongs to him. He explains that Rambam discusses a wall separating two neighbors. Even if one party has several ceilings supported by the wall and the other only one, the latter is believed when he claims that he was a partner in the wall from the time it was built. It is difficult, however, why Rambam first writes, “When does this apply? When he admits,” and so forth. He should be believed by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed, “I am a partner in the entire wall.” The Sema, in seif katan 23, therefore explains that the case is one in which he is compelled to make the admission, but this is strained. There is no difficulty. Rivash understands all of Rambam's words as referring to a wall between neighbors. The principal basis of the prescriptive right is that the owner remained silent and thereby waived his objection. A migo is therefore irrelevant. We certainly believe the claimant's statement that the owner waived his rights through silence, but it is self-evident that he did not waive the entire wall merely because of this use. Rambam therefore says, “When does this apply? When he admits,” and so forth. If, however, he expressly claims, “I am a partner in this wall, whether through purchase or gift,” he is believed. All this follows Rivash's interpretation in Beit Yosef, but Rivash himself did not accept it, for he writes that it remains difficult why the beam is needed at all if the wall separates them, as Beit Yosef records. It therefore appears to me that Rambam discusses a wall known through witnesses to belong exclusively to one party, as implied by the arrangement of Rambam's rulings and by the expression, “One who established use in this wall,” which refers back to the beginning of seif 7, where the wall belonged to Reuven alone. Rambam follows Ramban, cited by the Tur here, that inserting a beam is comparable to consuming the produce of a field. Rambam nevertheless holds that it establishes a right only regarding use of the wall, as implied by his words, “He is believed and may use all of it,” meaning that it is effective only for use. Thus, in the first clause there is no difference between their claims. He seeks, by virtue of the owner's waiver, to place one additional beam, and the owner of the wall prevails. In the latter clause, however, he claims, “I acquired a share in this wall from you, and my use of it with beams is proof.” He is believed and may use it. I later found that Kesef Mishneh cites another responsum of Rivash explaining that the wall is known to belong to one party. The first clause concerns a claim based on waiver, while the latter concerns a claim that he is a partner, meaning that he paid for half of it and does not rely upon waiver. This expressly accords with what I wrote, and Rivash himself retracted his earlier interpretation.
