And by kinyan sudar. See Baer Heitev regarding Mahari ben Lev's doubt whether kinyan sudar is effective in a place where deeds are customarily written. In Mahari ben Lev itself it is clear that where there are both kinyan sudar and payment of money, the acquisition certainly takes effect. Regarding the basic rule, Responsa Chut HaMeshulash, part 4 of Tashbetz, third column, end of siman 4, writes that Ramban and Rashba state in their novellae that the force of kinyan sudar is strong enough that even where the custom is to write a deed, if the parties made a kinyan sudar the sale is valid although no deed was written. The view of the author's grandfather, Rashbetz, appears to be that in a place where deeds are written, kinyan sudar alone does not acquire until the deed is written. That seems surprising. Chut HaMeshulash therefore suggests that Rashbetz spoke only where the delay in writing and signing the deed resulted from the seller, who created excuses and obstacles. In such a case we infer that from the outset he did not rely on kinyan sudar alone to effect the transfer. At first glance, Tur and Rama at the end of siman 192 also appear to hold that kinyan sudar works even in a place where deeds are normally written, because they rule there that chazakah works even in such a place. If so, kinyan sudar should certainly work, since Ramban, who holds that chazakah does not work in a place where deeds are written, nevertheless holds that kinyan sudar does. This would show that kinyan sudar is stronger. Responsa Radbaz I:323 discusses one who sold the possessory right to his shop before witnesses and with a kinyan, but did not write the evidentiary deed. He rules that if the local custom is that a sale does not become effective except through a deed, then the buyer does not acquire until the deed is written. But where the local custom is to validate transactions through kinyan sudar, Shimon acquires the right to the shop even without the deed. This also indicates that kinyan sudar alone works even in a place where deeds are generally written, unless there is a definite custom that the transaction is ineffective without the deed. If no such clear custom exists, the Torah law should govern, and under the authorities cited above kinyan sudar alone is sufficient. On further reflection, however, the inference from Tur and Rama may be rejected, and in fact their view may be the opposite. They expressly write the special rule only regarding chazakah in siman 192, based on the Rosh, who explains that chazakah is powerful because the buyer performs an act on the land itself, analogous to meshichah. Kinyan sudar involves no act upon the land, and therefore it may indeed fail without a deed where deeds are customary. Sema in siman 195, seif katan 17, supports this distinction. Responsa Rishmei She'elah 52 likewise states simply that in the Rama's view kinyan sudar alone does not acquire in a place where a deed is written after payment. Since the Rama expressly added in siman 192 that chazakah works even in a place where a deed is customarily written, but did not make a parallel statement in siman 195 concerning kinyan sudar, this implies that kinyan sudar does not suffice there. It further writes that according to our custom, where a deed is regularly written after every kinyan sudar in land sales, both parties may retract from the underlying sale until the deed is written.
