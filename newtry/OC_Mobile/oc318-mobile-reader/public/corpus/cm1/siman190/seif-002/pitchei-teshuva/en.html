With money, how? See Baer Heitev, which states that acquiring through a debt is not an acquisition for this reason. This is not unanimous. Rambam, Hilchot Mechirah 7:4, treats acquisition through a debt like acquisition through money; Raavad disagrees. See Shulchan Aruch below, siman 204:10, and the Sema there. Most authorities follow Raavad, which is the dissenting view cited there. Baer Heitev also cites the Shach's case in which Reuven gave Shimon a promissory note payable by Levi as the price of land, and the Shach ruled that the sale was acquired by delivery of the note and neither party could retract. The Shach adds, however, that if the note on Levi were of a type that Reuven could later forgive, the land would not be acquired. Although in kiddushin such a case creates doubtful kiddushin, in a sale the Shach rules that no acquisition occurs at all. Ketzot HaChoshen cites Tosafot in Bava Batra 157 and Bava Metzia 16 that in a sale the acquisition does take effect. The special concern in kiddushin is that a woman does not confidently rely on the note because if it is forgiven she is embarrassed to sue her husband; that concern does not apply to a seller. Netivot HaMishpat cites this as well. It would appear that the Shach's ruling that delivery of a promissory note acquires the land applies specifically where, as in his case, Reuven gave Shimon a note that Reuven held against Levi. If the purchaser gives the seller his own promissory note, even though there is no concern that he might forgive a debt owed by himself, the delivery may not operate as payment. The Shach derives his rule from kiddushin, and in kiddushin a person's own note does not create kiddushin, as ruled in Even HaEzer 29:6. Ketzot HaChoshen below, seif 9, discusses this at length. Machaneh Efrayim, Hilchot Kinyan Maot 5, also analyzes this question and cites Sefer HaMekach VeHaMemkar of Rav Hai Gaon, gate 13, indicating that a person's own promissory note can function like money and is even stronger than money for movable property, because the concern that "your wheat may burn in the seller's attic" does not apply when the seller has not yet received actual payment. He concludes that Rashi, Ritva, and Ran treat a note as equivalent to money for a sale, while Rashba remains uncertain. The Shach at the end of siman 39, seif katan 49, cites Mabit concerning one who sold wool for a fixed price and received the purchaser's note for that amount, after which the purchaser died. That source indicates that when a buyer gives his own note in buying an object, the seller may be obligated to deliver the object, not because the note itself effects a monetary acquisition but because the seller became obligated to deliver through the purchaser's acquisition of the note. According to the Shach, the seller is at least obligated to provide some wool of the agreed type. Ketzot and Netivot discuss this there. Responsa Beit Efrayim, Choshen Mishpat 20, disputes Machaneh Efrayim's interpretation of Rav Hai Gaon and also disagrees with the Shach's ruling in siman 39 that the seller must at least provide wool. Ramban's principle that one who accepts a kinyan and encumbers his assets in exchange for a loan thereby becomes obligated applies where the other party undertook to lend him money. It does not follow that in a sale of wool, where there was no kinyan in the wool itself and the purchaser merely issued a promissory note for the price, the seller acquires an obligation to deliver wool. The note mentions only the monetary debt and creates no acquisition in the wool. Beit Efrayim concludes that where one purchases land and gives a note for the price, according to Ramban this is not equivalent to payment because the money has not left the buyer's possession. Nor can the buyer acquire the land by exchanging the seller's resulting claim for the land. Even if the seller now has a loan claim against the buyer, a loan ordinarily does not acquire property, as stated in siman 199. Some authorities recognize a loan arising from a prior sale as usable consideration because it is treated as though the original merchandise still existed. That rationale does not apply where there was never actual money or merchandise in the seller's hands, only a newly created land lien. Such a lien does not itself operate as exchange property. He therefore rules that if the promissory note merely states that the buyer's lien is for money owed as the price, and the buyer seeks to acquire the object through chalipin, it certainly does not work, because the note speaks only of money and contains no reference to the object. Even if it expressly states that his lands are encumbered in exchange for a particular object, it may still be ineffective, because a land lien is not itself acquired through chalipin and cannot serve as the chalipin object. If the buyer seeks to acquire the property as payment, the case returns to the dispute concerning "there is no maneh here," and according to Ritva there is room to say the purchase takes effect. Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 18, writes similarly.
