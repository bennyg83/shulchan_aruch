With money, how? The Shach rules that if land is purchased by transferring to the seller a debt instrument owed to the buyer by a third party, the land is not acquired because the seller does not rely fully on that instrument. He might fear that the original creditor will waive the debt. The Shach reads Rashi as implying that although a woman may sometimes accept such a document for kiddushin because she assumes the creditor will not harm himself by waiving, an ordinary land seller does not acquire through it. Tosafot appears to disagree. In Bava Batra they considered proving that one who waives a sold debt pays only the amount received for the sale of the debt. They note that if he had to pay the full face value after waiver, a woman receiving such a debt instrument for kiddushin would certainly rely on it. Tosafot rejects the proof because a woman may be embarrassed to sue her husband if he later waives. That reason is absent in an ordinary land sale, where the seller is not embarrassed to sue the purchaser. This implies that land can be acquired by such a debt instrument. Tosafot in Bava Metzia likewise says that the rule that a loan does not buy refers to a debt owed by the seller himself, while a debt owed to the buyer by a third party can function differently. Their language suggests such a debt can serve as purchase consideration in an ordinary sale. The Shach cites Ran as saying that kiddushin with another person's debt instrument fails because the woman fears waiver, "and in a sale he does not acquire." But the punctuation is uncertain, and the phrase may still be referring to the kiddushin case. Thus there is substantial support in Tosafot that a third party debt instrument can acquire land, at least where the seller can rely on reimbursement if the debt is later waived.
