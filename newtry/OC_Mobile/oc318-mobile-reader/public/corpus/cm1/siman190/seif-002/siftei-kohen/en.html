How is acquisition effected by money, etc. The Gemara in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh, Kiddushin 46a, explains that just as one who betroths a woman with a loan does not effect kiddushin because loaned money is given to be spent, so too one who purchases with a loan does not acquire for the same reason. This is also written by Baal HaIttur, entry agav, folio 7d, by the Ritva in Kiddushin, who cites the Jerusalem Talmud in the name of Rabbi Yochanan, and by the Rosh in the first chapter of Kiddushin. See also Tosafot, Bava Metzia 16a, s.v. hai shtara, and the glosses of Maharsha there. A case came before me in which Reuven purchased land from Shimon but did not take possession of it; instead, he gave Shimon, as payment, a bearer note that he held against Levi. Some wished to say that because documents are not intrinsically monetary property and are excluded from the laws of overreaching, custodians, and similar matters, a sale cannot be acquired through them. I ruled that the sale was acquired by delivery of this bearer note and that neither party may retract. The proof is the Gemara at the end of Kiddushin 47, concerning betrothal with another person's debt document. Rabbi Meir says she is betrothed and the Sages say she is not. The Gemara explains that they dispute Shmuel's rule that one who sells a debt document may later forgive the debt, or, according to another explanation, all accept Shmuel and dispute whether the woman relies upon the document, saying that he will not abandon her interest and forgive the debt to another. It follows that even a woman may be betrothed by such a note, and their disagreement concerns only whether she relies upon it because the creditor might forgive the debt. Although we rule that betrothal with another person's debt document is doubtful kiddushin, where that concern does not apply the kiddushin is certain. A bearer note written from the outset in favor of whoever produces it cannot be forgiven, as explained above in siman 66, seif 1; consequently, it certainly effects kiddushin, and all the more so it effects a sale. The Gemara in the first chapter of Kiddushin establishes that whenever an instrument cannot acquire in a sale, it cannot effect kiddushin either. Although debt documents are excluded by Scripture from oaths, overreaching, and other laws because they are not intrinsically monetary property, that rationale does not govern kiddushin or sale. Those depend upon the benefit received and the parties' resulting resolve to obligate themselves. If, however, he gave the seller an ordinary debt document against Levi as payment for the land, in a form that the transferor could later forgive, the land would not be acquired at all. Although such a case creates doubtful kiddushin, in a sale there is no acquisition and no doubt. This is implied by the Gemara's wording, 'Here they dispute regarding a woman,' and Rashi explains explicitly that if land were transferred for this debt document, the creditor certainly would not acquire the land because the landowner does not rely upon it; Rabbi Meir's view regarding a woman is different because she assumes that, as his wife, he will not abandon her interest and forgive the debt to another. One cannot answer that this applies only according to those who do not impose liability under the law of causative damages, whereas according to our law the seller relies upon the document because if the creditor forgives it he must pay. The Gemara is speaking according to Rabbi Meir, who himself imposes liability for causative damages. Moreover, the Ran writes that even when a debt document is transferred by writing and delivery, the woman is not certainly betrothed because the debt may be forgiven, and in a sale there is no acquisition. The doubt regarding kiddushin exists because perhaps the law follows one of the Gemara's other explanations, under which all accept Shmuel but a woman nevertheless relies upon the document. Regarding any other person, however, all agree that he does not rely upon it, as the Gemara and Rashi imply, because a woman is exceptional. All this concerns land. With movable property, this rule of reliance does not apply, but the debt document is no better than money itself, and movable property is not acquired by payment but by meshichah. Rashi therefore spoke specifically of land. All this is clear.
