At the current market rate. See Sema, seif katan 11, who concludes that according to Rosh and Tur, where the seller repeatedly demands payment, the entire sale is canceled, except that the party who retracts has the lower hand. The buyer can insist on money, so the seller must sell whatever he has to provide cash; or the buyer can demand land, in which case the seller must give superior land at its current value even if he has cash. This is difficult. Rosh at the beginning of HaOmanin explains that with a donkey the sale is canceled entirely because the donkey cannot be divided and the seller does not wish to remain a partner, but with divisible property the sale is canceled only against the amount still unpaid. According to Sema's reading, however, Rosh also holds that with land the entire sale is canceled, merely subject to the rule that the retracting party has the lower hand. If so, Rosh's question from the donkey case would have been only why the rule of the retracting party having the lower hand does not apply there, and his answer about divisibility would not address that problem. Tur's wording also indicates a substantive distinction between the unpaid portion and the portion already paid. Rosh's responsum cited at the end of siman 226 likewise implies that the part corresponding to the money already paid remains a valid sale, not merely a debt collection at current value. I therefore understand Rosh as follows. Regarding the unpaid balance, the seller can cancel that portion of the sale as a matter of law. If the seller is willing to leave intact the portion corresponding to the money already paid and the buyer wishes to retract from the entire purchase, the buyer has the status of the party retracting and has the lower hand, because cancellation of the unpaid portion arose through the buyer's fault in delaying payment. Likewise, if the seller retracts from the entire purchase, even after the buyer has already retracted from the unpaid portion, the seller is the retracting party with respect to the portion paid and therefore has the lower hand. This precisely fits the wording of Rosh and Tur: the sale is canceled against the amount still owed, and from that point onward either can retract, but the retracting party has the lower hand. It also resolves Rosh's question from the donkey. With divisible land, the sale remains effective for the amount already paid, so if the buyer retracts from that existing portion he is a retracting party. With an indivisible donkey, cancellation of the unpaid portion necessarily cancels the whole sale immediately. The case is therefore like ona'ah exceeding one sixth, where the sale is void rather than a case of one side retracting, and the rule of the lower hand does not apply. Rosh's responsum is likewise clear. Initially he considered a disputed half-field comparable to a seller repeatedly demanding payment, because the purchase might remain effective to the extent of the payment already made. He then distinguishes that there the partial cancellation was caused by the buyer's failure to pay, and therefore the seller's willingness to keep the paid portion forces the buyer who wishes to abandon it to bear the law of the retracting party. In the case of a title challenge, by contrast, the defect arises from the seller's side and the buyer is not at fault; once part of the purchase fails, the whole sale may be void like a sale with excessive ona'ah, without treating the buyer as the retracting party. Sema's statement that Rambam agrees that the entire sale is canceled is also very strained. Taz, seifim 13 and 17, is primary: according to Rambam the sale unquestionably remains valid for the amount already paid. As to Sema's question on Rama, that regarding the buyer's retraction Rama says the seller gives inferior land at the current rate, while regarding the seller's retraction he says at the agreed rate, the question is indeed strong. The language itself suggests a transposition. Where the seller gives inferior land from that very field, he necessarily gives it as fulfillment of the purchase, for if it were debt collection he could satisfy from other inferior land. Mechaber himself says, "or he acquires from the land against his money." Therefore the proper measure there is the agreed rate, as Ran expressly writes. Conversely, in the clause where the seller retracts and the buyer takes from the superior portion, the wording "takes" indicates collection rather than acquisition. Rama's addition should therefore read that he gives it at the current rate, meaning as debt collection. If the buyer instead compels performance of the sale at the agreed rate, the seller need give only inferior land, as Ran says. In any event, the principal halachah follows Ran: at the agreed rate the seller need give only inferior land, even when the seller is the one retracting; superior land is required only when payment is made as debt collection at the current rate. Rosh and Tur, as interpreted by Sema, are exceedingly difficult, for there is no basis to force the seller to provide land specifically where he has cash. Even a damager cannot be compelled to pay land when he has money, since the Torah says money or superior property.
If the seller retracted. The buyer's ability to compel the seller to preserve the part of the sale corresponding to the money already paid applies specifically where the buyer says that he is still prepared to pay the balance and the seller wishes to cancel because he repeatedly demanded payment and was put off. But if the buyer admits that he still has no money and wants the seller to wait until he later obtains it, the seller can cancel the sale entirely, because he sold only on the understanding that the price would be paid. Shitah Mekubetzet at the beginning of HaOmanin states this explicitly in the name of Rif's responsum: if neither wishes to retract, the seller received part payment and presses for the balance, while the buyer says, "Wait until I obtain the rest," the purchase is not completed for the buyer, neither wholly nor partially. It also appears that in such a case the seller need not have demanded the money repeatedly on the very day. Once he demands payment and the buyer says he has none, the seller may cancel. This is evident in Even HaEzer siman 120, where if the price of the get was not paid the get is regarded as stolen in the recipient's hand. We do not treat the unpaid price as a mere debt while leaving the transaction intact; rather, failure to pay allows cancellation of the transaction. Machaneh Ephraim writes similarly.
