The purchaser did not acquire the whole, etc., until "at the price they fixed." See Darkei Moshe, where Rama likewise writes that when the purchaser retracts, the seller gives him land according to the current price, while when the seller retracts, he gives the purchaser land according to the originally fixed price. Rama based this upon the Ran's explanation in Kiddushin. This is astonishing to me. I will therefore set out the baraita in Bava Metzia 77b and the interpretations of the Rambam, Rosh, Tur, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef. The baraita states: Whoever retracts has the lower hand. If one sold a field for one thousand zuz and the buyer paid two hundred, then if the seller retracts, the purchaser has the upper hand. He may say, "Give me my money," or, "Give me land corresponding to my money." From where does he collect it? From the best land. If the purchaser retracts, the seller has the upper hand. He may say, "Here is your money," or, "Here is land corresponding to your money." From where does he collect it? From the worst land. The Gemara gives two explanations of "best land." One means the best portion of that field, imposing a penalty upon the retracting party. The other means the best of all his properties, because one who buys a large field may have sold movable property cheaply to raise the money, so the seller who causes him loss is treated like a damager, and damages are assessed from superior property. The Rambam and Mechaber, who write "from the best part of it," follow the first interpretation. The Rosh and Tur, who write "from the best of his properties," follow the second. Regarding the substantive law, I demonstrated in the Perishah from the Rosh's rulings and responsa and from the Tur that when the seller repeatedly presses for the unpaid balance, both parties may retract from the entire sale. Since the sale is void with respect to the unpaid portion, because the seller can say, "I sold only on the assumption that you would pay the entire price at the agreed time," it is void also with respect to the portion already paid. He can say, "I needed the full price." And once the seller can retract from the whole, the purchaser likewise may retract from the whole, because there is no reason to maintain half a sale. The baraita's phrase "when the seller retracts" therefore means that he retracts even with respect to the portion already paid, and the same for the purchaser. Whoever retracts has the lower hand. If the seller retracts from the entire sale, the purchaser may force him to return the initial money. If the seller lacks cash, he must make every effort to sell property and obtain cash. Alternatively, the purchaser may say, "I do not want repayment in cash. Give me land corresponding to my money." Although the seller may retract from the original low agreed price, he must at least give land at the current price. He must give from superior property at the current price because his retraction caused loss to the purchaser even with respect to the money already received. If the purchaser retracts from the portion already paid, he need not maintain the original sale at the high price or for whatever other reason led him to retract. Yet he has the lower hand. The seller may return his money, or may say, "I will not give cash. You must take land corresponding to the money you paid, at the current price," and may give inferior land, because by Torah law a creditor collects inferior land and only because of the concern of closing the door before borrowers did the Sages enact intermediate land. That concern does not apply here. Thus, according to the Rosh and Tur, when the seller presses for payment both parties may retract even from the portion already paid, but the one who retracts has the lower hand. In both cases, when land is given against the money, it is valued at the current price. The Ran, however, appears to understand the baraita differently. He writes that when the seller presses for the money, the purchaser does not acquire the entire field, but regarding the portion already paid the baraita says whoever retracts has the lower hand. If the seller retracts, the purchaser may say, "Give me my money," or, "Give me land corresponding to my money," and receives according to the price fixed for the sale, meaning according to the agreed value of the entire field, and receives the inferior portion as one who bought half a field. If he instead wants his money, he collects from the superior portion at the current value because he is collecting a debt. If the purchaser retracts, the seller may say, "Here is your money," or, "Here is land corresponding to your money," and he collects inferior land at the current price because this is debt collection. According to the Ran, therefore, the phrase "from where does he collect" does not refer to the immediately preceding option of taking land under the original sale, but to the option of recovering money. The Nimukei Yosef similarly says that the purchaser always acquires land corresponding to the money paid if he chooses, as half the field at the agreed price, while if he asks for his money he collects from superior property. Thus, the Ran and Nimukei Yosef understand that when the seller presses for payment, the buyer fails to acquire only the unpaid portion, while the paid portion remains binding unless the other party elects to retract in a manner that gives the opposing party the upper hand. This explains the Ran's phrase, "Nevertheless, whoever retracts has the lower hand." The practical and interpretive differences are therefore clear. The Rosh and Tur hold that both may retract from the whole sale and that land given in consequence of retraction is valued at the current price. The Ran and Nimukei Yosef hold that the original acquisition remains with respect to the amount paid, and if that part of the sale is retained, the land is valued at the original agreed price. Even so, it appears that the Ran and Nimukei Yosef would agree that if the party with the upper hand says, "I wish to maintain the original sale only to the extent of my money, but give me land according to my money," and the other has elected to retract, the land can be taken at the current price from the superior portion, while in the reverse case the seller may give inferior land at the current price. Their statement that "from where does he collect" refers to asking for the money does not mean literal cash only, because if the debtor has no cash, land is necessarily collected. Rather, "give me my money" means, "I no longer wish to maintain the original purchase even for the portion paid." In such a case he collects as a creditor. If he insists specifically on cash, the retracting party with the lower hand should have to sell property and obtain cash, and I believe the Ran and Nimukei Yosef agree. The Rambam at the beginning of Hilchot Mekhirah uses the language copied by the Mechaber here. Although he begins, "the purchaser did not acquire all of it," like the Ran, his conclusion that the party may acquire land corresponding to the money and that the seller gives inferior land appears to interpret the baraita's "from where does he collect" as referring directly to land received, like the Rosh. His Commentary to the Mishnah and the Kesef Mishneh likewise indicate this. Therefore it is possible that the Rambam follows the Rosh and Tur both in law and in interpretation. His phrase "did not acquire all of it" does not mean that the paid portion is absolutely beyond retraction. Rather, because he did not acquire the whole and a sale is not naturally divided in half, both can retract even from the portion already paid, though the retracting party has the lower hand. If so, Rama's distinction here, using the current price when the purchaser retracts and the fixed price when the seller retracts, fits no clear authority. The Rosh, Tur, Ramban, and Maggid Mishneh indicate the current price in both cases. The Ran's interpretation points to the original price in both cases when the original paid portion is maintained. It is strained to interpret the two clauses differently. Moreover, if in the latter case the purchaser takes at the fixed price because the original sale remains, why should he take superior land? According to the Ran and Nimukei Yosef, when the original purchase remains he takes the inferior portion like one who bought half a field. This requires study. The Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef also require careful examination on this matter. What appears correct to me I have written.
