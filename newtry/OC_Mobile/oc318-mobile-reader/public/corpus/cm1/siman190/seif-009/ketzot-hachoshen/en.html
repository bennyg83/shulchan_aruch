And he left a pledge. Kiddushin states, "There is no maneh here and there is no pledge here." Rosh explains that a person's property can serve as security only after the person's body has first become obligated. Property is the guarantor of the personal debt. If there is no debtor, there can be no guarantor. Under this approach, if the obligor gives his own valid debt instrument, that instrument can in principle function as money for kiddushin or land acquisition. Ramban and Rashba take a different approach. They say the pledge may indeed be validly encumbered, but it does not function as the money of kiddushin or purchase because the principal money has not left the giver's control. The essential monetary obligation remains with him, and the pledge is still tied to him. According to them, even his own debt instrument may fail to function as purchase money for the same reason. There is some evidence against treating one's own debt instrument as actual money from the law of redemption of a firstborn son. If a person writes the kohen a debt instrument for five sela, Ulla says that by Torah law the son is redeemed only once he actually pays. If the obligor's own note itself counted as money, redemption should take effect immediately. One can answer that redemption is different because Scripture excludes documents and land, and a debt instrument is not itself bodily money. This distinction does not necessarily apply to kiddushin or land acquisition. The matter therefore remains bound up with the different conceptual understandings of the phrase, "There is no maneh here and no pledge here."
A further proof appears in the opening of Kiddushin. The Gemara derives that a woman can be acquired by document and asks whether this can be learned a fortiori from money. It rejects the proof because money can redeem consecrated property and second tithe, whereas a document cannot. Rashi explains "document" as the obligor's own written promise to the Temple treasury for the redemption money. This strongly suggests that an obligor's own debt instrument can function as the monetary element of kiddushin or land acquisition, for otherwise the Gemara could have rejected the comparison much more directly by saying that such a document cannot acquire a woman or land at all. Rashi had to explain the document as the redeemer's own obligation because the discussion includes second tithe, which is movable produce and cannot be acquired through an ordinary land transfer document. Thus the relevant document must be a note promising the redemption money. Even so, the fact that the note cannot redeem consecrated property or second tithe may reflect the special biblical requirement of actual money in those laws, not a general inability of the note to function as consideration elsewhere. This supports the view that one's own valid debt obligation can sometimes serve as money for ordinary acquisition.
Rashba nevertheless explains the same sugya differently, treating the "document" as an acquisition document written by the Temple treasurer for the consecrated field. He also cites Ulla's law of firstborn redemption as proof that one's own debt instrument does not itself count as money until payment. His interpretation of second tithe remains difficult, because second tithe is movable produce and is not normally acquired through a land transfer document. This requires further study. Ran cites Ramban and Raavad in the dispute over a pledge. Ramban says that even if the woman is told to acquire the pledge for the security of a maneh, she is not betrothed because her primary right remains a monetary claim against the giver. Raavad says that if the pledge itself is validly transferred to her for the amount of the maneh, kiddushin can take effect. The true difference is narrower than later authorities sometimes suggest. Raavad too agrees that a pledge cannot become encumbered unless an actual personal obligation exists first. If the man merely says, "I will give you two hundred and here is a pledge," without creating a present debt, the pledge is ineffective. But if he says, "Acquire in the body of this pledge a value of one maneh," the woman obtains an actual proprietary share in the object. She can later be redeemed from that share with money only because of the condition. Once she owns part of the object itself, there is actual property in her hand and kiddushin can take effect. This reading is explicit in Ritva, who presents it as Raavad's position. It also means that Ramban and Rashba should agree where she acquires an actual share of the physical pledge rather than merely a lien. The later statement that Ramban rejects even an acquisition in the body of the pledge is therefore not persuasive. Bach also attempted to distinguish kiddushin from ordinary sale and to say that Ramban and Rashba reject a pledge only in kiddushin because the woman's reliance is weaker. Their own language shows otherwise. They regard the problem as one of what counts as purchase money while the principal obligation remains tied to the giver, and the same principle applies to sale. If an actual physical share of the pledge is transferred, however, the problem disappears.
