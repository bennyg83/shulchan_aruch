And my field shall be sold to him, etc. This is derived by combining the law of a guarantor and the acquisition of a slave. Just as a guarantor becomes obligated although he received nothing from the person to whom he became liable, so Reuven can become obligated to give his field to Levi even though Levi gave him nothing. If you object that the cases differ, because there the lender suffered a monetary loss in reliance on the guarantor and it is therefore proper for the guarantor to become obligated, whereas here Levi suffered no loss, so why should Reuven become obligated to him, the acquisition of a slave proves otherwise, for the slave suffers no monetary loss and nevertheless acquires. If you object that there the master who transfers ownership receives something, the law of a guarantor answers that objection. We therefore return to the combined derivation from both cases.
