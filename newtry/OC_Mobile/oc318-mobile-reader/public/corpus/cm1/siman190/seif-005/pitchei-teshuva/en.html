He does not acquire. Rashi, Kiddushin 26a, explains that because people are accustomed to writing a deed, the purchaser does not rely on payment alone. Responsa Rishmei She'elah 52 writes that it is obvious that the seller too may retract, even if the purchaser does not wish to retract. This follows from the next seif, where the Shulchan Aruch says that because of a certain condition the seller cannot retract, implying that without that condition he could. Similar proof may be brought from siman 227:4 and from the rule of one who repeatedly demands the unpaid price in this siman, seif 10, where the purchaser too may retract. These sources show that where the transaction has not been completed from one side, the other side may also retract. Baer Heitev to siman 195 likewise states this expressly. Rishmei She'elah then discusses a case in which Reuven sold land to Shimon with kinyan sudar. The money had not yet been paid and no deed had been written. The scribe merely recorded privately that Reuven sold the land to Shimon by kinyan sudar and that payment would be made after Reuven's wife accepted a kinyan under the customary formula. Before payment, the wife refused to accept the kinyan. The purchaser said that he was now willing to buy even without her kinyan and accept the risk that she might later recover the property, but Reuven the seller also wished to retract. The judges argued for the purchaser from siman 195, which states that kinyan sudar acquires without money and without a deed. The questioner responded that this was mistaken. Kiddushin 26 and siman 190 teach that where the custom is to write a deed, money alone does not acquire because the purchaser does not rely on it. This shows that halachah follows established practice. Even if the purchaser now says that he is willing to acquire without a deed, the seller may retract before the deed is delivered. The Taz in Even HaEzer 50 similarly rules that where parties wrote preliminary terms, even if they did not expressly say that the arrangement was conditional on a later formal document, the regular practice of writing such a document makes it as though that condition were stated. Here too, the customary practice was that the wife accepted a kinyan, so from the outset the purchaser did not rely on the transaction without that kinyan. Rishmei She'elah answers at length that the seller may indeed cancel the transaction. He first explains why the wife's kinyan has practical significance. Although a husband's sale of his property is ordinarily subject to his wife's later collection rights despite her subsequent kinyan, Beit Shmuel writes that if the contractual responsibility is expressly imposed on both husband and wife, a kinyan may be made even from the husband first. Under the standard wording of our deeds, responsibility is explicitly undertaken by the wife as well. Therefore the customary kinyan from the wife serves to impose her contractual responsibility, and the purchaser may reasonably have relied upon receiving it. He also accepts the questioner's premise that once the purchaser is legally entitled to escape the transaction, the seller may likewise retract. Nevertheless, he hesitates whether lack of reliance can truly be asserted here, because without the wife's kinyan the purchaser's risk is not certain. She may die first, or sufficient other property may remain from which she can collect her ketubah. It is therefore possible that even if the purchaser initially demanded her kinyan, beit din would not listen if he later tried to cancel solely for that reason, certainly where, as here, he expressly agrees to proceed without it. The scribe's note that payment was to be made after the wife's kinyan would require investigation to determine whether the parties actually formulated that as a full legal condition, since conditions concerning land must satisfy the formal rules of conditions in siman 207. In his final analysis, however, he rejects the judges' reliance on the rule that kinyan sudar acquires without money or deed. In his view that rule applies only where no deed is customarily written after payment. Where a deed is ordinarily written after payment, kinyan sudar is no stronger than money, and both parties may retract until the deed is written. He understands the Rama similarly. Accordingly, even had the wife's kinyan been obtained, so long as no deed had yet been written, under our practice either party could retract from the sale itself. He therefore rules in favor of the seller's right to cancel, though he states that he offers the conclusion for halachic study and not as a practical ruling unless another renowned rabbi agrees.
