Seif 17: Except that movable property differs in this respect. The wording might imply that land never admits this distinction, and the Sema writes so. He overlooked the Ran's explicit statement that land too can be divisible where each resulting portion contains the minimum measure of a field. Logic also requires this. One cannot compel a person to take a useless fragment merely so that he may later sell it to an adjoining owner. The Sema himself says that a kosher animal is considered indivisible because it is worth less when divided than alive. All the more so, land below the minimum useful measure is not meaningfully divisible. The Rosh's language likewise includes every genuinely divisible item. Rabbeinu's special mention of movable property addresses the ordinary case of a large parcel of land, which is generally divisible, while even large movable items may be indivisible. A further distinction exists. With movable property, if it is divisible each party takes his proportional share; if not, the sale is entirely void and the money is returned. With land, even when divisible, the retracting seller may be compelled to compensate the purchaser at the present price from superior land, because the purchaser is treated as injured. All movable property is legally superior property, so that remedy has no separate significance. If the purchaser retracts, land and movable property are alike: the seller may give him the inferior portion. The Rambam, however, appears to apply the land rules fully to movable property as well.
