An important person. Ran writes that where an important person accepts a gift from someone, the giver receives a legally significant benefit from the honor of having his gift accepted. Rabbeinu Tam therefore says that if a person gives a maneh to an important person and the important person agrees that his own property should be transferred to the giver, the benefit can function as consideration, even as a form of barter. This requires examination. Even Levi, who holds that barter can be performed with the transferor's utensil, still requires an actual utensil comparable to the shoe mentioned in Scripture. Tosafot explains that coins and ordinary produce are excluded. If so, even if an important person's acceptance creates benefit, why should that intangible benefit itself count as the required utensil? Indeed, according to our accepted law like Rav, barter requires the purchaser's utensil to be received by the transferor. The transferor's pleasure that an important person accepted something is no more tangible than any other benefit and is not itself an utensil. Therefore it seems difficult to treat the benefit of an important person as barter under our law, even if the important person accepted an actual utensil. Rabbeinu Chananel can be understood consistently according to his own broader definition of the object usable for barter. He holds that the exclusion of the shoe is directed chiefly against perishable produce, while other enduring items can function in barter. An animal works because it is treated as an implement capable of work. On his view, the enduring social value of having an important person accept one's gift can likewise be treated as sufficiently stable benefit for the barter mechanism. But according to our ruling that even nonperishable objects do not work unless they have the halachic character of an implement, the benefit created by an important person's acceptance should not function as barter. It can certainly function as money value in contexts such as kiddushin or land acquisition, where benefit equivalent to money suffices, but it does not appear to satisfy the special requirements of sudar kinyan. This is my conclusion.
The seller benefits from accepting it from him. Ran asks how the doctrine of an important person's acceptance can work according to Rav, who requires the purchaser's utensil for barter and does not permit the transferor's utensil. He answers that the special benefit of an important person's acceptance exists only when the important person receives an outright gift. An ordinary sudar kinyan is not a true gift but is understood as given only for the acquisition and normally returned. The giver receives no comparable honor from such a temporary transfer. Bach inferred that even a formal gift on condition of return cannot create the important person benefit. I do not think that follows. A gift on condition of return is a genuine gift. Rosh explains that ownership passes fully, subject only to a condition requiring later return. The giver can therefore still feel honored that the important person accepted his genuine gift. An ordinary sudar kinyan is different in kind: it is not a gift at all but an instrument of acquisition. According to what I wrote above, however, there is another answer to Ran's question. Under our law like Rav, the pleasure received by the giver is not itself an utensil and therefore cannot serve as barter even with an important person. It can serve as money value where money suffices. Ran's question is chiefly relevant according to Rabbeinu Chananel's broader barter theory. Ran also asks why Levi, who allows barter through the transferor's utensil because of the pleasure of acceptance, does not require the recipient to be important. He answers that every recipient gives some pleasure to the giver by accepting the gift, but with an ordinary person that pleasure is worth less than a perutah. Barter does not require a perutah, while kiddushin does. An important person's acceptance produces benefit worth a perutah. This resolves a question in Orach Chaim. A person who receives new utensils as a gift recites the blessing of the Good and the Beneficent because both recipient and giver experience benefit. Magen Avraham asks that in Even HaEzer only an important person is said to confer benefit upon the giver. Ran's distinction answers it. Even an ordinary recipient gives the donor some pleasure, though less than a perutah. Kiddushin needs perutah value; the blessing does not. Thus the giver can properly be considered a beneficiary even when the recipient is not an important person.
Shimon cannot say, I collect this maneh toward my debt. In my view this is true only where Shimon had already agreed to sell the land for that maneh. If Reuven simply handed him a maneh and Shimon received it silently, Shimon may say, "I took my own money," meaning repayment of the existing debt. Hagahot Ashri and Hagahot Mordechai speak where Shimon had first agreed to the sale. The law of kiddushin is different. If a woman is owed money and the man gives her a coin without specifying repayment and then says, "Be betrothed to me with it," she may sometimes not be believed to say she accepted it merely as repayment, especially where they had previously arranged the kiddushin. Rashba explains that this rests on the factual presumption that women ordinarily wish to marry. It is not a general monetary rule. Rashba says explicitly that in ordinary monetary law, if Reuven owes Shimon one maneh and Levi another and tells Shimon, "Take this maneh for Levi," Shimon can receive it silently and later say, "I took what was mine." Therefore we cannot derive sale law from the special presumption in kiddushin. Accordingly, in a sale, if Shimon had already agreed to sell and then accepted the money silently, the context can establish that he accepted it for the purchase. But if the money was initially identified as repayment, or if no prior sale agreement existed, Shimon can say it was received toward his debt. Even where a prior agreement existed, if Reuven first expressly said the payment was repayment and only afterward tried to apply it to the sale, the special marriage presumption does not exist and Shimon can retain it as repayment.
