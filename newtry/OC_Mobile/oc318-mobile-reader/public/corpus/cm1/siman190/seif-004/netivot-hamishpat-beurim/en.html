Ketzot HaChoshen asks that this is an absolute benefit, and one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence, as is evident in Bava Kamma concerning a dyer who dyed garments for them, where the woman acquired although she knew nothing of it. He answers that the case is where the money was not given as a gift, but on condition that it be repaid, so it is not necessarily a benefit. In my view this is not correct. If the case were a loan, there would have been no need to derive the rule from Rava's statement in Kiddushin concerning a Canaanite slave. There the point is that the recipient acquires although he suffers no loss. According to Ketzot, the recipient does suffer a loss because he has borrowed the money and must repay it, so why would the rule need to be learned from a Canaanite slave? Tur in siman 183, cited by Shach there, simply says that it is treated as though the money had been lent to him. Moreover, Rama states the same rule in seif 5 regarding one who buys chametz for another, where the case cannot be explained as a loan. Rather, the case is straightforward. A person purchases a field for one thousand zuz and another gives the seller one hundred as a gift in order to effect acquisition by money, while the buyer must pay the balance. The acquisition is not complete until the buyer consents, because perhaps it is not a benefit for him to acquire a field for one thousand merely because one hundred was gifted on his behalf. This differs from the dyer case, where everything was given to the women as a gift and the benefit was certain. See siman 195, where I wrote that even if the other person gives the entire purchase price as a gift, the sale is not completed until he appoints him as agent or tells the seller, "Your field is acquired to me."
