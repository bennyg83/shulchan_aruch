But in a place where a deed is required. Ketzot HaChoshen asks from the law of a woman who admits that part of her ketubah was reduced, and distinguishes between a deed of evidence and a deed of acquisition. That distinction is sound, but his further conclusion, that if one obligated himself by deed for five hundred and wrote one thousand merely as an accommodation the obligation does not take effect, does not appear correct. Tosafot in Ketubot 102 explain that an obligation deed does not operate because movable property is acquired by deed; rather, since he troubled himself to write a document, he fully undertakes the obligation upon himself. According to Rambam, even "you are witnesses" can create such an obligation. An obligation deed is therefore more like an evidentiary deed than a conveyancing deed. Accordingly, in a place where the custom is that money does not complete the land purchase until an evidentiary deed is written, partial acquisition can be effective. Beit Yosef distinguishes that where the custom is specifically to write a conveyancing deed, the purchase is not completed until the deed is written. But where the deed is only evidentiary, the acquisition is deemed completed retroactively from the payment, because the deed itself does not effect the purchase. Such a deed can therefore operate with respect to a partial acquisition. By contrast, a conveyancing deed cannot acquire only half where its legal form requires the whole, and therefore there is likewise no rule of the retracting party having the lower hand. Once the sale is void entirely, this resembles movable property that is not divisible: there is no partial subsisting acquisition to which the rule of the lower hand can attach.
However, if he stipulated. It appears that the reference marked by Maggid Mishneh to chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah is mistaken. Rama's intent here is necessarily that even in a place where the custom is to write a deed, if the parties stipulate that money alone shall acquire, the condition is effective. It follows that if the seller repeatedly demanded payment, the buyer acquires to the extent of the money paid. The rule that such a stipulation works is explicit in Kiddushin. Maggid Mishneh's novelty in chapter 11 is different: even where the parties stipulate that only the deed shall acquire, that condition is effective. That law is not relevant here, because if they stipulated that only the deed should acquire and the seller repeatedly demanded payment, the entire acquisition would fail, since a deed cannot effect only a partial acquisition.
