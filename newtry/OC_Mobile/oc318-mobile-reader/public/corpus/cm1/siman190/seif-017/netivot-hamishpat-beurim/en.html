But in this there is a distinction regarding movable property. See Sema, seif katan 28. The Taz disagrees and rules that even with land that is not subject to division, partial payment does not acquire a corresponding fractional share. This is the principal view, as Beit Yosef writes. Nevertheless, the language "in this there is a distinction" is difficult, because according to that ruling there appears to be no distinction at all between land and movable property. The Taz explains that the distinction concerns divisible land: when the seller retracts, he is at a disadvantage, and if the buyer demands land the seller must give superior land, whereas with movable property there is no obligation to give a superior category because all movable property is legally considered superior for collection. This is strained. Ketzot HaChoshen explains that where a person bought land and thereby subjected his property in order to acquire land, the seller must specifically give land upon retraction even if he has cash, whereas in a purchase of movable property, where the buyer never intended to acquire land, the seller need not give land. But even this is difficult. At least with respect to the rule that a seller who retracts from a land sale must sell whatever property he has in order to return cash specifically, why should movable property differ? The same difficulty applies to the suggestion that land need not be given specifically in a movable sale, and all the more so according to the view that the seller must give specifically the best land he owns even when willing to pay cash or other superior property. What source do the authorities have for distinguishing movable property from land in these respects? In my humble opinion the distinction is straightforward. Siman 203 states that if a person says, "a cow and a lamb for a kor of wheat," and the buyer draws the cow but not the lamb, he acquires wheat corresponding to the value of the cow. Yet regarding land, siman 182 explains that the buyer can say, "I intended to buy only the entire two seah together," regardless of whether the land is divisible. The distinction must be the one stated by the Ran at the beginning of the second chapter of Kiddushin concerning a transaction measured by a rope. With land, which cannot be completed from outside the particular property, if part of the transaction fails the buyer can say that he wanted the whole property together. With movable property, which can be supplemented from elsewhere, he cannot make that claim. The transaction remains effective to the extent of the consideration actually given. I explained this at length in siman 182. Accordingly the Shulchan Aruch properly writes that "in this there is a distinction" between land and movable property. With land that is divisible, if the seller cancels the transaction as to the unpaid portion, the buyer can also cancel the portion corresponding to the money already paid and demand his cash, saying that he needed the entire purchase. With movable property, by contrast, even if the seller cancels the unpaid portion, the buyer cannot cancel what remains, as in siman 203. The seller can compel the buyer to accept movable property corresponding to the amount already paid.
"Let my earnest money acquire," he acquires according to the amount of his money. I am surprised that the Mechaber did not cite Tosafot in Bava Metzia 48, s.v. Akhpol, who hold that the buyer may cancel the entire purchase because he can say, "I want the complete purchase." All the more so where the property is not subject to division, he can say, "I do not wish to become your partner."
