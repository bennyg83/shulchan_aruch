If the seller seized. Mishneh LaMelech asks why seizure by the buyer should not likewise help. Sema's explanation is very strained, as Taz observes, and Taz's own formulation is somewhat difficult. In my view Taz can be explained through the rules of seizure. Where the Gemara leaves a doubt and one party seizes while asserting with certainty the factual side that would entitle him under that unresolved rule, we do not remove it from him. By contrast, where his own claim is uncertain, seizure does not help. Here the Gemara's doubt is whether the seller's repeated demand for payment was because he urgently needed the money or merely because he feared the buyer would retract. When the seller seizes, he claims with certainty that he demanded the money because he urgently needed it. He therefore asserts a definite fact corresponding to one side of the Gemara's doubt, and seizure helps. The buyer, however, cannot know why the seller was demanding payment. His assertion is necessarily uncertain, and therefore his seizure does not help. This also explains Mechaber's language that neither party may retract. As Taz says, one cannot destroy a certain completed acquisition merely because of the prior owner's chazakah when the condition for cancellation is itself doubtful. Rashba similarly rules that a certain acquisition is not undone because of doubt about a condition. If the seller seized and asserts with certainty that he demanded payment because of urgent need, and seeks to cancel the sale as to the unpaid balance, we do not remove what he seized. But he certainly cannot cancel the part corresponding to money already paid, because Rambam holds that even where urgent repeated demands are certain, the sale remains valid to that extent.
