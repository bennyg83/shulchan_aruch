Although a beit din of three is a complete beit din, the greater the number of judges, the more praiseworthy it is; it is preferable for the judgment to be decided by eleven rather than ten. All who sit on the beit din must be Torah scholars and fit to judge. A wise person may not sit in judgment until he knows with whom he is sitting, lest he sit with unworthy people and be counted among a band of traitors rather than among a beit din. If one is not an expert and the litigants did not accept him, his ruling is invalid even if he received authorization from the Exilarch and even if he did not err; either litigant may demand that the case be reheard before a beit din. {Rama: Authorization granted nowadays by a non-Jewish king has no effect. If the community accepted the judge on the strength of the king's writ, however, he may judge (Tur). Some say that if he is learned and capable, authorization from the king, or from the official appointed over his city, is effective (Rivash, siman 271), because the law of the kingdom includes authority to appoint whatever judges and magistrates it chooses. Nevertheless, one who does so without the community's permission causes the public distress and will ultimately be held to account (Responsa of the Rashba, siman 637).}
