The king's authorization helps him. See Urim. Bach writes that whether such authorization exempts a judge who erred is itself disputed. Shach, seif katan 15, agrees with Rivash and writes that even if some disagree, Rivash's reasoning is sound: if the authority of the Exilarch, whose office was established under the king, is effective, then certainly authorization from the king himself should be effective. This requires study. Shach's proof comes from Rashi, who explains that the Nesi'im are called "the lawgiver" while the Exilarch is called "the scepter," because the Exilarch had authority from the Persian kings and could chastise the people with a rod. Shach assumes that this royal authority is why the Exilarch could grant judicial authorization even from Bavel into Eretz Yisrael, and therefore the same should apply nowadays when a territorial king authorizes someone to judge. In truth that is not the principal reason for the Exilarch's wider power. The Exilarch descended from the male line of the house of David, whereas the Nesi'im of the line of Hillel descended from the house of David through the female line. The Exilarch therefore possessed a stronger hereditary authority. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 5a, s.v. "here is the scepter," explicitly explain that this is why the Exilarch had greater power to remove money than the Nesi'im of Eretz Yisrael. The Yerushalmi says likewise. This is directly contrary to Rivash, who makes royal permission the essential reason. I also do not understand Rivash historically. Why should authorization granted by the Persian king empower the Exilarch to confer jurisdiction in Eretz Yisrael? In the days of Rebbi and the generations after him, Eretz Yisrael was not under Persian rule at all. It was under the Roman emperors. Antoninus, in Rebbi's era, granted Tiberias special civic status. The Persian monarch had no governmental power in Eretz Yisrael. How then could his grant authorize judicial power there? If anything, Rebbi himself certainly possessed authorization from the Roman emperor Antoninus to judge and compel recalcitrant parties. Roman dominion then extended across much of the world, the fourth empire seen by Daniel. If secular royal authorization were the key, Rebbi should have been able to grant jurisdiction even in Bavel and throughout the Diaspora. Necessarily the matter did not depend upon the secular king. It depended on Davidic lineage and the heavenly authority associated with the royal house, as Scripture says, "House of David, judge justice in the morning." The Nesi'im of Eretz Yisrael descended from David only maternally and therefore lacked the same force. See below, siman 149, where Rosh explains that no chazakah is established against the house of the Exilarch because they are of royal descent. Rashi's statement that the king granted the Exilarch authority means only to explain how they could actually wield coercive power over the people while living under a sovereign ruler. Without the king's permission they could not have used force in practice. Rashi does not say that royal permission was the source of their halachic authority. Beit Yosef likewise implies that royal permission was practically necessary because otherwise they could not judge coercively, not that the secular king created the Torah authority itself. Rivash's proof therefore requires substantial study. Tashbetz also questioned Rivash from a different angle. The king had granted Rivash an exclusive right that no other person could judge besides him. Tashbetz argued that this creates a problem: if someone has a legal claim against Rivash himself, no court can judge him. The rule "adorn yourself and then adorn others" implies that one who cannot be judged should not judge others. A similar idea is said regarding a king, who does not judge because others cannot judge him. Kenesset HaGedolah cites the same concern. But Tashbetz's objection itself can be answered. The royal decree must have meant that Rivash alone could judge without another judge joining him. Otherwise the objection has little force, because Tosafot in Sanhedrin 18b explain that even a king can participate in a court together with others, since the final judgment does not rest solely on him. The principle applies when he alone cannot be subjected to the same judgment he imposes. Even if Rivash indeed had authority to judge alone, the objection still fails. The king of Spain did not rule over the entire earth. If someone had a claim against Rivash, he could go to a beit din outside the Spanish realm and litigate there. If Rivash refused to obey that court, they could place him under cherem and use the remedies of Torah. Thus he remained a person who could, in principle, be judged, albeit at a distance. A Jewish king is different. Chazal decreed that no Jewish beit din may judge him. He is therefore outside the ordinary judicial process altogether, and for that reason he does not judge others under that enactment. Accordingly, Tashbetz's objection to Rivash is not compelling. Rivash remained subject to judicial process somewhere, even if the local king's grant made him exclusive judge within Spain. Even so, the more fundamental objection remains: Rivash's attempt to derive halachic judicial authority from secular royal permission does not appear supported by the sugya of the Exilarch.
