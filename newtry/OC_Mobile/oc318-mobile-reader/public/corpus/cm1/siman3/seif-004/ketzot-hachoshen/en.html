He should seat others with him. The Shach concludes that when a true single expert is accepted by the parties, he may adjudicate even initially. Even without an express acceptance, if the parties voluntarily come before him without compulsion, that itself counts as acceptance. Today, when there is no formally ordained expert, those who customarily judge alone do not act properly unless they expressly tell the parties that they do not know how to judge them according to strict Torah law, or the case is so simple and familiar that expertise in such matters exists even today. In my view one should not be lenient at all. There is no case simpler than a partial admission, a rule known even to schoolchildren, yet as explained above a single expert cannot adjudicate it in the full formal sense, because an admission before him is not a judicial admission. The same applies to other matters dependent upon admission and denial, for a party may change his claims from liability to exemption unless he became fixed as a false denier before three. Knesset HaGedolah writes that even before two accepted judges the admission does not have the same force. As Rashbam explains in the case of three who entered to visit a sick person, if the matter remains retractable it has not yet become a judicial ruling at all. Matters not dependent upon admissions and denials likewise require substantial analysis. Therefore one who fears Heaven should distance himself from judging alone, in accordance with the teaching of the Sages, "Do not judge alone."
