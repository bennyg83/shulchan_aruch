In the Beit Yosef in this siman. He cites the baraita, “The Sages taught: monetary cases are judged by three,” and the comments of Tosafot and the Rosh, from which he derives a rule. It is difficult why they did not derive the same point more simply from the opening clause, that monetary cases require three. Necessarily, if the litigants accepted the judges, even one would suffice. The responsa attributed to the Rashba answer that the opening clause may refer to judges whom the litigants accepted while saying, “Judge us in the manner in which you ordinarily judge.” Tosafot similarly write later that with such wording three may judge, but one may not. I do not understand this answer, for then the latter clause also provides no proof. Perhaps the litigants granted authority to the expert only when they used that particular wording, but not against an unwilling litigant. My brother, Rabbi Yitzchak HaLevi, of blessed memory, answered that no proof may be brought from the opening clause, because it may indeed concern accepted judges, and the requirement of three may be stated merely as good counsel, that one should not sit alone but with three. The latter clause cannot be explained that way, for if it were merely good counsel, the same counsel would apply even to an expert. It must therefore state a legal rule, and the inference is sound. Rabbi Yitzchak likewise wrote this.
