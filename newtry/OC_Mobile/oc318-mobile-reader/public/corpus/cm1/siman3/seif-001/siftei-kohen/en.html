A beit din may not consist of fewer than three, etc. The Sema writes that this requirement is rabbinic, whereas by Torah law one judge suffices. He says that the Rambam states this explicitly in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sanhedrin, ruling like Rav Acha, and that the Rosh at the beginning of Sanhedrin implies the same, contrary to the Ir Shushan. In my humble opinion, however, the Ir Shushan is correct. The Gemara asks what difference exists between Rava and Rav Acha, and answers that according to Rava the ruling of Shmuel, that two who judged have rendered a valid judgment, does not apply, whereas according to Rav Acha it does. Since we rule against Shmuel, as stated at the beginning of seif 2, we necessarily follow Rava, who requires three ordained judges by Torah law from the three appearances of the word elohim in the verse. The Sages merely enacted that three laymen suffice, so that the door not be closed before borrowers. The Rosh's view is also clearly so, since he rules like Rabbi Abahu and against Shmuel. His citation of Rav Acha at the beginning of the chapter teaches only that one of the three must be learned and competent, a point on which Rava does not disagree, for otherwise the Gemara would have listed this as another difference. This appears to me to be the Rosh's position, contrary to the Sema and Bach. The Rosh also writes explicitly that the law follows Rabbi Abahu because from Abaye and Rava onward the law follows the later authority, and the Gemara states that Rava rejects Shmuel. Beit Yosef cites this, and it is a major difficulty against the Sema and Bach's understanding of the Rosh. The Ir Shushan's words are exact, for in seif 2 he writes that since Scripture teaches from the three instances of elohim that three are required, a judgment rendered by fewer than three is invalid. This precisely follows the Gemara's analysis. Conversely, the Rambam is difficult. Since he rules that a judgment rendered by fewer than three is invalid, how can he also write in accordance with Rav Acha, when the Gemara says that Rav Acha accepts Shmuel? The same difficulty applies to the Semag. There is another difficulty. In Bava Kamma, chapter HaChovel, and Gittin, chapter HaMegareish, the Gemara explicitly says that monetary admissions and loans are adjudicated because the courts outside Eretz Yisrael act as agents of the ordained judges. The Rambam himself in chapter 3 of Hilchot Sanhedrin and the Semag likewise write that admissions and loans may be adjudicated outside Eretz Yisrael even though its judges are not elohim, because they act as agents of the courts in Eretz Yisrael. This proves that by Torah law even admissions and loans require ordained judges, in accordance with Rava, whereas according to Rav Acha one lay judge would suffice by Torah law. Perhaps the Rambam and Semag can be reconciled with difficulty, but the primary conclusion is that we rule like Rava and against Shmuel, as is implied in Bava Kamma and Gittin, which require elohim. This is also clearly the Rif's view, since he omits Rav Acha and presents the opening discussion in Sanhedrin according to Rava, that three ordained judges are required by Torah law. Raavan, Nimukei Yosef, and others write likewise. When I first wrote this, the Kesef Mishneh was unavailable to me because I had fled from empty wars. I have now seen that the Kesef Mishneh notices the contradiction in the Rambam, who rules like Rav Acha and immediately rules that two who judged have rendered no valid judgment. He cites a responsum of the Rashba reconciling the Rambam, and the Lechem Mishneh does likewise. In my humble opinion their explanations are strained, and the language of the Gemara, 'What is the difference between them,' does not support them. Moreover, the other difficulties I raised remain unanswered. See there.
For it is impossible that one of them is not knowledgeable, etc. The Sema writes that this does not mean one who is both learned and analytically proficient. This is obvious from the Gemara and authorities. Nevertheless, it may be that he must at least be learned, as the Gemara's plain language, 'one who is learned,' implies. Maharam Padua writes this in responsum 43. The Bach disputes him, saying that full learning is unnecessary and that some learning, sufficient to understand a degree of legal reasoning, is enough. His words are not compelling. Responsa Mahari ben Lev, volume 1, siman 123, writes that the possessor may claim, 'I rely on the Ramah, who requires all three to be learned.' This does not appear correct to me. Rather, the Rosh's reading of the Gemara is primary. It is possible that even the Ramah agrees and means only that there must be judges among them who are learned. The Tur therefore says, 'It appears from his words,' meaning that it appears so at first glance but is not conclusive. The views of Rabbi Zerachiah HaLevi, Ramban, and the other authorities cited below in siman 25 likewise imply that one learned judge among them suffices. This is the primary ruling.
