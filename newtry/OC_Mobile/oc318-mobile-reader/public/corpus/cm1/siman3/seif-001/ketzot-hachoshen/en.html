A beit din is not fewer than three. Rambam rules that by Torah law a single judge may adjudicate, from the verse, "with righteousness shall you judge your fellow," while rabbinic law requires three. This follows Rav Acha and Shmuel. Yet the sugyot in Perek HaChovel and Perek HaMegarash imply that even ordinary monetary admissions and loans require three ordained experts by Torah law, for they say that our courts act as agents of the earlier judges. Several authorities cite this as proof against Rambam. It appears, however, from Tosafot that even if a single judge can issue a monetary ruling by Torah law, coercive enforcement certainly requires ordained judges. Tosafot distinguishes between ordinary adjudication and the use of judicial compulsion, deriving the latter from "these are the judgments that you shall place before them," which includes the court's instruments of enforcement and refers to the ordained elders. Thus Rabbi Yosef could answer Abaye, who challenged his coercive handling of a get on the ground that we are lay judges, that our courts act as agents of the earlier courts just as in admissions and loans. According to the opinion that a debtor's property is not biblically encumbered, repayment is a mitzvah and beit din coerces the debtor to fulfill it. Even according to Ramban, who allows descent to the debtor's property, that descent is itself a form of judicial coercion to fulfill the mitzvah. Hence agency from the earlier courts is needed for coercion, even if the underlying judgment can be issued by one judge. This also vindicates the Bach, who makes this distinction. According to Rashi's explanation of "before them" as referring to the seventy elders, the distinction is particularly clear: ordinary judgment may not always require ordination, but coercive authority does. This resolves Rambam's ruling. The sugyot requiring the agency of the earlier courts concern enforcement, not the bare intellectual act of deciding the monetary law. The broader discussion also resolves the apparent contradiction from the requirement of experts in the first chapter of Sanhedrin. By Torah law a learned individual can render a monetary ruling just as a qualified scholar can rule on prohibitions. But an admission creating the status of partial admission is different. Admission before beit din is not merely a matter of legal expertise. It requires a formal Torah beit din, namely three ordained judges. Thus the verses concerning three judges apply to the formal admission under "when he says, this is it," while the bare ruling itself can sometimes be rendered by one. Where the text also places three ordained judges in the laws of custodians even without the partial admission element, that proves that the body of judgment in robbery and injury cases itself requires three. This approach resolves the opening sugya of Sanhedrin concerning whether the sections are intermingled. If the sections are intermingled, the requirement of three experts extends to loans because formal partial admission requires beit din, while the custodial section separately demonstrates a requirement of three for the substantive adjudication of robbery type matters. If the sections are not intermingled, the verse can be understood as speaking only of the formal admission, and no proof remains that every substantive ruling requires three. Rambam's language in Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 5, that admissions and loans are adjudicated outside Eretz Yisrael because our courts act as agents of the courts of Eretz Yisrael, is then exact. "Admissions" includes partial admission and the legal fixing of a party as a false denier. Those formal statuses require a Torah beit din and are available to us through agency. But the underlying monetary judgment in ordinary loan matters can be made by a learned individual. Kesef Mishneh's question from Rabbi Meir's comparison of monetary disputes to leprosy cases is likewise no difficulty. Rabbi Meir himself holds that compromise requires three because he holds ordinary judgment requires three. We do not rule like Rabbi Meir on this point. Thus Rambam's position remains clear and consistent.
