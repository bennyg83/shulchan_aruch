A beit din cannot consist of fewer than three. See Shach, seif katan 1, who leaves Rambam's ruling requiring study, as I explained in Urim. At the beginning of Sanhedrin we explain that monetary cases, meaning admissions and loans, are heard by three lay judges, whereas robberies and personal injuries require three expert judges. The Gemara asks: if the sections are intermingled and the requirement of three derives from the three occurrences of the word "elohim" in the parashah, then experts should also be required. If the sections are not intermingled, why require three at all? Rava answers that the sections are intermingled. D'oraisa experts are required, but because of the concern that lenders would otherwise stop lending, three lay judges suffice, as explained above, because we act as the agents of the ordained judges. R. Acha son of Rav Ika answers that the sections are not intermingled. D'oraisa even one judge is valid, as the verse says, "With righteousness shall you judge your fellow," but Chazal required three because of unlearned judges who sit at street corners, since among three there will ordinarily be at least one who has learned the law. The Gemara asks what practical difference exists between Rava and R. Acha, and answers that the rule of Shmuel is the difference. Shmuel says that if two judged, their judgment is valid, though they are called an impudent beit din. Rava rejects Shmuel, while R. Acha accepts him. Accordingly, Rambam appears difficult. He rules like R. Abahu and against Shmuel, that if two judged their judgment is invalid. That seems to require Rava's explanation rather than R. Acha's. How, then, can Rambam also rule like R. Acha that d'oraisa one judge is valid? Many great scholars have already struggled with this, and the proposed answers appear strained. The correct approach, in my view, begins with the dispute recorded by Tur between Ramah and Rosh whether all three judges must be learned or whether it suffices that one of the three is learned. This dispute plainly depends upon the above sugya. If d'oraisa three judges are required and Chazal merely allowed three non-ordained judges because of public need, then all three should still have to be learned. If d'oraisa one judge is valid and Chazal required three only because of the concern for unlearned judges, then one learned judge suffices, since among the three he supplies the necessary competence. Indeed, according to Rashi's explanation of the Gemara's question, "If so, if they erred should they not pay, since they came with permission?", there is nearly a proof for Ramah. Otherwise the same question should arise regarding any panel of three, since their very authority to judge comes from Chazal. Why should they pay for an error? One must say that without this additional consideration we would have attributed the enactment to the danger of unlearned street-corner judges. Then if they were exempt from payment, many more such unlearned judges would sit and judge. But if that is so, even according to R. Acha we could say the same: his reason that three were required because of unlearned judges might still allow that all three must be learned once Chazal established the court. Why does the Gemara not say this? The better inference is that according to R. Acha all three are indeed learned. Ramah's position is therefore strongly supported. If so, the Gemara could have stated a different practical difference between Rava and R. Acha: according to one all three must be learned, while according to the other one learned judge suffices. The answers given to this question are strained. Rambam was troubled by this as well. He was also troubled by the Gemara's question what difference there is between Rava and R. Acha. Perhaps there is no practical difference at all, and they disagree only in how to explain the Mishnah. Rava may have found it implausible that Chazal would require three merely because of unlearned judges, and therefore interpreted the Mishnah according to the view that the sections are intermingled and that Chazal allowed three lay judges because of the needs of lenders. R. Acha may have accepted the street-corner concern and therefore interpreted the Mishnah even according to the view that the sections are not intermingled. The practical law could still be identical. Rambam, however, appears to have held two principles. First, he follows Rashi later on Sanhedrin 5 that a single expert may judge alone because d'oraisa one judge is valid, from "With righteousness shall you judge your fellow." He does not accept Tosafot's suggestion that even if d'oraisa three are required, Chazal independently enacted that one expert may judge alone. Rambam did not recognize such an enactment, and indeed there is little need for it, since three lay judges are ordinarily available. Why would Chazal uproot the Torah requirement of three merely for this purpose? Rather, the verse itself teaches that one can judge monetary disputes. The three occurrences of "elohim" therefore cannot teach that every admission and loan requires three judges. According to the view that the sections are intermingled, they instead teach that the single judge must be ordained and expert. According to the view that the sections are not intermingled, the three occurrences of "elohim" do not apply to admissions and loans at all, and d'oraisa even one non-ordained judge can be valid. Thus all opinions agree that one expert can judge d'oraisa. They disagree only whether expertise and semikhah are required. This also resolves Tosafot's question that R. Yochanan himself holds in Gittin that the sections are intermingled and yet allows one expert to judge. There is no contradiction. Even according to the intermingling view, the verse "With righteousness shall you judge your fellow" teaches that one judge suffices, while "elohim" still teaches that this one judge must be an ordained expert. Second, Rambam held that even Shmuel, who says two judges' judgment is valid, agrees that initially one should convene three. His wording, "if two judged," implies only after the fact. Tosafot write similarly in many places. This must be so, because R. Acha accepts Shmuel while the Mishnah still says monetary cases are before three. Thus Chazal required three initially because of the concern for unlearned judges, but Shmuel holds that the enactment was not strong enough to invalidate a judgment already rendered by two. This is so only if the sections are not intermingled. Then d'oraisa no ordained expert is required, and the entire three-judge requirement is rabbinic. But if the sections are intermingled, then at least one ordained judge is required d'oraisa. Our present lay judges can act only because they are agents of the earlier ordained judges. An agent who deviates from the principal's authorization loses his agency. All the more so here: the earlier ordained courts authorized lay judges to judge only in accordance with the form Chazal prescribed. If two lay judges act where Chazal required three, their agency is absent. Even if the numerical defect by itself would not invalidate the result after the fact, their status as unordained judges does invalidate it, because the delegated authority never extended to a panel that violated the conditions of delegation. This idea appears to be implicit in Rashi as well. That is why Rashi explains R. Acha as holding that the sections are not intermingled. Tosafot ask that perhaps R. Acha too agrees they are intermingled but the verse "With righteousness shall you judge your fellow" teaches that one judge suffices. According to what I have written, that cannot be, because if ordained expertise remains d'oraisa, Shmuel's rule could not apply to two lay judges. Once they depart from the delegated authorization, their entire power to judge collapses. Since the Gemara says R. Acha accepts Shmuel, Rashi was compelled to explain that R. Acha holds the sections are not intermingled and that no d'oraisa expert status is required at all. Now the Gemara's question and answer are precise. Rava too must accept the baraita that a single expert may judge, because d'oraisa one judge is valid. According to him, however, two enactments are necessary. First, Chazal required three because of the danger of unlearned street-corner judges. Second, although d'oraisa the one judge must be ordained, Chazal permitted three non-ordained judges because of the practical needs of lenders, through the mechanism of agency. The Gemara therefore asks what practical difference there is between Rava and R. Acha. We cannot say they merely disagree over whether to explain the Mishnah according to intermingled or non-intermingled sections. Rava too accepts that one expert is d'oraisa valid, so he also needs the street-corner enactment to explain why the Mishnah says three. Why then burden his explanation with the additional agency enactment rather than simply say, like R. Acha, that the sections are not intermingled? The Gemara answers that Shmuel is the difference. Rava must reject Shmuel. The Mishnah's requirement of three is therefore indispensable even after the fact. Since Rava holds the sections are intermingled, the lay judges have power only as agents of ordained judges, and that agency was never given to a panel of fewer than three. R. Acha can accept Shmuel. He reads the Mishnah as requiring three only initially. Since d'oraisa no expert status is required at all, violation of the rabbinic three-judge enactment does not invalidate the judgment after the fact. This also answers the question from Ramah's view whether all three must be learned. Even Rava agrees that d'oraisa one ordained expert suffices and that the requirement of three is only because of the street-corner concern. Therefore one learned judge among the three can suffice, exactly as Rosh holds. Ramah's inference is not compelled. We thus obtain the following rule: one ordained expert is d'oraisa sufficient according to everyone. Non-ordained judges require three, and if fewer than three judge, the judgment is invalid because the delegated authority from the ordained courts was not given for that form of court. This, in my view, is the structure of Rambam. At the end of chapter 2, halachah 10, Rambam writes that d'oraisa one judge is valid because Scripture says, "With righteousness shall you judge your fellow." His intent must be one ordained judge, because elsewhere, in chapter 5, he clearly holds that the sections are intermingled and that present lay courts function as agents of the ordained judges. When Rambam later rejects Shmuel and rules like R. Abahu that if two judged their judgment is invalid, he is speaking of two non-ordained judges. Regarding ordained experts, the baraita and all the poskim agree that even one expert can judge alone. There is therefore no contradiction at all. His words are clear and consistent. Sema's statement that d'oraisa one judge is valid can likewise be read as referring to one ordained judge, especially because at the beginning of seif 1 he himself writes that the sections are intermingled and that even admissions and loans d'oraisa require ordained judges. What Sema says about Rosh, however, is more difficult. Rosh cites R. Acha's view that d'oraisa one judge is valid. It does not appear that Rosh adopts R. Acha as the final halachah. Rosh rules against Shmuel, and according to Rosh's own understanding R. Acha says no expert is d'oraisa required in admissions and loans even if he agrees the sections are intermingled. That would contradict Rosh's own opening statement that lay courts today judge only through delegated agency. Therefore Rosh did not cite R. Acha because he rules like him. He cited R. Acha in order to learn from his reasoning that one learned judge among three suffices. Rava does not disagree with that practical point. Had Rava required all three to be learned, the Gemara could have identified that as the practical difference between Rava and R. Acha. Since it did not, we infer that Rava too accepts that one learned judge suffices. This is the source for Tur's statement that Rosh disagrees with Ramah, even though commentators struggled to locate it explicitly in Rosh. Taz writes that Rosh rules like R. Acha but nevertheless rejects Shmuel because R. Acha himself would require three rabbinically in a manner that invalidates even after the fact. This is not tenable. According to Rosh, R. Acha does not require d'oraisa experts for admissions and loans, while Rosh plainly holds that lay courts today need the agency of ordained judges. Therefore Rosh cannot be following R. Acha's entire legal position. Note by the author. One might still ask: according to R. Acha, when the Gemara challenges that if the sections are intermingled experts should be required and if they are not intermingled three judges should not be required, why can we not say that the sections are intermingled, but "With righteousness shall you judge your fellow" teaches that one judge suffices? One answer follows Rashi's explanation of the question, "If so, if they erred should they not pay, since they came with permission?" There is an apparent difficulty. Even if this explains admissions and loans, what about robbery cases that were judged by lay courts through delegated authority? If they erred, should they not likewise be exempt because they entered with permission? Tosafot answer that for a robber it makes no practical difference whether the judges personally pay after error. So long as courts exist, people will refrain from robbery. One could also say that Chazal did not distinguish among categories once they established payment for judicial error. If in loan cases the judges must pay to deter incompetent judges, the same applies in robbery cases. Accordingly, even if the sections are intermingled, there is no question why three are required. Chazal could simply have made a uniform rule modeled after robbery cases. The Gemara's sharper question is why the result is invalid after the fact under R. Acha. If the requirement of three were merely rabbinic and based on uniformity with robbery cases, then after the fact the judgment should remain valid. This is why Rashi was compelled to explain R. Acha as holding the sections are not intermingled. And in robbery cases themselves, where three experts are d'oraisa required, Ramah's rule that all three be learned is in fact well grounded. According to R. Acha the same would apply there. This also resolves why Rashi needed to say R. Acha holds the sections are not intermingled rather than accept Tosafot's suggestion. Rosh, however, does not follow Rashi's understanding. He allows that R. Acha may hold the sections intermingled and yet not require ordained judges for admissions and loans. That creates the above question regarding the judges' payment for errors in robbery cases. Rosh therefore must reject Ramah and hold that even under R. Abahu, where three experts are formally required, Chazal permitted one learned judge among the three in the later delegated system. This is why he can still be concerned with unlearned judges and their liability. There is also another possible way to understand Rambam. Perhaps Rambam agrees with Kesef Mishneh that although the three-judge requirement is only rabbinic, if two judged their decision is invalid because Chazal made the requirement indispensable. The Gemara's proof that R. Acha accepts Shmuel would then depend on the prior discussion about liability for error. According to the view there that judges must always pay only when they directly transferred property, the proof changes, and Ramah's position can survive. One might further say that the Mishnah originally belonged to an era in which ordinary robberies were still judged specifically by ordained experts. The requirement of three in admissions and loans could then have been established by analogy to robberies. In that era, if two lay judges acted, the ruling could remain valid after the fact under Shmuel. In our later era, however, when even robbery is judged by lay courts through agency, Chazal may have insisted that all delegated courts conform to the three-judge structure, even after the fact. Rambam could therefore rule that two lay judges' judgment is invalid nowadays without contradicting the earlier sugya. In any event, Rambam's rulings can be reconciled, and the claim that they are inherently contradictory is unfounded.
One of them knows the reasoning, etc. Here Shulchan Aruch rules like Rosh and against Ramah, that it is not necessary for all three judges to be learned; one learned judge suffices. There is a difficulty from siman 28, seif 21, where Mechaber cites Rif's responsum that acceptance of testimony requires three expert judges. This implies that one learned judge among three does not suffice. It is strained to distinguish that case by saying the judges there must be expert in examining witnesses and in the subtleties of testimony. Although Rif's responsum mentions such expertise, Mechaber's plain wording does not indicate merely technical skill in interrogation. "Experts" ordinarily means people of Torah learning and legal knowledge. The better explanation is that Mechaber's reason for ruling against Ramah here is his acceptance of Rambam's principle, discussed above, that d'oraisa one judge is sufficient. Once one d'oraisa judge suffices, the rabbinic requirement of three exists only because of the concern for unlearned street-corner judges. It is therefore enough that one of the three be learned. Acceptance of testimony is different. D'oraisa it certainly requires three judges, for otherwise testimony would be testimony from the mouth of another witness rather than testimony before beit din. Since d'oraisa three are required, all three must be learned, in accordance with Ramah. Therefore Mechaber requires three experts for receiving testimony. From this it also appears that in common robbery and personal-injury matters which we adjudicate nowadays by acting as agents of ordained judges, all three judges should be learned. In those cases everyone agrees that d'oraisa three are required, since three occurrences of "elohim" are written in the relevant parashah. The rabbinic dispensation made them non-ordained, but did not remove the need for legal competence. Thus Ramah's requirement of three learned judges governs such cases. Even if, as I explained above in Rosh, according to Rava one learned judge can suffice in ordinary admissions and loans, Mechaber did not rely merely on Rosh for that rule. He relied on the broader halachah that one judge is d'oraisa valid in that category. That rationale does not apply to robberies and personal injuries.
