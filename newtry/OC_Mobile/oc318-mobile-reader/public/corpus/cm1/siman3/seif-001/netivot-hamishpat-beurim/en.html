A beit din is not fewer than three. Shach asks against Rambam and Sema, who write that by Torah law one judge suffices according to Rav Acha, while they also rule that two laymen who judged have no valid judgment and that Torah law requires 'gods,' meaning ordained judges. These are indeed serious questions. The principal answer is essentially that of Urim VeTumim, with the following clarification. Rambam holds that even Rava and the other Amoraim accept the verse 'with righteousness shall you judge your fellow,' which validates one judge in ordinary civil adjudication. Otherwise the Gemara should have asked what Rava does with that verse. Sanhedrin likewise explicitly teaches that a single expert may judge alone. The distinction is that the Torah's verses requiring ordained judges apply to matters that specifically require the status of beit din, such as a claim that a custodian stole, where the oath must be before beit din, and the partial-admission framework derived from 'this is it.' For such matters three ordained judges create the judicial status. The verse 'with righteousness shall you judge' concerns ordinary legal decision, which does not inherently require a formal beit din, just as a halachic ruling in other areas may be issued by one. Yet according to the view that 'before them' excludes laymen, the single judge must at least be an expert. Thus Rava can require an expert even though one judge can suffice for the adjudicative act. The Sages later permitted three laymen because of practical necessity, but two laymen remain invalid where the Torah still requires a qualified judge. This resolves Rambam's rule that a single public expert may judge yet is not a beit din for every purpose. Ketzot HaChoshen further says that before one judge a partial admission cannot generate an oath because the admission can be retracted. This cannot be correct. No decisor states such a rule, and where the admission is made in a binding form or supported by one witness the oath can certainly arise. His further claim that otherwise no deposit case would ever require beit din is likewise incorrect, because a claim of theft by a custodian specifically requires beit din. His suggestion that coercion of a debtor requires a formal beit din is also not compelling. Coercion to fulfill a mitzvah resembles compelling one who refuses to build a sukkah; every Jew has some responsibility to separate another from violation. See Bava Kamma concerning a Hebrew slave whose term ended. Whether three are required for final judgment will be discussed in siman 5.
