In the Tur. “It appears from his words that all three must be learned, but my father the Rosh,” etc. At first glance this is difficult, because the very formulation cited in the name of the Rosh is explicit in the Gemara's answer of Rav Acha son of Rav Ika at the beginning of Sanhedrin. He says that by Torah law one judge is valid. The Gemara asks that, if so, even with three judges we should decree because of unqualified marketplace judges, and answers that among three it is impossible that not even one knows the law. How, then, does the Ramah explain the Gemara? One cannot answer that the Ramah rules like Rava, who says that by Torah law three expert judges are required. First, even according to Rava there is no proof that he rejects the principle that among three there will certainly be one learned judge, or that one learned judge would not suffice because of the concern that borrowers not be discouraged. Second, the Gemara identifies the practical difference between Rava and Rav Acha as Shmuel's ruling that when two judges adjudicated, their judgment is valid, although they are called an insolent court. According to Rava, Shmuel's ruling is rejected; according to Rav Acha, it may be accepted. Why does the Gemara not say that they differ over whether all three must be learned? At minimum it should have mentioned this additional difference, which would be a significant novelty. Plainly, Rava's words do not establish that conclusion. It therefore appears to me that both the Ramah and the Rosh follow Rav Acha. One might object that Rav Acha can accept Shmuel, while the Rosh rules in the first chapter of Bava Kamma like Rabbi Abahu, that fewer than three judges produce no valid judgment, as Rabbeinu writes here. This is no difficulty. Rav Acha's answer can certainly be adopted even if Shmuel is rejected in favor of Rabbi Abahu, since rabbinic law in any event requires three judges. Rashi's explanation in the first chapter of Sanhedrin also indicates this. He writes that Shmuel calls two judges an insolent court because they transgressed the enactment of the Sages. Rav Acha may hold that it is not enough merely to call them insolent, but that their judgment is wholly invalid. When the Gemara says that Rav Acha can accept Shmuel, it means only that his reasoning is compatible with Shmuel, unlike Rava, who necessarily rejects him. A further proof is that Tosafot write that the Baal Halachot Gedolot rules like Shmuel, yet they do not cite Rav Acha as proof that Shmuel is correct. Evidently Rav Acha's words do not compel Shmuel's ruling. The Ramah and the Rosh disagree in their interpretation of Rav Acha. The Ramah explains the statement that among three it is impossible that not even one is learned as follows: Even if one of them is learned, the panel is still invalid. The Gemara had said that one judge does not suffice rabbinically because of the decree against unqualified marketplace judges. It then asked that the same decree should apply to three, and answered that among three unqualified men it is impossible that none possesses some knowledge of legal reasoning. The resulting defect is therefore not absolute but only a stringency, and the Sages did not impose a decree out of concern for such a case, since even if it occurred the defect would not be complete. Therefore, according to the Ramah, all three must actually be learned. The Rosh, however, holds that if none is learned the panel is invalid, and consequently if one is learned it is valid. He understands the Gemara's statement, that among three it is impossible that not even one is learned, to mean that the presence of one learned judge validates the panel.
