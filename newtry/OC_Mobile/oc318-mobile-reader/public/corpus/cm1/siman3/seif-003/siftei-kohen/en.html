Although, etc., it is a mitzvah of the Sages, etc. Responsa Maharshal, siman 35, rules that even where the litigants accepted him and he is an expert, he may not judge alone. He brings proofs. Responsa She'erit Yosef, siman 17, disputes him and rejects his proofs, and the Bach rules likewise. In truth, the incident in the Yerushalmi cited by the long Mordechai and Baal HaItur refutes Maharshal. It states explicitly that Rabbi Abahu judged alone. His students asked him how he could do so when the Mishnah says, 'Do not judge alone.' He answered that since the litigants came voluntarily, it was as though they had accepted him. This is even clearer in the Yerushalmi itself at the beginning of Sanhedrin. Nevertheless, regarding practice nowadays, when there is no fully qualified expert, the matter requires study. Perhaps Rabbi Abahu was different because he was an expert, and acceptance is effective only for an expert. The Yerushalmi itself indicates this when it interprets the Mishnah in Bekhorot, 'If one judged, what he did stands and he must pay from his own funds,' as a case where the parties said, 'You are accepted by us as three on condition that you judge us according to Torah law.' He must pay because he presumptuously undertook to judge Torah law, despite the teaching, 'Do not judge alone.' Baal HaItur cites this. One cannot distinguish the case on the ground that they expressly stipulated Torah law, because the Rosh and Tur write below in siman 25 that ordinary acceptance of a nonexpert is presumed to be for Torah law. Thus it may be that a nonexpert should not judge alone initially. Rash in the chapter Arba'ah Dinei Mamonot writes explicitly that since he undertook to judge and was not an expert, he should pay from his own funds, and although the parties accepted him, he should not have judged alone. Accordingly, those who customarily judge alone when litigants come voluntarily, or even when they expressly accept the judge, do not act properly, because nowadays there is no fully qualified expert. Perhaps those who do so deal only with common and straightforward matters in which they are experts. Alternatively, perhaps the very absence of full experts nowadays means that ordinary acceptance includes acceptance both for correct judgment and for error, similar to the Nimukei Yosef's statement that if parties accept unlearned judges, their judgment stands and they are not liable for error, since the parties cannot say they accepted them only to judge according to Torah law, as everyone knew they did not know Torah law. Yet this remains difficult, because most authorities do not cite the Yerushalmi's statement that nowadays no one is wise enough to judge Torah law. The Semag and Tur cite it only to teach that judges should make every effort to arrange a compromise. Their wording indicates that only initially should judges avoid accepting a case for strict Torah adjudication. Certainly, even nowadays judges know how to adjudicate many laws explicitly stated in the Gemara and authorities. Therefore, when litigants initially come before a judge and accept him, they presumably accept him to judge according to Torah law, as the authorities, Rosh, and Tur write below in siman 25. One should therefore be careful not to judge alone even when accepted, unless he expressly says, 'I do not know how to judge you according to Torah law,' or unless the matter is simple and he is expert and accustomed in it, for in such a case an expert exists even nowadays.
