An admission made before them. I was uncertain about a single expert judge who adjudicates a case and before whom the litigant admits part of the claim. Can that admission create the Torah oath of a partial admission? Since an admission before a single expert is not legally the formal admission of beit din, perhaps there is no partial admission. Or perhaps, so long as the litigant has not retracted, the statement can still be treated as an admission sufficient for the oath. Tosafot in Shevuot, discussing one who is sued for wheat and admits barley, suggests in one answer that even if Rabbi Gamliel agrees that the barley itself is not collectible, the statement may still count as an admission for purposes of the oath. Nevertheless, reason does not support creating an oath from a statement that is not legally an admission. Rashi explains that the principle that a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses is learned from "when he says, this is it," meaning that the Torah itself relied upon his partial admission. It follows that where the statement is not legally a binding admission, the category of partial admission does not apply. Tosafot's point concerning Rabbi Gamliel will be explained in siman 87. It therefore appears clear that an admission before a single expert does not create the Torah oath of partial admission. This also resolves Lechem Mishneh's question against Rambam. Rambam accepts the doctrine that the sections are intermingled, so the term "judges" also applies to loans, yet he rules that one judge is sufficient. The answer is that one learned individual can decide monetary law by Torah law, much as a scholar rules on prohibitions. Formal admission, however, requires beit din, and a Torah beit din for this purpose means three ordained judges. Thus the requirement of three experts in the loan section applies to the formal partial admission under "when he says, this is it," while the substantive judgment can be issued by one. The opening sugya in Sanhedrin can now be explained coherently. If the sections are intermingled, the fact that the custodial section also contains three judges even though custodial liability does not require partial admission proves that the substantive adjudication of robbery and injury matters itself needs three. By the principle of one law, the same would ordinarily extend to other judgments, unless a special verse authorizes one judge. If the sections are not intermingled, the three judges in the custodial section can be tied to the partial admission requirement and no proof remains that the body of judgment needs three. This also explains Rambam's statement that our courts outside Eretz Yisrael adjudicate admissions and loans as agents of the courts of Eretz Yisrael. Formal admissions and fixing a party as a false denier require a Torah beit din. Through agency, three lay judges may create those statuses. A single expert, however, does not. The bare monetary ruling itself may still be rendered by a single learned person. Kesef Mishneh's question from Rabbi Meir's comparison of disputes to leprosy cases is no difficulty, because Rabbi Meir himself holds that ordinary judgment requires three. We do not rule like him.
