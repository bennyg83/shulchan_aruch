Experts. This implies that if they are expert judges, even if fewer than three, an admission made before them constitutes a judicial admission. This is the view of the Rosh. Rambam, however, holds that any panel of fewer than three, even if its members are expert and formally ordained, does not render an admission before them a judicial admission. It is surprising that the Rama did not present this as a dispute with the Mechaber. One may answer, with difficulty, that by "an expert accepted by the public" the Rama means one whom the public formally accepted upon themselves, in which case the admission is effective. Thus concludes the Sema. The Shach disagrees. There is no sound basis to distinguish such a case, for even if the public accepted them, fewer than three still do not constitute a beit din. The reason the Rama did not formulate the matter as a dispute is that the Mechaber's own wording is not conclusive and may be understood as referring to ordained judges who are not expert. In practical law, however, even if they are ordained and expert, the admission is not a judicial admission, contrary to the Rama. This also appears to be the view of the Ir Shushan. See there.

The Shach disagrees and maintains that even if he said, "You are my witnesses," they have only the status of witnesses, not of a beit din. Thus he is not established as a false denier if he later says, "I paid you," as explained in siman 79, seif 9.
