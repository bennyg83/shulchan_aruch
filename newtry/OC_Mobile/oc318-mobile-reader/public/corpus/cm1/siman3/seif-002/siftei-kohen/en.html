Their judgment is invalid, etc. The Bach writes that regarding kiddushin one should be stringent for Shmuel's view that their judgment is valid. In my humble opinion, the primary rule is that we do not follow Shmuel, as ruled by the Rif, Rambam, Rosh, Tur, and many other authorities, and as demonstrated by the Gemara in Bava Kamma and Gittin, as I wrote above in seif katan 1. The same appears from Ketubot regarding validation of documents and from Sanhedrin regarding an acknowledgment. There the Gemara says, 'Perhaps the scholars of Rav Ashi's academy hold like Shmuel.' Tosafot's explanation that they follow Shmuel only concerning document validation and acknowledgments, while we do not follow him concerning judgment, is very strained. First, the Gemara's wording does not imply this. Second, Shmuel stated his ruling only about judgment. Third, why should three be required for validation and acknowledgment more than for judgment? Raavan, Baal HaItur, Nimukei Yosef, Semag, and Ritva in Ketubot also rule against Shmuel. This is the primary ruling.

And they are not publicly recognized experts, etc. The Sema writes that according to the Rambam even one expert, and even two experts, do not constitute a beit din. This is why the Mechaber concludes, 'even if they are ordained,' since ordained judges are ordinarily experts. He expresses surprise that Rama combined two opinions without presenting them as a dispute, as the Tur does. He suggests with difficulty that Rama's phrase, 'an expert recognized by the public,' means one whom the public formally accepted, in which case his authority is effective. This is not persuasive. Public acceptance does not make one person a beit din, the language does not imply it, and there is no source for such a rule. Rather, Rama intended the simple meaning. He did not present it as a dispute because the Mechaber's wording can be read as referring to ordained judges who are not experts. The Rosh and Tur hold in siman 25 that ordination can be effective even for one who is not an expert. Ir Shushan likewise writes that two, even if ordained, do not render a valid judgment, and immediately thereafter writes that one expert may judge coercively. By contrast, the Rambam's own language, cited by the Tur, explicitly includes even an expert, so the Tur presents the matter as a dispute. Rama follows this method in many places. Regarding practical law, the Tur's inference that the Rosh equates one expert with three lay judges for every purpose appears incorrect to me. The Rosh equates them only with respect to rendering judgment, not with respect to an acknowledgment. The Rambam and Mechaber are primary. Baal HaTerumot, Baal HaItur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Ritva, and Maharshal likewise indicate that where a matter requires a beit din, one expert, even the greatest of his generation, does not suffice. Thus an acknowledgment before a sole expert is not a judicial acknowledgment.
He is not established as one who denied an obligation. The Sema explains that this applies so long as he did not say before them, 'You are my witnesses.' It appears to me, however, that the case is even where he did say, 'You are my witnesses.' The ruling teaches that the two do not have the status of a beit din but only of witnesses. Therefore he is not established as a denier for the future, such that he could not later say, 'I repaid you,' as explained below in siman 79, seif 9. This is clear.
