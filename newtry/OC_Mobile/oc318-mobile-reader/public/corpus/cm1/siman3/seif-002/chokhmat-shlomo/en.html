Seif 2. With fewer than three judges, their judgment is not a valid judgment. Note: We learn in Sanhedrin 87b, regarding monetary law, that this depends upon the dispute between Shmuel and Rabbi Abahu. See Rashi there, beginning “in the dispute of Shmuel and Rabbi Abahu,” and his words, “since two judges removed the money,” etc. At first glance this is very difficult. Why should the matter depend on whether their decision has the force of a judgment? If the law is in truth with the prevailing litigant, then the money is his. What difference does it make that their judgment was invalid? Ultimately the money belongs to him and he has already acquired it. Conversely, if according to the true law the money is not his, whether because the judges ruled incorrectly or because he made a false claim and knows that he took it unlawfully, then even if their judgment is formally valid, the money is still not his and kiddushin effected with it should not take effect. At first I thought to explain that the case concerns shuda dedayanei, where the result depends upon the judges’ discretionary assessment. If their judgment is valid, the property belongs to the party whom they selected on the basis of their assessment; if their judgment is invalid, it does not become his, and other judges might assess the matter in the opposite way. In truth, however, this cannot be correct. Even according to the opinion that two judges who adjudicated have rendered a valid judgment, that applies to an adjudication of law, not to a matter dependent on the personal judgment of the judges. Such a matter is comparable to appraisal, for which all agree that three are required. In matters whose determination is explicitly legal one may distinguish between one judge and several, but where the outcome depends on personal assessment and judgment, one cannot rely upon an individual, and three are required. Thus, although we rule that a single expert judge has the status of three for adjudication, he does not suffice for an appraisal, which requires three. The same should be true according to the view that the judgment of two judges is valid. Moreover, if the Gemara meant only an appraisal or something similar, it could more naturally have spoken of compromise and connected the issue with the dispute of Rabbi Meir and the Sages in the first chapter concerning whether compromise may be effected by one or requires three. I then thought that the case might concern judges who erred in a matter of discretionary judgment, where the rule is that the judgment stands. This would indeed create a difference: if the judgment of two judges is valid, the judgment stands and the property belongs to the winner despite their error; if their judgment is invalid, the judgment is void and his possession is robbery. Yet on reflection this also does not suffice. The reason a judgment based on discretionary error stands is that, where the halachah has not been conclusively determined among the authorities, the judgment cannot readily be reversed. If the successful party has already taken possession, he may in any event say, “I maintain that the halachah follows this opinion,” even where it is a minority view against a majority, and he need not return the property. Even if one rejects that point and requires restitution, there is still no clear halachah, so the ruling could not produce the full liability of a rebellious elder, since perhaps the law follows the opinion that the property belongs to the successful party independently of this beit din. Why, then, should the rebellious elder incur liability merely because he ruled that the money belongs to that party? Even if his reason concerning the two judges is wrong, the same result might follow for another reason. If the judges erred in an explicit Mishnah, on the other hand, the judgment is reversed, so again it is difficult to find a case in which the winner’s title depends specifically upon the judges. It would seem that this sugya might support the rule that when one sells an item and the sale is later found to have been a mistaken transaction, if the seller meanwhile used the money to effect kiddushin, the kiddushin is valid because the money in his hand is treated as a loan rather than as stolen property, as discussed in Even HaEzer siman 28. According to this, if the decision of the two judges is a valid judgment, then even if they erred in an explicit Mishnah, the successful party received the money by authority of beit din. His receipt is therefore comparable to receipt under a mistaken transaction rather than intentional robbery, and the money remains in his hand as a loan pending restitution, so kiddushin effected with it is valid. But if their judgment is no judgment, he took it as outright robbery, and kiddushin with stolen property is invalid. In truth, however, this is not a necessary proof. The practical difference may instead be like that stated by the Shach in Choshen Mishpat siman 25, seif 4, concerning fruits. If the successful party went and effected kiddushin with the produce of the property that the two judges awarded him, then, if their judgment is valid, the produce is his. If their judgment is invalid, he has no right to the produce until a valid beit din awards the property to him, and the produce in his hand is therefore stolen. This appears correct to me at present; examine it carefully.
