Fewer than three have no authority, etc. Rambam writes likewise in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sanhedrin, excluding the Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot. The Tur further writes that one who received authorization from the Reish Galuta or the Nasi in Eretz Yisrael could judge alone even if learned but not especially skilled in reasoning. Since nowadays we have neither Nasi nor Reish Galuta from whom to receive such authority, the Mechaber did not elaborate and relied on the end of this siman.
Or he is an expert accepted by the public. The Tur describes such a person as one of the standing of Rav Nachman in his generation, proficient in Mishnah and Talmud, skilled in legal reasoning, who has studied judicial law for many years and repeatedly been tested without error. Such a person is called a public expert. 'Expert' here means tested and experienced, like an amulet established as reliable for Shabbos purposes.
Nowadays we do not judge, etc. The Tur writes similarly in Yoreh Deah siman 228 regarding release of vows by one expert and in siman 334 regarding payment of a litra of gold for humiliating a Torah scholar.
And they are not public experts. This implies that if they are experts, even fewer than three, an admission made before them has legal force. The Tur writes this in the name of the Rosh. Rambam, however, holds that an admission is not legally binding even before experts and ordained judges unless there are three. The Mechaber concludes, 'even if they are ordained,' which ordinarily means experts. Rambam expressly says this even of one who is a public expert. It is therefore surprising that Rama combines the two opinions rather than presenting them as a dispute. With difficulty one may say that Rama's phrase 'public expert' means a person whom the public formally appointed, in which case even Rambam may agree.
He is not established as a false denier. This applies so long as he did not say before them, 'You are my witnesses,' which is effective according to all views before ordinary witnesses, as explained in simanim 32, 81, and 79. Even without that phrase, where he used the language of a full and formal admission, Rambam holds that it is effective. The present case concerns an informal statement that did not constitute a full admission.
