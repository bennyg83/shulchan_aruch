Fewer than three, etc. In Urim I cited Bach, who writes that in matters of kiddushin one should be stringent and take account of authorities who follow Shmuel. Shach objects that most poskim rule like R. Abahu and against Shmuel. He is correct as a matter of ordinary monetary halachah, but nevertheless stringency is appropriate for kiddushin. Rashba in a responsum, as cited by Kesef Mishneh, explains Rambam's rulings by saying that even R. Acha can agree with R. Abahu. D'oraisa one judge is valid, but Chazal required three in an indispensable manner, so that if fewer than three judged, their judgment is invalid. According to that approach, Rambam's ruling means that the judgment is valid d'oraisa and invalid only rabbinically. The money therefore belongs to the party awarded it on the Torah level, and holding it contrary to the rabbinic ruling constitutes only rabbinic theft. In matters of kiddushin, rabbinic theft is insufficient to eliminate concern for valid kiddushin, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 28. The Gemara in Sanhedrin says that one practical difference between the dispute of R. Abahu and Shmuel can concern a matter whose intentional violation incurs karet. That can be understood exactly this way, such as a man who betrothed a woman with money awarded by two judges. One cannot object that according to everyone the judgment is d'oraisa valid. The statement in the Gemara is made by Rava, and Rava does not follow R. Acha; according to Rava, d'oraisa three are required. But according to our halachic ruling like R. Acha that one judge can be valid d'oraisa, there remains a genuine concern for kiddushin even though rabbinically the judgment of fewer than three is invalid.
Even if they are ordained. Rambam writes that an admission before a single expert judge is not considered a judicial admission. Sema, following his own approach, understands that the same applies before two experts: an admission is not a binding beit din admission until made before three. In my view this is incorrect. There is a real distinction between one expert and two, and both logic and the Gemara compel it. As a matter of credibility, the rule is that two witnesses are like one hundred. Why then should an admission before two not be effective when one before three is? The answer cannot lie in evidentiary reliability. Rather, two ordinary non-ordained judges are not themselves a beit din. An admission before them therefore lacks the legal force of a judicial admission and can be retracted; it can be treated as mere conversation. But two ordained experts are different. Each of them is independently qualified to judge. Certainly two together constitute a beit din no less than three for the purpose of receiving an admission. There is then no distinction between two and three either in credibility, because two are like one hundred, or in judicial status, because the two experts can adjudicate. The Gemara also proves this. The poskim who reject Shmuel derive their conclusion, as Rosh explains, from the passage concerning a written admission, where the Gemara asks, "Perhaps he holds like Shmuel." This shows that Shmuel's rule that two who judged have a valid judgment would also make an admission before two a judicial admission. These poskim do not accept Tosafot's distinction between adjudication and admission there. Accordingly, from Shmuel we can learn the rule for experts under R. Abahu. Just as Shmuel treats two ordinary judges as a beit din and therefore an admission before them as a beit din admission, R. Abahu should agree that two ordained experts, whose judgment is unquestionably valid, likewise constitute a beit din for admission purposes. One cannot reintroduce a distinction between judgment and admission, because the entire proof against Shmuel used by these authorities depends on rejecting that distinction. Therefore Rosh and Rambam disagree only regarding an admission before one expert, as Rambam expressly says. Rambam holds that before one expert the defendant could still deny the event itself and say, "You speak falsely," because a single witness is not believed in monetary matters even if he is as great as Yehoshua bin Nun. Taz says this explicitly. Therefore even if the defendant presently does not deny it, his statement before one judge is not elevated to the status of a beit din admission; it remains something less formal. Rosh agrees that the defendant could deny the judge's report, but so long as he does not deny it, the admission itself is binding because the person before whom it was made has judicial authority. This also answers Shach's question on Rosh from the case of a written admission before two, where the document says, "Rav Ashi told us that he joins with them." According to Rosh, why were the other two needed at all if Rav Ashi alone was an expert? The answer is that even according to Rosh, a single judge can be contradicted as to what occurred. Therefore the others were needed as witnesses so the debtor could not say, "This never happened." Strictly speaking one additional witness alongside Rav Ashi would have sufficed. It happened that two were present, and no inference can be drawn from the extra person. This also resolves Rama's wording, which implies that if the judges were experts of the public, the admission can be effective. There is no contradiction with Mechaber's statement that even ordained judges do not help, because Rama can be speaking of two experts, while Rambam and Mechaber speak of a single expert. Sema initially considered this distinction but rejected it as implausible. According to what I have explained, the distinction is both correct and compelled. Therefore Shach's ruling regarding Rambam is correct for an admission before one expert. But before two experts there is no dispute at all. According to everyone it is a judicial admission. This avoids multiplying disputes among the poskim without necessity.
