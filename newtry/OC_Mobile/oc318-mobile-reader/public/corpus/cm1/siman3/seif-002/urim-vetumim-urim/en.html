Fewer than three, etc. Bach writes that with respect to kiddushin one should be stringent and take into account the authorities who rule like Shmuel. Thus, if two judges adjudicated a monetary case, transferred money from one litigant to the other, and the recipient then betrothed a woman with that money, Sanhedrin 6b indicates that according to Rabbi Abahu the kiddushin is ineffective because the money never legally became his, while according to Shmuel their judgment is valid and the kiddushin takes effect. In such a case Bach requires a get out of doubt. Shach disagrees, but see Tumim, where I concluded that Bach's stringency is proper.
And they are not experts, etc. Sema asks why the Rama did not introduce this as a dissenting view. The Tur holds that an admission before an expert can have judicial force, whereas the Mechaber follows Rambam, who says that even ordination in Eretz Yisrael does not necessarily give a single judge that force. Sema answers that a judge accepted by the public may be stronger than one merely ordained. Shach objects that public acceptance should not exceed ordination. The better explanation is that Rambam says specifically that an admission before one expert judge does not have the full force of an admission before beit din. But where two experts actually sit and adjudicate, even Rambam can agree that an admission before them is effective. That is the Rama's case. See Tumim.
He has not been established as a denier. Meaning, he has not been established as a liar before witnesses. But if he made the statement with "you are witnesses" or in a form of formal admission that, according to Rambam below in siman 81, he cannot later deny, then he is established as a denier before these judges just as before witnesses. Sema. Even if witnesses established his prior denial, he remains believed regarding future repayment. By contrast, if the admission was made before a full beit din and he was thereby established as a denier, the later rules of siman 79 apply. Shach.
Although, etc. The Sages taught that a single judge should preferably seat others with him. If the parties come voluntarily without coercion, some authorities say this is as though they accepted him and he may adjudicate alone. She'erit Yosef and Bach rule this way, and Taz reports that his grandfather, a great scholar, practiced accordingly, against Maharshal. Their proof is from the Yerushalmi, which records Rabbi Abahu judging alone and answering the objection, "Do not be a lone judge," by saying that since the litigants came of their own accord it was as though they accepted him. Shach correctly adds that ordinary acceptance normally means acceptance to adjudicate according to Torah law. Therefore, a litigant's simple voluntary appearance does not necessarily authorize a lone judge unless the judge states that he cannot give them the same procedural framework as a full court, or the issue is exceptionally simple and clear. In Metz the communal regulation provided that claims below twenty five Reichstaler could be adjudicated by the av beit din alone in order to avoid multiplying judges' fees. In my view this is valid because the community accepted the regulation. Nevertheless, unless the issue is completely straightforward, I personally avoid judging alone and join other judges, so that responsibility for a possible error is shared. May Hashem protect us from mistakes.
