(And if they customarily build a light partition). These words astonish me both regarding their source, the Ra'ah in Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Batra, and regarding Rama's formulation. The Ra'ah writes that where the custom is to build a stone wall and one neighbor says, “I will build within my own property, using brick,” the other may object, saying, “I want a wall upon which I can place supports.” Although the builder offers permission to rely upon it, the other may say, “I do not wish to accept gifts,” as in siman 171. This is astonishing. In siman 171 the case is a courtyard too small for compulsory division, namely four cubits, where one wishes to divide and give the other four cubits. The other necessarily needs to use the full four cubits and may say, “I do not wish to benefit from my fellow's gift; I will therefore refuse division and continue using it jointly.” Here, however, the only duty is to remove visual intrusion. Once one builds on his own property and removes the intrusion with a stone wall, who can object? The assertion that the other wants a wall on which to support beams is exceedingly difficult. From where did he acquire such a right? The Sages required both parties to build a partition to prevent visual intrusion, not to provide the benefit of supporting beams. Consider a case where one neighbor leaves his courtyard and does not wish to use it at all. Can the other compel him to join in building a partition? The same applies here. Once he constructs a separating wall and removes the visual intrusion, it is as though he no longer resides there. I am also astonished at Rama. The Ra'ah stated this only where the custom is a stone wall and one party seeks the customary wall so that he can support upon it. Rama, however, wrote it where the custom is to make a light partition, which is unfit for supporting anything. Why should the other prevent him from building at his own expense? This is nothing but astonishing.
