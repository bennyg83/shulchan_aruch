Terumat HaDeshen discusses two partners who divided a house. A stone wall stood between their portions, but the stones on Reuven's side were more valuable dressed stones than those on Shimon's side. Reuven claimed the valuable stones because they were physically on his side. Terumat HaDeshen rejected the claim. He reasoned that even if the better side lies closer to Reuven, one can ask why the thickness of the wall should be divided vertically rather than crosswise. The analogy is to a gold embroidered garment jointly held by two people, or a tree standing on a boundary, where proximity of the valuable part to one person does not itself determine ownership. Terumat HaDeshen notes a possible distinction. A garment or tree is plainly a single jointly owned object, so the accidental location of the better portion proves little. With a wall, perhaps each party's side should presumptively belong to him because that is the side he uses. He ultimately proves equal ownership from the Mishnah concerning a house adjoining idolatrous property. When a wall belongs jointly to a Jew and idolatrous property and then falls, its stones can be prohibited because there is no clear allocation. This suggests that all stones on both sides are jointly owned rather than each owner automatically owning the stones facing him. The proof from the gold garment is not conclusive. Where two parties actually grasp different portions of a garment, each takes what is physically in his hand. The equal division rule applies where they hold only the fringes. Here the land beneath the wall is physically divided, and a courtyard is like one's hand. There is therefore reason to say the stones on each side should follow the land beneath them. The proof from the idolatry Mishnah is stronger, but even that depends on how the early authorities understand the wall, as discussed in the next seif katan.
Although Rashi's language in the Mishnah concerning a wall shared with idolatrous property supports Terumat HaDeshen's proof, Rambam appears to rule differently. Rambam says that when the wall is half Jewish property and half idolatrous property, the half facing the Jew is permitted. This follows the Jerusalem Talmud and is codified by Tur and the Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah. Ran explains that the two approaches are not necessarily contradictory. Rashi understands the entire joint wall as a partnership in every stone, and therefore even after physical division the prohibited part cannot be identified because there is no retroactive clarification. The Jerusalem Talmud, however, can be read to say that half the wall is permitted when the Jew recognizes the stones corresponding to his side. Even though ordinary partners share throughout the wall, they are presumed to have entered the partnership on the understanding that, upon division, the side facing each would become his entirely. That mutual arrangement benefits both sides. Ran's explanation therefore actually supports Terumat HaDeshen's broader point that, before an effective division, each partner has a half interest throughout the wall. Rosh also writes that one who establishes a right to place beams on another's wall can, after the requisite possession, acquire half the entire wall, which again suggests a half interest throughout rather than exclusive ownership of the stones physically on one's own side. Bach reads the dispute about raising a wall above four cubits differently. He says the opinion allowing one neighbor to raise the entire wall assumes each partner owns an undivided half throughout its length and width. Rosh, who lets the other neighbor object, supposedly holds that each owns only the half width facing his property. I do not think that is correct. Rosh too can accept that each has an undivided half in the stones. The objection to building higher can rest on ownership of the air above one's own strip of land from the depths to the heavens. Each owner can insist on preserving the airspace above his half of the land even if the stones themselves are jointly owned. This is the more plausible reading.
Nimukei Yosef says that when one neighbor later agrees to participate in the upper portion of a wall above four cubits, his courtyard automatically acquires half the wall because the wall stands partly on his land. Maggid Mishneh uses the same reasoning: upon payment of half the expense, the neighbor acquires half the wall because the place belongs to both and his courtyard acquires for him. This creates difficulty if one assumes each partner owns an undivided half throughout the whole thickness. How can the courtyard acquire the half of the wall resting on the other neighbor's land? His own courtyard reaches only the stones above his own half of the soil. At most it would acquire half of that half. One can answer from the law of partners who rent a place and place their respective vessels there. Once the underlying place is held in partnership, the property placed there for partnership can be acquired as joint property even though each object is not physically on every part of the courtyard. The original wall up to four cubits is already partnership property. The later upper wall can therefore be acquired into the existing partnership when the second neighbor manifests willingness to participate. Still, Maggid Mishneh's language seems to suggest something more concrete: the courtyard itself acquires half because it is built on his share of the land. This points toward treating the wall as divided by the ground beneath it. The Jerusalem Talmud records a dispute concerning a wall between courtyards. One view assigns half to each side, so even a found object in each half belongs to the corresponding owner. The other treats the whole wall as undivided partnership property, so whoever first takes a found object acquires it. The first view fits the idea that each owner has a defined half of the wall corresponding to his land. The second fits an undivided courtyard partnership. Maggid Mishneh appears closer to the first view, whereas Terumat HaDeshen and Ran's ordinary partnership analysis fits the second. The issue therefore remains conceptually divided among the sources.
