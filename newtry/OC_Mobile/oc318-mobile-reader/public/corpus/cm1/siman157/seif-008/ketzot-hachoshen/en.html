If one neighbor claims he already paid his share of rebuilding the wall, the Mishnah says he is presumed to have paid up to four cubits. The Gemara, according to the view that visual harm is not ordinarily actionable, explains the special case as a wall that had fallen after the neighbors had previously agreed to build. Rashi explains that their agreement had been formalized by a kinyan. This raises a problem. A monetary obligation fixed by kinyan resembles a written obligation, and one normally cannot say, "I paid," against such an obligation without proof. If someone accepted by kinyan to support another for ten years, he could not ordinarily defeat that obligation merely by alleging payment. Why then is the neighbor presumed to have paid his half of each row? Tosafot avoids the difficulty because they explain the fallen wall case by the fact that the parties had become accustomed to privacy, not by a prior kinyan. According to Rashi, the answer follows Rosh's rule that a kinyan "to build" is not a mere acquisition of words. The kinyan creates an enforceable duty to perform construction, but after one neighbor actually builds the wall with his own materials and seeks reimbursement, the original construction duty has been converted into an ordinary oral monetary debt for expenditures. Rivash states a parallel rule. If a person accepts by kinyan to deliver a specific object, the kinyan prevents denial of the duty while the object exists. But if the specific object is later gone and the claim has become only for its monetary value, the resulting claim can have the law of an oral debt and the defendant can say he paid. So too here. Before construction, the duty to build remains fixed by the kinyan and cannot be evaded by an ordinary payment claim. After the wall has been built from one neighbor's funds, the only remaining claim is reimbursement of half the expenditure. That is an oral debt and the defendant can say he paid. This resolves Rashi.
