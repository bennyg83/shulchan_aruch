A courtyard wall, etc., that fell. This is also the Mishnah's wording. It states that the wall fell to teach that even if the original dividing wall was higher than four amot, when it falls one partner can compel the other to rebuild only up to four amot.
They compel him to pay his share for two additional amot, etc. This applies only to the length of wall he built opposite him. If the dividing wall is twenty amot long, while the wall he built opposite it is only ten amot long and six amot high, he need contribute to the extra height only for those ten amot, not the remaining length. The Tur states this explicitly.
Likewise, if he cut grooves into the top of the wall between them. This is Rambam's wording. According to it, perhaps even without building a corresponding wall in his own courtyard, he must pay for the entire additional height. The Tur, however, says 'if he cut grooves into its top,' referring to the wall he himself built opposite the partition, and Rashi explains the Gemara likewise. Rama's gloss about length also concerns the wall he supported against the first. Perhaps they do not disagree in practical law. Still, where the grooves are in the dividing wall itself, we must know that the defendant personally cut them. Otherwise he can say that the owner who raised the wall cut them without his knowledge so that the wall would not later be damaged when they agreed to build an upper story on it.
For all six amot. By cutting places for beams into the wall he shows that he intends to raise his own corresponding wall to the full height of the dividing wall. So writes the Tur.
Even though he did not build the entire wall. It appears this means that the defendant built only the lower four amot of the dividing wall, not the two extra amot his fellow added, and also built no corresponding wall in his own courtyard.
He is liable for the entire addition, etc. This applies specifically to the length of the wall. It is not by itself an indication obligating him for height beyond four amot. See the Tur.
