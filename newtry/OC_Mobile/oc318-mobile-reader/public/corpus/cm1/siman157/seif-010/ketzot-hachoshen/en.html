Rama says one may at least build on one's own share of the wall. Darkei Moshe cites Nimukei Yosef. In my view Nimukei Yosef actually indicates that one partner may build across the entire width of the joint wall, not merely upon the half facing his own courtyard. Nimukei Yosef distinguishes a jointly owned courtyard wall from a house and upper story. The owner of an upper story has only a servitude over the house below. If the house was subjected to a limited load, he may not increase it. A wall between two courtyards is different. The soil and stones belong to both. Each partner may build higher and add weight wherever he wishes, as one builds within his own property, so long as expert builders say the structure will not be damaged. The Mishnah says only that one neighbor cannot compel the other to participate above four cubits. It does not say the other may prevent him from building. Darkei Moshe understood "he builds in his own" as meaning only on the half of the wall situated on his own land. But Nimukei Yosef's proof shows otherwise. He says that if the other interpretation were correct, it would be obvious that one could prevent building on the other's side, and the Mishnah would not need to say merely that he cannot be compelled to build. The implication is that one cannot prevent unilateral construction even over the entire width. Rashba states this explicitly in a responsum. A jointly owned wall is not merely a servitude. Each partner built together in his own and in the other's, and therefore either may build higher as he wishes, provided he does not impose such excessive weight that experts say the building will be damaged. Thus Nimukei Yosef follows the opinion cited in Tur that a partner may raise the wall across its full width. Rama's formulation limiting him to his share does not reflect Nimukei Yosef's actual position.
