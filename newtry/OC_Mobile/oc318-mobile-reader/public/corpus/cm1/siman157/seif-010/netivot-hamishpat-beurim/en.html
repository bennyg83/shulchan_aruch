This seems difficult in light of siman 158, where a mere indication of intent does not always obligate the enclosed owner for the full expense, and where if the fourth side was fenced only with reeds he pays only the cost of reeds. Here, however, the case is one where it is evident that he intends to build and support a ceiling on the shared wall. In that case he clearly seeks to acquire a half interest in the shared wall, because without acquiring such a right he may not place his ceiling upon another's wall. By contrast, one enclosed by another's fences seeks only to benefit from the neighbor's walls, not to use them as his own property, and therefore pays only for the benefit received. This follows the language of Nimukei Yosef, Ramban, and the Yerushalmi, which make the obligation depend on his desire to rest a ceiling on the wall. Accordingly, if it is clear that he does not intend to support a ceiling there, he pays only according to the benefit. Thus, if he built only a wooden wall opposite it, he pays only the value of wood. Rama's concluding rule likewise says that where one benefits from another's building without any other sign, he pays according to the benefit. Mordechai applies this even where one adjoins his house to another's wall. Although ordinarily one who benefits while the other suffers no loss is exempt, here the adjoining structure causes some deterioration of the wall, because the ordinary rationale that an inhabited house preserves itself does not apply to an external wall that the owner continues using from inside.
