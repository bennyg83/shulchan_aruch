Shach says that a protruding marker can serve as evidence of who built a wall, contrary to Beit Yosef. In my view Tosafot does not prove that such a marker is ineffective in a courtyard. The reason a marker is normally ignored in a courtyard is only after we know the law compels both neighbors to build. Since unilateral construction is then unusual, a marker can be dismissed as having been made deceptively. Without the compulsory building rule, a courtyard marker should have the same evidentiary force as in an open field. This is precisely why Tosafot could ask what novelty follows from the word "therefore." If there were no compulsory joint building rule, ordinary uncertainty and the absence or presence of a marker would determine the parties' shares just as in an open field. The compulsory rule itself makes the marker unreliable. Thus Tosafot does not show that a marker is intrinsically meaningless in a courtyard. The same applies according to the view that visual intrusion is not legally cognizable and that "partition" in the Mishnah means the physical wall. On that view there is no compulsory joint wall merely from visual harm, and a marker in a courtyard can again be meaningful. Tosafot's question remains understandable precisely because in that framework a courtyard and an open field are comparable.
