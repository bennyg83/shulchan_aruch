Tur says that even if the defendant claims he paid before completion of the wall, he can be believed, because each row has its own due time. Commentators ask why the special due date reasoning is needed. He should have a migo that he could have said, "I paid after the due date." Bach answers that the person who built the wall is the possessor because everyone knows he alone physically constructed it. The other neighbor, who wishes to use the wall and claims he already contributed, is seeking to extract an ownership share from the builder and therefore bears the burden of proof. On that view the due date reasoning is necessary. Sema's approach is more convincing. The builder is not the possessor of the other half of the wall once the second neighbor is prepared to pay. Maggid Mishneh and Nimukei Yosef explain that the second neighbor's courtyard acquires his half of the wall, and Rama rules similarly. The builder is therefore left only with a claim for reimbursement. The defendant is the monetary possessor and can rely on a payment plea. Rashba's responsum makes this explicit. He rejects an opinion that a land purchaser who cannot prove payment is presumed not to have paid. Rashba proves from the wall above four cubits that once a buyer agreed to pay for a share and the time for payment arrived, he can say he paid. The land or wall is already acquired, and the unpaid price becomes an ordinary oral debt. The defendant therefore remains the possessor of his money. The same logic applies below four cubits. The neighbor's courtyard acquires his wall share as the wall is constructed. The dispute is then only over money. He can therefore say he paid, including by a migo from a post maturity payment plea. Sema is correct.
