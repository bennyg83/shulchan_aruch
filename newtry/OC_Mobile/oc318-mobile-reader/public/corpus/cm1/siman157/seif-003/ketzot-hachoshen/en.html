Tosafot asks why the Mishnah needs the word "therefore" when it says that if the courtyard wall falls, its place and stones belong to both parties. Even without a compulsory building duty, if neither side had made a distinguishing marker the wall would ordinarily be treated as jointly owned. Tosafot explains that the novelty concerns a case where the stones fell into one party's possession and remained there unusually long. One might think that he should then be believed to say he built the whole wall, through a migo that he could have said he bought the other half. The Mishnah teaches that because both were legally obligated to build, such a migo is against the established circumstance and fails. Tosafot adds that the case is unlike ordinary disputed property where the rule might be whoever is stronger. Here both parties stand adjacent to and are effectively holding the wall. The uncertainty therefore resembles two people holding a garment. I find Tosafot's characterization in terms of an inherent monetary doubt difficult, because by their own definition such a doubt exists where beit din would be uncertain even without the parties' claims. Here the doubt arises only because of the claims, just as with two people holding a garment. The cleaner distinction from an unheld boat is simply that both parties are effectively possessors of the wall, since each stands beside it, much as two people sitting beside a pile are treated as jointly holding it. Rivash uses that exact reasoning concerning a roof spanning two houses. Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash explains the Gemara differently. Once the Mishnah has already taught that both parties are obligated to build, it is obvious that the wall is joint. The novelty is that even when the stones fell into one party's domain, he cannot claim exclusive ownership merely from possession. Partners are not ordinarily strict with one another about temporary possession. His language suggests this remains true even after an unusually long time. Rif similarly says that property known to be partnership property is divided equally. On that approach, Tosafot's special long possession migo is unnecessary. There is then a different explanation of "therefore." Without the legal duty to build jointly, an uncertain wall might be divided according to the ordinary rules of joint possession, and if one party admitted only half the other would receive only the appropriate quarter, as in the opening Mishnah of Bava Metzia. Once the law establishes that both had to build, each has a definite half right. Even if one says, "All is mine," and the other says, "Half is mine," the latter still receives his full half. Alternatively, following Rashi's approach that where one of two claimants is certainly lying the item may be left until Elijah comes, the compulsory joint building rule supplies the certainty that both are owners and therefore justifies division. Ramban raises a further question. If the rule that both must build is what makes later possession ineffective, why does the Gemara concerning an open field ask that neither party make a marker? Without markers, one might think the same joint rule should apply. Ramban answers that in the courtyard no marker system was enacted because joint building is the legal norm. In an open field, where joint building is not compulsory, distinguishing markers are needed. Once the later conclusion requires markers even when both build, the absence of a marker can no longer itself prove that one did not build. This explains why possession after collapse can become significant there. Accordingly, once the Talmudic marker system is taken into account, even the open field can allow the present possessor to prevail in some circumstances, while the courtyard remains governed by the fixed joint building duty.
