Nimukei Yosef, in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, explains that once he fixed the structure permanently, his liability became clear and the rationale of 'who says the Sages will obligate me' no longer applies. This is likewise the reason for the rule below that if he consented to raising the wall, his liability is clear and that rationale no longer applies. There is, however, a difficulty in the sugya in the first chapter of Bava Batra 5a. The Gemara challenges Abaye and Rava, who hold that a person may pay within the term, from the latter clause of the Mishnah: for a wall above four cubits, he is presumed not to have paid. If the case were after the due date, why should he not be believed? It must therefore be before the due date, showing that a person does not pay within the term. The Gemara answers that this case is different because he says, 'Who says the Sages will obligate me?' Yet the principal obligation for the wall appears to arise when he places his roof upon it, as Rashi explains on 6a concerning Rav Huna's statement that if he built an adjoining wall up to half the height, he is deemed to have accepted the whole, because he will ultimately finish the wall and place the roof upon it. If so, once he knows that when he places the roof he will certainly become liable, why, according to Abaye and Rava, should he not pay in advance in order not to be troubled afterward? According to Nimukei Yosef, however, who asks why he must pay for the whole wall when perhaps he will never place a roof upon it and answers that once this wall serves as a partition for his house he is liable, one can explain that the liability does not arise from placing the roof but from its serving as a partition. In that respect the legal result is not obvious, and he can say, 'Who says the Sages will obligate me?' One cannot answer that he intended eventually to place the roof and therefore knew his liability with certainty and paid early, because the Yerushalmi records a dispute whether he is permitted to place the roof, as cited in Ramban's novellae. Abaye and Rava may hold that he cannot do so and that his neighbor may object. His liability therefore rests only on the wall's serving as a partition, which is not an obvious rule.
We, however, rule like the Yerushalmi view that the neighbor cannot object to his placing the roof. Accordingly, where he built another wall alongside, he is not believed to say he already paid. Before he placed the roof, although he was already liable because the wall served as a partition, that obligation was not obvious and he could say, 'Who says the Sages will obligate me?' If he says he intended to place the roof, then since we rule that a person does not ordinarily pay before the due date, he should not have paid before the later obligation matured through placing the roof.
This also resolves Rambam's ruling that the claimant takes a stringent oath and collects, against Ramah's objection. Ramah inferred from the Gemara that the rationale of 'who says' is stronger than the presumption against early payment, because Abaye and Rava reject the latter presumption and nevertheless agree that 'who says' creates a presumption of nonpayment. Since we rule that within the term the claimant collects without an oath, all the more so, Ramah argues, where 'who says' applies he should collect without an oath. According to what I wrote, however, under our ruling that the roof may be placed without the neighbor's objection, the defendant's lack of credibility does not rest upon 'who says' alone, for his eventual liability through the roof is certain. Nor can the presumption against early payment alone suffice, because even before the roof was placed he was already liable due to the partition, only that liability was not obvious. His liability therefore rests on the combination of both considerations: people do not ordinarily pay before the due date, and as to the present liability he could say, 'Who says the Sages will obligate me?' Since neither presumption independently suffices, their combined force is weaker, and the claimant must therefore swear. Where the ordinary presumption against early payment alone suffices, it is strong enough that no oath is needed. Likewise, if 'who says' alone would suffice, the claimant would not need an oath because it is even stronger than the ordinary within-term presumption, as Ramah states. Here, however, the claimant succeeds only through the combination of both, and therefore must swear. This is precise.
