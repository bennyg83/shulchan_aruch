Ramban holds that even above four cubits a unilateral marker does not prove exclusive ownership. He explains that below four cubits the place and stones are certainly owned by both because each can compel the other to build. Therefore, if one side alone has a marker, we assume he fabricated it. Otherwise both sides would need markers to prevent one party from later claiming the whole. One might ask that above four cubits there is no compulsory building duty. Why not believe the party whose side bears the marker regarding the upper wall? Ramban answers that once the ground and lower wall are definitely held jointly, one who claims the upper stones as exclusively his is seeking to remove something from the other's established possession. A marker is not complete proof, because it can be fabricated. Moreover, the Sages never established a marker for half a wall in the way they did for a separate open field wall. Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash similarly says a marker does not help in a courtyard. Rosh questions this and says that if a local custom uses clear interior windows or another reliable sign of ownership, and witnesses saw the sign made during construction, such a reliable sign should work. Rosh argues that even a marker in an open field can theoretically be imitated by entering the neighbor's field, yet it still has force. All the more a sign not susceptible to deception should work. Ramban's deeper point is that the ordinary protruding marker itself is weak, and once definite joint ownership of the lower wall is established it cannot dislodge the other party's possession. A different sign that is truly reliable and not subject to deception can still be effective. Thus even under Ramban, the issue is not that no evidence can ever prove upper ownership, but that the standard protruding marker is insufficient against an existing joint presumption.
