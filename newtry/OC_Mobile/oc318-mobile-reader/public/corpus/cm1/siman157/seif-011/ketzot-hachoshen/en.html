Where one neighbor benefits from the other's construction, Tosafot and Mordechai write that if his conduct reveals that the expenditure was desirable to him, he must pay even in a case where one benefits and the other suffers no loss. In Bava Kamma Tosafot initially seeks to prove the general exemption of one who benefits without causing loss from the Mishnah of one who surrounds another's field on three sides. Rabbi Yosei says that if the enclosed owner later builds the fourth side, the entire expense can be apportioned to him. Tosafot asks: if benefit without loss is exempt, why should his later construction matter? They answer that by building the fourth side he revealed that the expense was desirable to him. This differs from living in another's unused courtyard, where the occupant reveals only that he wants free use, not that he accepts the expense. Tosafot in Bava Batra applies the same idea to one who places a ceiling upon the wall above four cubits. Even though the builder may have suffered no additional monetary loss from the other's use, the user's conduct reveals that the construction was desirable to him and he must share the expense. Tosafot offers an alternative explanation that the builder did suffer loss because he raised the wall in order to remove visual harm completely. Maharshal challenges the first Tosafot approach. He says one cannot derive a new rule from an initial Talmudic assumption that is rejected in the conclusion. Once the Gemara explains the enclosure case through the additional expense caused to the outer owner, the proof that revealed desire alone creates liability disappears. He also notes that Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov contemplated charging the surrounding owner himself even in a benefit without loss case, which shows the initial passage cannot settle the broader law. Maharshal therefore concludes that benefit without loss remains exempt even where the beneficiary reveals that the expenditure pleased him, unless he actually caused the owner some loss. He notes that Rosh did not adopt Tosafot's distinction and treats actual loss as necessary. See my discussion in siman 158, seif katan 7, where I address Tosafot's reasoning.
