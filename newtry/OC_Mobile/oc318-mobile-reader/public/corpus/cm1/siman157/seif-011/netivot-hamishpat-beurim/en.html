He swears while holding a sacred object. See Shach and Taz, who question this ruling. The primary answer is that of Tumim, though he elaborates more than necessary. I will state it briefly with some change of wording. In the issue expressed as, "Who says the Sages will obligate me?" the law follows Rashi as understood by the Rosh in Bava Metzia 17. When beit din says, "You are obligated to give him," before the decision is fully settled, their position resembles uncertainty, which is exactly analogous to the argument, "Who says the Sages will obligate me?" Even so, Rashi and Rashba write that the defendant swears while holding a sacred object. This proves that the presumption against payment is weaker there than during a fixed term. The reason is that even where liability has not yet been formally imposed, a person who himself knows the law clearly may pay in advance because he wishes to avoid the embarrassment of a beit din judgment. Therefore an oath is required. According to Abaye and Rava, who hold that a person may sometimes pay even within the term, a fixed term is still a stronger presumption than this unresolved judicial stage. In the case of one who attached a wall to his neighbor's, however, there is no unexpired term at all. Each stage of attachment is immediately due with respect to the portion attached, so the ordinary term presumption does not apply according to anyone.
