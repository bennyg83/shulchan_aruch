The Mishnah teaches: “Therefore, if the wall fell, the site and stones belong to both.” Rashi explains that since at the outset the Sages obligated both to build, if the wall falls after many years the one into whose domain it fell cannot say, “I built it.” Tosafot explain, according to the Gemara's conclusion, that the Mishnah teaches a novelty even though the stones remained a long time in his possession. Although partners generally do not insist upon immediate removal from one another, here the delay greatly exceeds the normal conduct of partners. Nevertheless he cannot claim that he built the entire wall, because we are witnesses, as it were, that both built it, since both were obligated. The Rif, however, gives only the reason that partners generally do not insist upon one another. The Maggid Mishneh writes that according to the Rif the Mishnah concerns stones seen in his possession and identified by witnesses, for otherwise he would be believed through a migo that he could have claimed, “I returned them.” The Maggid Mishneh thus makes two approaches. According to Tosafot, no migo is accepted even after a long delay and even when the stones are not identified. According to the Rif, a migo is accepted after a long delay, unless the stones were seen and identified, leaving no migo of return. It appears obvious to me that Tosafot reject the migo only for the claim “I built it,” because our virtual testimony makes this a migo against witnesses. For a claim that he purchased the stones, however, a migo is effective unless the circumstances eliminate it, as under the Rif's approach. A difficulty nevertheless arises according to the Rosh, cited by the Tur, who rules that where the custom is a stone wall and one says, “I will build on my property of branches and reeds,” the law follows him. Why, then, can the possessor not claim that he alone built the entire stone wall? Although both initially bore an obligation, he could say: “When the wall was built, I wanted only a wall of branches and reeds and refused to assist even by giving half the width of the site. I was therefore compelled to build on my own property and chose stone because I did not wish to spend money on branches and reeds.” This difficulty apparently also applies to Rabbi Yonah's ruling, cited by Rama, concerning one poor partner and one wealthy partner, for there too the wealthy one could say the other was poor and he wished to withdraw into his own property. Yet poverty at that time is presumably a fact capable of verification. The difficulty remains, however, according to the Rosh.
