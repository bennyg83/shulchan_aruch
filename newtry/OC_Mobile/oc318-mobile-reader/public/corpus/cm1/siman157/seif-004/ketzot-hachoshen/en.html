Taz asks why one who holds the fallen stones cannot say, according to Rosh, that the local custom was stone, but his neighbor refused to contribute even to the minimum partition, forcing him to build the stone wall entirely on his own. If so, he would have a migo supporting exclusive ownership. The answer follows Nimukei Yosef's principle in labor law that one cannot use a migo to overturn established local custom. A claim that the parties deliberately departed from the standard custom is treated like a migo against witnesses. The Geonim and commentators say similarly that where the community has a settled practice, a party is not believed merely through migo to say they arranged otherwise. Here, if the local custom is to build with dressed or rough stone, we assume both neighbors agreed to the customary construction. The possessor cannot say, merely through a migo, that the other refused and he therefore departed from the joint customary arrangement. The custom supplies the stronger presumption. This resolves Taz's question.
