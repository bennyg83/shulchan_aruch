Collected note: The width of the wall. This is the Rambam's wording. Tosafos and the Rosh debate whether the minimum widths stated by the Sages are fixed regardless of local custom or depend on custom. The Rambam's omission of the standard materials suggests that even thickness follows local practice, though the Gemara appears closer to Tosafos and the Rosh. Ramban likewise discusses this.
As partners customarily build. See Beer HaGolah. The Rosh derives this from the Mishnah's extra wording "where they customarily build," meaning where they customarily build that kind of dividing wall.
Collected note: When they divide between themselves. See Beer HaGolah, seif katan 50. The Rosh writes so, while Ramban disagrees.
Provided that. Tosafos in the name of the Aruch explains the relevant masonry as one full of openings.
Collected note: Provided that... The Aruch's explanation is also that of Rabbeinu Chananel. Ramban connects it to the description of a stone with four exposed faces, meaning a wall constructed with many openings.
If they customarily. See Tosafos there.
Collected note: If they customarily build with less... Hagahos Ashri and Ramban disagree with allowing custom to reduce the minimum.
If there is no. The measures given by the Sages were for ordinary mortar construction. With stronger lime mortar less thickness may suffice. See siman 155, seif 2, in the gloss.
If they made a kinyan. See Tosafos Bava Basra 3a and Kesubos 53b concerning a mere kinyan on words, which is ineffective. Compare the final chapter of Avodah Zarah concerning a promise to sell land to a particular person.
Some disagree. According to the first version, the Gemara's objection can be answered by saying that a person obligated himself monetarily for half the wall, which is more than a mere kinyan on words. The Avodah Zarah case likewise can be understood as an immediate conditional sale. See Nimukei Yosef, siman 245, and Even HaEzer 55.
If... some say his fellow. If one wishes to build less substantially than local custom, the other may say he fears it will fall and does not wish repeatedly to litigate to compel rebuilding. Nimukei Yosef in the name of Raah adds that the other can prevent him even from building entirely on his own property, because each partner is entitled to use half the common wall. Even permission to use it need not satisfy him.
Collected note: If they customarily... some say. The Gemara's law of a parapet shows that even where a person builds on his own side, the neighbor can sometimes insist on a durable stone structure.
Others disagree. Since he sets it entirely within his own property, even a light partition is sufficient, as with a vineyard partition and a roof beside another's courtyard. See the Rosh for both views.
Collected note: Others disagree. The Gemara's use of "parapet" implies even a minimal barrier. The complaint about weakening a wall concerns a flimsy structure that will not last and will cause repeated trouble, not a fixed right to stone construction. The vineyard case also supports this. The Rosh explains so.
