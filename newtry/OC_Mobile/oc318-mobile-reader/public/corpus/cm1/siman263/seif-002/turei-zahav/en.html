(Likewise, if he found.) The word “likewise” implies that this applies specifically to an animal, because it has learned to wander far afield, and therefore he must announce it even in the city. But if he found utensils in a place where it is beneath his dignity to take them, he would not be obligated. Yet the concluding reason, “since he began the mitzvah,” implies that the same applies to any object once he began attending to it. Beit Yosef and Nimukei Yosef cite this in the name of Ran. This is difficult, for in Bava Batra the Gemara proposes that regarding a utensil, once he lifted it he must effect a complete return, and it brings proof from Rabbah’s ruling that once he struck an animal and caused it to move, he became obligated. The Gemara rejects the proof because an animal is different, since it has learned to wander. It appears to me that Rambam understands the unresolved question here itself to concern this point. Where it is his way to return an item in the field but not in the city, do we say that Rabbah’s reason in “once he struck it he became obligated” is specifically the animal’s tendency to wander, and not the principle that once one becomes obligated he remains obligated? If so, here he need not return it in the city, and since he is not obligated in the city he is exempt in the field as well, because a complete return is required. Or perhaps Rabbah’s reason is that once one begins an obligation, the obligation continues, so here too he becomes obligated in the city. The Gemara’s question is thus literal: do we require a complete return and exempt him entirely, or do we follow the beginning and obligate him through the end? The reason the Gemara considers the second side is Rabbah’s ruling, which may show that we follow the beginning. Or perhaps Rabbah’s ruling rests only on the animal’s wandering tendency, in which case reason alone indicates that a complete return is required. Since the question was not resolved, we rule stringently. Rambam therefore writes, “Likewise, if he found an animal,” referring to Rabbah’s law, and adds, “because he began the mitzvah,” to show that we adopt the possibility that this is the governing reason. This appears to me to be Rambam’s meaning. Although Beit Yosef was uncertain and Maggid Mishneh inclined to limit the law to an animal, explaining “because he began” to mean that failure to complete the return will cause loss through the animal’s wandering, I have written what appears correct. According to this, there is no distinction between an animal and utensils. Whenever he began, we tell him to finish, even if he did not begin by way of return but only incidentally, like the goats that were standing at Abaye’s house in Elu Metziot. This also resolves Raavad’s objection to Rambam.
