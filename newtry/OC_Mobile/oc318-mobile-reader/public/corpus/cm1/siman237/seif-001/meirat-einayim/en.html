Because ownerless property and gifts are not commonly available. This differs from an ordinary purchase, where a person can generally seek to buy a similar item elsewhere, even if doing so requires effort. Those who disagree hold that even with a gift or ownerless property the second person is called wicked, because once the first person is already pursuing and seeking to acquire it, the second is like one who takes what the first has already nearly acquired. In the Perishah I explained two approaches. One compares the case to peah, which is ownerless, where if one poor person falls upon it another may still push him aside until he actually takes it. The other distinguishes peah because all poor people regularly pursue it, whereas an ownerless object or gift appears only occasionally and a person does not know whether he will find another opportunity.

Even according to the opinion, etc. Meaning even according to the latter opinion, which holds that a person can be called wicked even for taking a found object, ownerless property, or the like, all agree that if the first person seeking to acquire it is wealthy and another comes to take it before him, the second is not called wicked merely because the first had begun pursuing it. Since the first has not yet acquired it and, being wealthy, can obtain a similar benefit elsewhere by paying money, no wrongdoing exists. The exception is an opportunity that even the wealthy person cannot obtain elsewhere even for money, in which case the wealthy and poor are equal. This is exactly how the Ran writes in Ramban's name. The Ir Shushan's text appears to reverse Rama's meaning and also omits the important exception for something unobtainable elsewhere. It likely suffered a printing omission. The correct sense is that even according to the stringent opinion, the special concern applies specifically to a poor person, while a wealthy person is not protected unless the opportunity is genuinely unavailable elsewhere even for money.
