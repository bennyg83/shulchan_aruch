Alef) This is the language of the Tur, based on the incident of Rav Gidel in the third chapter of Kiddushin 59a.
Bet) It is learned from the phrase "a poor person who is turning over a cake," because the cake is movable property.
Gimel) The Sema writes that one publicly announces in the synagogue that he committed such a wicked act.
Dalet) There in the name of Rabbenu Tam. Tosafot likewise cite him there.
Heh) There, and Tosafot likewise write in his name. The Rosh proves it from Mishnah Peah 4:3, cited in Bava Metzia 10a: if one fell upon ownerless produce or spread his garment over it, others may remove it from him. This implies that the person who takes it is not called wicked, because the object is not readily available elsewhere. The same concept underlies the case of a poor person seeking a cake: he offers his labor to a householder hoping to receive bread occasionally, because such an opportunity can be found elsewhere.
Vav) This is Rashi's view and explanation there: even where one seeks to acquire ownerless property, the law of a poor person turning over a cake applies. The Ran writes similarly in the name of the Ramban. The Mishnah's rule that others may remove the produce from him is because he is not the only person seeking it; all the poor are equally pursuing the peah.
Zayin) See above, siman 157, seif 5 in the gloss.
