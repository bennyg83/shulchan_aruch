This. Meaning, even according to this latter view, which holds that one is called wicked even when interfering with a find, ownerless property, or the like, they agree that if the first person seeking to acquire is wealthy and another comes before him to take the ownerless property or other item obtainable as a gift, the latter is not called wicked. The first has not yet fully acquired it and can seek something similar elsewhere, even if he must pay money for it, and because he is wealthy this does not matter. The exception is something that even a wealthy person cannot obtain by paying money, in which case the wealthy person is equivalent to a poor person. This is how I explained the Rama. The Ran writes this explicitly in the name of the Ramban, and the Beit Yosef cites it, contrary to Ir Shushan. Sema.
A Jew. Meaning, not only if the seller is Jewish, where the seller's welfare requires this rule, because otherwise once one Jew began negotiating and offered less than market value the seller would be forced to accept, since no other Jew could purchase. Rather, the rule applies even if the seller is a non-Jew, as the Mordechai writes and the Darkei Moshe cites. See Maharik, shoresh 132; Lechem Rav, siman 145; Pnei Moshe, part 1, siman 39 and part 2, siman 122. Ritva in Kiddushin, in the incident of Rav Gidel, cites Rabbeinu Tam that wherever the Sages call someone wicked, beit din compels him to return the purchase. Ritva disagrees and writes that although he is called wicked, he is not compelled to return anything, in the name of his teacher. See Maharashdam, simanim 225 and 334. One who is seeking to acquire a chazakah and another takes it first is not called wicked. Responsa R. Aharon Sasson, siman 137. See Responsa Maharit, part 1, siman 95. Bnei Chayei.
