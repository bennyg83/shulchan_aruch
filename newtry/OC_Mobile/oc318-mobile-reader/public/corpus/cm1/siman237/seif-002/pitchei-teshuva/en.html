He is called wicked. Eretz DeRabbanan raises whether the label “wicked” in this context is itself a Torah designation or may also apply to conduct prohibited only rabbinically. He points to Rama, Choshen Mishpat siman 34, who says that one who raises his hand to strike another is disqualified from testimony rabbinically, and infers that one may be called wicked even for a rabbinic violation. However, the Sema there explains that raising a hand is itself a Torah prohibition, and the disqualification is only rabbinic because the act carries no lashes, so this proof is not decisive. Maharit, in his novellae to Rif, Kiddushin chapter 3, writes that the poor person who is pursuing an ownerless opportunity which another then seizes is called wicked on the basis of Scripture. He connects it to the teaching at the end of Sanhedrin that one who descends into another's livelihood is included among those of whom Scripture says, “the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him.” Although directly invading another's established trade is more severe because it destroys his livelihood, entering an ownerless rental or purchase that another is already actively pursuing is analogous. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 79, approves this explanation and elaborates it. Baer Heitev cites Ritva in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that wherever the Sages call a person wicked, beit din compels him to return the transaction, while Ritva himself disagrees. Nimukei Yosef in Bava Batra likewise records that later authorities concluded that even where one is called wicked, beit din does not compel return, citing the Gemara in Kiddushin. This is contrary to Rabbeinu Chananel's view. Maharik, Maharashal, and Masat Binyamin also rule that the transaction stands and the wrongdoer is merely branded wicked. Maharashal strongly doubts that Rabbeinu Tam ever required legal rescission. Chemdat Shlomo agrees that there is no law of mandatory return. At most, beit din may publicize the person's wrongful conduct and perhaps impose an appropriate penalty to deter repetition, but title itself remains valid. If he acted inadvertently and did not know another person was already pursuing the transaction, no stigma applies; Rabbi Yitzchak's suggestion that he give it to the first party is then only a matter of piety. Chemdat Shlomo further questions whether a deliberate wrongdoer fully repairs the sin merely by returning the item. Where halachah does not impose legal restitution, the return may be no more than a voluntary gift unless accompanied by genuine teshuvah and regret. Returning solely to avoid public embarrassment may not itself erase the wrongful act. See also below, siman 259 seif 7.
