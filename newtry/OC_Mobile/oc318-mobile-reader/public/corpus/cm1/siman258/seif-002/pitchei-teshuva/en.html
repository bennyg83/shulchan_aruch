He acquires the corpus immediately. Baer Heitev cites the Sema’s explanation of the Tur, concluding that the document must therefore reach the recipient. Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 47, writes that the Sema’s words are difficult. If the acquisition is defective because the document says “after death,” how does delivery of the document help when the document itself expressly postpones acquisition until after death? He recalls a note he had written on his Shulchan Aruch at siman 258: the rule there appears to contradict siman 195, seif 5. In siman 195, if the acquirer says, “acquire this kerchief and thereby acquire the object for me after thirty days,” he does not acquire, because by the time acquisition is to take effect the kerchief has already been returned. Here, however, a document saying that acquisition occurs after death is held effective where it also contains a kinyan, because the corpus is deemed acquired immediately even without a date. The distinction is that here the giver also gave a written document. The additional kinyan was therefore unnecessary unless intended to strengthen the recipient’s position. Bava Batra 136 initially distinguishes where the kinyan language is repeated, on the theory that repetition demonstrates an intent to strengthen the gift. Although the Gemara ultimately does not make that formal distinction, the underlying rationale remains that an otherwise unnecessary kinyan must have been added to give present effect. By contrast, siman 195 concerns a kinyan alone. One should not distinguish land from movable property. The Talmudic source in Nedarim speaks simply of “his assets” without specifying land, and there is no conceptual reason for a distinction. Just as land can be given with the corpus now and produce after death, so too movable property may have present ownership of the corpus while use or rental income remains with the giver during life.
