One who steals and slaughters on Shabbat, etc. The Tur's presentation is difficult. The Gemara states that one who slaughters through another person on Shabbat is exempt from fourfold or fivefold payment according to the opinion that an act of slaughter prohibited on Shabbat is not legally fit slaughter, whereas one who slaughters an animal later found to be tereifa is liable according to the Sages. The Rosh's abbreviated wording was misunderstood by the Tur as though he ruled that slaughter through another on Shabbat is exempt because we follow Rabbi Yochanan HaSandlar, and that slaughter on Yom Kippur through another is liable. In fact, the Rosh merely summarizes the Gemara: agency for slaughter is derived from the verse, but the baraita's Sages exempt slaughter on Shabbat because it is unfit slaughter according to Rabbi Yochanan HaSandlar. The Rosh cites this only to establish the special agency rule for slaughter and then discusses whether the law follows Rabbi Yochanan HaSandlar. It does not follow that an animal found tereifa is exempt. We rule like the Sages of the Mishna that slaughter of a tereifa generates fourfold or fivefold payment. Rabbeinu Yerucham states the law correctly: one who personally slaughters on Shabbat is exempt because of the death penalty; if another slaughters for him, he may be liable under the special agency rule, subject to the issue of legally fit slaughter. One who slaughters on Yom Kippur without warning, or through another, is liable because no lashes are imposed; where he himself was warned and incurs lashes, he does not both receive lashes and pay. Maharshal also rules this way.
The Rambam rules that one who steals teruma from its Israelite owners after they separated it does not pay double, because their only interest is the right to choose the recipient, and such a benefit is not legally monetary. This follows the conclusion of Kiddushin, and the Maggid Mishneh likewise considers the Rambam's view primary. Rabbeinu Chananel, the Ramban, the Rosh, Ran, and Ritva appear to treat the benefit of designation as monetary, relying on Rava's discussion in Nedarim. After analysis, however, the Rambam's position is correct. Even if Rava there appears to treat the benefit as monetary, the anonymous conclusion of Kiddushin is later authority and rules that all agree it is not money. The passage in Nedarim is different because vows prohibit even minor benefit, including a concession that is not monetary. Rava therefore need not maintain that the right of designation is monetary in ordinary civil law. This also reconciles the Mishna in Nedarim and the positions of Rabbi and Rabbi Yose. Maharam of Rothenburg likewise writes that Rava himself may hold that the benefit is not monetary, while treating vows more stringently. The Tur in Yoreh Deah and his abridgment of the Rosh expressly write that the benefit is not monetary, notwithstanding the text of the Rosh attributed to Rabbeinu Chananel. It is possible that a later student added the concluding formulation, or that the Tur understood Rabbeinu Chananel as speaking only within the law of vows. The Rama's rulings in several places also accord with the Rambam. This is the principal law.
