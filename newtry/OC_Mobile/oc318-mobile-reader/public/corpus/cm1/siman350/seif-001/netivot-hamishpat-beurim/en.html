He stole forbidden fat. See Shach, seif katan 1, who challenges Rosh and Tur. In my view their position can be defended as follows. According to R. Yehudah HaSandlar, who holds that prohibited Shabbat labor renders the product forbidden, even if an invalid slaughter is technically called slaughter, there is no fourfold or fivefold liability where the thief appoints another person to slaughter on Shabbat. If he specifically instructed the agent to slaughter on Shabbat, another prohibition was inserted into the agency and the ordinary rule that there is no agency for a transgression applies. If he simply told him to slaughter, and the agent chose Shabbat, the agent departed fundamentally from the mission. The sender sent him to slaughter in a manner that would permit the meat, not to render it prohibited. That is a material change of agency, just as in me'ilah a deviation from the mission prevents liability from attaching to the sender. Even if the thief authorized him to slaughter in whatever manner he wished, including on Shabbat, there is another reason for exemption. At the first cut the animal becomes prohibited through the Shabbat act, which is a recognizable injury. A recognizable injury can constitute a legal change. The thief therefore acquires the animal through change before the slaughter is completed, and at completion he is slaughtering his own property rather than the owner's. The Gemara still needs the opinion that an invalid slaughter is not called slaughter for the parallel cases of idolatry and an ox condemned to stoning, where the first cut does not create the same new change. Thus the sugya's formulation can remain consistent. This also explains why Rosh cited R. Yehudah HaSandlar even though we do not rule like him in every respect. His reasoning remains relevant to the practical exemption from fourfold or fivefold payment through change of agency or change in the animal. Ketzot HaChoshen further writes that where a person admitted a fine before one beit din and later witnesses came before another beit din, he can still be liable because the first court did not finish the matter. In my view this cannot be correct where the first admission was a valid admission before beit din and can be proved. Bava Kamma 75 regarding Rabban Gamliel implies that the defect there was precisely that the admission was outside beit din. Once a valid judicial admission exempted him, why should a second court revive the fine merely because witnesses later appeared there? Ketzot compares this to a case where witnesses appeared in the first court but judgment was not concluded and the defendant later admitted elsewhere. The comparison is not apt. There, when he comes before the second court no witness-evidence is presently before that court, and he is still incriminating himself. Here, by contrast, a valid prior judicial admission already created the exemption. Rashba's responsum, part 3 siman 110, supports this. Where the thief physically restored the stolen item before the case came before beit din, the exemption from double payment is not necessarily a function of admission at all, but of the statutory requirement that beit din condemn him while the theft remains chargeable in the relevant manner.
