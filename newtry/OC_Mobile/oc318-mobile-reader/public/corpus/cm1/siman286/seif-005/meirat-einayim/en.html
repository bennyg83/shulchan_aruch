Until he was a virgin, he said, “When he was married, and he was honored as a way that he was sent to him in marriage, and that he would be sent to each of his sons to return to each of his marriage, and that it was, and that it was, and that he had been married to each of them, and that he had no obligation to return to each of them, and therefore, if he had a few of them, if they were married him, he had given him
And what he writes, "it goes to the common estate." Meaning that all the brothers divide that portion. This is the Rambam's view. The Raavad, Ri Halevi, and Tur hold that it belongs to the particular brother who married. The Maggid Mishneh explains the Rambam's reason. If the other brothers never marry, or marry in a manner unlike the brother for whom the reciprocal wedding gift was sent, the sender will never have to return anything to them. Their father intended the original gift to benefit all his sons. Why should only the one brother who marries derive the entire benefit while the others receive nothing from their shares? We do not abandon a certain right based upon a doubtful future possibility. Moreover, the plain wording of the baraita, which says that it returns to the common estate, supports this. The Tur and the other authorities understand differently. When their father originally sent the wedding gift in all their names, it was as though he gave each son his proportionate share of the gift to send independently in his own name. The recipient would then owe a corresponding return to each brother separately when that brother married in the same manner. See the Derishah.
