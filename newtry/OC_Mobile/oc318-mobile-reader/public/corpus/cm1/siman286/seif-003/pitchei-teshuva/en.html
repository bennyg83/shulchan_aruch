One whom his father redeemed, etc. Shaar Mishpat above, siman 79, seif katan 2, cites Responsa She'elat Yaavetz, siman 55, that this rule applies specifically to redemption from captivity. Since the father is obligated to redeem his son, we do not presume that the money was intended as a gift. But where the father voluntarily gives money, we do presume a gift, because otherwise why did he give it? Shaar Mishpat writes that the cited proof is not decisive, but the reasoning itself is compelling. Certainly, where a father gives money without qualification and then dies, the other heirs cannot extract it merely out of doubt. Responsa Panim Meirot, volume 2, end of siman 133, agrees. On that basis he ruled concerning a father who ordered that his firstborn son not take a double portion. That instruction itself is ineffective, as stated in Shulchan Aruch, siman 281, seif 4. The heirs nevertheless claimed that the father had given the firstborn approximately five hundred Reichsthaler and therefore had ordered that he not take a double portion, and they sought to deduct that amount. The firstborn claimed that all the earlier transfers were outright gifts. Although Rama in siman 286 rules that one whom his father redeemed from imprisonment has the amount deducted, She'elat Yaavetz explains that captivity compelled the father to pay and therefore the son cannot claim a gift. Where there was no such compulsion, the son may claim that it was a gift. The father's statement gives him no greater credibility. Accordingly, once the father's attempt to deprive the firstborn of his double portion is legally void, the situation is like one who states that a certain person owes him a sum and that person responds that it was given or forgiven. The alleged debtor swears a heset oath and is exempt. So too here, the firstborn swears a heset oath to the other heirs that his father gave him the money as a complete gift, and then receives his double portion according to Torah law. See there.
