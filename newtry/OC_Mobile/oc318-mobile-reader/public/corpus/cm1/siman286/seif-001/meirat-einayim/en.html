This is specifically where they protested, etc., and some disagree. In the Derishah and Perishah I explained the rationale of the dissenters, who hold that the other siblings can demand an accounting even for past excesses that have already been consumed or lost. They also do not distinguish between clothing worn by the siblings themselves and clothing of their wives and children. Yet in siman 288 the Tur and Mechaber make those distinctions and say that what has already been consumed is not appraised. I offered several reconciliations there, none entirely satisfying. It therefore appears preferable to distinguish the cases. In siman 288 the siblings are equal adults or otherwise equivalent partners, and the only issue is that one spent more on clothing for himself, his wife, or children than another. There one can say that until someone expressed objection, their original understanding was not to be particular. Each could dress as he wished, and if one saw his brother obtaining better clothing he could do likewise. Here, however, the case concerns adults and minors whose needs are structurally different. Adults need more clothing, while minors need more food. There is no reason to assume they originally consented to unequal consumption of fundamentally different kinds. Therefore Rabbi Yeshayah holds that they can demand reimbursement from one another even for what has already been consumed.
