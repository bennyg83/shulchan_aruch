He does not, etc. Since the borrower makes a definite claim and the lender is uncertain, everyone agrees that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one where the rule that the claimant must produce proof does not apply. This is so even if the document contains a trust clause. The borrower indeed trusted him, but here the lender says, "I do not know." If the lender holds collateral, he must return it to the borrower, Responsa Rashba, siman 976. If the borrower also says, "I do not know," the law is explained below in siman 82, that the lender may collect even without an oath.
He collects with it. If the borrower demands it, the lender must return his document. If the borrower does not demand it, then if the document was found among the lender's torn documents, the lender is forbidden to test the borrower by suing him merely to see whether he will admit or deny the debt. The borrower might forget and admit by mistake. But if the document was not found among torn documents, the lender is permitted to demand payment. If the borrower admits, he may accept payment. If the borrower says it was paid, the lender must return the document. I established this in Tumim. If an heir says, "I do not know," it is obvious that the lender may collect, because the uncertainty of an heir does not weaken the document.
He may again collect with it. Sema writes that this includes collection from encumbered property. Shach writes that this applies to purchasers who bought after the lender remembered. But purchasers who bought during the interval between the lender's statement that he did not know whether the document was paid and his later statement that he remembered may say, "We relied on your earlier statement of uncertainty." Purchasers who bought before that first statement or after the later recollection are plainly subject to collection. Shach nevertheless says the matter still requires study.
