Sema explains the rule by saying that where the lender says with certainty that the debt remains unpaid and the borrower says he does not know, a certain claim is stronger than an uncertain claim. If the borrower dies and the lender faces heirs who themselves are uncertain, he likewise cannot collect. One cannot argue that the rule of a superior certain claim requires a good certain claim against a weak uncertain one, while the heirs' position is not a weak uncertainty, because the lender cannot take the required Mishnah oath against heirs while himself saying he does not know. This resembles a person suspected regarding oaths who produces a document against heirs and cannot collect. A credibility clause does not help against the lender's own uncertainty. There is, however, a difference between a living borrower and heirs. Against the living borrower, the document is removed from the lender and returned to the borrower. The lender cannot insist that it remain in beit din in case he later remembers. Although one ordinarily does not extract money merely because one party has certainty and the other uncertainty, possession of a document is not treated like possession of money, because the document itself is not the money. Therefore the lender's certain claim does not preserve his possession of the paper once he admits uncertainty about the debt. Against heirs, however, we do not remove the document and return it to them, because beit din does not make an affirmative extraction claim on behalf of heirs merely from uncertainty. Still, the lender cannot keep it in his own hands because of the concern that it may later fall before the heirs and be collected improperly. It should therefore remain deposited with beit din. This also explains the Geonic interpretation of the Mishnah concerning a document found among one's papers whose status is unknown: if the borrower is dead, the instrument is left deposited rather than returned to the lender or heirs. The rule that a lender who says he does not know cannot collect applies where he is uncertain whether this very debt was paid, because payment would extinguish the document. But if he admits the document itself was not paid and the borrower merely asserts an independent counterclaim against him, the document is not weakened. The lender swears that he does not know of the counterclaim and remains exempt from that separate claim while collecting his own debt. If the lender dies, his heirs ordinarily take the heirs' oath and collect. But if they admit that their father received some payment and do not know how much, the document is weakened and they cannot collect even if the borrower is also uncertain. If witnesses only say they saw some payment but do not know the amount, their testimony establishes only the minimum they can state with certainty. Anything beyond that is incomplete testimony and does not itself impair the document.
I examined my accounts. If the lender first said, 'I do not know,' and later says, 'I remembered,' but the document is no longer in his possession because it was destroyed by unavoidable accident, I believe he cannot collect if beit din had already compelled him to surrender the document after his initial uncertainty. Were it otherwise, even after the court removed the document from him he could later claim, 'I remembered,' and restore collection. Shach implies that he cannot do so once the borrower demanded return and the court removed it. The reason is not merely that witnesses to unavoidable destruction are equivalent to holding the document. Once beit din has issued a binding ruling requiring return of the document and the litigant has left the court, he cannot later revise his plea even by explaining it, because we are concerned that someone taught him a new argument. This is the ordinary rule after final judgment and departure from beit din. Even authorities who sometimes permit a later claim of remembered facts agree once a binding judgment was issued. If, however, the borrower did not demand the document and the court merely said that collection could not presently be made because the lender's claim was uncertain, that is not a final adjudication on the merits. It is as though the court did not entertain the claim at that stage. He can therefore later say, 'I remembered,' even if the document meanwhile was destroyed through unavoidable accident. The same rule applies in an oral claim. If the claimant initially makes an uncertain allegation and the defendant is released from an oath, but afterward the claimant remembers the facts, the defendant can still be required to swear, because the earlier ruling was not a definitive judgment of exemption. The same applies to heirs where the document was merely deposited with beit din and not judicially removed from the lender as the result of a final ruling.
