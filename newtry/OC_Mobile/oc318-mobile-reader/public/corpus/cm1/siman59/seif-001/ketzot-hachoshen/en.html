He says, 'I do not know.' Baal HaTerumot cites two views and concludes that the creditor cannot collect, because it resembles a suspected oath-taker who produces a document but cannot swear. We likewise rule that he cannot collect, but not for Baal HaTerumot's reason, since we rule that a suspected oath-taker can collect without taking the oath. Rather, the reason is the rule of a definite claim against an uncertain claim. Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah hold that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one, while we ordinarily rule that the possessor's monetary chazakah outweighs the definite claim. Here, however, the debtor's definite claim is supported by his monetary possession, so his definite claim prevails over the creditor's uncertainty even against a document. This is proven from Ketubot 12a concerning a woman who says, 'I was violated after betrothal.' She is believed because a definite claim prevails over an uncertain claim. The Gemara challenges Rav Nachman and answers that there her bodily chazakah supports her. Thus where a chazakah supports the definite claim, definite against uncertain prevails even to extract money. All the more so here, where the debtor makes a definite claim and the creditor an uncertain one, and the debtor's monetary chazakah supports his certainty. We therefore apply definite against uncertain to weaken the document. See what I wrote in siman 92, seif katan 10. There is further proof from chapter HaKotev. The reason a woman who admits partial payment of her ketubah must swear is that one who pays is careful, while one who receives payment may not be careful. There the Sages certainly do not suspect deliberate lying, as Tosafot explains. Her migo that she could have denied any partial payment is ineffective only because she may honestly be imprecise about the payments. If a creditor who is uncertain whether payment occurred could nevertheless collect, why would this oath be necessary? Even if she is not precise and tells beit din that she does not know, she would still collect. This shows that a creditor who is uncertain about payment cannot collect.
He cannot collect. A case came before us where a creditor produced a promissory note. The debtor said, 'You owe me an offsetting amount,' and the creditor answered, 'I do not know.' Some wished to compare this to our case of a debtor with a definite claim and a creditor with an uncertain one. In my opinion it is different. Here the creditor himself is uncertain whether his own debt was paid, and a document is intended for payment. The debtor's definite claim therefore prevails over the creditor's uncertainty, as explained in seif katan 1. But a claim, 'You owe me a separate amount against this debt,' does not itself constitute a defense to the original debt. Although in siman 82, seif 4, we explain that the creditor must swear when the debtor claims an offset, that is because the debtor can compel the oath through a migo that he could have claimed, 'I paid.' The migo cannot entirely destroy the creditor's right. Had the debtor actually claimed payment, the creditor, who knows with certainty that he was not paid, would collect after an oath. The debtor's monetary chazakah does not help with the separate offset claim, because such an offset does not automatically discharge the debt. Rather, each party collects from the other, as explained in siman 85. Thus the debtor remains obligated on the original document and is separately asserting a new claim against the creditor. The case therefore returns to the ordinary rule: one who says, 'I do not know whether I became obligated,' is exempt. This is clear.
