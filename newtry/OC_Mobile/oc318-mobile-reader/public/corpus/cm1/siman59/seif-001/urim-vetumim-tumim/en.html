He does not collect with it. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 53, cites two opinions and rules that nothing can be collected with such a note, comparing the creditor to a person suspected concerning an oath who cannot swear on his note. According to Tur's ruling below in siman 82 that a suspected creditor can nevertheless collect on his note, Baal HaTerumot's stated reason would not control. Tur must therefore hold that even without that analogy there is no basis for collection. The reason is that where the debtor is the possessor and makes a definite claim while the claimant is uncertain, the definite claim prevails. R. Yehudah says in general that definite against uncertain favors the definite claimant; R. Nachman disagrees because the burden of proof lies on the claimant. But where the possessor himself has the definite claim and the one seeking to extract money is uncertain, everyone agrees the possessor prevails. This also resolves Maharsha's question in Bava Kamma 35b. Tosafot ask in the case where the injured party is uncertain and the damager definite that we should say definite beats uncertain. Maharsha asks why they do not raise the same point in the earlier case where the injured party is definite and the damager uncertain. There is no difficulty. In that earlier case R. Nachman can simply answer that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. But where the injured party is uncertain and the possessor is definite, even R. Nachman agrees the definite possessor prevails. Tosafot therefore ask a question according to everyone.
He may collect with it. If the borrower demands return of the note, the lender must return it, and the lender has only a general cherem against him. Shach writes this briefly, but there is a substantial dispute in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, between Baal HaTerumot and R. Yehudah Albargeloni. Baal HaTerumot holds that if the borrower expressly demands return of the note, it must certainly be returned. If the borrower does not demand it, however, the lender may retain the note in case he later remembers the circumstances. He may even approach the borrower and ask whether the debt remains, unless the note was found among cancelled notes. In the latter case, the lender may not test the borrower, because perhaps the borrower will mistakenly admit owing money that is not truly due. Baal HaTerumot interprets the Mishnah, "If one found a note among his documents and does not know its status, it shall remain until Eliyahu comes," as referring to a note found among cancelled documents. Why else would its mere location among papers matter? The following clause, concerning a receipt found among them, then refers back to those cancelled papers. If a receipt is found there, its contents are followed. R. Yehudah Albargeloni disagrees. He holds that if the lender himself is alive and does not know the note's status, he may not retain it at all, even if the borrower has not yet demanded return. The concern is that the lender may die and the note fall to his heirs, who may collect improperly. The Mishnah therefore concerns heirs who found a note among cancelled documents and do not know its status. They cannot collect unless the borrower admits, and the note remains until its status is clarified. Gidulei Terumah asks on this interpretation. The Gemara challenges the rule that a receipt found in the lender's possession is invalid from the Mishnah's statement that if there is a receipt among the documents one follows it, and answers that the receipt was found among cancelled notes. But if the opening clause itself already concerns heirs and a note found among cancelled papers, why would the questioner not realize the same context governs the receipt clause? There are two answers. First, at the initial stage the Gemara assumed that a receipt found in the lender's hand was valid. On that assumption, the Mishnah could speak of a living lender who may retain an uncertain note because he can protect against future harm by placing a receipt beside it. Once the Gemara concludes that a receipt in the lender's possession is generally invalid, the opening clause itself becomes difficult and the answer that the documents were found among cancelled notes resolves both clauses. A better answer is that R. Yehudah Albargeloni understood from the outset that the opening clause concerns heirs and cancelled notes, but the receipt clause could have been a new independent case. The questioner therefore asked: if the note in the receipt case was not among cancelled documents, what does the lender's receipt prove, given the rule that a receipt in his hand is invalid? The Gemara answers that the receipt itself was found among cancelled papers. That location makes the receipt credible, because if the lender still needed it he would not have placed it among documents treated as cancelled. Thus no difficulty remains. Still, R. Yehudah Albargeloni's practical rule that heirs cannot collect a note found among cancelled papers is not our accepted halachah, as explained below in siman 65, seif 18. Therefore his interpretation of the Mishnah does not control. Baal HaTerumot's approach should be followed. Even though most other commentators explain the Mishnah through a third-party context rather than his precise reading, we do not find a clear authority disputing his practical distinction, and his interpretation has support in the sugya. Accordingly, if the borrower demands his note back, it must be returned; if he does not, the lender may retain it in the limited manner described above.
