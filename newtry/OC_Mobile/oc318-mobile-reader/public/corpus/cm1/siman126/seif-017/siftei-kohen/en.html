Reuven leased a tax-farming right from the community, and the community owed. This is the wording of the Tur and the Rosh's responsum. The Mechaber adds that Reuven obligated himself for the amount of the lease, payable at a specified time, based on Bedek HaBayit. The point is that the lessee assumed a present debt through an acquisition, written instrument, or other binding obligation. Otherwise, rent is not payable until the end of the rental term, and no present debt would yet exist, as stated in the following responsum concerning a rented object. The specified due date is mentioned only because that was the factual case. The same law certainly applies where the debt was immediately due.
With Reuven's consent. This apparently means that Reuven also agreed to cancel the lease, as occurred in the Rosh's responsum. The same rule applies if the lease was later canceled for another reason, even against Reuven's will, because at the time of the assignment he owed the community. This is why the Mechaber later says generally, “Although the lease was afterward canceled.” Rama's addition, “with his consent,” is unclear. Perhaps he meant that consent makes the case even more novel, or perhaps the text should read “with their consent,” referring to the community. The Rosh discusses a merchant who told a broker to receive money for him from a money changer and placed the broker with the money changer. The money changer then retained the funds because of a debt allegedly owed by the broker. If the broker truly owed the money changer, the retention was lawful and is treated as though payment reached the broker, who must therefore give the merchant the merchant's money held by him. If the broker denies owing anything and the money changer wrongfully retains the funds, the broker swears that he owes nothing and is exempt from the merchant. Beit Yosef struggled with the case and omitted it. Darkei Moshe interpreted it as an ordinary agency: once the merchant instructed the money changer to pay the broker, payment to the broker discharged the money changer. If the broker admitted owing the money changer, setoff was equivalent to receipt, and the broker owed the merchant. If the broker denied the debt, the money changer had not fulfilled the agency and remained liable to the merchant, while the broker was exempt. This explanation is difficult. Even if the broker owed the money changer, the retained funds belonged to the merchant, and the money changer had no right to set them off. It also does not fit the responsum's statement that the broker is exempt from the merchant, since under this interpretation the merchant suffers no loss because the money changer remains liable. Bach explains that the merchant privately asked the broker as an act of kindness to collect the merchant's money. The merchant then assigned the money changer's debt to the broker without informing the money changer that the broker was merely an agent. Since an assignment in the presence of all three is irrevocable, the money changer was discharged from the merchant and could set off the broker's debt. If the broker denied owing the debt, he would swear and be exempt, while the merchant bore the loss caused by making an unqualified assignment. This too is unsatisfactory. The responsum does not say that the assignment was unqualified or that the money changer asserted discharge, and it does not require the money changer to swear in the final case. Moreover, if the merchant never entrusted anything to the broker, a doubtful claim cannot be combined with the money changer's assertion to impose an oath on the broker. The correct explanation is straightforward. A broker ordinarily sells merchandise, receives the buyer's money, pays the merchant, and retains brokerage fees. Here the broker held sale proceeds belonging to the merchant and wished to retain his commission. The merchant needed the cash and therefore said, “Come, I will place you with a money changer who will lend me money and pay your commission for me, and you will then give me the sale proceeds you hold.” The merchant did so and directed the broker to receive payment from the money changer. The money changer then retained the funds because of a debt owed by the broker. If the broker truly owed that debt, the setoff was valid and treated as payment of his commission. The broker must therefore deliver the merchant's sale proceeds, since his commission was already satisfied. If the broker denies owing the money changer, then he received no payment of his commission. He swears that he owes nothing, retains from the merchant's sale proceeds the amount of his commission, and is exempt from the merchant regarding that amount. The oath is proper even though the merchant's claim is doubtful, because the merchant's money was certainly in the broker's hands, and the money changer's definite claim may be joined to that fact. In any event, the broker must answer the money changer's definite claim under oath. If he refuses, the money changer's claim is accepted, meaning the broker's commission has already been paid through setoff. This reading also explains the Rosh and Tur's exact phrase that the broker “must give the money to the merchant,” rather than merely “must pay the merchant.” The money already held by the broker belongs to the merchant.
