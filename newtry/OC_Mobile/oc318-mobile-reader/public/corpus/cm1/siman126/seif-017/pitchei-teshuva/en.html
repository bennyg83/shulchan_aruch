Responsa Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 4, discusses Reuven, who married his daughter to Shimon and asked Yehudah to issue a transferable note to Shimon for one hundred gold coins, payable after six months. After the wedding, Shimon repeatedly quarreled with his wife until she rebelled against him. She seized movable property, including this note, and delivered everything to her mother. Yehudah separately owed Reuven three hundred under another note and refused to pay until Shimon gave him a release or the first note was returned. Beit din ruled according to the law of the yeshivah academies that the wife could retain her dowry, and also ordered Yehudah to pay his three hundred debt to Reuven, but required Reuven to deposit one hundred with beit din. If the couple reconciled and resumed married life, that one hundred would be paid to Shimon. Yehudah requested return of his note. Reuven and his wife claimed that it had burned and were prepared to state so under a general cherem, and beit din considered publicly canceling the note. Levi, a creditor of Shimon, then appeared and sought to attach the one hundred held by beit din. Levi claimed that Shimon had transferred that sum to him through a three party settlement. Yehudah admitted the transfer and stated under cherem that before accepting it he had seen his note in the possession of Shimon's mother in law. When he accepted the transfer, however, he assumed that the spouses had reconciled and that the note had been returned to Shimon. Since he was now being compelled to deposit the money with beit din, he asked that beit din pay whoever was legally entitled. The rabbi who first ruled held that Levi had no claim to the one hundred because the wife could seize her dowry under the law of the academies, and even without actual seizure the husband was obligated to restore it. Moreover, even absent the marital dispute, a creditor ordinarily does not collect from the wife's dowry while the dowry funds remain identifiable. He further held that Levi had no claim against Yehudah, because Yehudah could say that his earlier admission was mistaken and rely on a migo. Since Levi's claim against Yehudah was at most uncertain, Levi could impose only a general cherem. Shev Yaakov challenges this ruling at length. First, if Yehudah's position is being treated as a claim of accounting error, why is he exempt without an oath? His credibility rests only on a migo that he could have denied the debt or claimed payment, and either of those claims would have required an oath. The same oath should therefore attach to his present claim. More fundamentally, Shev Yaakov says the analogy to an accounting error is mistaken. At the time Yehudah accepted the three party transfer to Levi, the one hundred was still legally presumed to belong to Shimon. Although later litigation and the wife's oath established that she was entitled to retain her dowry, that later determination does not retroactively nullify the transfer. It is even possible that at the time of the transfer she had not yet developed the marital revulsion on which her later claim rested. The case instead resembles siman 126, seif 17, where a person who owed money to a communal body transferred it to another while the debt was then valid, and a later cancellation of the original obligation does not undo the transfer. The Shach expressly extends that law to a debt later canceled for another reason against the transferor's will. So too here, when Yehudah accepted Shimon's direction to pay Levi, Yehudah was still indebted to Shimon. No beit din ruling had yet established the wife's right, nor had she made the required declaration under cherem. Yehudah had learned of the marital dispute only from Reuven's wife. He could therefore have paid Shimon at that time, and consequently Shimon's three party transfer to Levi was effective. Shev Yaakov concludes that this also undermines the first ruling concerning the wife's dowry. The rules protecting her dowry apply while it has not yet been paid away to a creditor. Once Shimon lawfully transferred the one hundred to Levi through Yehudah, it is as though Shimon had already paid that money to Levi. Yehudah then became Levi's debtor rather than Shimon's. When the spouses later came before beit din, the one hundred had already left Shimon's patrimony, just like other dowry funds he had already paid to creditors, which are not recovered from them. Accordingly, Yehudah need not deposit the one hundred for the wife's benefit. Levi acquired it when Yehudah became obligated to him through the three party settlement, and at that moment Shimon's payment to Levi was complete. Shev Yaakov concludes that this follows both law and reason. See there at length, and Responsa Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 107, on a related matter.
