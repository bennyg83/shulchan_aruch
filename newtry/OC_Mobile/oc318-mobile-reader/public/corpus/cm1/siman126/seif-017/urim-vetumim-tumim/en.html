Reuven hired, etc. The Tur cites a responsum of the Rosh concerning a merchant who told a broker to collect money for him from a moneychanger. The moneychanger withheld the money because he claimed that the broker owed him. The Rosh ruled: if the broker indeed owes the moneychanger, then the moneychanger lawfully retained the funds and the broker must pay the merchant. But if the broker says that he owes the moneychanger nothing, the broker swears and is exempt from the merchant. The Beit Yosef struggled greatly with this responsum. The Shach in seif katan 74 explains that the broker himself held money belonging to the merchant and was withholding it for brokerage fees that the merchant owed him. The merchant then directed him to the moneychanger for those fees. If the broker admits that the moneychanger offset the broker's debt, he has effectively received his brokerage fee and must return all the merchant's money without deduction. If he says the moneychanger unjustly blocked him and did not pay, the broker swears and returns the merchant's money after deducting the brokerage fee. This explanation has no intelligible fit with the Rosh. It omits the essential fact that the broker supposedly already held the merchant's money. Where is that mentioned? Moreover, on that reading the case has nothing to do with maamad sheloshtan. The whole issue would merely be whether the broker has been paid his commission, a matter belonging in the general laws of claims and counterclaims, not here. There is no need to enumerate the other forced elements. The straightforward explanation is that of the Bach and Perishah, though each expresses it somewhat differently. The broker was the merchant's agent to collect the merchant's money from the moneychanger. When the merchant placed the broker before the moneychanger, however, he did not expressly say, "This man is my agent." He simply said, "Give it to him." The moneychanger later says, "I did not believe he was merely your agent. I understood that he himself acquired the money by maamad sheloshtan, and I therefore retained it against the debt he owes me." If the broker admits that interpretation and also admits owing the moneychanger, he must plainly reimburse the merchant, because his personal debt was paid using the merchant's money. But if the broker denies owing the moneychanger, what could the broker have done? The moneychanger falsely withheld the merchant's funds from him. The broker swears and is exempt. This is simple and correct. Although at the beginning of this siman I brought proof for Maharam Alashkar that maamad sheloshtan does not apply where the assignee remains obligated to the transferor, that is where the moneychanger knows this relationship. Here the moneychanger says, "I do not believe that he is merely your agent. I say that the money was his, either as a gift or as payment of his own claim." In that situation the moneychanger can assert maamad sheloshtan. This is simple. The Shach asks what basis there is for the broker's oath. The merchant has no definite claim. The money was never in the broker's possession, and the moneychanger's assertion cannot transform the merchant's uncertain claim into a definite one for purposes of imposing an oath, as explained above in siman 121. There is no difficulty. The case concerns a broker who regularly conducts business for the merchant, repeatedly handling commercial transactions on his behalf, as the Shach himself describes. Such a broker is among those subject to the Mishnah's oath on an uncertain claim, like agents and household managers who conduct ongoing business within another's affairs. See what I wrote above in siman 93. This is why the Rosh and Tur specifically use the term "broker" rather than an ordinary one-time agent. With a single isolated agency one would not ordinarily say that the agent rationalizes for himself sufficiently to require an oath, as explained at the end of siman 93. The terminology is therefore exact. I have elaborated somewhat because my heart was stirred by the Shach's statement against the Bach that "the mighty men did not find their hands." In truth the Bach's interpretation is correct. His strength in Torah was great, and he was a man who upheld the yoke of Torah.
