Nevertheless, even according to this view, he cannot claim. The Mechaber's wording is imprecise. The Rosh and Tur say that he should not initially claim against Reuven. As I explain below in seif katan 58, this is the proper procedural order because Reuven is only analogous to a guarantor. Certainly, if collection from Shimon is not readily possible, Levi may collect from Reuven. This is also evident from the Mechaber's implication that, according to the final view, Levi may return to Reuven when Shimon delays or refuses payment. If Reuven were literally only a guarantor, Shimon's refusal would not matter, because Levi could compel Shimon to pay. Rather, the meaning is as stated: so long as Levi can collect from Shimon in the ordinary course, and Shimon is willing to pay but merely delays briefly, he should not first pursue Reuven.
It appears to me that one should rule according to the final view. In my opinion, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, because Rif, Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot rule that the recipient may not return to the original debtor. Ramban in Sefer HaZechut, cited by Baal HaTerumot, and in his novellae to Bava Batra, appears to agree with Rif. Rashba likewise agrees in responsa and in his novellae to the end of the first chapter of Gittin. Nimukei Yosef writes that Rif ruled that once the recipient acquired the debt, he may no longer return to the original creditor, because the acquisition is regarded as though he expressly released him, and that the commentators consider this the primary view. Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, Semag, and Rabbeinu Eliezer ben Natan also clearly support it. Had Rama seen all these authorities, perhaps he would not have ruled otherwise, for Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite only Rif, Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot. Ritva argues that the assignee may return to the assignor so long as he did not expressly release him. Ritva reasons that if the assignor were discharged merely because the assignee accepted the assignment, the assignee would already be legally liable for causing the assignor's lien to be lost, and there would be no need to characterize the enactment of the presence of all three parties as a law without rationale. In my view this is not decisive. Without the enactment, the assignee would acquire nothing, the debtor could retract, and plainly the assignee could also retract. The Sages enacted that the debtor cannot retract and that the assignee acquires. Once they enacted acquisition, the assignee likewise cannot retract, because he relied upon collecting from the assigned debtor and is regarded as having expressly released the assignor.
Accordingly, we do not require the distinctions written below. The Sema explains that seif 10 says that if the assigned debtor is found to be poor and collection is impossible, the assignee may return to the assignor. According to the final view, poverty should be irrelevant, because whenever the assigned debtor refuses payment after demand the assignee may return to the assignor. The Sema nevertheless says that seif 11, concerning a debtor who was wealthy and later became poor, applies even according to the Rosh. The assignor can say, “Your fortune caused his poverty,” or, “Had you not accepted the assignment, I would have collected before he became poor.” The Sema cites a responsum of the Rosh as support. He also says that the rule in seif 10, that one who knew the debtor was poor cannot return, applies according to the final view because the assignor may say, “You knowingly accepted the risk.” These words do not appear correct. If the assignee retained the right to return, why should later poverty be attributed to his fortune? The debtor did not become poor within the assignee's exclusive legal domain, since the assignee could have returned to the assignor at any time. Unless the assignee negligently delayed collection, his acceptance caused no loss. Rama's wording also plainly indicates that none of the distinctions in seifim 10 and 11 apply according to the final view. Nimukei Yosef and Rama further state that if the assignee negligently delayed until the debtor became poor, he cannot return to the assignor. Rav Hai Gaon is cited likewise. This implies that if he was not negligent, even though the debtor later became poor, he may return. The Sema's proof from the responsum is not compelling, for several reasons noted by Bach. The Sema's further statement that even the final view agrees that one who knew of the poverty cannot return also requires study. The assignee may say, “I accepted only because I believed he would exert every effort to pay, not because I knowingly waived recovery from you altogether.” We are not dealing with fools who knowingly accept a debtor certain not to pay.
