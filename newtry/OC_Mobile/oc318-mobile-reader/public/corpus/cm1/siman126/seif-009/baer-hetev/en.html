First. The Mechaber's wording is imprecise. Rosh and Tur say that Levi may not sue Reuven first. The proper order is therefore to seek payment initially from Shimon, like a guarantor. But if Levi cannot collect from Shimon in the ordinary way, he certainly may collect from Reuven. The later opinion that Levi may retract when Shimon merely delays him proves this, for if Reuven were only a guarantor with no continuing liability until exhaustion of remedies, mere delay by Shimon would not justify retraction. Thus, when the Mechaber says that Levi cannot return to Reuven as long as he can collect from Shimon, he means that Shimon is readily collectible and merely delays him somewhat. Shach.
The latter opinion. Shach writes that in his view the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, because most early authorities rule that Levi cannot retract. See there at length.
Below. This refers to seif 10, where it says that if the assigned debtor is found to be poor and has no maneh from which to collect, Levi may return to Reuven. Sema explains that the distinctions of seifim 10 and 11 can be reconciled with the latter opinion by saying that if the debtor later became poor, Levi bears that risk, or that had Levi not accepted the assignment Reuven would have collected immediately before the impoverishment. Shach rejects this. If Levi had a right to retract beforehand and did not negligently delay collection, there is no reason to say that the debtor became poor at Levi's risk. Rama's wording also indicates that the distinctions of seifim 10 and 11 do not apply according to that latter opinion. Sema further says that even the latter opinion agrees that if Levi knew the debtor was poor he accepted the risk. Shach writes that this too requires study, for Levi can say that he accepted only because he expected the debtor to exert himself and pay what he could; one cannot assume he knowingly accepted a worthless claim.
The first opinion. Nevertheless, the distinctions stated below apply where Reuven expressly released the original debtor. Sema says so, and Darkei Moshe states explicitly that if there was deceit in the arrangement, such as the assigned debtor already being poor or not owing anything at all, everyone agrees that Levi may return even if he expressly released Reuven. Shach.
He showed him. Nimukei Yosef says that it is as though the debtor showed the creditor a place where his money lies bundled and ready. One cannot infer broadly that if a debtor tells a creditor, your money lies bundled in my house, come take it, and the creditor fails to do so and the money is then lost, the debtor is exempt. Here the place may be adjacent and convenient, or the creditor may have indicated that he does not mind going there. If the creditor objects to having to go to the debtor's house, this is not repayment and the debtor remains liable for unavoidable loss. Even ordinarily, the debtor is not exempt until the creditor shows that he does not object. See the end of siman 120. Sema.
