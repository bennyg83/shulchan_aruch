If the payer can prove by witnesses that he erred, that proof is effective; if he has a migo that he paid, why not believe his claim of error directly? Moreover, if the Tur is explaining why he did not investigate before admitting, the same question remains in the second case, where even witnesses were present but he can still claim error through a migo of payment. Sema writes that he may say he relied on the fact that before the due date the recipient could not cause him loss. The Tur should then have stated that reason. It appears that one is not concerned about making a doubtful admission when no witnesses are present, because he can later explain it. The Tur gives this reason to show that the Rosh, cited afterward, also agrees here despite holding that a person ordinarily investigates before admitting. Rama therefore properly writes that the stricter rule applies specifically where the admission was before witnesses. Even the Rosh agrees that without witnesses we do not say that a person necessarily investigated. The rule that a person investigates applies only where his sole defense is a migo, not where witnesses prove the error. This is also indicated by the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Gittin, who compares the case to one person saying, “You owe me a maneh,” and the other replying, “Tomorrow give it to him,” then later denying the debt. The parallel case in Bava Batra 6 expressly concerns an admission before witnesses. Likewise, the Tur in siman 79 speaks of being established as a denier, which cannot occur from a private admission. This appears clear, notwithstanding Maharshal's disagreement with Sema.
