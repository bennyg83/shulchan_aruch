And some say that he can retract, etc. The Tur cites this in the name of his father the Rosh, who writes so in chapter HaMekabel. The Beit Yosef is surprised because the Rosh there discusses workers and a shopkeeper who holds no money belonging to the employer. The Perishah answers, and the Bach inclines similarly, that since the Rosh holds there that even if the worker released the employer, the release is a mistaken waiver where the shopkeeper refuses to pay, the same should apply here to maamad sheloshtan. Maamad sheloshtan should not be stronger than an express release. Their words are not understandable. The Rosh says that the release is mistaken specifically where the shopkeeper retracts and refuses to pay the workers. If the shopkeeper remains willing to pay, the Rosh's view is that an explicit release of the employer is effective. Rabbah's ruling that the worker may retract concerns a case where he did not expressly release the employer. Perhaps the Tur follows the Sema's view stated above, that even where the worker released the employer, unless he expressly said "whether the shopkeeper pays or not I release you," Rabbah allows retraction. The Tur might therefore understand that maamad sheloshtan is no stronger than an ordinary release. But this is strained. Who fixed such a measure? Perhaps the Sages' acquisition mechanism of maamad sheloshtan is equivalent to an explicit comprehensive release in all respects. In my view the Tur's proof is different. The Rosh writes there that the workers' case does not involve maamad sheloshtan because the shopkeeper held none of the employer's money, and the Rosh therefore had to reconcile the Yerushalmi, which implies that the arrangement nevertheless works. It would have been much simpler for the Rosh to prove that it cannot be maamad sheloshtan because Rabbah holds that the worker may retract, whereas maamad sheloshtan is said to "acquire," implying that the original claimant is completely removed. The Rosh's failure to use that short proof demonstrates that he does not understand the word "acquire" in the maamad sheloshtan rule as necessarily preventing the original creditor from retracting. The word "acquire" may be stated principally because of a gift, as the Rivash writes. With a transferred loan, the original creditor may perhaps still retract. Therefore the Rosh needed another reason to show that the worker's case was not maamad sheloshtan. Once the word "acquire" itself does not compel complete removal of the first creditor, from where would we know that the lender cannot retract? Maamad sheloshtan is a rabbinic enactment, and wherever its language leaves doubt we revert to Torah law, under which the original borrower's lien to the first lender has not been extinguished. This explains the Tur. It is also possible that the Tur inferred from the Rosh's statement that according to Rav Sheshet, if the worker cannot retract, the shopkeeper becomes liable as a guarantor at the time the obligation is undertaken. The Ritva asks, as I wrote in siman 91, why the Sages then needed the enactment of maamad sheloshtan at all. One answer is that people do not wish to release the original debtor absolutely because they want to retain the ability to return to him. This itself supports the Tur's view that maamad sheloshtan does not necessarily eliminate that ability.
And it appears to me that one should rule according to the latter view. See Shach seif katan 38, who cites many poskim holding that the original creditor cannot retract. He also cites the Ritva in Kiddushin as supporting retraction. The Ritva argues that if the creditor could not retract, then even without the special enactment of maamad sheloshtan the second debtor would become obligated under Torah law once the recipient relied on him and released the first debtor, through the law of guaranty. Why then did the Sages describe maamad sheloshtan as a halachah without an ordinary rationale? The Shach rejects the proof, saying that the recipient cannot retract precisely because the Sages enacted maamad sheloshtan; without the enactment, the words would have had no legal force and he could have retracted. There is no substance to that response. The Ritva's intent is as follows. Why did the Sages need to create a novel enactment without a Torah-law basis merely for commercial convenience, as the poskim explain and as the Shach himself writes in seif katan 7, that a purchaser who lacks cash can place the seller in direct relation with his debtor? Torah law already provides a mechanism. The recipient can say to the new obligor, "I rely on you," and release the original purchaser or borrower. The new obligor would then be liable under the law of a guarantor, and all these consequences would follow by Torah law without uprooting or innovating anything. We must therefore say that the enactment was designed to work even where the recipient does not wish to release his lien against the original debtor. He wants both avenues available, to collect from whichever one he chooses. This proves that under maamad sheloshtan the recipient can return to the original debtor. The Ritva's reasoning is sound. To answer this proof according to those who hold that the recipient cannot return to the original debtor, one can say that the principal purpose of the enactment was for gifts. In a gift, the recipient has no preexisting claim and no Torah-law device equivalent to guaranty that could create the transfer so easily. The Sages therefore enacted maamad sheloshtan for gifts. Once the enactment existed, they applied the same mechanism to payment of debts so that debt transfers should not be weaker than gifts. But debt transfers alone would not have justified the enactment. The Ritva, however, holds as I wrote above that the commercial need that generated the enactment was specifically for sellers and lenders. A gratuitous gift is uncommon and does not require a market-facilitating enactment. The gift rule was added incidentally. His argument is therefore strong. In my view one can add that even according to those who ordinarily hold that the recipient cannot retract under maamad sheloshtan, this is only where nothing was stipulated. We then say that he knowingly accepted reliance on the second debtor. But if, at the time of maamad sheloshtan, he expressly stipulated that he retains the right to return to the first debtor, the transfer should still be effective according to its condition, and he may collect from whichever he chooses. This also answers the Ritva's question. Without the enactment of maamad sheloshtan, such an arrangement would require the recipient to release the first debtor absolutely in order to create the second debtor's Torah-law guaranty. The common commercial arrangement where the recipient wants to retain both claims would not be possible. The Sages therefore needed maamad sheloshtan.
