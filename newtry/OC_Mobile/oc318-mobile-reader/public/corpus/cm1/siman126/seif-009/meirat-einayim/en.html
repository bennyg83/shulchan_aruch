The Tur in the Rosh's name says that even if Levi remained silent, he can tell Reuven: "I agreed to accept from Shimon for your benefit, to make things easier for you, but I never released you if he fails to pay me." Ma'amad sheloshtan is an unusual enactment made for the benefit of the lender or depositor, and Chazal did not design it to cause loss to the recipient.
We do not need the distinctions written below in seif 10 according to this view. There the Mechaber says that if the designated payer turns out to be poor, the recipient may return to the giver. According to the Rosh, even if he is not poor, once he fails to pay upon demand the recipient may return to the giver, unless the giver was expressly released. Still, the case in seif 11, where the payer was wealthy and later became poor, applies even according to the Rosh: the giver can say that the recipient's own mazal caused the later poverty, or that had he not accepted the transfer the giver would have collected immediately before the payer became poor. The Rosh disagrees with Rif only where the designated payer simply delays and says "go and return" despite remaining able to pay. Likewise, if the recipient knew from the outset that the payer was poor, even the Rosh agrees that he accepted that risk. And where the recipient expressly released the giver, the later clause concerning a payer who was already poor at the transfer still matters if the giver deceived him about the payer's condition.
It is as though he showed him money to take for his debt and he did not take it. Nimukei Yosef compares it to showing the creditor a place where the debtor has tied money and saying, "Take it from there." This does not prove that a borrower who says, "Your money is tied in my house; come take it," is automatically exempt if the money is later lost. The comparison assumes the money is conveniently available to the lender, or the lender agreed to go there and does not object to the location, just as the recipient here agreed to collect from the designated payer. If the lender insists that repayment be brought to him, merely identifying money in the borrower's house is not payment unless he clearly accepts that arrangement.
If the bailee says he gave it, etc., see siman 91. There, where an employer prepaid a shopkeeper to provide food to workers and the workers agreed in ma'amad sheloshtan, the workers are removed from the employer and deal with the shopkeeper. That passage does not make the explicit distinction stated here by the Rosh because there the shopkeeper claims he actually paid the workers. Here the designated payer admits that he has not paid, and the dispute is whether the recipient may return to the original giver absent an express release.
