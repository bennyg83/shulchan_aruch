Levi claimed that Shimon had been poor, etc. Gedolei Terumah infers from the formulation that the case is not where Levi says, “I did not know that he was poor,” while Reuven says that Levi knew, for example because Reuven told him privately and Levi nevertheless accepted the assignment. He suggests that Levi would not be believed in such a case, because we are not dealing with fools who knowingly throw away money, and even with an oral debt this would be a migo against witnesses. In my view this is not correct. With a documented debt, if Reuven asserts with certainty that Levi knew of Shimon’s poverty, such as where Reuven had informed him, Reuven can require Levi to take an oath of heset that he did not know. This is no weaker than a claim that the creditor waived a documented debt, where the authorities indicate that an oath can be required after payment. With an oral debt, Levi can claim, “You knew he was poor and accepted him nevertheless,” with a migo that he could have denied the debt or claimed payment. We do not say that waiver is impossible because only a fool would waive a debt; perhaps the debtor appeased the creditor and he agreed to waive. The same possibility exists here. Reuven may have been persuaded to accept payment from Levi even knowing that Shimon was poor, perhaps because he expected Shimon to make every effort to pay, or even because he agreed to release Reuven entirely. The reason the authorities do not discuss this unusual case is simply that ordinarily the assignee would not knowingly accept an insolvent debtor. Another reason is that the discussion is framed around whether the document contains a credibility clause. A claim that “you knowingly accepted the poor debtor” resembles a claim of waiver, and regarding waiver there are two views whether a credibility clause prevents an oath at all, so the usual distinction between a document with or without credibility does not fit. By contrast, a claim that Shimon was solvent and later became poor is equivalent to a claim of payment, where the presence or absence of a credibility clause does matter.

Reuven must bring proof, etc. Gedolei Terumah asks why the ruling does not distinguish between an ordinary credibility clause and a clause granting credibility like two witnesses. In my view it is obvious that an explicit clause granting credibility equal to two witnesses defeats contrary testimony. The discussion concerns only ordinary credibility. There was no need to restate the law of enhanced credibility, since its effect against witnesses parallels the effect of ordinary credibility against an oath, as explained above in siman 71.
