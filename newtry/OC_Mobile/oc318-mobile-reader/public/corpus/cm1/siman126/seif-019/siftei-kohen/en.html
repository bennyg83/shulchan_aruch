It is like a guarantor at the time the money is given. The Tur explains that the guarantor becomes liable without an act of acquisition because Levi previously owed Shimon nothing and now undertakes the obligation during the very transaction. Since he becomes guarantor at that moment, his liability is like a guarantor at the time a loan is made. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes that although a guarantor who undertakes liability after the loan requires an acquisition, here the recipient had no prior claim against the assigned debtor. The assigned debtor now accepts the obligation and the recipient relies upon him, so the case resembles the time of the original loan and no acquisition is needed. This is certainly true where the recipient had a claim against the assignor and now releases him in reliance upon the guarantor's undertaking.
There is one who disagrees. The Mechaber's words here and in Beit Yosef require study. Beit Yosef cites Baal HaTerumot's rule and then says that Rabbeinu Yerucham records a dissenting opinion requiring an acquisition, based on Tosafot in Gittin. On that basis he writes here, “There is one who disagrees.” Ir Shushan's explanation is unclear. I examined Rabbeinu Yerucham and found only a case of one who becomes guarantor for a person who formally obligates himself to give a gift. Although the recipient suffered no monetary loss, the guarantor may become liable because the undertaking was not made in performance of a mitzvah. Rabbeinu Yerucham cites a dispute whether this liability requires an acquisition and favors the view that it does not. Beit Yosef apparently assumed that a gift and an assignment before all three follow the same rule. This is not necessary. In a gift case the recipient has suffered no loss at all. In an assignment, the assignor originally owed the recipient, and the recipient now substitutes the assigned debtor, who undertakes payment. That transaction resembles the time a loan is made because the recipient relinquishes his prior debtor. Baal HaTerumot himself proves that the cases differ. In gate 28 he rules without qualification that a guarantor of an assignment before all three is liable because the transaction resembles the time of the loan. At the beginning of gate 35 he cites Raavad that a guarantor of a gift requires a complete acquisition. Thus a gift requires an acquisition, while an assignment does not. Where the assignor actually owed the recipient and the recipient expressly released him, even Beit Yosef must agree that the guarantor is liable without an acquisition. There is no greater reliance and loss than releasing the original debtor because of the new undertaking. The Mechaber's phrase, “There is one who disagrees,” must therefore refer either to a case where the assignor owed nothing, or to a case where he owed money but was not expressly released, according to the view that the recipient may still return to him. According to my conclusion above that whether the recipient may return is a doubtful issue and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, the recipient generally cannot recover from the assignor. Therefore, where the assignor originally owed him and the recipient released him, the guarantor is liable because the recipient suffered actual loss. The guarantor cannot argue that perhaps the law permits recovery from the assignor and therefore no loss occurred. Since beit din cannot compel the assignor to pay, the assignment and guarantee caused the recipient to lose his original claim, and the guarantor is liable. This differs from a slaughterer who creates only a doubtful tereifah and is exempt, because there the animal may truly be kosher and no liability may exist at all. Here the guarantee certainly occurred, and the only question is whether another avenue of recovery remains. Since it cannot be judicially enforced, the guarantor's undertaking caused the loss.
