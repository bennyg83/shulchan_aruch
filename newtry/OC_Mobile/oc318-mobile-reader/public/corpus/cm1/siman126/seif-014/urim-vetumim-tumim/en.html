This requires study. There is another difficult statement in Rashba. At the end of the first chapter of Gittin he writes that if the owner of the money does not know that an error occurred but the transferor says that he erred, and the intermediary has no assets, the transferor is not believed. One cannot say he has a migo that he could waive the debt, because that case involved a deposit and waiver does not apply to a deposit: immediately upon the maamad sheloshtan acquisition, the property entered the assignee's domain. I am astonished. If it is a deposit, the intermediary should plainly be believed to say that he erred by a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident, as we say everywhere. The Shulchan Aruch's closing phrase, "the depositary must give it to the assignee," likewise shows that it discusses a deposit. Why all the difficulty if there is a migo of unavoidable accident? For the Shulchan Aruch one can say the case concerns a specific object transferred in maamad sheloshtan and there are witnesses who saw it, so the depositary cannot claim unavoidable accident. He can only say, "I erred, for it was never yours." But Rashba's original incident involved money, regarding which an unavoidable accident can still be claimed under the ruling like Ran. The matter therefore requires study.
And he then recovers it from the transferor. The Shach, seif katan 67, asks why, since the transferor's role is only indirect causation. In truth one can say this is dina d'garmei. The transferor said, "You have one hundred of mine in your possession, give it to so and so." Because maamad sheloshtan works even against the holder's will, the holder, who believed he owed the money, was compelled to admit and pay. Now that it is established that the transferor was mistaken, it was through his direct causation that the holder was made to pay improperly, and he is therefore liable. It appears, however, that the Tur and Baal HaTerumot follow their position that dina d'garmei is substantive law. On that view, even if the transferor erred, he still caused the loss and must pay. According to authorities cited by the Shach below in siman 386 who treat liability for garmei as a penalty, what penalty is appropriate here? Both parties innocently erred without attention. There is no misconduct to punish. According to those authorities he should therefore not be liable. This ruling depends upon the underlying dispute. We must also say that the transferor continues to maintain that he erred. If he retracts that statement, he may be able to say, "I admitted the matter only in order to fend off my creditor, but in truth I do have money in your hands," as explained above in siman 47. The distinction made there, that the admission occurred without a creditor's demand, does not apply here. A formal transfer against him is itself the strongest possible demand.
