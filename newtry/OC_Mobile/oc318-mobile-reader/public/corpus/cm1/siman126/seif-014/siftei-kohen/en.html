If the assignor admits that he erred in the accounting. This refers to a case where the assigned debtor is not independently believed to say that there was an error, under the rules stated in seif 13.
If the assignor has assets from which to pay, etc. The Rashba writes that even if the assignor can pay, his admission of error does not automatically free the assigned debtor. The recipient can say: if the assignor is telling the truth, let him pay now, either to me or to the debtor. This is unlike a person who admits that a document was merely one of accommodation, because there the obligor can satisfy the claimant from other assets. Here the recipient already withdrew from the assignor and relied on the assigned debtor in his place. I understand the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber differently. Where the assignor has assets, is ready to pay, and admits an accounting error, his admission is inherently credible because it causes him a loss while causing the recipient no loss at all. The recipient can collect from the assignor just as he would have collected from the assigned debtor. It is therefore improper for the recipient to insist that he already withdrew from the assignor when he suffers no prejudice and the assignor’s truthfulness is evident. Compelling the recipient to accept payment from the assignor is a case of preventing the attribute of Sodom, especially where the assigned debtor never owed the recipient independently. If, however, the assignor is a violent or inaccessible person, his admission is not accepted because it would cause real prejudice to the recipient, who may then say that he already withdrew from the assignor and relied on the assigned debtor. This also answers the Bach’s question why the Tur and Mechaber in siman 47 did not distinguish between an ordinary and a violent obligor. There, even if he is violent, the debtor can satisfy the claimant from other property and is not required to surrender the particular document.
And even if there is one witness, etc. The Ran explains that the oath imposed because of one witness testifying to payment is only a rabbinic device in this context: where the claimant can swear, he swears; where he cannot, as with a recipient who does not know the underlying facts and cannot contradict the witness, he does not lose merely because he cannot swear. The Sema understands the Rama to apply this not only to the specific episode discussed by the Ran but even to an unrelated witness. The Rashba says similarly. Where the assignor has assets, the assignor is not considered interested testimony merely because he admits the error, and even so his admission alone does not defeat the recipient after the recipient has withdrawn from him. The Rama’s gloss concerning one witness appears to refer to the case where the assignor has no assets. There the assignor is interested, because there is concern for collusion between him and the assigned debtor; even with another witness, the combined testimony is still not enough to defeat the recipient in the manner proposed. A difficulty remains where the assignor has assets but is a violent person. In that case he is not interested, because he will have to repay the recipient, and if there is an additional witness there would seem to be two witnesses freeing the assigned debtor. It is forced to say that the Rama still treats the assignor as interested because he might divide the money with the assigned debtor and make collection difficult. Perhaps the Rama’s gloss applies only where the assignor has no assets. This requires study.
One witness who supports his statement is ineffective. The case is one where the assigned debtor is not otherwise believed to claim an error by a migo of payment, and without the witness he would be required to pay. A single witness cannot directly exempt him from money. If, however, without the witness the assigned debtor would be believed to claim error by a migo that he could deny the transaction or claim payment, but would merely have to swear, then one supporting witness exempts him from the oath, even though his substantive credibility rests on a migo, as explained above in siman 87, seif 6.
But if it was a gift, he is not believed at all, etc. His admission would prejudice the gift recipient, because the donor need not repay a gift recipient. The donor can say, “Had I known there was an error, I would never have directed the assigned debtor to give you the money.” A person is not believed by an admission that harms another, because there is concern for collusion: perhaps he now regrets the gift, says that the assigned debtor’s claim of error is true, and intends to divide the money with him.
And he returns and collects it from the assignor. The Tur writes that because the assigned debtor admits that he owed the assignor nothing, and through the assignor’s direction he became obligated to pay the recipient, he may recover that amount from the assignor, whether the transfer was made to satisfy a debt or as a gift. The Ir Shushan and Sema follow this. I find the ruling difficult in the gift case. The assignor can say: had I known there was an error, I would never have instructed you to pay; the fact that beit din compelled you to pay because of your own admission is at most indirect causation, for which one is ordinarily exempt. Moreover, I too made an innocent accounting error, and you caused your own loss by also failing to remember the error and by admitting liability. Where the assignment satisfied the assignor’s debt, repayment to the assigned debtor is understandable because the assigned debtor paid the assignor’s debt with authorization and under legal compulsion, which is like paying with the debtor’s knowledge. But a gift remains difficult. I found this rule in Baal HaTerumot, but it appears to have been written summarily and should be limited to a case where the assignor undertook to reimburse the assigned debtor if it turned out that nothing was owed. For example, the assigned debtor may have been uncertain and agreed to acknowledge the debt to the recipient only because the assignor assured him privately that even if an error were later discovered, he should lend the money and pay, and the assignor would reimburse him. Or the assigned debtor may have refused to acknowledge until the assignor promised to reimburse him if an error was later discovered. In such a case, once the assigned debtor relied on that promise and became obligated by his admission, the assignor cannot say that he should not have paid. Although these readings are somewhat forced, they are preferable to the plain reading, which produces a difficult and unsupported liability.
