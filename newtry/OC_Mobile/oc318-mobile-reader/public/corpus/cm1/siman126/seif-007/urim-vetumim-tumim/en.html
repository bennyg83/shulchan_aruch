He must give it to him against his will, etc. See the Shach, who cites many poskim holding that maamad sheloshtan cannot be imposed against the holder's will. I have a point of precision from the Ramban's chiddushim to Bava Batra, where his view appears also to be that it can operate against the holder's will. Otherwise, why did the Ramban labor in the baraita of the four rules governing sellers to debate the Rashbam's explanation whether it lists four distinct domains? The Rashbam explains that even in the seller's own domain the buyer can acquire if the seller rents or transfers use of the courtyard to him. See Rashbam and Tosafot. One could have interpreted the clause "in the domain of the person with whom the goods are deposited, once he accepts upon himself the buyer acquires" simply as maamad sheloshtan. The buyer or his agent must in any event be present, because the baraita speaks of removing or lifting the goods. Once the depositary accepts, all three are present and the buyer acquires immediately. This would indeed produce four distinct domains, because that mechanism would not apply in the seller's own domain. Tosafot likely refuse this explanation because in their view maamad sheloshtan works even against the holder's will. The baraita's statement that the depositary "accepted upon himself" would then be unnecessary, proving that the clause does not concern maamad sheloshtan. But if the Ramban held that maamad sheloshtan requires consent, he should have interpreted the baraita exactly this way and thereby preserved four distinct domains. Perhaps the Ramban found this reading strained because the baraita says specifically that the goods are "in the domain of the person with whom they are deposited," implying that actual presence of the goods in his domain matters. Under maamad sheloshtan, however, once the original depositary accepts the transfer it should not matter that the goods are physically elsewhere. Still, this seems a limited reason to reject the interpretation. It therefore appears that even according to those who hold that maamad sheloshtan cannot operate against an express protest, silence is sufficient. They require only that the holder not object. Therefore the baraita cannot mean maamad sheloshtan, because its requirement that the depositary affirmatively "accept" would be unnecessary according to all opinions. So too, the Mishnah says, "Give a maneh to so and so; they shall give it after death," and Rabbi Zeira explains it as maamad sheloshtan. The Mishnah does not say that the holders answered yes or expressly accepted. Evidently their silence is sufficient. This is clear.
