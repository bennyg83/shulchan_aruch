Even if he did not consent. Others dispute this, as the Tur records. The Mechaber follows his own ruling in Beit Yosef, based on his understanding that Tosafot, the Rosh, and Rashba agree. In truth, Rashba in his novellae at the end of the first chapter of Gittin rules the opposite. Beit Yosef also writes that the Rosh ruled oppositely in responsa, rule 69, although this requires study, for that ruling does not appear there, and the responsum seems to accord with the Rosh's published rulings. Maggid Mishneh on chapter 6 of Hilchot Mechirah records the dissent, as does Ran at the end of the first chapter of Gittin and in responsum 69. Baal HaTerumot, gate 28, also holds that an assignment in the presence of all three parties is ineffective against the assignee's will. Nimukei Yosef writes that if the debtor says, “I do not wish to give it to him,” the recipient does not acquire, because the enactment applies only with the debtor's consent. He cites this from later authorities. Nimukei Yosef also writes this in Bava Kamma in the name of Raah, and Ritva rules so in Kiddushin. Baal HaIttur likewise writes that the borrower may say, “I will pay only you.” This appears to be the primary understanding of the Gemara and of the expression “in the presence of all three,” which implies that all three stand together and consent. If acquisition occurred against the debtor's will, why was this special enactment needed? Absence should be no worse than an explicit refusal, and it would have sufficed merely to notify the debtor. The Sages instead required all three to stand together because their mutual consent is necessary. Therefore it is ineffective outside his presence, lest he not consent. Even where his consent is otherwise known, the Sages did not distinguish and required the formal presence of all three, where consent is certain. Maharshal rules likewise in Bava Kamma, siman 27. The Mechaber's position also requires study because in seif 5 he himself cites the dissenting view.
