He is believed to say, "I erred." See Baer Heitev and Knesset HaGedolah above, siman 79, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 9, who writes: "If one wrote and sent a reply to another person's complaints, carefully specifying that the account was such and such, it is a complete admission and he cannot say, 'I erred.'" This is from Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 71. Mahari Adarbi, siman 405, likewise writes that in letters sent by one engaged in business, a person is careful and precise in his accounting. Maharshadam, however, disagrees in the case of Maharibal concerning handwriting and holds that he is believed to say he erred. So too Sefer HaMapeh, siman 126, seif 13, and it appears that they likewise disagree with Mahari Adarbi and Mishpetei Shmuel. See Noda B'Yehudah, second edition, siman 15, concerning a similar incident. One person sent money to his merchant on account of his debt and wrote in a letter that he would soon send another fifty gold coins. He later claimed that he had erred in his account. Noda B'Yehudah cites the above Knesset HaGedolah and writes that at first glance, according to Sema, seif katan 41, it is not necessary to say that the Rama disagrees with Mahari Adarbi and Mishpetei Shmuel. The Sema writes in the name of Darkei Moshe that a person's signature is not like an admission before witnesses, because he can say that the document was merely an amanah or that he signed his name on a blank sheet. According to this, where the entire letter is in his own handwriting, he cannot say that he signed a blank sheet, and he cannot claim amanah because the letter contains other substantive matters. Such a letter would therefore be like an admission before witnesses, and he would not be believed to say he erred. After examining Darkei Moshe itself, however, Noda B'Yehudah concludes that Knesset HaGedolah is correct: the Rama also disagrees with Mishpetei Shmuel and Mahari Adarbi and holds that even a letter entirely in one's own handwriting, containing other matters, is not equivalent to an admission before witnesses. Although the writer cannot claim amanah or that he signed a blank sheet, he may nevertheless say "I erred" by virtue of a migo that he could say "I repaid." Moreover, the Shach rules that even against an admission before witnesses one is believed to say "I erred" with a migo of repayment. Noda B'Yehudah adds that even according to those who hold one is not believed against his own handwriting to say "I erred" with a migo of repayment, their reasoning is that a person ordinarily checks his accounts carefully before giving his written acknowledgment to the opposing party as evidence of the debt. This applies where the writing itself was given for that purpose, and likewise in the cases of Mishpetei Shmuel and Mahari Adarbi, where the main purpose of the letter was to state the account. In the case before him, however, the main purpose of the letter was to accompany the money being sent. The writer said, in effect, "Do not be upset that I am not sending more now, because I will send another fifty gold coins soon." The letter was not principally intended as an admission that he still owed fifty; the statement was written incidentally. Perhaps even the stricter authorities would therefore agree that he is believed to say he erred in his calculation with a migo of repayment. Accordingly, the questioning rabbi ruled correctly that the defendant should swear and be exempt. These are his words. See there.
