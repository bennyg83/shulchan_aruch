Maharam Alshich, responsum 124, discusses the following case. Shimon sold a house to Reuven, accepted responsibility to remove all claims against it, and subjected movable property together with land. Shimon later deposited gold rings with Reuven, and then instructed Reuven in maamad sheloshtan to give those rings to Levi. After Reuven agreed, an earlier purchaser appeared and dispossessed Reuven from the house. Reuven then sought to retain the rings, arguing that had he known this would happen he would never have agreed to give them to Levi. At first glance, since the rings had not come into Reuven's hands as collateral for the purchase price if an earlier purchaser should remove him, but merely as a deposit, Reuven could not have withheld them from Shimon before the dispossession merely out of concern that an earlier purchaser might later seize the house. Since Shimon could have taken the rings back, he could also transfer them to another by a kinyan or by maamad sheloshtan. Maharam Alshich nevertheless concludes on another ground that Reuven may retain them: even had they already been delivered to Levi, Reuven's earlier instrument, which included a lien on movables together with land, would allow him to recover them from Levi. Although nowadays one does not collect movable property from a purchaser because of market protection, the Rosh and Tur write in siman 60 that this protection does not apply to sold debt instruments, because such sales are uncommon and the note never entirely leaves the seller's control, since he retains the power to waive it. If so, all the more should Reuven be able to recover where the physical deposit is still in his own possession. Maharam Alshich further writes that even if Levi were an earlier creditor and Reuven a later creditor, if Reuven collected first he would keep what he collected, as the Beit Yosef establishes in siman 60, and all the more so if Levi received the rings as a gift. These words are surprising. If Levi is the earlier creditor, how can Reuven acquire the rings merely because a later creditor collected first? The rule that a later creditor who collected first keeps what he collected applies only while the movables remain the debtor's property. Once the debtor has sold them to another, a later creditor plainly cannot seize them, and if he does we remove them from him and return them to the purchaser. A maamad sheloshtan transfer is a complete sale for all legal purposes. Levi therefore owned the rings fully from the moment of the assignment. The reference to the Beit Yosef in siman 60 points in the opposite direction, for the Beit Yosef means that where an earlier creditor had a lien on movables together with land and a later creditor collected those movables, the earlier creditor may recover them from him, because market protection does not apply to a later creditor who did not purchase them. Thus, if Levi is the earlier creditor and has such a lien, he would recover from Reuven. Even according to what I wrote above in siman 60 that an earlier creditor does not recover from a later creditor who already collected, that is because the property was merely encumbered to the first creditor. Here, by contrast, the rings had already been sold outright to Levi from the moment of maamad sheloshtan. Even if Reuven is earlier than Levi, I remain doubtful. Since the accepted law is that today one does not recover sold movables because of market protection, Reuven could not recover them had they reached Levi's hands. Why then should their current physical location with Reuven make a difference? Levi acquired them absolutely from the moment of assignment. Debt instruments are different because their sale is uncommon and they never entirely leave the seller's control, so the Sages did not extend market protection to them. But with ordinary movables, the lien on movables together with land is treated as though not written as against a purchaser. It is therefore difficult to say that the protection applies only where the movables physically reached the purchaser's house but not where they remain in the earlier creditor's hands, once the purchaser already acquired them through a full kinyan such as maamad sheloshtan.
Maharam Alshich's initial statement also requires precision. He says Reuven could not retain the deposit merely because Shimon had been able to demand it back before the earlier purchaser appeared. Even if Shimon already owed Reuven a definite debt and Reuven therefore could have retained the rings, once Reuven did not retain them, admitted that they were held merely as a deposit, and agreed to give them to Levi, Levi acquired them. This is also implied by the authorities.
