He said to him in maamad sheloshtan, etc. The source of this rule is a responsum of the Rosh, klal 69. Reuven directed Shimon to pay Levi, Levi accepted the transfer, and Shimon already paid him part. Shimon now says that he was mistaken and in truth held nothing belonging to Reuven. The Rosh rules that there certainly is no obligation through maamad sheloshtan, because if Shimon was mistaken and held none of Reuven's property, maamad sheloshtan does not apply. Moreover, even if he did hold Reuven's money, since Reuven did not identify "the maneh in your hand" but said only generally, "Give Levi," maamad sheloshtan does not apply according to the Rosh. Nevertheless, what Shimon already paid cannot be recovered. Perhaps he preferred to uphold his own reliability. Moreover, he is not believed to say that he made a mistake regarding what he already paid, because before paying a person certainly checks carefully. Tosafot hold that one is not believed to say "I made a mistake" even through a migo that he could have said "I paid," because there is a presumption that a person checks carefully and does not err. All the commentators struggle with this responsum. If there were no witnesses proving Shimon's mistake, how could the Rosh exempt him regarding the unpaid balance on the ground that maamad sheloshtan does not apply because he held none of Reuven's money? Shimon should not be believed to say he was mistaken, and we should assume that he did hold Reuven's money. But if witnesses proved the mistake, how could the Rosh say that as to the amount already paid we assume Shimon checked carefully and did not err? The witnesses establish that he did err. See Shach seif katan 72, whose resolutions are all strained. The Perishah's answer is more plausible. He explains that the case was after the due date, so Shimon could in theory have claimed payment. Nevertheless, the Rosh did not believe the error claim merely through that migo, but there was another migo that he could have denied the underlying obligation entirely, and through that stronger migo he was believed regarding the unpaid amount. As to what he already paid, the Rosh reasons all the more strongly that there is no migo at all, since the money is already gone. The Rosh's citation that even a migo of payment does not support an error claim merely reinforces the point. The Perishah's wording is somewhat cumbersome, but the basic thought is clear: with respect to money already paid, there is no migo. Even so, if the crucial basis for Shimon's right to plead error is that no witnesses were present at the maamad sheloshtan, that essential fact is entirely missing from the Rosh's responsum. How could the Rosh omit the very circumstance on which the entire ruling depends? In my view no such assumption is needed. The Rosh and Ramban disagree in the rule explained above in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. The Ramban holds that even if the transferor did not expressly say "the maneh of mine in your hand," if such a maneh actually is in the holder's possession, a general instruction suffices for maamad sheloshtan. The Rosh disagrees. In this responsum the Rosh seeks to exempt Shimon according to either position. He therefore gives two reasons: first, even if one accepted the Ramban's view that this language can create maamad sheloshtan, the facts here still fail because Shimon says he actually held none of Reuven's money. Second, the correct law is the Rosh's own view against the Ramban, that maamad sheloshtan requires express reference to "the maneh of mine in your hand," and that language was absent here. Once this is understood, the apparent contradiction disappears. The presumption that one who accepts maamad sheloshtan checks carefully and cannot later say "I erred" applies where the transferor expressly said, "The maneh of mine in your hand, give it to Levi," and the holder accepted. He thereby explicitly admitted that he held the transferor's maneh, so we say he certainly checked and is not believed to claim error afterward. But where the transferor merely said, "Give a maneh to Levi," and the holder accepted, even if one follows the Ramban that maamad sheloshtan can technically operate when funds were in fact held, the holder is still believed to say later that he was mistaken. His words contained no explicit admission that he held the transferor's money and need not have been based on a careful accounting. It is common for one person to say to another, "Give me a maneh on credit and pay it to so and so." Such language does not itself show that the recipient carefully verified an existing balance. Therefore the Rosh correctly says that because Reuven did not mention any existing debt, Shimon is believed to say that he was mistaken, even if we assume for argument's sake that such language could have created maamad sheloshtan had money actually been owed. The Rosh then adds his own legal view that even if Shimon truly had held Reuven's money, the transfer would still not bind because the formula "the maneh of mine in your hand" was not used. Everything fits well. Finally, regarding what Shimon already paid, the Rosh says that he is not believed to recover it by saying he was mistaken. Here the presumption of careful checking returns with full force. A person normally checks before actually paying out money. The Rosh cites the broader principle that an error claim is so weak that even a migo of payment does not ordinarily support it. Shimon's error claim was accepted only because the language of the transfer never indicated that he had checked an actual existing debt. Once he actually paid, however, we presume he did check. There is no migo regarding money already paid in any event. In my view the Rosh's words are simple and clear and leave no real difficulty.
Admittedly there remains a substantive question where witnesses truly establish that Shimon was mistaken. Can he recover what he already paid? The Bach holds that he cannot, adopting as primary the Rosh's first reason that Shimon may have preferred to maintain his reliability. The Shach writes that the Rosh offered that only as an additional consideration, while the principal reason was that Shimon is not believed to say he erred. According to the Shach, if witnesses establish the mistake he may recover. The Shach leaves the matter requiring study. It appears to me that the Bach is correct, and this also seems to follow from the Shulchan Aruch's unqualified language. I would add another reason. If Shimon knew from the outset that he owed Reuven nothing, yet nevertheless, at Reuven's direction, gave Levi a dinar, would anyone imagine that Shimon could later take it back from Levi? A shopkeeper who, at an employer's direction, pays workers cannot recover the wages from the workers merely because he later decides he should not have paid. His claim lies against the employer whose instruction he followed. If witnesses afterward establish that Shimon was mistaken, we presume that before the actual payment he checked carefully and then learned of the mistake. This is an even stronger application of the presumption that a person checks before paying. He therefore knowingly chose to pay Levi on Reuven's instruction despite the mistake, and Levi acquired what he received. The witnesses can testify that objectively the original account was mistaken, but can they testify that Shimon remained unaware of that mistake at the moment of payment? Only Hashem knows what is in a person's heart. Since there are no witnesses to his subjective ignorance, the presumption that he checked before payment remains in place. We therefore say that he knew and nevertheless paid as Reuven's agent. His recourse is against Reuven, not Levi. This is simple.
