He cannot recover it from him. The Tur explains that no liability arises as a guarantor, because the undertaking was made after the underlying obligation, and the enactment of assignment before all three does not apply because nothing was actually in the debtor's hands. Nevertheless, money already paid cannot be recovered. We presume that he paid in order to uphold his word even if he knew that no formal obligation had arisen, and only afterward changed his mind regarding the balance. Moreover, one who seeks to recover money is not believed to say that he erred; we presume he carefully checked before admitting. The Sema understands the case as one where the original admission was not made before witnesses, because an admission before witnesses would bar a claim of error even regarding the unpaid balance. The Rosh's responsum, however, may be understood as involving witnesses. Initially the Rosh offered two possible defenses: a claim of error, following Baal HaTerumot, and the failure to specify the particular maneh. At the end he imposed liability on two grounds: that error cannot be claimed, following Tosafot, and that money already paid cannot be recovered because the payer wished to preserve his credibility. Mahari ben Lev reads the responsum this way. Baal HaTerumot would also agree that money already paid cannot be recovered, because once payment occurred there is no longer a migo of payment. The Rosh could first mention Baal HaTerumot's view as one of two grounds for exemption and later reject that ground while retaining the independent ground that no specific maneh was identified. The Rosh may even be speaking where the error can be proved by witnesses and still hold that paid money cannot be recovered because the payer effectively waived his rights in order to uphold his word. Bach reads it this way. Yet this practical conclusion requires study. Although the Rosh appears to hold so, common reasoning suggests that proven error should permit recovery. The Rosh's analogy to one who sells future palm fruit and allows the buyer to seize and eat it is not decisive. There, the seller could have objected and, by remaining silent, is presumed to have transferred the fruit fully. Here the money had already reached the recipient, and the payer sued as soon as he discovered the error. It therefore appears that the Rosh did not rely upon waiver alone. His principal reason was that the payer is not believed to claim error after actual payment.
