Whether a loan, etc. The Shach in seif katan 1 cites conflicting opinions whether maamad sheloshtan applies before the debt's due date. In my view, there is a clear proof from the Mechaber above in siman 122 that it does apply before the due date. At first glance there is a clear proof for the Rosh's view that an agent appointed before witnesses cannot compel the depositary to pay him, contrary to those poskim who hold that if there is no separate dispute the holder must pay the agent, as Maharshal and the Shach discuss at length above. The Rosh's reason, that we are concerned the principal may revoke the agency, also appears compelled, contrary to the reason stated in the Shulchan Aruch that perhaps the principal may die. The proof is the incident of Isur the convert. Rava was concerned about every possible method by which Rav Mari might acquire the money and said that if the transfer required maamad sheloshtan, he would not attend. Why was all this necessary? Isur could simply appoint Rav Mari bar Rachel as an agent before witnesses to collect his deposit. Rav Mari could go to Rava, compel him to pay, and thereby acquire the money for himself. Why was maamad sheloshtan needed? The Rosh himself proves from this incident that a person transferring a claim by maamad sheloshtan cannot simply appoint an agent to stand in his place. Otherwise, even if Rava refused to attend, Isur could appoint an agent to go to Rava. But the same question is stronger: why not appoint an ordinary agent before witnesses to collect his property from Rava, whether Rav Mari himself or another? We must conclude like the Rosh that a depositary is not compelled to give the property to such an agent. If an agent appears, the depositary can say, "You are not my litigant." There was likewise no concern that Isur could use a formal authorization. According to the Rambam, an authorization requires an intrinsically effective kinyan, and Rava expressly said that no such kinyan was possible there because coins are not acquired by chalipin and Isur owned no land for an agav transfer. Even according to those who disagree with the Rambam, that broader authorization mechanism follows the Raavan's explanation that it was a later enactment of the Nehardeans. Before that enactment, in the days of Rav Safra and Rava, agav karka was specifically required. Since Isur had no land, authorization was not a threat. The Rosh's ruling is therefore compelled. There remains, however, a question about the reason. If the concern is that the sender may die, this is understandable with ordinary people, because the heirs may later come and demand payment a second time if the agent suffers an accident. But with Isur the convert there were no heirs. If Isur died and the money was then lost through unavoidable accident in the agent's hands, there would be no one to claim from Rava. If Rava were somehow liable to pay, he would merely retain what had become ownerless. Nor can Rava refuse because perhaps Isur has already died, in which case Rava himself acquired the deposit and does not wish to give it to the agent. We certainly do not treat mere death as so likely. Indeed, even in the stricter law of forbidden relationships we do not ordinarily fear that a person has died, even an elderly sick person, as the Rashba writes in his chiddushim to Bava Kamma. The Sema and Perishah explain that in monetary matters the holder can protest, "Why should I risk losing my money because of the possibility that the principal will die?" The concern is accepted only because paying the agent may expose him to actual loss. Where there is no such risk but only the loss of a possible windfall, such as losing the chance to acquire ownerless property if Isur has died, the concern cannot be greater than in the law of a get, where death is not presumed. Moreover, Isur and Rava were in the same place. The agent would go from one house to another almost immediately. How could we realistically fear death within that moment? Therefore the Rosh's different reason is necessary: perhaps the principal will revoke the agency. That concern applies even to Isur. He could revoke the agency; if the deposit were then lost in the agent's hands and Isur recovered from his illness, he could claim it from Rava again. The Rosh's reasoning is therefore compelled. Yet according to the Shulchan Aruch, which as explained above is not concerned for revocation but only for death and follows the Baal HaItur, the original question returns. This is especially so according to those who do not follow the Raavan and hold that a simple authorization was always effective for collection, not only after the Nehardean enactment. Why could Isur not simply give an authorization? We must therefore say that the deposit held by Rava had a fixed term and that Rava was entitled to use the money until that term. The term thus benefited the depositary as well, and Isur could not demand payment before maturity, as explained above in siman 78. Even a thousand authorizations could not compel Rava to return it before the agreed term, because the permission to use the funds was part of the basis on which he accepted the deposit. The Sages' enactment of meshichah for guardians likewise prevents unilateral withdrawal before the term. But then another difficulty arises. How could Rava say, "If it is through maamad sheloshtan, I will not go"? If the debt was not yet due, perhaps maamad sheloshtan itself should not apply. We must therefore conclude that maamad sheloshtan is effective even before the due date. The rule stated above is thereby compelled. This is precise.
Whether he gave it to him in payment of his debt. The Shach in seif katan 6 cites Maharam Alashkar concerning Reuven, who said to Shimon in the presence of all three, "The maneh in your hand, give it to Levi so that Levi will purchase merchandise for me." Levi does not acquire the money. The Shach explains that maamad sheloshtan applies only where the assignee is completely substituted for the original creditor and the original creditor retains no right in the transferred money. Here, however, the funds Levi receives still belong beneficially to Reuven, because Levi is merely to buy merchandise for him. The Shach gives no proof, but the rule is evident from Bava Metzia 71b. The Gemara discusses a non-Jew who borrowed from a Jew and then, in the non-Jew's presence, instructed another Jew to pay the first Jew's money to him. The Gemara says that the prohibition in that case might be understood as a stringency, but then asks about the reverse case where a Jew gives his money in the presence of a non-Jew to another Jew, where the baraita permits it. Why should it be permitted? Since a non-Jew has no law of agency, the second Jew is seemingly taking interest directly from the first. The difficulty is obvious. Why not say that maamad sheloshtan applies and completely removes the first borrower from the obligation, so that the assignee acquires? Once the Sages enacted that the original debtor is discharged, the second party should be substituted completely. This is especially true in the case where the non-Jew was the debtor, because the second Jew would then become the direct borrower of the first Jew and the non-Jew would be fully removed. It should plainly be prohibited. Tosafot there, beginning "For example," ultimately conclude that we do not apply the transaction stringently merely because of the possible agency theory, and if the money did not physically pass from hand to hand it is in fact permitted. Yet the arrangement still looks exactly like maamad sheloshtan, which everyone agrees can apply even where one of the participants is a non-Jew, as stated below in seif 22. We must therefore say that where the transferee is not being paid his own debt and is not receiving an outright gift, but receives the funds only on credit or to use on behalf of the original creditor, the original creditor retains an interest in the funds and maamad sheloshtan does not apply. This is precisely the Shach's reason. In the Gemara's case everything concerns loans, so the original party's interest continues and maamad sheloshtan does not operate. The matter is thus well understood and precise.
The giver cannot retract or forgive it, etc. See what I wrote above in siman 66 at length regarding this. The Shach in seif katan 10 concludes that regarding kiddushin one must take into account those authorities who hold that even after maamad sheloshtan the original creditor can forgive the debt. According to that view the woman may not be betrothed because the consideration remains uncertain. This is correct and there is no doubt about it. However I saw that the Knesset HaGedolah cites Maharam Galanti as holding that according to the opinion that maamad sheloshtan does not effect kiddushin, it likewise does not effect purchase of land, because the two are dependent on one another. In my view this is not correct. The reason some authorities say that the woman is not betrothed is that the original creditor can forgive the debt. She therefore does not rely confidently on the transferred claim because he may later forgive it. Tosafot in Bava Batra, chapter Mi Shemet, ask why this matters, since one who causes such loss through actionable causation must pay damages, so if the husband later forgives the debt he would have to reimburse her. They answer either according to the preferred view that one who gives the claim as a gift and later forgives it need not reimburse the recipient, because he did not directly deprive her of her own property, or that a wife is embarrassed to sue her husband in beit din for damages and therefore does not rely on the claim. Neither answer applies to an ordinary purchase of land. Maamad sheloshtan itself is a full acquisition mechanism. Were it not for the special possibility of forgiveness, even the woman would certainly be betrothed. In a land purchase, where those concerns do not negate reliance, the acquisition therefore works. This is simple.
