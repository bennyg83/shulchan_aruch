A loan. There is no distinction between an oral loan and a documented loan. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch state in siman 66, seif 19, that even if the lender does not deliver the physical promissory note to the recipient, the recipient acquires the debt through ma'amad sheloshtan. Responsa Maharam Alshich 5 states likewise. Shach.
A deposit. The same applies where one became liable to another because he damaged or humiliated him, even though no property of the injured person ever came into the debtor's hands. For example, if Shimon struck Reuven and beit din imposed a monetary obligation, Reuven may transfer what is due from Shimon to Levi through ma'amad sheloshtan. This is clear from the authorities. Shach.
The three of them. Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Gittin write that ma'amad sheloshtan was not enacted for a ketubah, because the obligation might never become collectible. The Rashba there, the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, the Ramban, Nimukei Yosef, Baal HaTerumot, and Rivash 207 write similarly. This proves that ma'amad sheloshtan does apply to a loan whose due date has not yet arrived. Responsa Rosh likewise implies this, and Maharshal rules accordingly. Some wording of Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia and in HaChovel, and of the Rosh there, may at first appear otherwise. Nevertheless, as a matter of law the Shach concludes that ma'amad sheloshtan applies even before the due date, as is proven from all the authorities cited and appears from the Mechaber in seif 17. Moreover, the Ran holds that even a ketubah may be transferred by ma'amad sheloshtan, contrary to Responsa Maharashdam 359, which states that there is no ma'amad sheloshtan before the due date. See Responsa Mahara Sasson 111.
In your possession. The same applies if he says, "The maneh you owe me, give it to Levi." This is explicit in Responsa Rosh. The wording excludes only a case where nothing of his is presently owed or held by the third party, as stated in seif 2. Shach.
His debt. Responsa Maharam Alshakar 63 writes that ma'amad sheloshtan applies where the transferor owes the recipient, or gives him the amount as a gift. If he owes him nothing and gives him nothing, but merely instructs the third party to pay the recipient so that the recipient will buy merchandise for the transferor, the recipient does not acquire by ma'amad sheloshtan. He is only an agent. The reason is that ma'amad sheloshtan works when the transferor withdraws completely and the obligation shifts to the second party, leaving the first with no remaining right. Here the first party still retains an interest in the money, since it is to be used to buy merchandise for him. Therefore ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply.
Acquired. The Gemara describes ma'amad sheloshtan as a halachic enactment without an ordinary conceptual basis. Tosafot, the authorities, and the Tur in seif 13 explain that the Sages enacted this acquisition for commercial convenience, so that one who purchases merchandise without cash can direct the seller to his own debtor without waiting to execute another kinyan. The Rashba writes in his chiddushim that one who acquired a debt by ma'amad sheloshtan may himself transfer it to a third person by another ma'amad sheloshtan. Shach.
Shimon. Levi, however, according to some opinions may retract and resume collection from Reuven, as stated in seif 9. Shach.
And waive it. The Shach writes that even if Reuven has sufficient assets so that a waiver would not prejudice Levi, the original creditor still cannot waive the transferred debt. Otherwise the rule would be obvious, because even without ma'amad sheloshtan a creditor cannot waive a debt where doing so harms the rights of another creditor, as stated in siman 86, seif 5. Tosafot and the other authorities imply the same. Their reasoning is that in ma'amad sheloshtan, because all three were present, even the debtor's personal obligation to the original creditor ends and becomes an obligation to the recipient, leaving the original creditor no right that he can waive. By contrast, one who sells a promissory note without the debtor present transfers only the property lien, not the debtor's personal obligation, and therefore can later waive it, as stated in siman 66, seif 23. Ma'amad sheloshtan is fundamentally a special market enactment, and the Sages also enacted that the debtor's personal obligation shifts to the recipient. Otherwise merchants could not safely rely on the arrangement if the original lender retained a power of waiver. The Rosh writes this. The Kesef Mishneh suggests that the view denying the original creditor a power of waiver is linked to the opinion in seif 9 that the recipient cannot retract. The Shach disagrees. Tosafot and other authorities cite Rabbeinu Tam that the original creditor cannot waive, yet the Ran cites Rabbeinu Tam that the recipient may retract, and the Beit Yosef likewise records that as Rabbeinu Tam's view. The Rosh and Tur also hold that the original creditor cannot waive while the recipient may retract, and the Rama appears to rule the same way. Conversely, one could in principle say that even if the recipient cannot retract, the original creditor might still retain a power of waiver. See the Shach's lengthy discussion.
Was born. This excludes the suggestion in the Gemara that ma'amad sheloshtan works because the debtor is treated as having said, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you." Under that theory Levi would need to have been alive when the loan was made, as in siman 66, seif 26. The final Gemara instead explains ma'amad sheloshtan as a market enactment, so the Sages allowed acquisition even by a person born after the original loan. Sema and Shach.
