A three party arrangement applies to an oral loan even before its due date. Tosafot's statement that the enactment does not apply to a ketubah is because the ketubah may never become collectible at all. An ordinary loan, even one not yet due, is certain eventually to become payable. See what I wrote in siman 424.
Maharashdam discusses a case in which Shimon agreed in a three party arrangement to pay Reuven on Levi's behalf out of merchandise belonging to Levi that was in Shimon's possession, but only after Shimon first received payment of Levi's own debt to him. Maharashdam compares this to a ketubah and rules that where it is uncertain whether the merchandise will remain sufficient to cover both debts, the arrangement does not transfer a presently certain debt. The Shach limits the case to one where, already at the time of the arrangement, it was uncertain whether the merchandise was worth enough for both claims. If it was then clearly sufficient, he says the transfer takes effect even though the merchandise might later depreciate. In my view the limitation is unnecessary. Levi cannot compel Shimon to take from the merchandise the amount of his own debt and return the balance before he has actually been paid. As explained in siman 74, until full payment Shimon can retain the whole. Therefore at the time of the three party arrangement Levi has no presently collectible one hundred in Shimon's hands. This differs from an ordinary loan before maturity, which is a fixed debt certain to become collectible. Here, when the time for collection eventually arrives, the merchandise may have depreciated so far that nothing remains beyond Shimon's own claim. For that reason there is no three party transfer at all. Maharashdam's unqualified language supports this reading.
The Gemara says that a three party arrangement works for a deposit and, surprisingly, even for a loan. Rashi asks why a loan can be acquired, since the money is no longer physically present. The wording suggests that a deposit presents no such conceptual difficulty. The explanation is not that the enactment concerning three parties itself has no reason. Tosafot and Rosh explain that the Sages created it for commercial convenience, so merchants would not have to undertake separate formal acquisitions whenever they redirected funds. Once the Sages had reason to create an acquisition, there is no question why it can transfer a deposit, just as there is no question why rabbinic meshichah or incidental acquisition with land works. Commercial custom itself can create acquisition, as with situmta. The special difficulty is only a loan. Even the strongest form of ordinary acquisition cannot transfer something that is not presently in the owner's possession. A loan is not in the creditor's possession in the ordinary property sense. Thus the enactment that three party transfer works even for a loan is what the Gemara calls a law without an apparent ordinary legal rationale. Rashi's wording is therefore precise.

The accepted view of Tosafot is that once a loan has been transferred through a three party arrangement, the original creditor cannot later waive it. Maggid Mishneh and Ran support this from Rambam's ruling that a woman can be betrothed through such a transferred debt. If the original creditor retained a power of waiver, the woman might not rely upon the transfer, just as in the dispute over betrothal with an assigned debt instrument. Baal HaIttur and Raavad disagree and hold that even a debt transferred by three party arrangement can be waived. The Shach therefore treats the question as unresolved. The difficulty for that view is the Gemara in Kiddushin. When the betrothal is through a debt instrument, Rabbi Meir and the Sages dispute whether the woman relies upon it because the seller retains a power of waiver. When the betrothal is through an oral loan transferred by three party arrangement, the Gemara instead connects the dispute to whether the three party enactment applies to loans. If waiver were possible there too, the same reliance issue should have been raised. Ritva supplies the necessary distinction. A person is much more embarrassed to waive a debt after participating openly in a three party arrangement. The transferee can therefore reasonably rely on the transfer even if, as a technical matter, waiver remains possible. Ritva himself hesitates about the explanation, but some such distinction is necessary for the authorities who permit waiver. This also answers Rosh's proof that waiver must be impossible because the very purpose of three party transfer is commercial reliance. If the original creditor could freely waive, merchants would not rely on the enactment and its purpose would be defeated. According to the dissenting view, the merchant still relies because an open three party transaction makes later waiver socially and practically unlikely. The same principle supports Rabbi Eliezer of Metz, who held that the enactment does not apply to debts owed by gentiles because their word is unreliable. Maharit challenged this and argued that the recipient loses nothing by accepting the transfer. But if the whole enactment was made so that merchants could confidently rely on redirected debts, it makes sense that the Sages did not create it where the underlying debtor is a gentile whose promise is not trusted. The merchant would not rely on such a transfer, and the commercial purpose of the enactment would be absent.
