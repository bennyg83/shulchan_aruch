Whether a loan, etc. Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Gittin write that the Sages did not institute acquisition by assembly of three for a ketubah, because the ketubah may never become collectible. The Rashba, Rosh, Ramban, Nimukei Yosef, Baal HaTerumot, and Rivash agree that a ketubah is different because it is not payable during the husband’s lifetime and the wife may never become entitled to collect it. Rivash therefore ruled that a wife’s direction to her husband, in an assembly of three, to give portions of her future ketubah to her daughters was ineffective, because at that time the husband owed her nothing that had to be paid. From these sources it follows that an ordinary loan not yet due can nevertheless be transferred by an assembly of three. This also appears from a responsum of the Rosh and is ruled by Maharshal. Although language in Tosafot and the Rosh could be read to require a debt presently collectible, their intent is better understood as excluding a ketubah because its very collectibility is uncertain, not merely because payment is postponed. Maharam’s responsum in the Mordechai entertains a doubt about a tax obligation whose due date had not yet arrived, but the practical ruling, in my view, is that an ordinary loan is transferable even before maturity. This follows from the authorities cited and also from the Mechaber below in seif 16. I therefore disagree with the responsum of Maharshadam that an unmatured loan is excluded. Rabbi Shmuel de Medina discusses a case in which Shimon undertook in an assembly of three to pay Reuven for Levi out of Levi’s merchandise in Shimon’s hands, but only after Shimon first collected his own debt from that merchandise. He compares it to a ketubah where it was uncertain whether anything would ever remain to collect. This should be limited to a case in which, at the time of the assembly, the merchandise was not certainly worth enough to cover both debts. If its value then clearly covered both, the transfer takes effect even though its value might later decline.
A loan, etc. There is no distinction between an oral loan and a documented loan. Even where the creditor does not hand over the physical document, the underlying debt can be acquired through an assembly of three, as explained above in siman 66, seif 19, and in Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 5.
Or a deposit. The same applies to money owed because of damage or humiliation even though the claimant never previously possessed it. Thus, if Shimon injured or humiliated Reuven and beit din assessed compensation, Reuven can assign the amount due from Shimon to Levi by an assembly of three. This follows from the Rif, Tosafot, Rosh, Mordechai, and other authorities.
A maneh that I have in your hands, etc. This wording is not exclusive. The essential requirement is that the third party actually owe or hold something belonging to the assignor, in contrast to a case where nothing is in his hands, as explained in seif 2. Therefore, if he says, “The maneh that you owe me, give it to Levi,” the recipient acquires. This is explicit in a responsum of the Rosh cited below in seif 6, seif katan 27.
Whether he gave it to him as a gift. The Sema explains that the ruling teaches that the enactment applies not only to payment of a debt but also to a gift. In my view the emphasis is the reverse. Some early authorities held that an assembly of three works only as a gift and not as repayment of a debt. The Rif rejects that view. Thus the Mechaber teaches that the transfer works both for a gift and for repayment. This is also the clear implication of Beit Yosef and Bach.
In payment of his debt, etc. Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 63, limits the enactment to a transfer in which the assignor either owes the recipient or gives him the debt as a gift. If the assignor owes him nothing and merely directs the third party to give the recipient money so that the recipient can purchase merchandise for the assignor, the recipient does not acquire the money by an assembly of three and remains only an agent. The reason is that the enactment works where the assignor completely withdraws from the debt and substitutes the recipient, leaving himself no continuing right in the money. In the merchandise case, however, the money still remains economically the assignor’s because the recipient must use it to buy goods for him.
He acquires, and neither, etc. The Gemara calls this a rabbinic enactment without an ordinary kinyan rationale. Tosafot, the other authorities, and the Tur explain that the Sages created it for commercial convenience. A merchant may purchase goods without cash in hand and direct his own debtor to pay the seller, rather than delay trade until a formal acquisition can be arranged. The Rashba writes that a person who acquired a debt through an assembly of three can in turn transfer it to another person through another assembly of three.
And neither Reuven nor Shimon, etc. Levi, however, according to some authorities may still retract and return to collect his original claim from Reuven, as explained below in seif 9.
Even though he has not yet, etc. See what I wrote above in siman 195, seif 7.
And similarly the assignor cannot waive it, etc. Kesef Mishneh writes that the view that the assignor cannot waive the transferred debt depends on the view below in seif 9 that Levi cannot retract from the transfer. I do not think the two issues necessarily depend on one another. Tosafot and the Rosh hold that the assignor cannot waive, yet Rabbenu Tam, as cited by the Ran and Nimukei Yosef, allows the recipient to retract. The Rosh and Tur likewise combine these positions, and the Rama appears to do so as well. Conversely, the Ramban and others hold that the recipient cannot retract but that the assignor can still waive. Nimukei Yosef states explicitly that these are independent questions. Accordingly, the Mechaber’s categorical ruling here that the assignor cannot waive requires examination. The Beit Yosef did not cite the substantial body of authorities who permit waiver, and Kesef Mishneh’s inference from the Rif and Rambam rests on the assumption that the two questions are linked, which is doubtful. The Rambam does appear to hold that waiver is ineffective, as is evident from his ruling concerning betrothal by a loan transferred through an assembly of three. But the Ramban, Nimukei Yosef, Baal HaItur, Raavad, Rashba, and apparently the Maggid Mishneh recognize a serious view that the original creditor can waive. The Rashba and Raavad add that if the original borrower expressly undertook at the time of the loan to be obligated to the lender and anyone deriving rights from him, then waiver may be ineffective, though that qualification itself is disputed by the Rosh and other authorities. Since several great authorities permit waiver, the person in possession may have a valid claim of kim li according to them. At the very least, in matters of kiddushin one must treat the issue as a doubt. Indeed the Mechaber in Even HaEzer cites the view that a woman betrothed by such a transferred loan may not be certainly betrothed. It is therefore improper to rule categorically otherwise in a matter of ervah.
The assignor cannot waive. According to the authorities who say this, the rule applies even where Reuven has assets and the waiver would not otherwise prejudice Levi. If the only concern were prejudice to Levi, ordinary law would already prevent the waiver without any special rule of assembly of three. The Rosh and Ran explain that when all three stand together, the original creditor’s entire claim against the original debtor is displaced and the debtor becomes obligated directly to the recipient. In an ordinary sale of a debt document, by contrast, the purchaser receives the monetary right but the original personal obligation of the debtor remains tied to the original creditor, which leaves the creditor enough legal interest to waive. The assembly of three was instituted precisely for commerce, and the Sages treated even the debtor’s personal obligation as transferred so that the original creditor could not later undermine the transaction. Otherwise merchants could not rely on the enactment.
And even if Levi was born, etc. This excludes the Gemara’s initial suggestion that an assembly of three works as though the debtor had originally said, “I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you.” Under that rationale, the later recipient would have to have been alive at the time of the original loan, as explained above in siman 66, seif 26. The Gemara’s conclusion is instead that the assembly of three is an independent rabbinic enactment for commercial convenience, and therefore it works even for a recipient who was born only after the loan arose.
