Whether as a deposit. See Shach seif katan 1 through his statement that if the merchandise was worth enough at the time to cover both debts, the recipient acquires the amount of both debts through ma'amad sheloshtan even if the merchandise may later decline in value. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that Levi cannot compel Shimon to take merchandise equal to the debt and return the remainder, as explained in siman 74 seif 5. If so, Ketzot argues, it is not presently a case of "I have a maneh of yours in my hands," because the debt cannot presently be demanded from that merchandise. But according to what I wrote in siman 74, if the debtor tells the creditor, "sell whatever portion of the collateral you can, collect your debt, and return the surplus," the creditor can compel such a sale. Ketzot's objection therefore falls away.
In payment of his debt. See Shach, seif katan 6, through his conclusion that therefore the law of ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply. If he assigned him to give him money as a loan, Tumim holds that it is not acquired through ma'amad sheloshtan. His proof is from Bava Metzia 71b, where the Gemara discusses a non Jew who borrowed from a Jew, placed the matter before another Jew, and instructed that the money be given to the second Jew, which is prohibited. The Gemara asks that although the latter clause may be understood stringently, why is the first clause, in which a Jew transfers his money from another Jew in the presence of the non Jew, permitted? Since there is no agency for a non Jew, the non Jew is the one receiving the interest. Tumim asks that there should nevertheless be a ma'amad sheloshtan, so that the original borrower is discharged and the assignee acquires the debt. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that because loan money is given to be spent, it is comparable to a gift and is acquired through ma'amad sheloshtan. In my view, Tumim's proof is compelling. The enactment of ma'amad sheloshtan was primarily made for the benefit of the recipient, but it was not enacted so that the transferor should thereby acquire a personal and property lien against the recipient even before the third party has delivered the money in his possession. Since the recipient has not become obligated to the third party, the recipient likewise does not acquire. As for Shach's principal ruling that if one assigned another to give money so that merchandise should be purchased for him, the third party need not give it and there is no acquisition through ma'amad sheloshtan, this is not directed against the transferor and recipient. They may certainly retract from the proposed purchase even after the money has reached the recipient. Rather, it concerns the third party. Although the lender told him, 'Give it to so and so,' and siman 121 establishes that such an instruction is effective regarding responsibility once the money was actually given, before delivery he may retract from giving it, because he is merely the transferor's agent and an agent may terminate his agency. The recipient cannot sue him, because he did not become the transferor's agent and has no authorization from the transferor. The third party can therefore say, 'You are not my litigant.'
