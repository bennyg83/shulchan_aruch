He assigned him, etc. Because the words of the Mechaber and later authorities in this law require clarification, I must discuss it at length. The source is a responsum of the Rosh. Reuven claimed one hundred from Shimon. Shimon answered that he had indeed owed the amount, but had paid it because, in Reuven’s presence, he arranged with his mother to pay Reuven on his behalf, and she became obligated to Reuven through an assembly of three. Shimon brought witnesses that his mother said she would pay Reuven for her son and that Shimon told Reuven, “Do not claim anything further from me, because my mother now owes you,” and Reuven remained silent. Reuven answered that he never agreed to collect this debt from the mother; anything he collected from her was on another debt. Shimon then sought to swear that he or someone else had later paid the hundred. The Rosh ruled that Shimon must pay and cannot now swear that he later paid, because he had admitted the original debt and had treated the assignment to his mother as the very payment that discharged him. Since he believed he had already paid by that arrangement, we are witnesses that he did not then pay again. Moreover, the attempted assembly of three was ineffective because the mother did not actually owe Shimon or hold money of his. An assembly of three works only when the third party holds the assignor’s money or owes him a debt. Nevertheless, Shimon’s initial claim that Reuven collected part of this very debt from the mother in his presence is a definite and valid payment claim. He may swear how much of this debt Reuven received and must pay the balance.
The Ir Shushan explains that the witnesses must also have seen the mother pay some money to Reuven, though without knowing how much or on which debt, because otherwise Reuven should swear that any collection from her was on another obligation. I do not agree. The statement, “In my presence you collected part of this debt from her,” is itself a proper claim of payment. A debtor who borrowed before witnesses may still say, “I paid,” even without a migo, because payment itself is a substantive defense. Here the problem with a later payment claim is only that Shimon first treated the assignment itself as payment. By contrast, saying from the outset, “I saw her pay you on my behalf,” is equivalent to saying, “I paid you,” and needs no migo.
Indeed, had Shimon initially told beit din, “Although I assigned you to Levi, I later paid you myself,” he would be believed even if the assignment before them was known. The disqualification arises only after he first appeared before beit din and relied on the assignment itself as the discharge. Once he chose that position and was legally liable because the assignment did not take effect, he cannot later change his plea to a different payment claim. A litigant cannot retract from a plea that already rendered him liable merely because beit din had not yet pronounced the judgment, and a later migo cannot rescue him because we do not apply a retroactive migo. But the statement made at the outset, “In my presence you collected part from Levi,” remains credible by the ordinary migo that he could simply have said, “I paid you.” This is parallel to the rulings of Tosafot in Bava Batra and Ketubot concerning a litigant whose later argument is barred once he first chose a different factual basis.
This also explains the Rosh’s careful wording. He repeatedly emphasizes that Shimon said before beit din, “I owed you and I paid you because I arranged with my mother to pay you,” thereby identifying the supposed assignment as the payment. Since he formally described that as the discharge, his later claim of a different payment is a retraction from his first plea. The Mechaber’s broader wording, that whenever an ineffective assembly of three occurred the debtor is not believed to say that he later paid, therefore requires study. In my view, if he immediately tells beit din that after the ineffective assignment he later paid, he should be believed. Perhaps the Mechaber inferred otherwise from the Rosh’s phrase that we are witnesses that a person does not pay twice, but I do not think a monetary ruling should be built merely on that stylistic inference. The Rosh’s real reason is procedural: because Shimon came before beit din and relied on the assignment as payment without then alleging any later payment, his initial position was effectively an admission that no later payment occurred. A later assertion of payment is therefore a forbidden retraction. Even according to the Mechaber, this should apply only where there are witnesses to the original debt and to the ineffective assignment. If there are no such witnesses, and the debtor immediately says before beit din, “I paid you even though I had also assigned you to Levi,” he is believed through a migo that he could deny the debt entirely. The presumption that a person does not pay twice is no stronger than the explicit talmudic presumption that one does not pay before maturity, and even that presumption yields where the defendant has a valid migo. The Mechaber can at most mean that a bare claim of second payment is weak; where it is supported by a migo, it is accepted. The Sema further explains that the Mechaber is speaking where Reuven did not return to Shimon in the meantime and say, “Levi did not become obligated to me, pay me yourself.” This is correct. If Shimon initially says before beit din that Reuven did return to him with that demand and that he therefore paid, or that he himself realized Levi had not become obligated and therefore paid, and Reuven denies this, Shimon swears and is exempt. The case in which he is not believed is where he admits that Reuven made no such renewed demand, and nevertheless says, “Although I considered the assignment itself to be payment, I also paid you afterward,” without a migo. Finally, the Rosh and Tur say that Shimon swears concerning the amount already collected and pays the balance. The Bach understands this as a Torah oath because Shimon thereby admits part of the claim. In my view he owes only an oath of heset. He does not voluntarily admit any amount still owing; he says the entire debt was paid, and we impose payment of the balance only because one portion of his defense is not believed. As explained above in siman 75 and siman 87, liability for part of a claim resulting from a rejected defense is not considered a partial admission that triggers a Torah oath.
