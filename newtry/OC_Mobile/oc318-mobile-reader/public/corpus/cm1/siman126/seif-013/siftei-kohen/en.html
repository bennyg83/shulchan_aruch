He is likewise not believed to say, “I was joking.” An assignment in the presence of all three parties is comparable to an act of acquisition. It is not literally an acquisition, since he may still claim payment, but at the moment of assignment it effects transfer. One who made an acquisition cannot claim that he was joking. Moreover, why would he joke before a third person who had no knowledge whether the debt existed? Baal HaTerumot and the Tur state these reasons. It follows that even where the assignment was not made before witnesses, or no payment date was fixed and he could therefore deny the assignment or claim payment, he still cannot claim joking. Rashba states this explicitly, citing Raavad, who distinguishes between a claim of computational error, which may be believed through a migo of payment where there are no witnesses, and a claim of jesting, which is never accepted. The Tur and Mechaber state this rule at the beginning because no defense is effective there according to any opinion. In the later clause they cite a view that permits a claim of error, but all agree that a claim of jesting is ineffective. Siman 81:16 likewise rules without qualification that one cannot claim either jesting or an admission made to avoid appearing wealthy in an assignment in the presence of all three.

He is believed to say, “I erred.” The assigned debtor swears a rabbinic oath of hesset to the recipient and is exempt. According to the final view in seif 9, the recipient then returns to the assignor. According to the first view, under which the assignor was fully discharged, the assignor is exempt after swearing hesset to the recipient that the debt truly existed when he made the assignment. Maharit objects that the first view should apply only where the assigned debtor merely delays payment, not where he says that nothing was owed. There is no difficulty. The assignor says that no error occurred, that the recipient released him, and that the assigned debtor is lying. The recipient caused his own loss by failing to designate witnesses for the assignment. The assigned debtor's oath to the recipient is required because he seeks exemption from the recipient, who stands in the assignor's position, while the assignor asserts with certainty that there was no error. If the assignor cannot make a definite assertion, such as where he forgot the account, died, or went overseas, or if he is willing to waive the assigned debtor's oath and pay the recipient himself, the recipient ordinarily cannot impose an oath upon the assigned debtor, because the recipient has only a doubtful claim and never personally had funds with him. By contrast, in the later clause the assignor swears to the recipient even though the recipient has no certain claim, because the recipient's money certainly was in the assignor's hands and the assigned debtor asserts with certainty that the assignor erred. Their assertions are combined to require an oath, as explained above in siman 121. One may nevertheless argue that even in the first clause an oath is required despite the doubtful claim. The assigned debtor is believed only through a migo that he could have claimed payment. Since a claim of payment would require an oath, the claim of error supported by that migo likewise requires an oath. Similar rules apply where one is believed to say that he returned property only through a migo of unavoidable loss, in which case he must take the severe oath applicable to unavoidable loss. This yields a general principle: whenever a defendant is believed only because of a migo, he must take a hesset oath even if the claimant cannot assert certainty. Thus, one found on another's land who says, “It belonged to your father and I bought it from him,” and is believed only through a migo that he could have denied the father's ownership entirely, must swear hesset that he purchased it, even though the claimant lacks personal knowledge. The same applies in comparable cases.

Through a migo that he paid you. Maharit asks that according to the view in seif 9 that the recipient may return to the original debtor, there is no true migo. By claiming error, the assigned debtor harms the original debtor, because the recipient returns to him. Had he claimed payment to the recipient, the recipient would suffer the loss. Perhaps, Maharit suggests, a person permits himself to harm one litigant but not the other. This is no difficulty. The Gemara and all authorities recognize such a migo. An agent is believed to say that he delivered money to the creditor through a migo that he could have said he returned it to the borrower, except where he must swear and therefore cannot serve as a witness. Here, however, he appears as a litigant seeking his own exemption, and the need to swear does not impair the migo. Maharit further suggests that perhaps even when the assigned debtor claims payment, the recipient may swear that he was not paid and then return to the original debtor, as in the law of the shopkeeper and laborers. Maharit finds this difficult because the assignee's principal claim is against the assigned debtor, while the original debtor remains only analogous to a guarantor, and a creditor ordinarily cannot return to the guarantor merely because the principal debtor claims payment. In my view, according to the opinion permitting return, the recipient may indeed return even where the assigned debtor claims payment. No authority says that the original debtor remains literally only a guarantor. The Rosh merely writes that proper order suggests not suing the original debtor first so long as ordinary collection from the assigned debtor remains possible, because the assigned debtor appears to be the principal obligor. The Rosh does not mean that all laws of guarantors apply. Otherwise, if the assigned debtor traveled far away or possessed only inferior land, circumstances in which one may not ordinarily turn to a guarantor, the recipient could never collect from his original debtor. Rather, the original debtor remains liable, though orderly procedure directs initial collection from the assigned debtor.

Through a migo that he paid. Rama means that where witnesses establish the assignment, the assigned debtor is not believed through a migo of payment. Where he has a migo of complete denial, all agree that he is believed. Beit Yosef and Ran state this. The Sema infers from the Mechaber's phrase, “a migo against witnesses,” that even without witnesses to the assignment he cannot claim error. This inference is not necessary. The meaning is that, had the debt not been clear to him, he would not have admitted it before witnesses. Bach argues that the Rosh never accepts a claim of error, but his proofs are not compelling, and practical law does not follow that view. Where no migo exists, such as when witnesses testify that they remained with him continuously or that the debt is still within its term, all agree that he cannot claim error. The same follows from the Mechaber and Rama, contrary to Maharik. The special weakness of a migo of payment applies where he obligated himself through an assignment in the presence of all three parties, because there is a presumption that one did not err in such a formal admission. Outside that setting, even an admission before witnesses may be followed by a claim of error through a migo of payment, unless the admission was before beit din. There is even room to argue that in an assignment before all three he may claim error through a migo of payment. Tosafot and the Rosh derived the contrary rule from Rif and Rabbeinu Chananel, but their proof is not decisive because the talmudic case also involved a formal acquisition, where even a claim of payment would not be accepted.
Even if he gave him a handwritten acknowledgment. The Sema cites Darkei Moshe that a handwritten acknowledgment is not equivalent to an admission before witnesses, because one may claim that it was given in trust or that he signed a blank sheet merely to authenticate his signature and another person wrote the obligation above it. Rama's phrase, “contrary to those who disagree,” refers to Maharik, who treats handwriting like an admission before witnesses. Mahari ben Lev rules that one cannot claim error against one's handwriting, and several authorities dispute whether payment may be claimed against a handwritten obligation. For practical law, it appears that one may claim error through a migo of payment even against authenticated handwriting, because I concluded above that even after an admission before witnesses one may claim error through such a migo. Maharik himself appears to agree. In the responsum where he says that one plainly cannot claim that a writing was made in error, even where a migo exists, the facts contained no migo at all. His meaning is that according to Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam one is not believed even where a migo exists, and certainly not in the case before him where none existed.
