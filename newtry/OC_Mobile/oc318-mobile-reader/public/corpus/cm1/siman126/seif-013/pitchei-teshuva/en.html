He is believed to say, "I erred." See Baer Heitev and Knesset HaGedolah above, siman 79, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 9, who cites Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 71, that a carefully written reply specifying the account is a complete admission and the writer cannot later say he erred. Mahari Adarbi, siman 405, likewise emphasizes that business correspondence is written with care and precision. Maharshadam, however, disagrees in a case of handwriting and holds that the writer remains believed to claim error, and Sefer HaMapeh, siman 126, seif 13, rules similarly. See Noda B'Yehudah, second edition, siman 15, regarding one who sent payment on account and wrote that he would shortly send another fifty gold coins, then claimed he had miscalculated. After analyzing the Rama, Sema, and Darkei Moshe, Noda B'Yehudah concludes that even a letter wholly in the writer's own hand is not necessarily equivalent to an admission before witnesses. The writer may be believed to say he erred by virtue of a migo that he could say he repaid. He further reasons that even authorities who are strict with a written acknowledgment may agree where the statement of balance was merely incidental to a letter whose main purpose was to accompany a payment, rather than being written specifically as evidence of the debt. He therefore approved the ruling that the defendant swear and be exempt. See there.
