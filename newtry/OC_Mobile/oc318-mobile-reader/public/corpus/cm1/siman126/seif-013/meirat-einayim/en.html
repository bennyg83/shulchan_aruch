The giver is obligated, etc. This applies even according to the first view in seif 9, because here the transfer is shown to have been mistaken from the outset.
If he gave it in payment of his debt. This excludes a pure gift, where the giver can say, "Had I known he did not owe you, I would not have given you the gift."
He is not believed to say, "I made a mistake." Where he lacks a migo that the matter never happened because the transfer was witnessed, and he also lacks a migo of payment because the due date has not yet arrived, "I erred" is a weak claim. A person ordinarily checks his accounts carefully before acknowledging a debt.
"I was jesting with you." The Tur explains that ma'amad sheloshtan is like a kinyan, and after a kinyan one cannot say, "I was joking," even without saying "you are my witnesses." A formal kinyan is too serious an act to be treated as jest. Moreover, the ordinary claim of jesting applies where someone responds to an improper demand by making a false admission in order to mock the claimant in return. Here, even if the giver wished to jest with the claimant, why would he involve the innocent recipient, who does not know whether a debt exists?
"I was jesting with you." It is obvious that he likewise cannot say the admission was made merely so that he not appear wealthy, because that defense applies only to an unsolicited admission, not one made in response to another's demand.
With a migo that the matter never happened. The Tur says that if there were no witnesses, he was not afraid to acknowledge because he could later say, if he discovered an accounting error, that no transfer occurred. He therefore takes a hesset oath to the recipient and is exempt. According to the Rosh, the recipient may then return to the original giver; according to Rif, the giver too is exempt upon a hesset oath that the debt truly existed when he made the transfer.
Or with a migo that "I repaid you." Even if the original acknowledgment was witnessed, once the due date has passed he can say that the original accounting was mistaken through a migo that he could have claimed later payment. One might ask why he dared acknowledge before witnesses if he could be sued during the term without such a migo. He can answer that he expected no suit before the due date, and afterward he would be able to claim payment, even on the final day.
He is not believed to say "I erred" through a migo of payment. This is the Rosh's view: an acknowledgment before witnesses is so reliable that the migo is against a witness-like presumption, because a person checks carefully before acknowledging before witnesses. Without witnesses even the Rosh agrees that "I erred" is believed through a migo that the event never occurred. Although the Mechaber's broad wording could imply otherwise, Beit Yosef says explicitly that the denial is ineffective only after an acknowledgment before witnesses.
Even if he gave him his handwriting, etc. Darkei Moshe writes that a private handwritten acknowledgment is not necessarily equivalent to an admission before witnesses, because the writer may sometimes claim it was a trust document or that he signed a blank sheet later misused. Rama therefore rules that handwriting alone does not defeat the claim of mistake, unlike Maharik. Other responsa disagree, and siman 69 contains further views regarding whether payment may be claimed against one's own handwriting.
