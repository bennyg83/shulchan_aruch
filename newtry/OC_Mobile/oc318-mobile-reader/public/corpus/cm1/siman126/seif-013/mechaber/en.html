If, after the debtor or depositary agreed to pay the recipient, he says, "I reviewed my accounts and in fact owed him nothing; I admitted it by mistake," then if he can prove by witnesses that he is telling the truth, he is exempt even if his original agreement was made before witnesses. The original giver must then pay the recipient if the transfer had been made in payment of a debt. If he cannot prove the mistake, and the transfer was made before witnesses and a fixed date was set for payment to the recipient, then before that date he is not believed to say that he erred, nor may he say that he was merely jesting. If the transfer was not made before witnesses, some say that he is believed to claim error through a migo that he could have denied the transaction entirely, or through a migo that he could have claimed payment if the due date has passed, and the dispute is then between the giver and the recipient. Others say that he is not believed to claim error through a migo of payment, because this is a migo against the ordinary presumption: such an error is uncommon, for a person normally checks carefully before making an admission. Rama: This latter view appears primary. This applies specifically when he admitted the matter before witnesses. If he admitted it without witnesses, he may say that he erred. Ran, end of the first chapter of Gittin. Even if he gave a handwritten acknowledgment, he is believed to say that he erred through a migo that he could have claimed payment, as explained above, contrary to those who disagree. Maharik, shoresh 193; see above, siman 81, seif 23.
