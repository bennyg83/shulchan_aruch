With witnesses. If the transfer was made before witnesses, a due date was fixed, and the claim remains within that term so that the defendant has no migo at all, he is exempt when the mistake is established by witnesses. See siman 81, seif 20. Shach.
His debt. This excludes a case where the transferor gave the money as a gift. There the giver is exempt, because had he known of the mistake he would not have made the gift. Shach.
I was joking. Plainly he also cannot claim that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. Simanim 32 and 81 explain that this defense applies only where a person voluntarily admits a debt without being sued. Sema. The Shach writes that even if the transfer was not made before witnesses and no payment term was fixed, so that he has a migo of complete denial or payment, he nevertheless cannot say, "I was joking," because the legal reason for rejecting that claim still applies. See there.
I erred. This is difficult because the Beit Yosef writes that we do not rule like Baal HaTerumot on this point because he is a lone authority. One must say that the Mechaber reconsidered in the Shulchan Aruch. See siman 81, seif 23, where the Shach writes that Baal HaTerumot's view appears correct and is supported by the authorities. Nevertheless, for practical law one should not depart from the Rama's gloss here. Maharashdam 74 rules likewise. See also his simanim 56, 94, 302, 394, and 453. Mahari ibn Lev I, klal 16, siman 91; klal 18, siman 103; and II, 37, also appear to agree. Shach.
That I paid you. Where the transfer was made before witnesses and the term has passed, or no due date was fixed, the substitute debtor takes a shevuat heset to the recipient and is exempt. The recipient may then return and sue the original transferor according to the latter view in seif 9. According to the first view there, the transferor too is exempt, except that he takes a shevuat heset that the amount was in fact owed to him when he made the transfer. The Shach derives a new general rule from this: whenever a defendant is believed only through a migo, even if the claimant has no definite claim, the defendant must take a shevuat heset. For example, one asks, "What are you doing on my property?" and the possessor answers, "It belonged to your father and I bought it from him." He is believed only through a migo that he could have said, "It never belonged to your father," as stated in siman 149, seif 20. He must therefore take a shevuat heset that he bought it from the father even though the claimant cannot definitely deny the purchase. The same applies in similar cases.
That I paid. The Shach explains the Rama's gloss as follows. A migo of payment does not allow the defendant to claim mistake when witnesses establish the transfer. A migo of complete denial, however, does allow him to claim mistake according to all views. The Beit Yosef, Ran, and Darkei Moshe write this. The Sema understood the Mechaber's phrase "like a migo against witnesses" as meaning that even where there were no witnesses to the transfer, a claim of mistake is not believed. The Shach says this is not compelled. The meaning can be that had the matter not been clear to him, he would not have admitted it before witnesses. The Bach argues at length that the Rosh holds one can never claim mistake, but the Shach finds his proofs inconclusive. Even if that were the Rosh's view, we do not follow it as practical law, as explained in siman 81, seif 23, contrary to Responsa Rashach II, 148. The Shach further concludes there that where no migo exists, such as where witnesses testify continuously to the relevant facts or testify that the debt is still within its term, all agree that he cannot claim mistake. The Mechaber and Rama imply this, unlike Maharik, shoresh 65. See the Shach there for more.
Believed. Responsa Mahari ibn Lev I, 91, rules that one cannot say "I erred" against his own handwriting. See also siman 69, where the Sema cites several views that a person cannot claim payment against his own handwritten obligation. See the Shach and Responsa Mabit II, 154.
