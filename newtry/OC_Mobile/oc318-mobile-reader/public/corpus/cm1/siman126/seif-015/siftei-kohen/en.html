["And the giver must swear, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that this applies only according to the first view above, seif 9. Alternatively, perhaps the Tur holds that even according to the latter view the giver is exempt, because we say that the assignor is not released only where the person to whom the debt was assigned refused to pay. Even then, the recipient must pursue that person and make every effort to collect from him, and until he does so he may not return to his original debtor. Here, however, since by his own testimony he caused the debt not to be collected, while the borrower has already been released, he cannot return to the giver. The Ir Shushan and Sema quote only this latter explanation, and the Bach likewise agrees without giving a reason or proof. In my view this is entirely implausible. Why should he lose merely because he admitted the truth? Only when he can lawfully sue is he obligated to make every effort to do so. Here the recipient knows that the accounting was mistaken and can say: what can I do, shall I lie for your sake and take money unlawfully? This is especially so according to what I wrote above, seif katan 38 and 56, that he is not obligated to pursue the person to whom he was assigned except according to the strict law. See there. I later saw that the Gidulei Terumah also questions the latter words of the Beit Yosef and agrees with his first explanation. I add that although the Rosh follows the latter view in seif 9, the Tur nevertheless had reason to record this rule because it applies where the recipient expressly released the original debtor. The Tur has written in several places that according to the Rosh, an express release has the same legal effect as no release under the first view in seif 9. The Mechaber below, seif 22, must likewise be understood in this manner, as I explain there in seif katan 89. The Ran at the end of the first chapter of Gittin similarly writes that according to the view that the original debtor is not released unless expressly discharged, even if the recipient testifies that the assignment was mistaken, he may return and collect from the giver. The Beit Yosef cites this. The Ran further writes according to that view: the giver may then collect from the person to whom he assigned the debt; although the recipient testifies that the latter owed him nothing, that testimony is only one witness, so the giver may collect upon swearing, since he is able to swear. The Beit Yosef cites this as well. This refers to a case where the assignee cannot credibly claim that he was mistaken through a migo that he could have claimed payment, as explained above in seif 13. If he had such credibility, he would be exempt even without an oath, as I wrote above, seif katan 65. See also what I wrote above, seif katan 63."]
["He must swear to the recipient, etc. He can say: the accounting was correct, and you have no authority, by your admission to him, to impose liability upon me. Even if he says, I forgot the accounting, he is exempt, for this is comparable to one who is told, you have one hundred of mine in your possession, and answers, I do not know, who is exempt. It is not comparable to one who says, you lent me one hundred and I do not know whether I repaid you, because here he says, I certainly repaid you; only you now say that an error was found, and I do not know of that error. Nevertheless he must swear that he does not know, and is then exempt. So writes the Rashba in his novellae."]
["He is exempt without an oath. This is so even if the giver admits that he actually had nothing in his possession, because the gift was made in error. He undertook to give only because he thought that he held property belonging to the recipient; now that he does not, the recipient has no claim against him."]
