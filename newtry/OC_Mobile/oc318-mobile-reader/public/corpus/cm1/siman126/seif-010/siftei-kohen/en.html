The borrower was found to be poor. This implies that even if Reuven also did not know of the poverty, the assignment was nevertheless a mistaken transaction, as in siman 232. Sema and Bach understand it so, and the next clause, “If Levi knew,” indicates that everything depends upon Levi's knowledge. Even an express release would be a release made in error, because Levi would not have released Reuven had he known the debtor was poor. The plain meaning of the Yerushalmi, however, does not support this. Baal HaIttur says that liability exists only where Reuven knew of the poverty and acted deceptively. Baal HaTerumot cites Raavad similarly: only where there was deception is Reuven liable; if there was no deception, Reuven is exempt even though Levi did not know of the poverty. Ramban goes further and rules that only a substantial deception, such as sending the debtor overseas immediately after the assignment, creates liability. If Reuven merely believed the debtor wealthy when he was poor, whether Reuven knew the truth or not, Reuven is exempt because Levi should have investigated. The practical ruling therefore requires study. Mahara Sasson discusses the matter at length and concludes that one may plead that the law follows Ramban. He nevertheless ruled in his case that the borrower was liable because the creditor still held a written promissory note and the assignment was merely written upon it without an express release. He invoked Rambam's rule that where Levi claims the assigned debtor was poor and Reuven claims he was wealthy and later became poor, Reuven must prove his defense, just as one who relies upon a receipt must validate it. Mahara Sasson also argued that retention of the note shows that the creditor did not release the original debtor, and that Ramban would agree where the creditor repeatedly investigated and was falsely told that the debtor was wealthy. I do not understand this ruling. According to Ramban, the original debtor is exempt even without an express release written upon the note, because a valid assignment is itself a complete discharge. Rambam requires proof only because he holds that an assignment to one who was already poor is invalid. According to Ramban, where the facts are undisputed the debtor may plead that the law follows Ramban. Perhaps Mahara Sasson meant that a written note is treated as already collected and cannot be defeated by a doubtful plea of “the law follows this authority.” In my view, that is not correct. We are presently deciding whether the note was paid, and the debtor may assert that according to Ramban the note has no force, as explained in Tekfo Kohen. This is especially so where the assignment is written upon the note itself. There remains a question whether one may rely upon Ramban where the assignor knew of the poverty, since Rambam, Semag, Baal HaTerumot, Raavad, Baal HaIttur, Rav Hai Gaon, Nimukei Yosef, and the Tur's cited authorities appear to dispute him, making Ramban perhaps a lone view. Where Reuven did not know, however, Raavad and Baal HaIttur agree with Ramban, and only Rambam, Semag, and the Mechaber clearly imply otherwise. It therefore appears that Reuven may plead that the law follows Ramban in that circumstance. Perhaps had the Mechaber and the earlier decisor seen the positions of Ramban, Raavad, and Baal HaIttur, they would not have ruled as they did, especially since the plain Yerushalmi supports them.
