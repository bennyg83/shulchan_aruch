If the debtor assigned a debt through a three party arrangement and the transferred debtor was later discovered to be poor, the wording of the law implies that the transfer can be treated as a mistaken transaction even if the transferor himself did not know of the poverty. This is difficult because debt instruments are excluded from ordinary overreaching. Even if a debt worth one thousand is sold for one dinar or the reverse, the sale is not void for overreaching. Why then should acquisition of an oral debt through a three party arrangement be void because the debtor turned out poor? The Jerusalem Talmud limits the rule to a case where the parties acted deceptively, meaning that the transferor knew of the debtor's condition. That may avoid the problem. Even though debt instruments are excluded from the ordinary laws of overreaching, intentional deception is forbidden initially even with slaves, documents, and land, and the court can penalize one who deliberately deceived. Several authorities nevertheless explain the rule differently, saying the transferee should have investigated whether the debtor was poor. Baal HaTerumot in fact writes that discovery of poverty is not a mistaken sale of a debt instrument because the buyer should have investigated, and the issue belongs only to overreaching, from which documents are excluded. The Shulchan Aruch in siman 66 appears to follow that approach. This leaves the present three party rule difficult. The proper distinction is that a written debt instrument is excluded from overreaching because the paper itself is not money in substance. The Torah's exclusion applies to things whose body is not money. An oral debt transferred by a three party arrangement is different. The debt itself is the monetary subject being sold. It may be outside the creditor's possession for purposes of consecration, just as stolen property is, but it is nonetheless monetary in substance. Therefore ordinary overreaching can apply to the sale of an oral debt transferred by three party arrangement. This distinction has another consequence. If one witness testifies against the transferee concerning an oral debt acquired through a three party arrangement, the defendant can be required to take a Torah oath to contradict the witness, because the lawsuit concerns the body of a monetary debt rather than a debt instrument excluded from oath. The fact that the three party acquisition itself is rabbinic does not prevent the Torah oath. Once the Sages validly vested the debt in the transferee, the power of beit din makes it his property for monetary law, and denial of that right can generate a Torah oath, as I explained in siman 87.
