He told them in maamad sheloshtan, etc. The Sema in seif katan 23 holds that if the new obligor was wealthy at the time of the transfer and later became impoverished, then even according to the Rosh and those who agree with him the recipient cannot return to the original debtor, because the original debtor can say, "Your own mazal caused the loss." The Sema's proof is from the Rosh's responsum cited by the Tur. In a case where the creditor was directed to collect from another person who later became impoverished, the Rosh explained that the creditor could return to the first debtor because the second person did not actually hold any money belonging to the transferor. The Sema infers that had the second person held such money, the creditor could not return. This is not enough to create such a distinction on one's own, and the Shach already challenges it in seif katan 39. The Rosh's responsum can be explained as a case where the second obligor became impoverished because of the creditor's own delay. Had the creditor demanded payment at the proper time, he could have collected, but because he neglected to do so the obligor became impoverished in the interim. Even the Rosh agrees that in such a case the creditor cannot return to the first debtor, as the Rama states. The Rosh therefore gave the additional reason that there was never a valid maamad sheloshtan acquisition at all because the second person did not hold property of the transferor. Even though the creditor delayed, the first debtor remains liable because the second was never legally substituted at all. There is no difficulty. In truth, much of the concern among the poskim is to uphold the Yerushalmi's ruling in the case where Levi became bankrupt, that the original debtor has no further liability. I do not understand the proof. The Rosh and Rif themselves conclude in chapter HaMekabel that the Yerushalmi follows Rav Sheshet. Even in the workers' case, where the shopkeeper holds none of the employer's money, the Yerushalmi rules that once the workers relied on him they cannot return to the employer, although they did not expressly release him. The Rosh explains that according to Rav Sheshet such reliance itself is equivalent to waiver. If so, all the more so with maamad sheloshtan, a full acquisition mechanism, the Yerushalmi would hold that they cannot return. The Bach and Perishah indeed write that maamad sheloshtan is stronger than the worker's reliance arrangement. But our Gemara rules like Rabbah, who says the worker can return unless he expressly released the employer. According to that approach, the same can apply to maamad sheloshtan. The Yerushalmi therefore follows its own view and proves nothing for our ruling. There is no need to enter strained interpretations to preserve it. This also resolves the Tur's citation in the name of "some say" that if the second obligor later becomes impoverished, the creditor cannot return. The Beit Yosef asks that this is already explicit in the Yerushalmi. According to what I have written, the Yerushalmi is no proof because it follows Rav Sheshet, while we rule like Rabbah. The later authority therefore needed to state the ruling independently. See responsum of Maharam Sasson siman 104, who struggled with the Tur's wording. The Sema likewise writes that if the recipient knew at the time that the second obligor was poor he cannot later return because he knowingly accepted him. The Shach challenges this as well, and the Shach is correct.
