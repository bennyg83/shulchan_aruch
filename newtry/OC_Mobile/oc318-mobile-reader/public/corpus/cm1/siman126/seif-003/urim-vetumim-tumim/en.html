For presumably he referred to the maneh that he has, and the recipient acquired. This is the Ramban's view, cited in Baal HaTerumot, Gate 28. His principal proof is from the first chapter of Gittin. The Mishnah says, "Give a maneh to so and so," and the giver died, and the money is nevertheless given after death. Rabbi Zeira explains Rav's statement that the coins must be gathered and lying together as a case of maamad sheloshtan. The Mishnah does not expressly mention the deposit held by the third party and does not say, "Give him this deposited maneh," yet the transfer is effective. The Gidulei Terumah asks that according to Rav Pappa, who explains the Mishnah as a person on his deathbed and is concerned for a buried maneh, the Mishnah must really mean "this maneh," because otherwise there could be an undisclosed buried maneh, even though the word "this" is absent from the text. If so, Rabbi Zeira too, who explains it as a healthy person using maamad sheloshtan, can understand the unexpressed word "this" in the same way. One answer is that the word "this" can indeed sometimes be omitted by the Tanna and understood from the context, especially because it may refer back to the first clause. This works for a deposit, where one can say "this maneh." With a loan, however, even saying "this" does not help. The coins are no longer the creditor's specific property because a loan is given for expenditure. According to the Rosh, who disagrees with the Ramban, maamad sheloshtan over a loan works only where the creditor says, "The maneh of mine that you owe, give it to so and so." Merely saying "this maneh" cannot identify a loan claim. Therefore, if Rabbi Zeira were explaining the Mishnah as a loan, the wording would still be insufficient. The Gemara says that Rav Pappa disagrees with Rabbi Zeira because Rav holds maamad sheloshtan applies both to a loan and a deposit. That still does not resolve the wording. Rav's ruling could apply in principle to loans, while this Mishnah must concern a deposit because it lacks the language "the maneh of mine in your hand." The Gemara's question would remain. Another answer is that the Ramban's text in the first clause followed the Rif: "Give this get, etc." The Ramban, as appears from Milchamot, does not accept the Rif's distinction between preventing retraction and actual emancipation, and can establish that clause according to Rabbi Meir as Tosafot do. According to Rav Pappa, who explains the later clause as a person on his deathbed, one can say that the word "this" in the first clause grammatically governs the later clause as well. The Tanna often shortens his wording this way. But according to Rabbi Zeira, who establishes the latter clause as maamad sheloshtan, the two clauses necessarily function independently. Otherwise, if the same maamad sheloshtan setting applied to the first clause, the slave or recipient would be present and willing to receive the transfer. Maamad sheloshtan cannot be imposed against the recipient's will according to all opinions. In that setting, why would the law of emancipation differ? If the slave is present and agrees to receive the benefit, everyone, even Rabbi Meir, agrees that one may acquire a benefit for him. The document should therefore be delivered. We must say each clause has its own circumstances. The word "this" in the first clause therefore cannot be borrowed for the second. The second clause does not say "this," yet Rabbi Zeira still understands it as maamad sheloshtan. The Ramban's proof is therefore strong. This also explains the Ramah and Rosh, who derive their contrary rule from the same sugya. They accept the Gidulei Terumah's point and also follow Rabbenu Tam that the legal effect of "give" as "acquire" requires actual hand-to-hand delivery. Therefore Tosafot's question from "give" does not arise in the same way. They can understand the Mishnah as though "this" were stated and nevertheless derive that specific identifying language is needed for a loan. There is a further difficulty I raised above. How can the Mishnah be interpreted as a loan at all? Even if we imply the word "this," that does not identify a loan claim. The phrase "the maneh of mine in your hand" is missing. The Rosh and Ramah can answer that Rabbi Zeira holds maamad sheloshtan applies to both loans and deposits. If the Mishnah's purpose were specifically to teach the law of maamad sheloshtan, the Tanna should have taught the greater novelty, a loan, with the wording "the maneh of mine in your hand," and the law of a deposit would follow all the more so, since some authorities hold maamad sheloshtan does not apply to loans. The fact that the Mishnah does not use this more informative formulation shows that its subject is not maamad sheloshtan at all, but rather the law of a person on his deathbed. This also resolves Tosafot's question why Rav Pappa strained to establish the Mishnah as gathered coins and a deathbed gift if Rav himself holds maamad sheloshtan applies even to a loan. The answer is that one cannot comfortably interpret it as a loan because the necessary words "the maneh of mine in your hand" are missing; but if one interprets it as a deposit, that itself raises the question why the Mishnah did not teach the greater novelty of a loan. Therefore the Mishnah is not teaching maamad sheloshtan. It is teaching the law of a deathbed instruction. The Gidulei Terumah further challenges the Ramban from the Rosh in chapter HaMekabel. The Rosh writes that the shopkeeper is not obligated to pay the workers because he holds nothing belonging to the employer. Why does it matter that he holds nothing? Even if he did hold funds, according to the Rosh a general redirection without expressly saying "the maneh of mine in your hand" should not transfer them. The Rif writes similarly in chapter HaMekabel. The Shach in seif katan 21 attempts to distinguish. He says the Rosh denies acquisition only where Shimon holds exactly one maneh and the transferor says generally, "Give a maneh to Levi." If Shimon held more than one maneh and the transferor said, "Give one maneh to Levi," the Rosh would agree that the recipient acquires. This would answer the question. I do not understand the reason. If the Shach means that where more is held the transferor could not literally say "the maneh of mine in your hand" because there are several maneh, his language was hardly so limited. He could simply say, "From the money of mine in your hand, give a maneh to Levi." Who fixed a rigid formula that permits no added words? I do not see any other basis for the Shach's distinction. It seems more likely that a shopkeeper and moneychanger are different, and even the Rosh agrees with the Ramban in that commercial setting. The Nimukei Yosef cites a Gaon who holds that with a shopkeeper or moneychanger, maamad sheloshtan can obligate them even when they hold nothing at all belonging to the employer. The Rosh does not go that far. But where the employer has actually advanced money to a shopkeeper because he is occupied with workers and directs the shopkeeper to pay each worker, one a dinar, another an issar, and later settles accounts with him, the Rosh can agree that ordinary language is treated as though the specific funds were identified. This is the regular commercial arrangement for which the money was advanced. Regarding a shopkeeper or moneychanger, therefore, a general instruction is equivalent to an explicit one. There is no difficulty. This is the better ruling, and indeed with shopkeepers and moneychangers all should agree, since the Gaon would obligate them even without existing funds.
