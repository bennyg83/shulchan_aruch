Seif 13 in the gloss. (Even if he gave him a handwritten acknowledgment.) In the case discussed in siman 126, and similarly in the Shach at the end of siman 81, if the handwritten instrument records a kinyan sudar, a later claim of mistake should not ordinarily succeed unless the signer had been under a definite mistaken assumption that money was unquestionably owed. If he knew from the outset that the matter was uncertain, for example he hoped to recover from others and expected a profit, then he knowingly accepted that uncertainty and nevertheless obligated himself through a kinyan sudar or through an acknowledgment supported by such a kinyan. A kinyan sudar can remove ordinary asmachta concerns in a gratuitous obligation such as a custodian accepting the liability of a paid custodian. By contrast, a mere handwritten undertaking without a kinyan may not create liability for an uncertain future obligation, as the Shach explains in siman 40. The case is stronger where the instrument expressly records a kinyan before an important beit din in a form designed to remove asmachta. The same considerations that apply to a migo of payment may apply to a migo of forgery where the handwriting has not been authenticated. Yet a forgery claim may be a weaker migo than payment because the signer fears that the handwriting will be authenticated. Urim VeTumim distinguishes a document signed only by the debtor because he might claim that he forgot his own signature, but at first glance such a claim is weaker than a direct claim of forgery, and the rules at the beginning of siman 69 require further comparison.
