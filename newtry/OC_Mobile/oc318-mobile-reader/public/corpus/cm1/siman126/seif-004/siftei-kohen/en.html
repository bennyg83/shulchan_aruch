But if he said generally, “Give him fifty dinars from the wheat that I have in your possession,” etc. The same applies if he merely said, “Give him fifty dinars, for I have a kor of wheat in your possession,” as in the opening case. It is treated as though he said, “Give him fifty dinars from the wheat in your possession,” meaning wheat worth fifty dinars, and the recipient acquires. This is clear from the Baal HaTerumot, gate 28, end of part 3, and Responsa Maharit, siman 37. Maharit further writes that if the assignor specifies that the third party should pay from his own money, with the deposit remaining as collateral until reimbursement, or says, “Give him a certain sum because you are secured by the deposit I have with you,” the recipient does not acquire. See there.
It is treated as though he said, “Wheat worth,” etc. It appears that even according to the view above in siman 88, seif 10, that with a deposit one follows the literal commodity claimed, that case is different. There the claimant says, “I specifically deposited one hundred coins with you.” Here, the third party holds only wheat. Therefore, when the assignor says, “Give him fifty dinars, for I have wheat in your possession,” and all the more so when he says, “Give him fifty dinars from the wheat,” he means wheat worth that amount. This resembles the ruling below in siman 253, seif 19, concerning one who says, “Give one hundred to so-and-so from my estate.” Indeed, the present case is stronger. There one might have argued that he meant actual coins, perhaps lost or buried, yet we do not entertain that concern. All the more so here, where it is known that the third party holds only wheat. According to all opinions, he certainly means wheat of equivalent value.
And if it was a loan, etc. Although with respect to a partial admission we find that wheat and money are not regarded as the same species of claim, as stated above in siman 88, seif 9, that rule follows the claim’s original form. Here, however, we look to the resulting obligation. The debtor bound himself to the recipient by virtue of an existing obligation resting upon him. Whether the original loan was wheat to be repaid in wheat, or wheat whose value is to be paid in money, it remains a monetary obligation and the two are equivalent. The recipient therefore acquires. The debtor must give him fifty dinars’ worth from the wheat. This does not mean that if the wheat later depreciates and is no longer worth fifty dinars he is nonetheless bound to deliver only that wheat, nor that he must pay actual coins. Rather, the assignment gives the recipient a valid claim, and the debtor may satisfy it with money or with wheat worth fifty dinars at the market rate. Whatever he gives constitutes payment, because property of monetary value is equivalent to money. These are the Ramban’s words as cited by the Baal HaTerumot.
