The rule concerning a small gift does not apply here to a poor recipient in the ordinary manner. Sema writes that a promise to a poor person must be fulfilled even if it was made privately, because a declaration to charity has special force or because it is a vow. The Shach limits this where the subject is deposited money. He says that if the coins were loose rather than sealed in a way that prohibited use, and the custodian was permitted to use them, the poor person cannot acquire because the money is like property outside the owner's possession. In my view that is incorrect so long as the custodian has not actually spent the money. Permission to use deposited coins does not make them a loan immediately. Until the custodian uses them, the original coins remain the owner's deposit. If the owner demands them, the custodian must return those very coins and cannot substitute others merely because he had permission to use them. The custodian is only a paid custodian because he enjoys the right to use them. The money therefore remains in the owner's domain. The same is true of a borrowed object. The owner can consecrate it while it is in the borrower's hands, as explained in siman 211. Rabbi Tzvi Ashkenazi states this correctly in his gloss to Taz, siman 73. Accordingly, unused deposited money can likewise be consecrated or dedicated to charity even if the custodian was permitted to use it. It is unlike a loan, which is given to be spent and leaves the lender's present ownership.
