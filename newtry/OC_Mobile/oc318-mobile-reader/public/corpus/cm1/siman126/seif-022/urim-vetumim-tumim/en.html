And by this you will be exempt from me. Some say that the recipient acquires. The Shach, seif katan 93, objects that if the non Jewish debtor refuses to pay the recipient and the transferor can obtain the money through some device, he is entitled to do so. In the Shach's view the recipient never acquired anything. The only effect of the transferor releasing the non Jew is that the recipient resembles one taking abandoned property, and a non Jew has no power of zechiyah to transfer property to a Jew. His words are not understandable. The meaning is simple. The transferor told the non Jew: undertake by vow or binding obligation to pay so and so, meaning, create a complete personal obligation toward the recipient, and in exchange for that obligation you will be discharged from what you owe me. Instead of owing me, obligate yourself to him. The recipient can therefore collect from the non Jew by law, because the non Jew personally obligated himself. Even a Jew who says before witnesses, "I owe you one hundred," can become liable though he never borrowed anything. All the more so here, where the second Jew released the non Jew in exchange for the new obligation. No transfer through the non Jew is needed. Indeed, I do not know why Maharam needed elaborate methods to transfer a debt owed by a non Jew. The second Jew, the recipient, could simply say to the non Jew, "In reliance on your undertaking, I release the first Jew." The non Jew would then become liable under the law of a guarantor, as the Rosh writes regarding workers and as explained above. One must say that the case concerns where the second Jew does not wish to release the first. Under Maharam's arrangement he can still return against the transferor if necessary, while the non Jew is independently obligated to him by his undertaking.
To pay the Jewish recipient as well. The Shach, seif katan 99, argues at length that the second Jew did not acquire, because a non Jew cannot create zechiyah for a Jew through another Jew. If the reason for collection is only that the Sages deprived the non Jew of property, why should that operate where the result is a loss to the first Jew? Why should rabbinic law take money from one Jew for this purpose? I found in Asifat Zekenim to Perek Eizehu Neshech, in the name of Ritva, concerning a non Jew who borrowed from one Jew and delivered the money to a second Jew in the presence of the first lender. The Gemara treats the arrangement stringently even though a non Jew has no agency. Ritva writes that there is a substantive reason for the stringency: sometimes the non Jew gives the money to the second Jew so that he should acquire it completely on behalf of the first Jew. A non Jew cannot himself acquire on behalf of a Jew, and a Jew cannot acquire on behalf of a non Jew, but the non Jew can cause a Jew to acquire money for another Jew. That is precisely our situation. The non Jew directs the Jewish holder or debtor to make the money belong to another Jew, and according to Ritva the recipient acquires by law. It is true that other authorities do not sound this way, but the Shach's objection is not decisive for another reason. We rule that one may appropriate a debt owed by a non Jew. If so, the Jewish debtor could say that he retained the money for himself, in which case nothing of the non Jew remains in his hands and maamad sheloshtan should fail. Although he verbally agreed to pay, why should that create liability if the non Jew's loan is regarded as available for appropriation? This initially seemed to me a serious difficulty. Perhaps the case is where the recipient is himself a creditor of the non Jew. Then, because under their law the non Jew cannot retract from the transfer, the two Jewish parties are discharged against one another. The Jewish debtor could become obligated to the recipient through the law of guarantor, since in reliance upon his undertaking the recipient released his claim against the non Jew. But this is strained in the Rosh. He speaks as though the rule follows maamad sheloshtan, not guarantor law, and he writes generally whether the recipient is owed a debt or receives a gift. Moreover, if the recipient were already the non Jew's creditor, Rabbi Natan's rule would itself create a Torah claim against the Jewish debtor where the non Jew has no other accessible property, making maamad sheloshtan unnecessary.
However. The main answer to the Shach is therefore either that the Rosh follows Ritva, as stated, or that the Rosh understood the enactment of maamad sheloshtan itself as applying equally here. We do not find two separate enactments, one for Jews and another for non Jews. Either the institution applies only among Jews, or once the non Jew's money is held by a Jew who is legally capable of acquisition and the non Jew directs him to transfer it to another Jew, the Sages made the second Jew acquire. Indeed, even among Jews, what ordinary zechiyah exists in a loan already spent and no longer physically present? A debtor cannot simply "acquire" the creditor's intangible debt for another person through ordinary possession. Maamad sheloshtan is itself a special rabbinic institution. Therefore the fact that a non Jew lacks ordinary zechiyah does not necessarily prevent the enactment from applying when the actual holder is a Jew. One can formulate it still more simply. The non Jew withdraws his claim to the money, making it effectively ownerless, and the first Jew acquires it on behalf of the second Jew as his agent. Tosafos in Perek Eizehu Neshech writes a similar idea. This may be the Rosh's intention: because under their law the non Jew cannot retract, he has effectively removed his own right, and the Jewish holder can acquire for the second Jew. There is therefore no contradiction. The earlier difficulty from appropriation of a non Jewish debt can perhaps be answered by saying that so long as the Jew has not expressly declared that he is appropriating and extinguishing the non Jew's debt, the debt remains legally the non Jew's. Permission to appropriate is not an automatic royal confiscation. Only when the Jew expressly says that he is taking it for himself does that rule operate. Until then the debt remains in the non Jew's legal domain for ordinary purposes. This point deserves further study, because if correct it resolves difficulties in several places.
All that he spent upon it. The Shach, seif katan 102, asks why the Rama omitted the Beit Yosef's dissenting view and the opinion of Rabbi Moshe of Pontoise in Mordechai. He answers that they disagree only where the robbers could not themselves seize the deposit, but if they had power over it everyone agrees that the custodian recovers what he spent. In my view, the Rama's unqualified wording shows that he ruled like Hagahot Mordechai and rejected Maharam's dissent. Maharam's difficulty is obvious from the case where robbers attacked a man because they believed him wealthy. If they came specifically because of entrusted money, he may hand over that money to save himself. Here too, if the entire violence arose because of the deposit, then if the depositor would not reimburse his loss, the custodian should have been permitted to surrender the very deposit the robbers sought and thereby save his own property. Maharam apparently follows Rashba's distinction concerning the rule that even under compulsion, one who physically hands over another person's property is liable. Rashba distinguishes whether the robbers already had control and could take the property themselves. If they did not know where the deposit was and could not take it without the custodian's help, he may not hand it over. Maharam therefore regarded the custodian as prohibited from surrendering the deposit and hence not automatically entitled to reimbursement. But Tosafos and Maharam elsewhere distinguish a custodian from an ordinary person whose friend's property happens to be in danger. A custodian accepted the deposit with the implicit understanding that he would not be required to lose his own property in order to protect another's. Maharshal, Sema, and Shach later accept this distinction in siman 386. Once that is accepted, Hagahot Mordechai's position is correct. A custodian is different. Surely he did not undertake to be captured or to lose his own property for the sake of the depositor's property. The Rama therefore correctly ruled like Hagahot Mordechai without qualification.
