Some say that there is no distinction between a Jew and a non-Jew with respect to ma'amad sheloshtan. Therefore, if a Jew says to a non-Jew, in the presence of all three, "Give the hundred zuz that I have in your possession to this Jew," once the recipient agrees, he acquires it. Likewise, if a non-Jew says to a Jew, "Give the hundred zuz that I have in your possession to this Jew," in the presence of all three, and the depositary or debtor says, "I will do so," the recipient acquires it and the holder must give it to him; he cannot claim that he acted under duress. Others disagree and hold that if the depositary or debtor is a non-Jew and the Jewish depositor or creditor tells him, in the presence of all three, to give it to another Jew, the recipient does not acquire it. Rama: Nevertheless, if he says, "Give it to so-and-so and vow to him that you will give it, and thereby you will be discharged from me," some say that the recipient acquires it. Mordechai, chapter Mi Shemet, and Mahariv, siman 33. In every case, if the non-Jew has already delivered what he held to the Jewish recipient, it is not taken from the recipient. Likewise, whenever a kinyan cannot legally take effect but the recipient has already taken possession, it is not removed from him. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 313. If the depositary or debtor is Jewish and the recipient is a non-Jew, the depositary may give it to the non-Jew so long as the Jewish giver has not retracted. If the giver retracts, the non-Jew did not acquire it, and the depositary must return it to the depositor. If, however, the depositary cannot avoid the non-Jew because under their law he is obligated to pay once he agreed to do so, he may pay the non-Jew and is exempt toward the depositor. If the giver is a non-Jew and both the recipient and the depositary are Jews, the Jewish recipient acquires it and may recover it from the depositary in beit din. If the non-Jewish giver is coercive and forcibly takes the money back before it is given to the Jewish recipient, then if the underlying obligation was a loan, the debtor must also pay the Jewish recipient. If it was a deposit and the non-Jew entered the depositary's house and forcibly took it, the depositary need not pay the Jewish recipient. Even if the depositary used his own property to save the deposit, he may reimburse himself from the deposit for all that he spent on it. Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 3 of Bava Batra.
