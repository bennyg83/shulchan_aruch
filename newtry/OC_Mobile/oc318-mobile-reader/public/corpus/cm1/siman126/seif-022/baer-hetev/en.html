To a non-Jew. Responsa Maharashdam 359 writes that just as, according to Ri, ma'amad sheloshtan does not operate with a non-Jew, the same is true of a Jew who has converted away from Judaism. The Shach rejects this. Ri's rule regarding a non-Jew is based on the idea that ma'amad sheloshtan cannot be stronger than a full halachic acquisition, and a non-Jew has no halachic acquisition through another's agency. A Jewish apostate, however, still has halachic capacity to acquire, as the Gemara and authorities state in many places, including Even HaEzer 140. Knesset HaGedolah cites Responsa Rashach I, 153, that a Jewish apostate who is treated like a non-Jew for certain monetary matters, including lending with interest, is likewise treated as a non-Jew for this law.
And agreed. Meaning that the recipient cannot return to the Jewish debtor who originally owed him, because he caused his own loss by agreeing to release himself from the Jewish creditor and pursue the non-Jew instead. So states the Tur. This is true specifically according to the first opinion in seif 9. According to the latter opinion there, the recipient may return to the original debtor unless he explicitly released him. The Beit Yosef, Bach, and Responsa Maharashdam 86 write so. Accordingly, the Mechaber's unqualified wording here is somewhat difficult. One must say that he relies upon the two opinions already cited in seif 9, from which it follows that this case too depends upon that dispute. Shach. The Taz disagrees. In his view even the latter opinion in seif 9 agrees here that the recipient has released the original debtor, because under the non-Jewish legal system the non-Jew is already discharged from the transferor. This can be learned from the later case where the recipient is a non-Jew and the bailee is exempt because he cannot avoid paying him. Here too the recipient himself caused the transferor's loss.
I will do so. The Tur uses the same wording. Although the substitute debtor's consent is ordinarily unnecessary, as stated in seif 7, the case of a non-Jew differs because he could claim that he remained silent only because of coercion. So writes the Sema. The Taz explains differently: here we do not otherwise know that he owes the money, and by saying "I will do so" he admits it. The same would be necessary with a Jew in such circumstances. The novelty is that with a non-Jew one might have thought the admission ineffective because it was made under coercion. The Shach writes that if he merely remained silent, practical law requires study. Silence can be treated as admission, so perhaps he has admitted the debt. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch may use "I will do so" only to exclude a case where he says, "I do not agree because I hold nothing of yours, but I will be forced to admit because of coercion." In that situation he can later say that he acted under duress and that the creditor should not have released the original debtor based on such a transfer. A similar rule appears below in siman 131, seif 6.
Coerced. The Tur writes that even if the non-Jewish transferor is a government official, the substitute debtor cannot later say, "I was coerced." There is no distinction here between monetary coercion and danger to life. Maharashdam, Choshen Mishpat 55. Knesset HaGedolah.
Give. The Shach writes that the Rama's words require study. What difference does it make that the transferor instructed the non-Jew to give it? According to these authorities, a non-Jew cannot acquire through the relevant halachic mechanism. See there.
Seized. In siman 66, seif 17, the Shach wrote that the Rama's general rule concerning cases where the underlying kinyan is ineffective is incorrect. Nevertheless, the present law is correct. Once the non-Jew has actually transferred what he held to the recipient, even without ma'amad sheloshtan the recipient acquires, because the transferor authorized the payment and the non-Jew carried out his agency. It is as though the transferor himself handed it to the recipient. Shach.
To give it. Even if the transferor tells the Jewish bailee privately, "Give the maneh I have in your possession to the non-Jew," it is obvious that he should give it so long as the Jewish owner has not retracted. Tur and Rosh. Tosafot writes likewise. Shach.
And is liable. This is the view of the dissenting authority. Their reason is that the Sages did not enact that a non-Jew should acquire through ma'amad sheloshtan, a special rule lacking an ordinary conceptual basis. Shach.
From the depositor. The depositor caused his own loss by instructing the bailee to give the property to the non-Jew, knowing that the bailee would have to comply. Tur and Rosh. Shach.
Acquired. The Tur and Rosh reason that if the Sages displaced a Jew's property through ma'amad sheloshtan, all the more so they could displace a non-Jew's property. Their wording implies that even if the non-Jew does not demand payment from the bailee, or if the non-Jew dies, the recipient may still extract from the Jew. The Shach later writes that this requires study and that Tosafot and other authorities appear not to agree. See Responsa Maharit I, 36.
To pay. The Tur says that the substitute debtor already became obligated to the Jewish recipient, and if the non-Jew later coercively takes money from the debtor, the Jewish recipient should not lose because of that. The Shach writes that this law requires study because it is unclear what created the substitute debtor's obligation to the Jewish recipient. See his lengthy discussion.
A deposit. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch state this law in siman 292, seif 8. It applies specifically to an actual deposit, not where a person is coerced to bring another's money to an oppressor, as stated in siman 388, seif 4. Sema.
Took. Meaning that the non-Jew took it. So reads Hagahot Mordechai. The Rama may also mean that if the Jew gave his own money and thereby saved the deposit, because otherwise the non-Jew would have taken the deposit, the case is treated as though the non-Jew himself took it. See the gloss at the end of siman 128, seif 1. Shach.
That he expended. The Shach writes that the Rama's unqualified ruling requires study because Hagahot Mordechai concludes that Maharam of Pontoise disagrees. See the Shach's resolution and distinctions. The Taz writes that he found an emended Mordechai in which Maharam's statement belongs before this passage and refers instead to the earlier matter. If the deposit had already been delivered to the Jew, see the Shach regarding the law. The matter requires study.
