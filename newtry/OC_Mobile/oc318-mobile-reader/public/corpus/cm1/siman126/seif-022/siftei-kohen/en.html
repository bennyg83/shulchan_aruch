["Since the recipient consented. Meaning, the recipient cannot return against the Jewish debtor, because he agreed to remove himself from the Jewish debtor and seek payment from the non Jew; he therefore caused his own loss. This is the language of the Tur. It applies only according to the first view above, seif 9. According to the latter view there, the recipient may return against the original debtor unless he expressly released him, as the Beit Yosef and Bach write, and likewise Maharshal in responsum 86. Accordingly, the Mechaber's unqualified ruling here is somewhat difficult. It must be that because he already brought both views in seif 9, it is understood that this ruling follows the first view, whereas the latter requires an express release."]
["I will do so, etc. The Sema writes that the Tur and Mechaber deliberately state that the Jew said, I will do so, even though the consent of the person against whom the assignment is made is generally unnecessary, as stated in seif 7. A non Jew is different, because he could say that he remained silent only from duress. This explanation is internally inconsistent. At the beginning the Sema refers to one who expressly says, I do not want to, since he cites seif 7, where the Tur expressly rules that the transfer may be effected against the assignee's will, while at the end he says that silence may be explained as duress. As a matter of law, silence requires study, because silence is treated as admission, and he has thereby admitted that he will pay. The phrase, I will do so, used by the Tur and Mechaber need not be literal. It may only exclude a case where he said: I do not want to, because I owe him nothing and I will be forced by duress to admit that I will pay you. In such a case he can say that he acted under duress and that the recipient had no right to release the non Jew on the basis of this assignment. A similar rule is explained below, siman 131 seif 6."]
["And he cannot say that he was coerced, etc. The Baal HaTerumot and Tur state that the non Jewish giver was a ruler, and nevertheless rule that the Jew cannot claim, I was coerced."]
["He did not acquire. Even if the giver had attempted to confer the right through a non Jew, the recipient would not acquire, because a non Jew has no legal capacity of zechiyah, and maamad sheloshtan is no stronger than a full acquisition by zechiyah. This is the Tur and the authorities who follow this view. Maharshal writes in responsum 359 that just as, according to Rabbenu Yitzchak, maamad sheloshtan does not apply to a non Jew, so too it does not apply to a Jew who converted out of Judaism. In my view this is incorrect. Only a non Jew lacks this rule because maamad sheloshtan is no stronger than zechiyah and a non Jew has no zechiyah. A Jew who converted nevertheless retains the legal capacity of zechiyah, as is established in many places in the Gemara and authorities, including Even HaEzer siman 140."]
["Nevertheless, etc., some say that the recipient acquired. The Rama's words require study. What does it help that he used this language to the non Jew, if according to these authorities there is no zechiyah for a non Jew? I examined Mahari Weil, Mordechai, and Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, responsum 68 of Maharam, and they do not use the formulation quoted by the Rama. Their words can instead be explained as follows: since the giver said to the non Jew, promise to pay him, and by this you will be released from me, as soon as the non Jew made that promise he became discharged from the giver, as though the giver had waived his claim against him. The giver plainly can waive a claim against a non Jew. The recipient may then collect from the non Jew because the giver's language amounted to a waiver in his favor, and the recipient takes as one taking ownerless property. The Rama's language does not appear to mean this, because he places the rule under maamad sheloshtan and says that the recipient acquired, implying a full legal acquisition. On that reading, even if the giver could later extract payment from the non Jew through some device he would be forbidden to do so. According to my explanation, however, there was only a waiver. The recipient may collect from the non Jew as one who takes from ownerless property, but if the giver can extract payment from him he may do so as well. The Rama's formulation therefore requires study. The Ir Shushan omitted this gloss, perhaps for this reason."]
["And in every case, etc. I already wrote above, siman 66 seif 17, that the Rama is incorrect in his general statement that whenever the acquisition itself does not take effect, if the recipient nevertheless obtained the money we do not remove it from him. See there. The present ruling, however, is correct. If the non Jew already gave what was in his possession, then even without maamad sheloshtan the recipient acquires it, because the giver authorized him to pay, he carried out that authorization, and the payment is treated as though the giver himself made it."]
["He may give it to the non Jew. Even if the giver said privately, I have one hundred in your possession; give it to the non Jew, it is obvious that the holder must give it to him so long as the Jew does not retract. So write the Tur and Rosh, and Tosafos likewise."]
["The non Jew did not acquire. This is the view of the dissenting authority. Its reason is that the enactment of maamad sheloshtan, which is a law without an apparent reason, was not instituted for a non Jew so as to give him an acquisition through it."]
["He shall give it to the non Jew. The Jew caused his own loss by instructing him to give it to the non Jew, knowing that the custodian would then be required to do so. So write the Tur and Rosh."]
["The Jew acquired. According to the Tur and Rosh, he may even recover from the custodian through beit din, because if the Sages divested a Jew's property through maamad sheloshtan, all the more so may they divest the property of a non Jew. Their language implies that even if the non Jew no longer sues the custodian, or if the non Jew died, the recipient may still recover from the Jewish custodian. Below I explain that this requires study and that Tosafos and other authorities appear not to agree."]
["The borrower must also pay the Jewish recipient. The Tur, following the Rosh, explains that the borrower already became obligated to the Jewish recipient, and therefore if the non Jew later coerced the borrower to pay him, the recipient does not bear that loss. This requires study. On what basis did the borrower become obligated to the Jew? According to this view maamad sheloshtan is no stronger than full zechiyah, as the Tur and Rosh themselves write. Just as a non Jew cannot acquire for a Jew through zechiyah, neither can a non Jew confer acquisition upon a Jew through another Jew, as stated explicitly in Bava Metzia 71b: agency and zechiyah do not operate for a non Jew, neither from them to us nor from us to them. It follows that the Jewish recipient never acquired this money. Rather, if the non Jew comes before our court to retract from the assignment, or if the Jewish custodian or borrower seeks to return the deposit or money to the non Jew because he retracted, we rule that the non Jew may not retract. Since the Sages divested a Jew's property through maamad sheloshtan, a law without an apparent reason, all the more so can they divest the property of a non Jew; the non Jew should not profit from being a wrongdoer. But there is no reason to make the Jew personally liable if the non Jew later takes the money from him by force. This is also implied by Tosafos, the source of this rule. Tosafos write that where the giver is a non Jew, it appears that the recipient acquires, because if the Sages divested Jewish property through maamad sheloshtan, certainly they may do so with non Jewish property; moreover, we judge him according to their law and say to the non Jew that this is also your own law. Their meaning appears to be that where the non Jew submits to Jewish adjudication and seeks to retract, or where the custodian or borrower wishes to return the property because the non Jew retracted, we rule that he cannot retract and the recipient acquires. But if the non Jew simply extracts the money by force from the Jewish custodian or borrower, why should that Jew be liable, when the recipient never acquired the money through zechiyah or agency? Mordechai likewise indicates this. He writes that if the giver is a non Jew and the custodian and recipient are Jews, the recipient acquires; although zechiyah is derived from agency and there is no agency for a non Jew, there is a fortiori reasoning that if the Sages divested Jewish property through the unexplained law of maamad sheloshtan, they certainly may divest non Jewish property. This still assumes that there is no agency for a non Jew. It means only that the non Jew's property is divested and he is compelled to give it and cannot retract, not that the custodian becomes personally liable if the non Jew takes it by force. The Agudah, Semag, and Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Kinyan likewise support this reading. The Rosh, however, appears to understand that because the non Jew's own legal system recognizes such a transfer, the Jew is judged under that system as well. His reasoning requires study, for why should one Jew be judged against another Jew according to non Jewish law? Perhaps, since the money belonged to a non Jew and he transferred it in a manner effective under his own law, we apply that law even as between Jews, comparable to siman 66 seif 25 and the end of siman 369 concerning a Jew who acquired a debt instrument from a non Jew under their law. Yet even there it requires consideration whether, if the non Jew later forcibly collected from the debtor, the debtor would be exempt. Maharam of Lublin, responsum 22, expressly ruled that a Jew who purchased a note from a non Jew must provide security that if the non Jew later brings a false claim and collects from the debtor, the purchaser will indemnify him. That implies that if the non Jew collects by force, the debtor must be reimbursed, even though there were witnesses that the note had been sold under their law. All the more so here should the Jew be exempt. Even if one distinguishes the sale of a note, because the instrument itself was made in a non Jewish court and the purchaser stands in the non Jew's legal position as though named in the document, that rationale does not apply here. It is also difficult to understand by what acquisition the Jew would acquire. One must therefore say either that once the Sages divested the non Jew's property they did not distinguish further and treated the Jewish recipient as having acquired even against the Jewish custodian, or that the rule that there is no agency or zechiyah involving a non Jew applies only where a Jew would acquire from another Jew through the non Jew. Here, by maamad sheloshtan, the non Jew is first removed from the property by rabbinic divestment, and then the Jew holding the money transfers it to the other Jew by his own act, not through the non Jew. The matter still requires study. See Maharshal, responsum 155."]
["He is not required to pay. In a deposit, the first Jew is merely a custodian for the second to the same extent that he had been custodian for the first, and he remains exempt from unavoidable accidents. Even if the non Jew coerced him until he brought the deposit into the non Jew's house, he is exempt. This is not considered saving himself with another's property, because the coercion was directed only at that deposit. With a loan this reasoning does not apply, because money lent is given for expenditure, and forcing him to pay is equivalent to forcing him to give other money. So write the Tur and Rosh. See also siman 292 seif 8 and siman 388 seif 4."]
["And even if he took, etc. The Hagahot Mordechai states this rule regarding a Jewish deposit, as explained below. The Rama records it here regarding a non Jewish deposit in accordance with the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber, who hold that the Jewish holder already became obligated to give the deposit to the Jewish recipient. According to what I wrote above, however, that position of the Rosh requires study and the recipient may not have acquired against the Jewish holder. If so, the holder may plainly deduct from the deposit whatever he was forced to expend because of it, and even Rabbi Moshe of Pontoise in Hagahot Mordechai would agree."]
["He took his own, etc. The Rama's unqualified ruling requires study. Hagahot Mordechai concludes that Rabbi Moshe of Pontoise disputes Rabbi Yitzchak on this point, and the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 72 likewise writes that Rabbi Yitzchak's view does not appear correct. The Darkei Moshe cites this disagreement, which suggests that he sided with the Beit Yosef. One cannot simply say that here, because the deposit belongs to a non Jew, even the Beit Yosef and Rabbi Moshe would agree on the ground that the Jewish recipient never acquired it, for then the Rama should not have accepted the Mechaber's ruling regarding a loan. Perhaps the Rama later retracted and accepted Rabbi Yitzchak's position, as may be inferred from his responsum 86. Alternatively, perhaps the Beit Yosef disputed only a case where the coercers seized Reuven's pledge for Shimon while believing it was Shimon's property. In that event Reuven's own misfortune caused the loss, since they did not know it was his, and perhaps he should have informed them. But where they knowingly seize Reuven's property on account of Shimon until Reuven gives them Shimon's property, it is as though they seized Shimon's property itself, and the Rama's gloss refers to such a case. Although Rabbi Moshe of Pontoise appears to dispute even this, his proof from the Yerushalmi, that one seized for another is generally not reimbursed except in cases of royal tax, is not decisive. There the seized person held none of the other's property. Here he does hold the other's property and could have surrendered it, since the coercion was directed specifically at that other person. Therefore the Rama may reasonably hold that he may deduct from the deposit whatever he expended because of it. His responsum, however, seems to indicate that Hagahot Mordechai itself involved a case where the coercers knew the property was Reuven's and nevertheless seized it for Shimon, and that the Beit Yosef still disagreed. As a practical matter, where they merely thought the property was Shimon's, Shimon appears exempt. Another possibility is that the Beit Yosef disputed only where the coercers could not themselves seize Shimon's pledge and therefore seized Reuven's until he would deliver Shimon's. In such a case Shimon is exempt because the coercers could not have reached his property, and the seizure of Reuven's property is not treated as a direct taking from Shimon. But if they could have seized Shimon's pledge, knew exactly where it was, and simply said that they preferred to take Reuven's property rather than trouble themselves, it is as though they actually seized Shimon's property. Reuven may then deduct from Shimon's deposit whatever was taken from him on Shimon's account. This is the case intended by the Rama's phrase, even if they took his own property and he thereby saved the deposit, meaning that by taking Reuven's property they spared a deposit that they could otherwise have seized."]
["His own, etc. Meaning that the non Jew took Reuven's property because Reuven protected the deposit and refused to hand it over, as in Hagahot Mordechai. Perhaps the Rama also means that Reuven voluntarily gave his own property in order to save the deposit, for if he had not done so the non Jew would have taken the deposit; this is then treated as though the non Jew himself had taken the deposit. See below, siman 128, end of seif 8 in the gloss."]
["He deducts from the deposit. At first glance this is not literal. Even if he had already returned the deposit to the Jew, the latter should have to reimburse what the custodian expended, because the liability arose when the custodian could have handed the deposit to the coercers. Hagahot Mordechai must likewise not mean only actual retention of the deposit. Perhaps, however, because the Rama saw that Rabbi Moshe of Pontoise disputes the rule, he deliberately wrote that one deducts from the deposit while still in possession. As the holder, he may retain it. But if he already returned the deposit, the other party cannot be compelled to pay, because in a monetary doubt we do not extract from the possessor."]
