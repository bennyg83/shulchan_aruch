To a gentile. Responsa Maharshdam, siman 359, writes that just as ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply to a gentile according to Rabbeinu Yonah, so too it does not apply to an apostate Jew. The Shach disagrees, because the rule concerning a gentile is based on the fact that ma'amad sheloshtan cannot be stronger than a full acquisition, and a gentile has no halachic mechanism of zechiyah. An apostate Jew, however, does have legal capacity for acquisition, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 140. The Shach's responsum, siman 153, similarly states that an apostate regarding whom the law is that we return his lost property, although it is permitted to lend him with interest, is treated like a gentile for this law. In truth, there is room to distinguish, and the practical law requires study.
And he agreed. Meaning, the recipient cannot then return against the Jewish debtor, because he caused his own loss by agreeing to release himself from the Jewish debtor and instead look to the gentile for payment. This is the Tur's ruling. It applies only according to the first view in seif 9. According to the latter view there, the recipient can retract unless he expressly released the first debtor. So write the Beit Yosef, Bach, and Maharshdam. The Mechaber evidently relied on what he wrote above. See Shach. The Taz disagrees with the Shach on this point.
I will do so. So writes the Tur. Although the transferee's consent is ordinarily unnecessary, as stated in seif 7, a gentile is different because one could argue that he remained silent only because of coercion. See Sema and Taz. The Shach leaves the case of silence requiring study, since silence is ordinarily like admission. He suggests that the Tur's words 'I will do so' are not literal, but merely exclude a case where he said, 'I do not agree,' etc.
Coerced. The Tur writes that even if the gentile transferor is a ruler, the recipient cannot say, 'I was coerced.' There is no distinction here between financial coercion and danger to life. So writes Maharshdam.
And you shall vow to him to give it. The Shach leaves the Rama's words requiring study. What is gained by saying this to the gentile, when according to this opinion a gentile cannot acquire for another through zechiyah? The Shach therefore rules that if the gentile refuses to pay the recipient and the original transferor can recover the money from him through some stratagem, he may do so, because the recipient never acquired it. Only by releasing the gentile does the recipient acquire it as from ownerless property, since a gentile cannot acquire for a Jew. See Tumim, where I concluded that the transferor tells the gentile to obligate himself directly toward the recipient in a complete obligation, and in exchange for that obligation he will be released from what he owes the transferor. No acquisition by the gentile on behalf of the Jew is then required. I further concluded that the Rama did not need this route at all. The first Jew can transfer the debt owed by the gentile to the second Jew, and the second Jew can release the first Jew on the basis of the gentile's acceptance, thereby obligating himself under the law of guarantorship. The Rama's case is where the second Jew does not wish to release the first Jew completely.
To give it to a gentile, etc. Even if the depositor said privately, 'The maneh I have in your possession, give it to the gentile,' it is obvious that the custodian should give it to him so long as the Jew does not retract. So write the Tur, Rosh, and Tosafot.
And the custodian is liable, etc. This is the view of those who disagree, whose reasoning is that the Sages did not enact that a gentile should acquire by ma'amad sheloshtan, which is itself a law without stated rationale. See Shach.
From the depositor. Because he caused his own loss when he instructed the custodian to give it to the gentile, knowing that the custodian was obligated to obey him. So write the Tur and Rosh.
To pay also, etc. The Shach discusses at length that the second Jew did not acquire, because a gentile cannot acquire for a Jew through another Jew, and the entire basis is only that the Sages confiscated the first Jew's property. Here, however, that confiscation causes the first Jew a loss. See there. In Tumim I discussed this at length and wrote that the Rosh holds that ma'amad sheloshtan applies in some form even with a gentile, and that we do not find two separate enactments within ma'amad sheloshtan. Since the special enactment applies, no ordinary zechiyah is necessary, because the rule is a law without stated rationale. This is also somewhat implied by the Shach and is correct. I also proposed a way to resolve the major difficulty that withholding repayment of a gentile's loan can be permitted. One must say that the Jew must expressly declare that he is withholding it under that legal license; ordinary retention is not enough. If this principle is correct, several difficulties are resolved. See there. The matter still requires study.
Even if he took, etc. Meaning, the gentile took it. So it appears in Hagahot Mordechai. It is also possible that the Rama means that if the Jew paid from his own property to save the deposit, it is as though the gentile himself took the deposit. See Shach.
All that he spent. The Shach asks why the Rama omitted the Beit Yosef's view and that of Maharam of Pontoise, who disagrees in the Mordechai. He answers that the Rama speaks where the gentile could exercise control over the deposit, a case in which Maharam agrees. See Tumim, where I wrote that this does not appear correct, since the Rama omitted such a distinction even in the Darchei Moshe. Rather, the Rama follows Hagahot Mordechai and does not accept Maharam's dissent, because regarding a deposit the custodian accepted responsibility with precisely this type of risk in mind. See there for the reasoning.
