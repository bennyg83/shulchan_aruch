Rama writes that where the debtor in the three party arrangement is a gentile, if the gentile has already paid the designated Jewish recipient, the money is not removed from the recipient. The Shach says that even if the three party acquisition itself was ineffective, the result is sound because the gentile paid pursuant to the transferor's authorization and therefore it is as though the transferor himself paid. I find this surprising. The Shach elsewhere insists that where an acquisition is legally ineffective, later seizure does not cure it. Moreover, a gentile cannot serve as a halachic agent for a Jew. If a Jew says to a gentile, "Give this object to so and so," and the gentile gives it, the recipient does not acquire merely by the law of agency. A gentile appointed to sell a Jew's chametz likewise cannot complete a halachic sale through ordinary agency. One might say this case is stronger because the transferor also told the recipient to go and receive the debt from the gentile. But the debt is property not presently in the transferor's possession. Telling another Jew, "Go take and acquire it," cannot transfer a debt that cannot otherwise be acquired. An ordinary instruction to pull and acquire works only for property presently subject to transfer. The correct understanding of Terumat HaDeshen is that actual possession can sometimes protect the recipient even where the original mode of acquisition failed. Thus the present rule is a law of seizure, not a law of gentile agency. This is exactly how Darkei Moshe and Gidulei Terumah understood Terumat HaDeshen, and the Shach's attempt elsewhere to deny that reading is difficult. The contrast with a Jewish messenger proves the point. If a Jew tells another Jew to give money to a third person and the messenger does so, ordinary agency makes the transfer effective. If the ultimate recipient is a gentile, a Jewish messenger can still act as the Jewish transferor's agent to deliver the gift. But when the messenger himself is a gentile, ordinary agency is unavailable. Even according to Terumat HaDeshen, only the money already held by the Jewish recipient is protected through possession. The gentile debtor himself is not discharged merely because he followed the instruction. If the gentile's payment did not legally transfer the debt, the original Jewish creditor can still demand payment from the gentile. This is like Terumat HaDeshen's own ruling concerning an improperly assigned debt instrument: if the purchaser seized from the debtor, we may leave the money with the purchaser, but the original creditor can still return and collect from the debtor because the debtor should not have paid without a valid assignment. The sugya of a gentile who borrowed from a Jew and attempted to repay through another Jew supports this directly. Because there is no halachic agency for a gentile, the first Jew's claim against the gentile can remain even though the gentile transferred the money to the second Jew. Thus possession protects the recipient but does not necessarily discharge the gentile debtor.
When the intended recipient is a gentile and the Jewish debtor has not yet retracted, the debtor may give the money to the gentile recipient. The ordinary basis is agency, because a Jew can serve as the Jewish transferor's agent and deliver money to a gentile. There is nevertheless a conceptual difficulty where the subject is itself a debt not presently in the transferor's possession. Nazir teaches that one cannot appoint an agent regarding a transaction that cannot presently be performed, such as appointing someone now to betroth a woman after she later becomes divorced. A debt is likewise outside the creditor's present possession. If so, perhaps the creditor cannot appoint an agent over the body of that future collected debt. This may mean that Rosh intended only that the Jewish debtor is permitted to pay the gentile so long as the original creditor has not retracted, not necessarily that the gentile acquires by a prior agency appointment before payment. Once actual payment occurs, the transaction can be effective because the debtor is himself presently capable of paying the designated recipient. The same question affects the Shach's ruling that if a creditor sold a debt without the required written assignment, but authorized the borrower to pay the purchaser and the borrower later did so, the payment works through agency. Since the debt itself was not presently transferable by the defective sale, appointing an agent over that very debt is doubtful. On the other hand, the borrower himself can presently pay the recipient, and authorization to do that may be enough without treating the debt itself as property transferred through agency. Accordingly, if the creditor tells a third person merely, "Go collect my debt and then give it to so and so," there is greater reason to say the recipient does not acquire merely through the agency appointment. The debt is not presently in the principal's possession and cannot itself be delegated as acquired property. This requires careful distinction from an instruction directed to the debtor himself to pay the designated recipient.
If the gentile took the deposit by force, the Tur says the custodian is not liable to the new recipient because he was only a custodian for the second person just as he had been for the first, and is exempt from unavoidable accident. This implies that if the loss occurred through his negligence, he would be liable as custodian to the second recipient. I do not know the basis for imposing full custodial liability upon him merely through a three party arrangement. The enactment certainly transfers ownership of the existing deposit to the recipient, so the custodian must return it to him. But liability for future negligence is a separate obligation. According to Rosh and the Tur in siman 341, a custodian's property does not become obligated for negligence until the negligence occurs. At the time of the three party transfer, therefore, there is no definite existing debt for negligence that can itself be transferred. Even according to Rambam, who views custodial obligations as rooted from the time of taking possession, the future negligence is uncertain and may never occur. It is no better than a ketubah, which cannot be transferred by three party arrangement because it may never become collectible. Perhaps the Tur speaks where the custodian later agreed to continue as custodian for the recipient. But where the three party arrangement was imposed upon him against his will, although the transfer of the deposit itself can take effect according to the Shulchan Aruch, I see no reason that he should become a new custodian against his will with liability for future negligence. The three party enactment reaches the existing property, not an uncertain future custodial obligation.
