Since he agreed, etc. The Tur says that he caused his own loss by agreeing to remove himself from a Jewish debtor and look instead to a non-Jew for payment.
He cannot say, "I was coerced." The Tur applies this even where the non-Jewish giver is a powerful ruler. The Mechaber carefully says that the Jew expressly responded, "I will do so." Although ordinary ma'amad sheloshtan does not require the designated payer's consent, with a non-Jew the Jew could otherwise say he remained silent only because of fear.
He did not acquire. Even a complete act of zechiyah through a non-Jew does not create acquisition because a non-Jew cannot serve as an agent of acquisition in the ordinary way. Ma'amad sheloshtan is no stronger in that respect.
He may give it to the non-Jew. The Tur says that even if the Jewish creditor privately tells the debtor, "Give what you owe me to the non-Jew," the debtor may do so so long as the creditor has not retracted.
The non-Jew did not acquire. Chazal did not establish the special, reasonless acquisition of ma'amad sheloshtan for a non-Jew to acquire through it.
He gives it to the non-Jew and is exempt. The recipient caused his own loss by instructing that it be given to the non-Jew, knowing that the debtor would have to comply.
The Jew acquires. Since Chazal have authority to uproot the property of a Jew through ma'amad sheloshtan, all the more so can the rule transfer the non-Jew's monetary obligation into the Jewish recipient's right.
Also to the Jewish recipient. The Tur explains that the designated payer has already become obligated to him. If the non-Jew subsequently coerces or robs that payer, the Jewish recipient does not lose his acquired claim merely because of the non-Jew's later act.
He is not obligated to pay. With a deposit, the designated payer becomes toward the second person only the kind of bailee he had been toward the first, and is exempt from unavoidable accidents. Even if the non-Jew coerced him until he brought the specific deposit into the non-Jew's house, he is exempt, because the coercion concerned that very deposited item and is not treated as saving himself with another Jew's property. With a loan the reason does not apply, because a loan was given to be spent and payment comes from the debtor's own money, so coercion is effectively to pay other money. This deposit rule is also stated below in siman 292, while ordinary coercion to hand over another person's money is governed differently in siman 388.
