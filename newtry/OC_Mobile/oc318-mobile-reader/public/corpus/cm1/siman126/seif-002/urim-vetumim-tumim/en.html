Even if the lender forgave the borrower, he can retract. The Sema in seif katan 9 explains that even if Shimon does not retract, Levi nevertheless has the right to retract. The Shach in seif katan 15 writes that the Rosh, Tosafot, and Nimukei Yosef in chapter HaMekabel imply that if Shimon does not retract, Levi likewise cannot retract, because then there is no mistaken waiver. I do not see anything decisive in the Rosh either way. The Rosh's statement that if Shimon does not retract the worker cannot retract was written according to Rav Sheshet, who holds that the worker may not retract. We rule, however, like Rabbah that he may retract. It is therefore possible that according to Rabbah he may retract even if the moneychanger remains willing to pay. The Rosh writes that Rabbah and Rav Sheshet dispute precisely where the moneychanger does not retract but the worker seeks to retract. Rav Sheshet holds that the worker cannot retract because by relying on the moneychanger he effectively waived his claim against the employer for so long as the moneychanger does not retract. Rabbah says he can retract because such reliance is not treated as a waiver. According to the Shach, one would need to interpret this as a dispute only where the worker did not use an explicit language of waiver but merely said, "I rely on the moneychanger." Rav Sheshet treats that as equivalent to waiver, while Rabbah does not. If the worker expressly said, "I waive your liability," Rabbah would agree. This is somewhat strained in the Rosh's wording. According to the Sema the matter reads more naturally. Rabbah holds that there is no effective waiver because the moneychanger himself retains the right to retract. Since the worker's reliance never removed that possibility, even an express waiver made on that basis lacks binding effect and amounts only to words. The Nimukei Yosef provides no proof to the contrary. He speaks of a worker who expressly released the employer in a formulation meaning that whether the moneychanger pays or does not pay, the worker will never return to the employer. The Nimukei Yosef therefore rules that the moneychanger becomes obligated through the law of guaranty. This demonstrates that the case involved an explicit and comprehensive release, as the Rama explains immediately afterward. Thus the sources do not decisively resolve the issue. Nevertheless, despite the somewhat difficult language in the Rosh, the Shach's substantive view appears more reasonable. If the worker expressly says, "I forgive you," why should his waiver be meaningless merely because the moneychanger retains a theoretical right to retract? But where the worker uses only language that implies reliance and not an explicit release, he may certainly retract, because we rule like Rabbah that the worker can retract even while the moneychanger remains willing to pay.
Likewise, if the recipient completely released the giver, etc. The Sema appears, from his words in seif katan 11, to understand that the release is accompanied by a kinyan. The Shach in seifim katanim 15 and 17 writes that the same law applies even without a kinyan, provided the recipient expressly and completely released him. In truth, if he clearly released the original debtor whether the second person ultimately pays or does not pay, the second certainly becomes liable under the law of a guarantor, as explained below in siman 129. The real question is where the recipient issued an unconditional release in general terms, saying simply that he will no longer return to the first debtor. That appears to depend upon how one understands the Rosh. The Rosh writes in chapter HaMekabel concerning the dispute of Rabbah and Rav Sheshet whether the worker may retract: if the moneychanger does not wish to pay, it is obvious that the worker may return to the employer, even if he said in maamad sheloshtan, "I rely on the moneychanger." The moneychanger himself can retract because none of the employer's money is in his hands. The Yerushalmi concerning a shopkeeper's ledger says that where the employer did not place the workers with the shopkeeper, the employer remains liable, but where he did, the employer owes the workers nothing. The Rosh explains that this Yerushalmi follows Rav Sheshet, who holds that the worker cannot retract, and that the shopkeeper becomes obligated to the workers under the law of a guarantor. Since the employer was already obligated to pay the wages immediately, the shopkeeper is considered like a guarantor who undertakes at the very moment the obligation is created. We do not rule like Rav Sheshet. The Rosh continues: are we dealing with fools, that the shopkeeper might refuse to pay whenever he wishes while the workers would release the employer and thereby lose their wages? Even if they waived the employer's liability, it would be a mistaken waiver. Rather, Rabbah and Rav Sheshet dispute only where the shopkeeper remains willing to pay. The Gidulei Terumah and Pilpula Charifta struggle with these words. If the Yerushalmi holds that because the worker cannot retract the shopkeeper likewise cannot retract, having become liable as a guarantor, why should we not say the same in our Gemara? The dispute itself could be that Rabbah holds the worker may retract, and therefore the shopkeeper may also retract because no guaranty became binding, while Rav Sheshet holds the worker cannot retract and therefore the shopkeeper likewise cannot retract because he became a guarantor. Tosafot indeed explain this way. Pilpula Charifta answers that although the Yerushalmi treats the shopkeeper as a guarantor, the Rosh found it strained to read the Bavli this way. In the Bavli the shopkeeper does not become obligated as a guarantor, so the case must be one where the shopkeeper chooses not to retract. This is the essence of his answer. According to that interpretation, even where the worker released the employer, the shopkeeper could still retract. Rav Sheshet holds that the worker himself may not return to the employer, yet according to this reading the shopkeeper did not become obligated through guaranty even though Rav Sheshet treats the worker's reliance as equivalent to a waiver, as the Rosh writes. The shopkeeper can therefore say, "I retract, and you too may retract your waiver, since it was made under a mistaken assumption." The same would seemingly apply even where the worker expressly waived. Although Tosafot do not appear to say so, one would have to say that Tosafot and the Rosh disagree. The party in possession could then plead kim li in accordance with the Rosh. This is the Sema's position, for he follows Pilpula Charifta's explanation. In my view, however, that reading does not fit the Rosh. Why should we assume without proof that the Bavli disagrees with the Yerushalmi? If we are prepared to create such a disagreement, it would be simpler to say that the Yerushalmi holds maamad sheloshtan applies even where Shimon holds none of the transferor's money, whereas our Gemara requires actual funds in his possession, as indicated by the formulation "the maneh of mine in your hand." Moreover, it is uncommon for the Rosh to disagree with Tosafot on a conceptual point without even mentioning their view. I therefore see another explanation. We must first ask what the Rosh hoped to establish by all this analysis of whether the shopkeeper retracts or does not retract. We rule like Rabbah that the worker may retract in any event. What practical difference remains? One cannot answer that perhaps Rabbah and Rav Sheshet dispute only where the shopkeeper retracts, while if he remains willing to pay even Rabbah agrees that the worker cannot return, so the Rosh needed to clarify that the dispute exists even where the shopkeeper does not retract. There is no need for the Rosh's lengthy discussion to establish this. The amoraim disagree without qualification whether he may retract. More importantly, Rabbah cites proof from the Mishnah that once the employer directs the worker to the shopkeeper he does not violate bal talin, yet the worker can still return. That Mishnah necessarily speaks where the worker actually returned and demanded payment from the employer. Otherwise, why would the employer violate bal talin at all? He had directed the worker to payment and the worker had not demanded a second payment. The Nimukei Yosef in the name of a Gaon says this expressly. If the shopkeeper had retracted, the employer would plainly remain obligated and there would be no reason to exempt him. The redirection would have collapsed entirely. The Mishnah must therefore concern a case where the shopkeeper remains willing to pay. Rabbah nevertheless holds that the worker may return to the employer, while the employer does not violate bal talin because the shopkeeper stands ready to pay. It is thus already obvious that Rabbah allows retraction even when the shopkeeper does not. I can understand the Rosh in three ways, and the reader can choose. First, the Rosh regards even the worker's consent to take payment from the shopkeeper as a form of waiver. According to the opinion that waiver requires a kinyan, this consent likewise requires a kinyan. If Rav Sheshet's case were one where the employer was fully released, we could therefore explain the kinyan mentioned by Tosafot as necessary for the waiver itself. There would then be no proof that ordinary waiver requires no kinyan. The dispute could concern what the worker intended by the kinyan: did he release the employer even if the shopkeeper never pays, or only on condition that the shopkeeper pays? This is essentially Tosafot's formulation. It would not be difficult to ask why a kinyan is needed at all, because according to the Rosh even agreeing not to return to the employer is itself a waiver that would require a kinyan. The Rosh, however, seeks to prove that a waiver needs no kinyan. He therefore says that if the shopkeeper refuses to pay, even Rav Sheshet must agree that the worker returns to the employer. The shopkeeper himself had the right to retract. Are we dealing with fools who would release the employer and lose their wages merely because the shopkeeper may choose not to pay? Even if the workers expressly waived, it would be a mistaken waiver. If one says that waiver requires a kinyan and the entire case involved such a kinyan, this still would not help. A mistaken kinyan can likewise be rescinded. Nor can we say that the shopkeeper becomes liable as a guarantor at the time of the underlying obligation, because, following Pilpula Charifta's point in reverse, once the shopkeeper retracts the worker can likewise retract his mistaken waiver, and the guaranty would never have taken effect. Thus even according to Rav Sheshet, if the shopkeeper refuses to pay the worker can return. The Yerushalmi, however, says that once the employer placed the workers with the shopkeeper, the employer owes them nothing, even where the shopkeeper does not pay. We cannot explain the Yerushalmi as simply assuming that the worker's kinyan meant a release even if the shopkeeper would not pay, because if waiver itself required a kinyan, the kinyan would not prove that broader intent. We must therefore say that waiver does not require a kinyan. Then, when an extra kinyan was made, it must signify something additional, namely an unconditional release even if the shopkeeper does not pay. The shopkeeper thereby becomes a guarantor at the time the obligation is created. This fits Rav Sheshet. But according to Rabbah, why can the worker return even when the shopkeeper remains willing to pay? We must say that Rabbah disagrees and holds that ordinary reliance on the shopkeeper does not itself amount to a waiver. Only an explicit release can do so. Rav Sheshet, by contrast, holds that ordinary reliance is enough while the shopkeeper remains willing. Thus the Rosh's entire discussion is intertwined: ordinary reliance is a waiver according to Rav Sheshet, but not according to Rabbah; an explicit release can be effective even according to Rabbah so long as the shopkeeper remains willing; and an unconditional release can create guaranty. A second explanation is possible without focusing on whether waiver requires a kinyan. The Rosh's purpose is to establish that even according to Rabbah, who allows the worker to return while the shopkeeper remains willing to pay, the employer no longer violates bal talin. One might have thought that Rabbah and Rav Sheshet dispute only where the worker gave an explicit release, so that while the shopkeeper remains willing, both would agree the worker cannot return, and their dispute would concern a shopkeeper who refuses to pay. If so, the employer's original wage obligation would truly have been uprooted and reduced to guarantor liability, explaining why bal talin no longer applies. But the Rosh proves that cannot be the dispute. If the shopkeeper refuses to pay, even Rav Sheshet should permit return, because it is a mistaken waiver. Nor can one invoke guaranty, because the shopkeeper can retract and thereby permit the worker to retract as well. Therefore the dispute must also concern ordinary reliance where the shopkeeper remains willing. Rav Sheshet holds such reliance bars return. Rabbah holds it does not. Yet the Mishnah still says the employer does not violate bal talin. Thus even where the worker retains the right to return, the original bal talin prohibition has been displaced by the redirection. On this explanation, when the worker expressly releases the employer, Rav Sheshet can read it as an unconditional release because otherwise the added release language would accomplish nothing beyond his existing rule. Rabbah can read it as conditional upon the shopkeeper paying, because under Rabbah an express release is needed to accomplish even that limited effect. This corresponds closely to the Shach's position above. A third and simpler explanation can be built from the law of guaranty itself. At the end of Bava Batra the Gemara rules that a guarantor who undertakes at the moment the loan is given does not need a kinyan, while one who undertakes afterward does. The formulation "the halachah is" implies that there is a tannaitic dispute. Ben Nanas and Rabbi Yehudah dispute the matter. According to Ben Nanas, even a later guaranty cannot be created by kinyan in certain circumstances, which implies that at the time of the loan he requires a kinyan; according to Rabbi Yehudah, a later guaranty can be created by kinyan, and at the time of the loan no kinyan is needed. We rule like Rabbi Yehudah. Accordingly, the Yerushalmi can say that because the worker cannot return to the employer, the shopkeeper becomes liable as a guarantor at the time the obligation is undertaken, and according to the accepted halachah no kinyan is needed. But in the Bavli dispute of Rav Sheshet and Rabbah we cannot interpret the entire matter as turning on guaranty, because according to the tannaitic view that a guarantor even at the initial stage requires a kinyan, the shopkeeper would not automatically be bound. One cannot say that Rav Sheshet stated his rule only according to the accepted halachah, because Rabbah brings proof from a Mishnah. Perhaps that Mishnah follows Ben Nanas, whom Rabbi Yehudah himself praised, and under his view the worker could return precisely because no guaranty arose. The amoraic dispute must therefore concern a case where the shopkeeper remains willing to pay, so that its logic does not depend on the tannaitic dispute over guaranty. There is yet another nuance in the Rosh's phrase, "since the employer is obligated to pay the workers their wages immediately." Why emphasize immediate payment? If a borrower owes a debt payable in a year and, in return for an express release, a third party undertakes payment, surely this can still be a guaranty undertaken at the time the new obligation is created. No authority makes immediate maturity an essential element. Rather, the Rosh is pointing to bal talin. The difference between guaranty undertaken at the time of the obligation and later guaranty is that in the former, the creditor extended value in reliance on the guarantor. Here, once the employer redirects the workers to the shopkeeper and they rely on the shopkeeper, they refrain from demanding payment from the employer that same day. After the day passes, the employer no longer faces bal talin. If the shopkeeper later retracts, the workers can return to the employer, but he can delay them like an ordinary debtor. The workers therefore materially relied on the shopkeeper by surrendering the immediate protection of bal talin. Because the wages were due at once, this reliance resembles extending credit on the guarantor's faith. That is why the Rosh emphasizes that the employer was obligated to pay immediately. This works where the shopkeeper does not retract that same day. If he retracts while there is still time for the workers to demand payment from the employer before sunset, they have lost nothing and can return immediately. The shopkeeper can say, "What loss did I cause you? Your bal talin right remains intact today." In such a case even the Rosh may agree that no guaranty arose. Rabbah's proof from the Mishnah must therefore concern workers who return to the employer that same day while the shopkeeper remains willing to pay. The Mishnah says the employer still does not violate bal talin. This establishes that the redirection itself displaced that prohibition even though Rabbah allows the worker to return for the money. These are three ways to understand the Rosh. A practical difference emerges. According to the first and third explanations, even an explicit release may not create guarantor liability according to Rabbah unless there is either a kinyan or a legally sufficient guaranty mechanism, so the Sema's requirement of a kinyan has substantial basis. According to the second explanation, because we rule that a guarantor undertaking at the time of the obligation does not require a kinyan, an explicit and comprehensive release may suffice without one, supporting the Shach. The matter therefore requires careful ruling. Maharash Sasson in responsum 103 writes regarding Tosafot's statement, that if the worker did not intend to release the employer completely then what purpose would the kinyan serve, that Tosafot do not mean the kinyan adds substantive force to the release but merely ask why a kinyan was performed. I do not understand his words. The Rosh explicitly says that if the intent was not a complete release, the kinyan is superfluous, and therefore the kinyan demonstrates that a complete release was intended.
