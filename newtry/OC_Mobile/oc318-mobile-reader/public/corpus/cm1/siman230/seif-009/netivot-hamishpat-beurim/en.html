Likewise one who receives barrels of wine. See Sema, seif katan 15. At first glance this law is very difficult. If the wine rises in value at the destination, the profit is divided. If one were to say that when it falls in price before arrival the depreciation is borne entirely by the investor, then the recipient would share in profit but not in loss. Yet they expressly agreed to share loss as well. It is strained to say that the recipient bears half the depreciation only when the wine initially rose in value at the destination, he failed to sell immediately, and it then fell. Daily commercial practice is plainly otherwise: one who receives an iska specifically to transport merchandise to a place shares the loss if the merchandise falls in price there. What basis would there be to give the recipient a stronger position than the investor, such that he shares the appreciation but not the depreciation? Siman 176, seif 40, in the gloss, says explicitly that merchandise is evaluated for appreciation and depreciation according to its market price when it is transported, implying that if the price falls after transport begins the loss is shared. All the more difficult is the view of Rabbeinu Chananel and Ramah cited by Sema, which would apply a similar rule even to an outright buyer, contrary to common commercial law. Tur states expressly that theft loss is shared, but does not explain who bears accidents. Shulchan Aruch omits the accident rule entirely. In my view Rama's rule in siman 176 comes from Mahari Weil cited by Beit Yosef, who says it is learned from the chapter HaMokher Et HaBayit and from this sugya. That indicates he understands the Gemara's phrase "before he brought it there, the price fell" as meaning before the recipient began transporting it to the cheap market. Nimukei Yosef likewise supports this reading. If the price falls after transport has begun, the loss is shared. Similarly, appreciation occurring before transport begins belongs to the investor, while appreciation after transport begins is shared. The reason is that before transport the recipient has not yet begun performing the enterprise and has not yet acquired a profit-bearing relation to the goods. Therefore, concerning depreciation he can say: since appreciation would still have been yours, the depreciation is likewise yours. This is the rule of siman 176. In our siman, however, the text follows Rashbam's interpretation, that the wine fell in price before arrival even though transport had already begun. Rashbam explains that the wine did not become cheaper only in the destination town, but across the entire region. This resolves the questions. If the wine falls only in the destination market, the recipient certainly shares the depreciation, because the entire transaction was expressly made on the understanding that profit and loss at that market would be divided. But if some general event causes wine prices to fall throughout the country, the investor alone bears the loss for a different reason. Tosafot in Ketubot 47 ask why in every purchase of an animal that later dies or becomes treifah we do not say the buyer never intended to purchase under such circumstances. They answer that where the matter depends on both parties, we do not infer such an unstated condition. Mishneh LaMelech questions this because in the case "he wrote for her only on condition that he marry her," both parties also have intent; likewise in a sale made because the seller needed money. He explains that where the inferred condition causes no real loss to the other party, we may apply the umdena. A widow whose husband dies simply returns to being unmarried; the husband has no posthumous loss from the condition. In a sale made only because the seller needed cash, if that need disappears, the buyer gets his money back and the seller gets his property back. By contrast, if a purchased cow later dies and the buyer recovers the price while the seller receives only a carcass or nothing, the inferred condition would impose a real loss on the seller. We therefore do not assume he agreed. This is analogous to Tosafot in Bava Kamma 110 concerning a woman who falls before a levir afflicted with boils. The husband's death does not create a conflicting interest for him after death. Even this explanation requires qualification. If one buys a house and later the king decrees that this buyer may no longer live in the city, why not say the buyer never intended the purchase under such circumstances, since the seller could simply take the house back and return the money? The answer is that Tosafot do not mean that every later catastrophe undoes a purchase before the buyer has actually used the item. Once the buyer has already received the very practical benefit for which he purchased, there is no such umdena. In marriage, Tosafot themselves distinguish that before full marriage there may be such a presumption, but after marriage there is not. The same applies to a cow or house. Once it became available for the intended use, even if the buyer had not yet actually used it, the acquisition benefit has already vested, like a bride entering chuppah and becoming fit for relations. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 79 similarly write regarding a specific ship that once payment was given the parties resolved that the money should belong immediately. Therefore Tosafot's original question concerns only a case where the cow died or became treifah immediately, before it became available for the purpose for which it was bought, such as where the parties had stipulated that it would remain in the seller's possession for a day or two before use. That resembles a betrothal that never reached marriage. Only there is their distinction needed: since the inferred condition would cause loss to the seller, we do not apply it. By contrast, if a house was purchased but was not yet available for use and, before it became available, a general event occurred that would clearly have prevented the buyer from purchasing had he known, the transaction can be void where returning the house and price leaves the seller without loss. This explains our case. A general event caused wine prices to fall throughout the whole country. This resembles a widespread catastrophe. The recipient plainly did not accept the iska on the understanding of bearing that kind of general market collapse. The investor suffers no special loss from recognizing the umdena, because he simply receives the same wine back and it would have depreciated even had it remained in his own house. Moreover, the event occurred before the recipient had become able to derive the intended commercial benefit from the iska. He was forbidden to sell the wine before reaching the destination. Therefore the umdena applies and the general depreciation falls on the investor alone. This also explains why in an outright sale the result differs. An outright buyer could ordinarily resell the wine immediately. From the moment of purchase the property was fully available for commercial benefit. Therefore the later regional fall in price does not undo his acquisition. But even in an iska, if the price falls only in the destination market, the recipient shares the loss because that is exactly the commercial risk the parties expressly agreed to divide. Even if one could formulate an umdena, the express condition controls.
