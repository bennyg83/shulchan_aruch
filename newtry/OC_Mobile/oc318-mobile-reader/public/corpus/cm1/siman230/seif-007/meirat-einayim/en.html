And it turned sour within three days. The Mechaber abbreviated Rambam's language. The Tur states more fully: if it turns sour within three days, the loss is the seller's, and even if the seller already received the money he must return it. Since it was found to be vinegar within three days, it certainly had already begun to turn sour earlier. Although wine that smells like vinegar but tastes like wine is legally wine, a person does not pay the ordinary price for wine that has already begun the process that will spoil it. Even if the purchaser tasted it and both smell and taste seemed like wine, we say he did not taste it properly. If it turns sour after three days, the loss is the purchaser's, and even if he has not yet paid he must pay. Since he tasted it and it was good, the presumption is that the spoilage arose later. If he did not taste it, however, the burden of proof lies upon the claimant because the matter is doubtful. All this applies where he left it in the seller's vessels. In the Derishah I explained why this differs from an animal found with a perforation in the reticulum after three days, where at the end of siman 224 and in siman 232 the Tur and Mechaber place the loss upon the purchaser even if it is doubtful whether the defect arose with seller or purchaser, and even require an unpaid buyer to pay. Beer is naturally destined to sour if left standing, so we cannot place it under a strong presumption of remaining sound. An animal has a presumption of bodily health. At the end of this siman Rama repeats the present law concerning a purchased barrel of beer without adding anything new. Perhaps he did not notice that the Mechaber had already stated it, because he copied it from the Tur where it appears again. We find many such repetitions. It is nevertheless surprising that Rama does not reproduce all the Tur's details. This requires study.
