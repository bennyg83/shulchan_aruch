Seif 9. Before it reached there the price fell, see the Tur. The Tur in siman 230 cites Rabbeinu Chananel and the Ramah that where no money had yet been paid and the purchaser disclosed that he was buying wine in order to take it to a place where it was dearer, he may withdraw when that purpose fails. One should not automatically extend that ruling to a case where transportation costs later rose because the roads deteriorated, or where transport later became impossible. We should not multiply disputes unnecessarily, and perhaps everyone agrees that because disclosed intent ordinarily has no effect in movables, as in siman 207, the buyer's statement that he was purchasing specifically to transport the goods does not itself create a condition. According to Shaarei Ephraim, however, a possessor may say kim li like the Ramah's view in the Tur that disclosed intent can be effective even for movables. On that approach, perhaps the onaah calculation might follow the market at the destination to which the buyer expressly said he was taking the goods, because the purchase was made for that place. Possibly, if the disclosed intent came from the seller, only the seller may invoke it, and if it came from the buyer, only the buyer may do so. Yet reason suggests that disclosed intent should not determine onaah at all. Every merchant who buys for resale intends to profit, and everyone knows he does not buy in order to lose money. If such intent controlled onaah, even a tiny loss to a reseller who stated his purpose should void the sale, and no authority has ruled so. Perhaps disclosed intent works only when a person sells something not ordinarily intended for sale, or where it concerns the essential character and grade of the object. Profit and loss, however, are commercial risks every buyer and seller accepts. Therefore, for onaah, all may agree that valuation follows only the actual place and time of the transaction, and a stated intention to transport elsewhere does not alter it. This still requires careful study.
