The witnesses to the note testify that they saw the loan, or other witnesses do so, etc. At first sight it is difficult why, regarding testimony that the loan was actually seen, the Mechaber specifically mentions the witnesses who signed the note. He could simply have written that witnesses testify either that they saw the loan or that they saw the witnesses sign. It therefore appears as follows. The Shach, seif katan 3, disagrees with the Sema and maintains that these forms of proof help only where the alleged debtor claims that he never borrowed, and he therefore gives the passage a strained interpretation. The primary view appears to be that of the Sema. As for the Shach's objection that there nevertheless remains concern that the debt was repaid or that the document was forged, the answer is that we suspect forgery only where the very existence of the loan remains doubtful. Where there is already an established presumption of a loan or of a valid document, we do not destroy that established status merely because of such a concern. We combine the circumstances in the manner stated by the Sema. There is clear proof from Ketubot 36, where the Gemara compares the rule here to the case of a woman whose bad reputation caused her to lose the statutory fine, namely, where witnesses testify, "She solicited us for forbidden relations," and on the strength of that testimony we treat her as sexually disreputable. Yet there the Gemara employs this testimony only to exempt from the fine for rape or seduction. It does not say that such testimony causes her to lose her ketubah. Granted, if the witnesses say that she solicited them before marriage, there is no basis to treat her as already non-virgin so as to reduce her ketubah from two hundred, since the husband did not claim at first intercourse that he found an open entrance, and if he did make such a claim he may already be believed under the rule in Ketubot 9. But if witnesses testify that she solicited them after marriage, why do we not treat her as an adulteress and cause her to forfeit the ketubah? No authority records such a rule. Evidently, this type of suspicious evidence can prevent creation of a new monetary obligation, such as the statutory fine, but where an obligation is already established, such as a ketubah, we do not impair the document on that basis merely because an evil report arose after marriage. The same applies in this siman, for the Gemara derives the two laws from one another. Where no debt was ever established, the suspicious circumstances can impair the note. But where witnesses establish the status of the note, such as by testifying that they themselves were appointed as witnesses to write the note, we do not impair that established status. This explains why testimony by unrelated witnesses that they saw the loan itself is not enough for all purposes. Such testimony does not establish that a written instrument ever existed for purposes of purchasers or for depriving the debtor of a claim that he paid. The Mechaber therefore speaks specifically of the witnesses to the note. They testify that they were appointed to write the note and remember that the debtor instructed them to write and deliver a note to the creditor, although they do not remember whether they actually completed it. We then rely upon the presumption that agents fulfill their mission and conclude that they made the note. Once the status of a note is thereby established, we no longer impair it because of the ordinary suspicion. See Shach, seif katan 3, where there is a scribal error. The text should read that the time for payment has not yet arrived, and in such a case we do not leave the note outstanding in this manner. The same follows according to the Rambam and the Mechaber. See also Sema, seif katan 6. When he says specifically that the witnesses saw that "this note" was signed, his intention is a note of this kind, as he himself explains in seif katan 1. This is clear from seif katan 7, where the Sema says that the Mechaber states three progressively stronger cases. If "this very note" meant that the witnesses possessed a distinctive identifying mark for the exact document, the middle case would be stronger than all the others. Rather, the meaning is as explained above. Nor need we fear that the physical document itself was forged, because such a forgery would be discernible to beit din. We do not construct the law upon the possibility that beit din itself will err.
