Seif 1. To forge a document for him. In siman 63, where a person was shown to have wished to forge a document, if one witness says that a document he now produces is visibly forged, there is room to say that he cannot collect on it even if two other witnesses appear on it and their signatures have been authenticated. Still, to invalidate an authenticated document merely because the holder once intended to forge something, two witnesses should be required regarding that misconduct. Otherwise the signature may have been placed in an ordinary context. Even if the supposed signer says, "this is not my signature," he is only one witness. If both named witnesses say the signatures are not theirs, perhaps their statements do not combine like an admission together with a loan, because they are coming to weaken the holder's established presumption of fitness. It may be necessary to have two witnesses concerning each signature. Moreover, unless it was proved that the holder himself attempted the forgery, he might say that he found the document or received it from someone else. If he claims that the borrower gave it to him and that the witnesses signed in his presence, the rule that a person does not make himself wicked may protect the legal characterization of his statement. Where the document has been authenticated, his migo is not considered a bare migo to extract money, because the document itself provides independent support in the normal manner of documents. Where the same instrument bears both witness signatures and the defendant's own signature, and one witness signature is proved forged, perhaps the defendant's signature is also undermined because all form one document. This may be stronger than the case of one who intended to forge an unspecified document, which the Sema says casts suspicion upon all his documents. On the other hand, one could argue that the second valid witness signature is no worse than another document. Yet if an attempt to forge a generic document weakens all of a person's documents, all the more should a proven forgery within this very instrument cast doubt upon its remaining signatures, including the defendant's. Even without proof of forgery, a claimant relying on an unauthenticated document can be met with a plea of payment through the migo that the document is forged. When collecting from heirs, beit din advances that plea for them, and the claimant may not collect until authentication. Those who authenticate by recognizing handwriting must therefore be especially careful when one of the signatures shows an unexplained discrepancy.
