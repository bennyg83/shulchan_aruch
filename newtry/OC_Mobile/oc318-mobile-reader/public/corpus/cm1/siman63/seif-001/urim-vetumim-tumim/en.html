Even though it has been authenticated, and so forth. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 17, part 1, writes: "The matter requires clarification," and then cites R. Moshe that even though the document has been authenticated, one never collects with it. Gidulei Terumah asks: is this not an explicit Gemara? And if Rambam's novelty is only the phrase, "even though it was authenticated," that too seems obvious, because an unauthenticated document cannot be collected in any event. In my opinion, the Gemara can indeed be discussing a document that has not yet been authenticated. One might object that then the same is true of every ordinary document. But the difference is that with an ordinary document there are witnesses available to authenticate it, and beit din will involve itself in validating it. With this defective document, however, beit din does not even undertake authentication. Why should the court expend effort validating an already impaired document? Ramban makes a similar point concerning the rule following Rabbah in Perek Chezkas. Thus Rambam teaches a substantial novelty: even if the document later is authenticated, the defect remains and it still cannot be collected. There is another explanation. Rambam comes to exclude Rabbenu Tam's view. Responsa Maharam, Prague edition, siman 125, cites Rabbenu Tam in the name of Rabbenu Chananel, Perek Shevuat HaDayanim, that whenever the Gemara says generally, "the document has been weakened," it means only that the holder of the note must first swear before collecting. This is implied by the language in Perek Eilu Na'arot, "We do not collect with this weakened document, nor do we tear it." Thus, according to Rabbenu Tam and Rabbenu Chananel, once the document is authenticated it can still be collected upon an oath. Rambam disagrees. He holds that even after authentication it is not collected at all, ever. Accordingly, Baal HaTerumot's citation of Rambam is not redundant. It comes to establish a real dispute and to exclude the more lenient interpretation of Rabbenu Tam and Rabbenu Chananel.
It always appears that this comes to teach us that even if the lender seized, we remove it from him. Nevertheless, we do not tear the document, since perhaps he will later clarify and prove the loan. So writes Sema. But he should not have stated the rule so categorically. Rosh writes in Shevuot, in the sugya of a creditor who says that the payment was applied to another debt, that when we say the document has been weakened, the meaning is that we do not collect with it, but we also do not tear it, like the weakened documents discussed in Perek Eilu Na'arot, where the Gemara says, "We do not collect with it and we do not tear it." Rosh therefore treats the weakened document in the set-off case and the weakened documents discussed here as governed by the same rule, and one is learned from the other. I also cited Rabbenu Tam above to this effect. Accordingly, since Rosh rules in the set-off case that if the creditor already seized before witnesses we do not remove the money from him, as I explained above in siman 58, the same should apply here. Therefore Sema's words are too absolute.
And if the witnesses to the document testify, and so forth. This is Rambam's view. The dissenting opinion brought by Rama is that of Ran. The later authorities struggled to explain the dispute. The matter appears as follows. If the witnesses to the document testify that the signatures are theirs and they also remember the loan itself, everyone agrees the document is valid. Even Ran concedes this, because we do not go so far as to suspect a forgery that deceived the very witnesses who remember the underlying transaction. This is unlike Shach, who writes that even then the document is invalid. That is not correct, as I explained in Urim. If the witnesses say explicitly, "These are our signatures, but we do not remember anything about the loan itself," then everyone, even Rambam, agrees the document is invalid. It is true that when the witnesses' handwriting is independently authenticated, Rambam ordinarily does not require the witnesses to remember the testimony, as I explained above in siman 46. But I also established there that in strict law, even where the handwriting is independently known, the witnesses really must remember in order to avoid the problem of testimony from writing. We simply presume that they do remember and do not believe them when they attempt to weaken a valid document by saying otherwise. Here, however, they have a migo. So long as they themselves do not testify to their signatures, the document remains unauthenticated, and ordinary outside authentication is not presently available. Therefore they are believed to say they do not remember, and the document falls. Shach also writes this. The actual dispute is where the witnesses simply testify, without qualification, "These are our signatures." Rambam follows the principle I explained in siman 46: they need not say expressly, "We remember." So long as they do not say, "We do not remember," their identification of the signatures places them under the presumption that they remember the substantive testimony. Here too, once they identify the handwriting without disclaimer, we treat them as remembering the loan itself. Since they both recognize their signatures and are presumed to remember the event, there is no further concern that the document was forged so skillfully as to mislead them. Ran disagrees because, in an ordinary document, once the witnesses identify their signatures, the document itself is thereby established as genuine. We then have reason to presume they also remember the underlying testimony, since witnesses ordinarily keep important testimony in mind. Here, however, even after they identify the handwriting we still have not fully established the document as genuine, because perhaps the forger imitated their signatures so perfectly that even they were deceived. If the document's authenticity itself remains uncertain, there is no basis to infer that they certainly remember the loan. Therefore, according to Ran, they must say explicitly that they remember the underlying transaction. That is the dissenting opinion brought by Rama. But if they explicitly say they remember, the document is valid according to everyone.
