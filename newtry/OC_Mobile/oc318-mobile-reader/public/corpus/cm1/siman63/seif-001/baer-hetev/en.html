This. The Sema writes that the word "this" is not literal. It means a document of this type: the claimant asked the witnesses to fabricate a document stating that Reuven owed him one maneh, and the document he now produces likewise states that same amount. We therefore say it is certainly the forged document. The Shach cites the Bach that if the claimant possesses two documents against the same person for that same amount, he may collect on one of them, and from purchasers he collects using the later-dated one. This differs from seif 2 in the gloss, where he said only, "Forge one document for me," without specifying an amount. There, because he named no sum, his request naturally could include a document for a large amount, so all documents fitting the description are suspect. Here he specified a particular sum. Shach.
Forever. Even if unrelated persons later testify that they recognize the signatures of those whose names appear on the document, we still say that he labored until he successfully forged them. Even if he subsequently seized property from the borrower, it is removed from him. Nevertheless, the document itself is not torn up, because perhaps he will eventually prove that it is genuine. The Shach writes that this applies specifically where no one saw the document in his possession before the period when he was seeking a forgery. If it was already seen in his possession before then, the document is not automatically invalidated. The Bach writes that this requires either that the document was authenticated when previously seen or that the witnesses had a distinctive identifying feature by which they can establish it is the same document. This helps against a claim that the underlying transaction never occurred. The debtor may still claim, however, "I paid, and this is a different forged document," if the earlier witnesses merely recognized the signatures. If the original document had already been formally authenticated or had a distinctive identifying mark, that proof also defeats a claim of payment. Shach.
The loan. The Sema explains that even if the witnesses do not know that the particular loan was made with a written document and it may have been only an oral loan, and even if the signatories themselves do not now see the document to identify their signatures, if other persons recognize the signatures their testimony can combine and collection may be made. The Shach disagrees and holds that recognition of the signatures is ineffective in every such circumstance. Even if the underlying loan is known to be genuine, the debtor can still claim payment. Their signatures. Darkei Moshe writes that according to Rambam, who holds that signatories coming to authenticate their own signatures must also remember the underlying loan, as explained at the end of siman 46, if they said, "These are our signatures," and then left, we ordinarily presume that they also remembered the loan. Sema.
They disagree. The Sema writes that no one disputes the first two cases, namely where witnesses actually saw the loan or where others testify that they saw the signatories sign. Likewise, if the signatories themselves testify that these are their signatures and remember signing this very document, there is no reason that should be worse than testimony of others who saw them sign. The Shach, however, follows the Ran, who disagrees. Even the signatories themselves may be mistaken. Because the claimant was known to be seeking a forgery, perhaps he forged the signatures so skillfully that the witnesses themselves think the signatures are genuine. Even if they also remember the loan, the debtor may still claim that he paid. See there.
