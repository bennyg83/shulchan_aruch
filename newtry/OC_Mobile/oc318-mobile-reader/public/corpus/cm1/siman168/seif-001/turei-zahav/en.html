(Seif 1: The value of the olive trees as they originally stood, etc.) The Sema was troubled by the repetition of this law and inferred that where the trees were uprooted together with their clods of earth, the owner of the field need not immediately pay their value. This is exceedingly difficult. How can the landowner compel his fellow to leave his olive trees in use when the owner protests that he does not wish to receive merely half the fruit? Moreover, the Tur postpones the law requiring payment for the olive trees not uprooted with their clods until after setting forth the laws of the fruit. He then states generally that the owner must pay the value of the trees as young plantings are sold, whether within the first three years or afterward. This implies no distinction between trees uprooted with their clods and those uprooted without them. In my view, this is precisely why the Mechaber repeats the law of payment for the olive trees: to teach that there is no distinction between the first three years and the later period. He incidentally repeats the Tur’s phrase, “as they stand for sale.” Earlier, immediately after stating that after three years all the fruit belongs to the owner of the field, he needed to cite Nimukei Yosef’s qualification: “All belongs to the owner of the field” does not include the value of the trees. It means only that the field owner need pay no more than the value they had at the time they were swept onto his land. It is therefore clear and simple that even during the first three years, the landowner must pay the value of the olive trees whenever their owner demands it. The usual course is merely that a person prefers to take half the fruit for as long as he is entitled to it, and afterward collect the value of the trees.
