Three. The reason is that during each of the first three years the owner of the tree can say to the owner of the land: Were it not for my tree together with the clod of earth around it, you would have no fruit, because even had you planted new olive trees their fruit would have been orlah. Through my clod of earth the orlah restriction is removed, since the tree can survive by means of it. The Maggid Mishneh writes likewise and concludes that although the clods can keep the trees alive and thereby save them from orlah, they cannot produce fruit by virtue of the clods alone. After three years, however, the landowner can say: Were it not for your tree, I would have planted new trees and their fruit would now be permitted. The first owner cannot answer, 'Just as you benefited from my tree during the first three years by receiving half its fruit, I should continue to benefit with you afterward,' because the landowner can answer: Had I planted new olive trees, although I would not have eaten their fruit during the first three years, the trees would still have been thin and cast little shade, so I could have sown crops beneath them and eaten those crops. Your tree, however, has thick branches and casts much shade, so I cannot sow beneath it. All this is stated in the Gemara. Sema.
Immediately. The Sema writes that although the fruit during the first three years is forbidden for benefit and the value of the olive trees must be paid to the owner of the trees, the phrase 'everything' means every increase in the tree, whether during the first three years if the trunk thickened, or the fruit after three years, belongs to the landowner. The Ran likewise writes that if the tree thickened or added branches during the first three years, that increase belongs to the landowner. The Taz rejects this explanation and explains the matter differently, identifying a basis for permitting the fruit with respect to orlah. See there.
