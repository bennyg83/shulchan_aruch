Because of settlement of the land. In the Perishah I explained that Chazal assessed that the original owner of the olive trees will certainly plant replacements in his own field, just as he planted these, whereas the landowner into whose field they were washed had not previously planted olives there. If the original owner removes them, the second person may again leave the field without olives. Nevertheless, if after the trees arrive the landowner says, 'I do not want them,' he cannot be compelled to retain them and pay for them. The Tur and Mechaber say this shortly afterward.
Rather, they remain in place. This is the minimum rule. The amount payable to the tree owner depends on how they were uprooted. If they came with clods of their original earth, during the first three years the landowner need not immediately pay the full value of the trees; instead the parties divide the fruit. After three years, when the tree owner has no further share in the fruit, the landowner pays the value of the trees as they were worth when first washed away, not their later added thickness, because that increase came from the new land. If they were uprooted without clods, the original owner gets no fruit even during the first three years. In Eretz Yisrael, where he may not remove the trees because of settlement of the land, the landowner must therefore immediately pay their market value. This explains the apparent repetition in the Mechaber and the change in wording between paying their value when first washed away and paying what they are worth for sale. The Mechaber began with Rambam, who does not mention a distinction outside Eretz Yisrael, and then adds the Tur's distinction between Eretz Yisrael and outside it. The Tur also makes clear that even when uprooted without clods, the landowner must pay for the trees. This excludes Maggid Mishneh's implication that if uprooted without clods the original owner may receive nothing because the water carried them far and he despaired of them. Kesef Mishneh challenges that reading.
For all three years, etc. During those years the tree owner can say: without my trees together with their original clods, you would have no permitted fruit, because newly planted olive trees would be orlah. My clods preserved their status because they can survive through them. Maggid Mishneh explains that although the clods can keep the trees alive and remove the orlah issue, they alone cannot produce this fruit without the new soil. After three years the landowner can say: without your trees I could have planted new ones and their fruit would now be permitted. The original owner cannot demand continuing half-benefit after three years in exchange for the benefit provided during the first three, because the new landowner can respond that if he had planted young trees, although he would have had no tree fruit for three years, their thin branches would have cast little shade and he could have planted crops beneath them. These mature trees cast heavy shade and prevented such crops. The Gemara explains this.
Everything belongs to the landowner. Although fruit during the first three years would be orlah and prohibited in benefit, the landowner must still pay the initial value of the trees as the Mechaber concludes. 'Everything' means all increase attributable to the new land: thickening of the trunk during the three years, which is comparable to fruit, and the actual fruit afterward. Ran writes similarly that if the trunk thickens or new branches grow during the first three years, that increase belongs to the landowner.
We listen to him. Meaning, even in Eretz Yisrael. Even there not every person is compelled to plant olives in his own field against his wishes.
