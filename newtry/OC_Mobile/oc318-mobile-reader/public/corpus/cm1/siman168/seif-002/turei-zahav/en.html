(See there: Everything belongs to the owner of the land.) This requires explanation. The Sema, following the Maggid Mishneh, understands the phrase to refer to the increase in value of the tree. This is very difficult, for even when the trees were uprooted with their clods of earth, the increase belongs to the owner of the field. Nimukei Yosef explains that the field owner can say, “Had I planted trees myself, the increase would have been mine,” and this is true even within the first three years. For this reason, the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber carefully say at the beginning that the parties divide the fruit, implying that they do not divide the increase in value of the tree. It appears to me that the matter may be explained through a permitted method even regarding the prohibition of orlah. The landowner may say to a non-Jew: “Cultivate my tree and take its fruit, and give me your tree whose orlah years have already passed so that I may take its fruit,” as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 294, seif 14. Alternatively, he may sell the tree to a non-Jew for three years. One might ask why, during the first three years, the field owner does not then receive all the fruit, since he no longer needs the clods belonging to the owner of the trees. The answer is that if he used such an arrangement, he would ordinarily have to reduce the price. Here he profits from the clods, and that benefit must be shared. Indeed, one must say this, for otherwise why should the owner of the trees receive half the fruit during the first three years? The field owner could sell or exchange the trees with a non-Jew. When the Gemara asks why the landowner may eat half the fruit despite orlah and answers that the case concerns trees whose clods are intact, it could also have answered through sale or exchange. It chose another valid answer. Therefore, where the trees were uprooted with their clods, which give the landowner a benefit he would not have obtained through exchange with a non-Jew, the Sages established a fixed rule that the two owners divide the fruit, because no precise alternative valuation is practicable. But where the trees were uprooted without their clods, the landowner must use the arrangement of exchange with a non-Jew. In that case he needs no contribution from the tree owner, and the tree owner has no share in the fruit that comes to the landowner during the first three years through that exchange. This explanation is correct and fitting in the words of the authorities. The explanation of Kaftor VaFerach differs, but apart from this approach there is scarcely one capable of resolving all the difficulties.
