All householders should be assembled. See Noda BiYehudah, siman 20. Municipal trustees have no superior authority merely by virtue of their office to impose a new tax arrangement that benefits some and burdens others. According to Maharam, Maharik, and Terumat HaDeshen, cited by Rama in siman 2, communal leaders may compel only in accordance with an established custom, not simply innovate a rule involving loss to one party and gain to another. Even according to Rama's practical ruling that local custom governs where the custom empowers the trustees, that custom must be fixed and known, and the trustees bear the burden of proving it. Moreover, in matters in which the trustees themselves are financially interested, they cannot act as judges in their own cause. Thus the gloss at the beginning of siman 163 requires that when taxpayers cannot agree on communal needs, all tax-paying householders be seated together. Accordingly, where a tax arrangement was adopted at a meeting from which one taxpayer was excluded, it does not help to say that even had he attended his view would have been outvoted. Majority rule presupposes that all were summoned into the deliberation and their arguments heard. A measure adopted without him must be reconsidered at a proper assembly, and he cannot be fined or placed under ban for refusing to obey the defective decision. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 61, similarly writes that even communal officers cannot act merely by their own majority in a matter affecting all. The accepted practice of following the majority at communal meetings means the majority of those properly assembled together and deliberating, not a group acting without the other entitled members. Noda BiYehudah further notes that if communal custom truly gives the trustees broad power, they still cannot use that power in a matter in which they are personally interested. Knesset HaGedolah states that majority rule is effective where all participants stand on equal footing, but if all combine to diminish one person's right they are all interested parties. Chatam Sofer, siman 116, discusses a new governmental levy. The community repeatedly summoned all householders to deliberate, but only about thirty came. Those present selected nine representatives, three wealthy, three middle-level, and three of lesser means, together with the existing trustees. The nine proposed a tax on a particular trade. One representative and one trustee objected because the burden affected them personally. Chatam Sofer initially notes the authorities requiring unanimous original authorization for communal coercion and the rule that when a specified larger panel is chosen all its votes may be required. He nevertheless rules for the community because public proclamations had summoned everyone; one who chose not to attend effectively entrusted his power to those who did attend. Moreover, no one had protested the selection of the nine representatives, even the later objector. Those nine were therefore chosen by the community to supervise the matter. Their division into three economic groups was intended to provide balanced representation, not to create a panel whose every member had to agree. The accepted custom in those regions was also to follow the majority in such communal matters, because otherwise public business could never be completed. Yet because communal regulations of this sort traditionally also carried the approval of the local rav, Chatam Sofer declined to endorse the particular burden until the community entered into some compromise with the affected individuals, in fulfillment of what is right and good. In siman 117 he discusses a community that had long funded taxes through a levy on each pound of meat, then for five years changed to individual assessments. A majority now wished to restore the meat levy, while the others objected that it disproportionately burdened poorer consumers and would also compel residents to purchase only locally certified meat. Chatam Sofer ruled for the objectors. A tax that according to law should be assessed by wealth cannot be shifted to consumption in a way that burdens some individuals unless all originally agreed or there is a valid established communal power to do so. The prior meat levy had been valid because everyone had then agreed, but after returning to the ordinary law one individual could again object. If all formal members of the congregation were to agree and only outside residents objected, the formal congregation might be treated as a distinct partnership or guild. They could not necessarily prohibit outsiders from importing meat, but they could condition sales from the communal butchers on the local ticket system. Because the outsiders are nevertheless city residents for many legal purposes, such a regulation would require the approval of the rav. Chatam Sofer also notes that if a valid communal prohibition against outside meat were adopted, meat brought in contrary to the prohibition would be forbidden to members of the congregation, together with its absorbed taste in utensils, while utensils used elsewhere for permitted meat would not become forbidden merely because they were later brought into town.
