(Seif 3, in the gloss.) The words, “If they assessed how much tax each person must pay and began collecting,” appear at first glance to require two things: the fixing of each person’s assessment and the beginning of actual collection from some members of the community. The Sema understood it this way. This is puzzling, however, for what legal effect does the beginning of collection have? Moreover, in Responsa Rashba, siman 807, the Rashba first uses similar language: “From the time the trustees fixed that tax upon the community and began collecting it, Reuven became obligated to pay everything that fell to his share in that tax, and it became a debt upon him like any other debt.” Yet later in the same responsum he writes that they are regarded as creditors “from the time the assessment was imposed upon the taxpayers,” which shows that the assessment alone is decisive, not the beginning of collection. The same is clear in siman 777: “From the time they entered it as a fixed obligation, even if he was wealthy and then became poor, he must pay even from the cloak upon his shoulders.” Accordingly, the assessment itself suffices, for from that time the debt rests upon him. This itself is called “collection,” because they have undertaken the collection process. The phrase excludes only a case in which a general obligation was imposed upon the community to pay a specified sum at a later time, but the amount payable by each person was not yet fixed. Since the time and manner of each person’s payment had not yet taken effect, liability then depends upon the assessment made at the later time. Thus, “they began collecting” here means that they established an assessment for immediate collection in accordance with that levy. The same applies under our custom, where each year or every two years an assessment is made of how much each member of the community will contribute toward all communal needs during that period. Each person becomes obligated from the time the assessment is made, even for sums collected only at the end of the year or the two-year period, because the community accepted the assessment from that time. Likewise, when the Rama writes that if one was poor and became wealthy we follow the time of collection, he means the time when the collection was established, namely the making of the assessment. When he later discusses one who came to the city between the time the community became liable for a tax and the time of collection, the meaning is that the community had already incurred an earlier debt before he arrived, and only after his arrival did they establish the collection by assessing each person’s share. We do not say that because he was present when the assessment was made, the old debt falls upon him. The community waived that claim because he had no connection to the original obligation. This is explicit in Rivash, siman 477, which rules that the community has no authority to impose upon a newcomer a share of prior debts. The Rama concludes, however, that some say that if the tax benefits those who arrive later, they must contribute even though the debt already existed. For example, if the community is obligated at the beginning of each year to pay a fixed sum for the entire year, and a person arrives after the year began, he must contribute even though he was not present when the original assessment was made. This is explained in Responsa Rosh, rule 4, siman 12. The case concerned a tax imposed by the ruler upon the community. The community borrowed the money at interest until a fixed date, and at the end of that period divided the expense among the members. Some people in the city had been exempt when the ruler imposed the tax, either because they were not then in the city or because they were poor and later became wealthy. They argued that had the community not borrowed at interest, the tax would have been paid immediately, when no liability rested upon them. The Rosh answered that if the tax was the regular annual assessment, the community’s payment secured exemption until the specified date, and therefore benefited anyone who came to reside there during that period. Such a person must pay the proportionate share that accrued to him. If he arrived or became wealthy halfway through the period, he pays half, each according to the duration of his benefit. But if it was a one-time extraordinary tax and not the regular annual assessment, those who came to the city or became wealthy only after the community agreed to pay it are exempt, even if the community borrowed the money at interest and repaid the loan after their arrival. Thus, liability does not depend merely on whether one was present when the assessment was made. It depends upon the period during which one benefited from the payment. The “new tax” mentioned by the Rosh means a one-time levy. If the ruler institutes a new recurring annual charge from that point onward, its law is like that of the regular annual tax described first. The Sema understood the Rama’s phrase “at the time of collection” to refer to the actual gathering of money from the community after the assessment had been fixed. He therefore required both an assessment and an actual collection, and was further compelled to explain that when a poor person becomes wealthy, liability always follows the time when the money is physically collected. On that view, a new assessment would have to be made each time money is collected during the year, which is neither heard of nor reasonable. Rather, it is clear that “collection” here means the establishment of the collection, namely the assessment itself. That alone suffices. From that point he is obligated for the entire period to pay whatever is collected according to that assessment, until a new assessment is made.
