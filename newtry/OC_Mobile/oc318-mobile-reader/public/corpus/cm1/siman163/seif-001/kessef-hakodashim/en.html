Seif 3, Rama. (According to an assessment.) Assessors were selected by lot to determine the communal levy owed to the government. Several distinguished members of the community challenged them before the secular judge, who then directed that I formulate an oath by which the assessors would swear not to distort the assessment out of fear or favoritism. After I was unable to reconcile the parties, I was compelled to draft a formula. I deliberately left blank the actual wording of an oath, both because I did not know whether the three assessors would swear together or separately and because I personally avoid, as a matter of pious caution, drafting an oath in the first person whenever I can properly avoid doing so. They went to the synagogue and the text was read before several distinguished members. The reader was the communal leader rather than the cantor who ordinarily reads such oaths. The cantor was present and later told me that no language of an oath had actually been pronounced; only the words that I had written were read. The distinguished members did not notice this. I rejoiced that the assessors had thereby been spared an oath, though at first I was troubled that this might amount to misleading those present, since they assumed that a full oath had been administered, and perhaps I bore some responsibility because I had omitted the words of oath from the text. After reflection, however, I concluded that according to Torah law there was no basis to impose an oath upon the assessors at all. Where each taxpayer states his own financial capacity there can be an oath akin to the oath of partners, as in siman 163. Assessors, by contrast, act in the capacity of judges. Once they were properly selected, especially by lot, they should not be placed under suspicion. The secular court imposed the oath, but we are not responsible to create such an obligation. Their providential escape from the oath, together with the failure of the distinguished members themselves to notice what was read, creates no valid complaint against those who knew. I acted innocently and no deceit was intended. Nevertheless, I admonished the assessor who best understood the matter that assessment is truly comparable to adjudication and must be performed with complete integrity, as though an oath had been taken. More generally, communal assessment for governmental taxes, when made by three assessors chosen by the majority or by lot, resembles valuation of movable property or land by three experts. Such an expert estimation is legally meaningful. Each assessor must therefore exercise great care and act honestly according to his own best judgment, for his role is comparable to that of a dayan. A demand that the assessor swear that he will assess honestly is not a valid demand. The oaths found in the laws of taxation concern the taxpayers themselves, who state the extent of their means so that their proper share can be calculated. That resembles the oath of partners. An assessor, however, is acting as a judge, and no oath, handshake undertaking, or similar solemnity should be imposed upon him. Even if the ruler expressly commands that assessors swear, it appears that dina demalchuta does not require Jews to create an oath where Torah law does not recognize such a requirement. Those being assessed can regard the matter as though they had already accepted the assessors' declaration, and they should themselves avoid causing an unnecessary oath. The general reason that courts do not impose oaths upon judges or witnesses is also relevant here. Oaths concerning money are ordinarily required because a monetary suspect may rationalize his conduct through an old counterclaim or temporary evasion. Those concerns do not sensibly apply to witnesses, judges, or assessors. We cannot reasonably suppose that an assessor knows of a hidden old debt between each taxpayer and every other taxpayer and therefore adjusts the assessment improperly, nor does the ordinary rationale of temporary evasion apply to him because the tax is not his own obligation. By contrast, the taxpayers themselves may rationalize understatement because of old claims against the community or money they previously advanced for public needs, even where Torah law would not strictly entitle them to reimbursement. Accordingly, assessors should not be sworn. As to the substance of an assessment, perhaps a person's means are judged according to his present capacity rather than according to profits he is expected to earn later from his money, even where those future profits are reasonably predictable. On the other hand, Shulchan Aruch indicates that business capital in his hands and a house that produces profit can enter the assessment, so the point requires careful study. The custom not to assess young married men during the period in which their parents provide their support appears to rest upon the fact that this support is given so that they can devote themselves to Torah study without the burden of earning a livelihood. For that reason communal levies are not ordinarily imposed upon them during that period. It may also matter that even where their money is placed in an investment arrangement, they are not actually engaged in ordinary commerce. A further local question arose concerning the governmental measure collected on the production of liquor. Formerly one tenth was collected. A governmental order later reduced the measure to one sixteenth, like the levy from ordinary milling, while a separate quota arrangement equalized it in practice to one tenth. The local mills were later discontinued because of changes involving the pond and waterworks above them. The lessees of the tax farm argued that when grain was milled in village mills for the purpose of making liquor, they were still entitled to the difference between the village rate of one sixteenth and the local rate of one tenth, together with the additional customary measure. The householders argued that the quota arrangement applied only according to the governmental privilege, whose wording covered only grain milled here. It appears that the tax lessees are not considered possessors against the townspeople on this disputed extension. The burden of proof is upon the party seeking to collect for grain milled outside the town. Still, because the quota obligation remains a continuing governmental power and the ruler can demand the relevant share of liquor, a lessee standing in his place may have power to alter the practical arrangement in a case where it was never clearly established that the agreement permitted residents to mill outside the town and produce liquor free of the corresponding levy.
