Maharik ruled that where creditors had despaired of a debt, later collection could be treated as acquisition from ownerless property, so those who collected were not necessarily required to share with people who had held interests in the original debt. Chacham Tzvi strongly attacked this. He argued that despair should not erase the borrower's mitzvah of repayment, just as one who took a lost object before despair for the purpose of returning it remains obligated to return it after despair. He further argued that if a third party receives the debtor's payment, that person can acquire it on behalf of the original creditor just as one Jew can act for another. In my view Maharik's ruling is sound. The lost object analogy must be analyzed through Ramban's explanation. A finder who takes the object in order to return it becomes a paid custodian for the owner. His hand is therefore like the owner's hand, and despair cannot acquire while the property is effectively still in the owner's possession. But one who took the lost object with intent to keep it, although he acted wrongly in intention, did not receive it through an act of theft from the owner. The object was already lost and came to him permissibly. Once the owner later despairs, it can be acquired as a lost object. A loan is stronger for Maharik. The money reached the borrower lawfully with the lender's consent. It is not in the lender's possession, and the borrower is not the lender's custodian. The lender cannot consecrate the outstanding oral loan because it is outside his possession. Therefore, if there is a genuine form of despair appropriate to a debt, there is substantial basis for saying the debt is acquired by the debtor and the original creditor's ownership is gone. Thus the ordinary mitzvah to repay does not by itself make the debt remain property of the lender after a legally effective despair. The analogy to a lost object taken for return fails because there the finder's hand remains legally the owner's hand. That element is absent in a loan.
The proof that despair can acquire a debt is strengthened by the Gemara at the end of Perek Kol HaGet. One who lent money to a kohen, Levi, or poor person in order to separate tithes on their behalf may continue doing so on the presumption they remain alive. But if the lenders despaired of the debt because the borrowers' fields were ruined, one no longer separates on that debt, because one does not separate on a lost obligation. Rashi explains that the lender said, in effect, "Woe for my monetary loss," and afterward the fields recovered. The Gemara says the novelty is that even though the borrowers later became financially restored, the earlier despair remained significant. This is an explicit source that despair can operate upon a debt. Pnei Yehoshua found this difficult because he assumed despair never works for debts and cited siman 98. But the Tur there did not establish such a rule. Beit Yosef, citing Maharik, distinguishes between ordinary discouragement because the borrower is poor or violent, which is not true despair since credit always carries uncertainty, and genuine despair arising from circumstances that ordinarily create final abandonment. The Shulchan Aruch and Rama follow that distinction. The ruined fields case is a paradigmatic genuine despair and therefore proves Maharik's rule rather than contradicting it.
Chacham Tzvi further argued that when the gentile debtor later paid money to Jews, those Jews automatically acquired it for the original Jewish creditors, because one Jew can acquire for another. This too is incorrect. Tosafot in Perek Eizehu Neshekh expressly says that when a gentile pays money through a Jew, the Jewish recipient does not acquire that money for another Jew merely because acquisition for another ordinarily works. The gentile is not abandoning the money. He intends to transfer his own money to the creditor through the intermediary. For the intermediary to acquire it for the creditor would therefore make him the gentile's agent in transferring the gentile's property, and there is no agency for a gentile. Accordingly, once genuine despair has caused the original debt to be acquired by the gentile debtor, the money he later pays is his own money. A Jewish recipient cannot automatically acquire it for another Jew unless a valid halachic transfer operates. The absence of agency for a gentile prevents the simple argument advanced by Chacham Tzvi.
There is nevertheless a difficulty in Tosafot's formulation. If a gentile debtor owes a Jew, why is the recipient's acquisition on behalf of the creditor described as agency for the gentile? A creditor can sometimes seize money for another creditor without the debtor's consent, at least where no one else is harmed. Money also ordinarily requires no appraisal, and a person can sometimes enforce his own monetary right directly. Perhaps Tosafot follows Rif's view that even where no other creditors are harmed, a third party may seize for a creditor without the debtor's consent only where there is a real danger of loss, such as where the debtor is dissipating assets or has become poor. If the debtor is financially sound, the seizure requires the debtor's consent. In that setting the recipient indeed functions through the debtor's transfer and, where the debtor is a gentile, the agency problem arises. In the case under discussion, however, the point is simpler. Once the debt has been acquired through despair, the gentile no longer owes anything at all. Any later money he gives is certainly his own money, and an acquisition for the original creditor requires a transfer from the gentile. Because there is no agency for a gentile, the Jewish intermediary does not automatically acquire it for the former creditor. This is clear.
I was surprised to see Noda BiYehudah dismiss the proposition that a Jew cannot acquire for another Jew from a gentile's money as "meaningless." He argued that the only disability is where a Jew seeks to acquire for a gentile or a gentile for a Jew, but where both the recipient and beneficiary are Jews, ordinary acquisition should work. Tosafot explicitly says otherwise. Tosafot in Gittin rules that if the donor in a three party arrangement is a gentile while the debtor and recipient are Jews, the recipient can acquire only because the Sages' special three party enactment can uproot ordinary property rules, or because secular law recognizes the transfer. If ordinary Jewish acquisition on behalf of another Jew worked with the gentile's money, Tosafot would not need those special reasons. Mordechai likewise says that although acquisition generally works through agency and there is no agency for a gentile, the special three party enactment can still operate where the donor is a gentile. This again shows that ordinary acquisition by one Jew for another does not transfer a gentile's property without a valid act attributable to the gentile. This distinction also sheds light on the prohibition against withholding wages. Tosafot asks why the Torah did not state the rule as ordinary theft and answer that theft is linked to the positive command of returning the stolen item and therefore ordinarily does not generate lashes. Withholding wages can generate lashes in a case where the employee has genuinely despaired of the debt. As explained above, a debt that came into the employer's hands lawfully can be acquired by genuine despair, and later payment would be a new gift. Theft is different because stolen property came into the thief's hands unlawfully, and despair alone does not remove the thief's obligation to restore the value. Thus withholding a wage debt can produce a case where the negative prohibition remains after the monetary obligation has fallen away, while ordinary theft cannot.
