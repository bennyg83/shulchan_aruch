Seif 6. (An idle person.) Elsewhere I discussed at length the teaching in the first chapter of Bava Batra concerning a person who is not included in the ordinary productive population. In our present governmental levy connected with Shabbat candles, it appears that compulsory collection applies only to those officially registered in the town's population count with the government and its officers, without concealment. Those who are not registered are not, as a matter of Torah law, included in the burden merely because they happen to be present. I wrote at length about this in a responsum concerning a partnership in leasing the governmental right to collect this levy. The lessees, however, paid no attention to the halachic issue. The original lessor stipulated with each sublessee that he would collect also from unregistered residents, and the rental price was increased accordingly. Before entering the lease, even observant lessees did not consult those knowledgeable in halachah. They now answer that they are merely agents of the original lessor. That defense is insufficient, since there is no agency to perform a transgression, but at this point it is also a matter in which admonition is unlikely to be heard. I have written several times that where there is a double doubt together with a strong practical assessment that rebuke will not be accepted, there is not even a pious obligation to rebuke. That situation exists here. First, perhaps the unregistered residents are not being compelled at all, but voluntarily compromise and pay so that they may light, since even visitors from outside the country who light are charged under the governmental order. Perhaps collection is made only from those who actually light, or only by consensual compromise. Second, perhaps the lessees would not heed rebuke, particularly since they paid extra to the lessor precisely for this right and can plausibly say that if they do not collect, the original lessor will do so himself. He is powerful in this matter and at times uses coercive collectors. Where a Jewish collector merely points out the property to a non Jewish officer, without himself taking the pledge, the issue is somewhat less severe. From the standpoint of agency, a non Jew is not a halachic agent, and there is no agency for transgression in any event. As to the underlying theft or oppression of the poor, the Jewish sublessees can argue that they are not causing a loss that would otherwise not occur, because the original lessor would collect it regardless. Openly confronting him may also expose all concealed residents to danger of denunciation to the authorities. Without a vow, I intend to instruct the local Jewish collector at least never to seize pledges personally, but only to leave any such seizure to the non Jewish officer. If the concealed residents in fact light and therefore owe the levy, this is not a case for saying, "Better that they remain inadvertent than become intentional sinners," especially when they protest that they do not light and the underlying prohibition of theft is explicit in the Torah. The rule that there is no agency for transgression is not stated expressly in the Written Torah, but because the substantive wrong is explicit, the matter should not simply be classified as a nonexplicit prohibition for purposes of that doctrine. In a doubtful case, moreover, the rule of preferring inadvertent violation does not straightforwardly apply. Nevertheless, in practice the double doubt and the strong likelihood that rebuke will not be accepted justify refraining from rebuke, and there is no legitimate complaint against one who remains silent.
