When the residents of a city are assessed for construction of the city wall, the assessment is made according to the proximity of each house to the wall, so that one whose house is nearer pays more. Some say that the assessment is made according to wealth, and after apportioning it according to wealth, proximity is also considered, with the nearer house paying more. Thus, if two houses are equally near and their owners have equal wealth, they pay equally. If one house is near the wall but its owner has no wealth, while another is farther away but its owner has wealth, nothing is collected from the nearer owner, because he has no property for which to fear. Rama: Whenever an assessment is made according to wealth, the majority is determined by the majority of the wealth. Therefore, wealthy persons who are a minority in number are regarded as the majority for this purpose. Accordingly, Mahari ruled concerning five residents of a city, two of whom were powerful and wealthy brothers who wished to select two arbiters connected with them, that the law was in their favor. If two houses contain equal wealth but one is farther and the other nearer, the nearer owner pays more. This applies only in peacetime, when the authority of the government is feared. In wartime, when kings wage war against one another, proximity is irrelevant, and the assessment is made only according to the value of the property. If the danger concerns life, the assessment also takes account of the number of persons, half according to wealth and half according to persons. For this purpose, wealth means movable property. Rama: Likewise, if a caravan becomes lost in the wilderness and lives are endangered, the expense is apportioned according to the number of persons as well. Concerning accusations and oppressive decrees imposed by non-Jews upon Jews, even when Jews are tortured, some say the assessment is according to Jewish wealth. Others disagree and hold that whenever there is danger to life, even where the authorities merely forbid the sale of bread to Jews, prohibit shechitah, or impose a similar decree, the assessment is according to persons. It appears to me that the judges should decide according to the circumstances. If city watchmen initially guarded personally at night and later reached an agreement with the ruler to pay a fixed annual sum, the sum is collected according to wealth, although at first each person had to guard equally. This applies where the ruler assumes responsibility for guarding. If non-Jewish guards remain in place and the Jews hire two or three persons to guard in their stead, the obligation still rests personally on each resident, and rich and poor pay equally. When an assessment is made according to wealth, money belonging to others with which a person does business is included. Even where the prior custom was not to assess such funds, the community may change the rule prospectively and assess them. Some disagree and hold that one need not pay on property belonging to others, and if the authorities suspect concealment, the residents should disclose the arrangement to the king. The custom follows the first opinion. If one holds another's capital under an investment arrangement, the recipient pays on one half and the investor on one half. Property belonging separately to a wife, or to minor sons or daughters, is likewise assessed. If the prior practice was to assess by appraisal, the community may change the rule and require sworn declarations, because the residents are partners and may impose the partners' oath upon one another. If all are required to swear and one says, 'I will not swear; assess me at whatever amount you wish,' some say he is not heard. Once the tax has been apportioned to each person and collection has begun, each share becomes a debt. Even if a person later becomes poor, he must pay the amount assessed against him. Conversely, if he was poor and later became wealthy, the controlling time is always the time of collection. A person who comes to the city after the tax obligation arose but before collection need not pay that tax, because it was already fixed, and the community may not stipulate otherwise. Some say that if the tax benefits newcomers, they must contribute their share. All tax matters follow an established municipal custom that has been followed three times. Even if the custom is defective, tax practice is not scrutinized closely. If a community lent money to a nobleman who promised to credit it against future taxes, then refused and died, so that the community despaired of collection, and his son later succeeded him and granted the credit, the community is deemed to have acquired ownerless property. It need not pay shares to those who were wealthy when the loan was made but later lost their wealth. A person who has collectible debts owed to him is assessed on them. Debts he genuinely owes to others are deducted. Accrued interest on pledged property is not assessed until it is capitalized with the principal, and rent not yet received by the owner is likewise not assessed. Funds designated for a mitzvah or charity are not assessed if the owner derives no benefit from them. If he receives the profits or his descendants will benefit from them, he pays according to the benefit. Some say that one who has a deposit held elsewhere, which he does not use in business, must pay tax on it even if he also pays where it is deposited. Others disagree. All agree that land situated elsewhere is not assessed. Where assessments are made by appraisal, a person cannot later claim that the assessors erred. Where they are made by sworn declaration, he may claim that they erred. Immovable property is generally not assessed. Rama: The exclusion of land applies to houses and similar property that produces no profit. If a person owns two or three houses, lives in one and rents the others, or owns fields and vineyards from which he profits, or conducts commerce through real estate, he must pay on them, though not at the same rate as on other wealth. Where houses are assessed, a more valuable and conspicuous house pays more. In all these matters the local custom governs. Wealthy residents may not impose a tax on houses where houses are not ordinarily assessed. Just as houses are not assessed, ordinary household utensils and books are not assessed. Jewelry is assessed, but at a lower rate than other wealth, whether attached to clothing or not, all according to custom. If there is concern that courtyards and land will be plundered, or that houses will be burned or demolished, the assessment takes all such property into account. Rama: Where the city employs a teacher for children whose parents cannot afford tuition, so that the community must pay his salary, the expense is assessed according to wealth. The same applies to the cantor's salary and to construction of a synagogue. All municipal needs must be supported even by those who do not personally require them, such as a wedding hall, mikvah, and similar institutions.
