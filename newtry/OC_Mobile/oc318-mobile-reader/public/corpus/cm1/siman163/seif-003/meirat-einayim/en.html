Whoever lives closer to the wall pays more. The reason is that when a city lacks a wall and robbers raid it, they naturally first attack the outer houses. Those houses therefore require greater protection than the inner ones.
The wealthy, though fewer in number, etc. In Responsa Rosh, the Rosh did not state this as a general rule. He was asked about a community wishing to impose a ban over the objection of a wealthy minority, and answered that since the ban concerned money, we follow the majority of wealth. It is improper for the numerical majority who pay only a small portion of the tax to impose a ban upon the wealthy as they wish. One can say that the Rosh meant only that the rest of the community cannot enact such a ban without the wealthy minority's consent, not that the wealthy minority itself counts as a majority entitled to impose its will. Perhaps the two groups are treated as equal halves and must compromise on such matters. This requires study.
Therefore Mahari Isserlein ruled regarding five people in one city, etc. See Terumat HaDeshen, siman 340. Rama shortened and altered the wording in a way that can mislead. The question concerned a community upon which a tax was imposed. Everyone agreed to choose five assessors to determine each person's share. Two powerful brothers wanted two assessors connected to them included among the five, while the rest of the community insisted that all five be independent. Mahari Isserlein answered that those who allow the wealthy brothers to choose some assessors act properly, provided the majority of assessors have no connection to them, because this will produce a more accepted result. He compares it to the rule that each litigant selects one judge. If the powerful wealthy people can select assessors they trust, they are more likely to accept the result and not disrupt the process, while the majority of assessors remain independent. This applies specifically to powerful wealthy people. Ordinary middle-status residents need no such accommodation because they will obey the process anyway. It also applies only if the wealthy select honest people, not persons known for deceit or manipulation. Thus Mahari Isserlein did not rule that a wealthy minority is treated as a majority. Rama's opening word 'therefore' is imprecise, and his abbreviated wording 'five people in one city' sounds as though the whole town had only five residents, whereas the five were merely the selected assessors.
If two houses have equal wealth, etc. The same principle applies when their wealth is unequal: the apportionment follows both wealth and proximity. The Tur and Mechaber stated the clearest case, that when wealth is equal the closer house pays more. See the Perishah.
But in wartime, etc. In peacetime, when the wall is built mainly against robbers who seize property and flee, they generally plunder first from houses near the wall. When kings wage war, by contrast, a victorious army enters and plunders the whole city. They ordinarily preserve the inhabitants so they can serve and pay taxes to their rulers. Therefore in such a case the levy is based only on wealth. If the threat also concerns lives, then both wealth and persons are considered.
Only according to the value of wealth. Meaning, according to the amount of wealth each owns.
According to the matter as it appears to the judges. Governmental accusations vary: sometimes the danger is monetary and sometimes to life.
Or his minor daughters. Maharam of Rothenburg writes that one who deposited money with a third party as his daughter's dowry, to be given to the groom if the match is completed, must include that money in the tax assessment. Darkei Moshe cites this.
'Assess me however you wish,' we do not listen to him. The reason is that the assessors can say, 'We cannot accurately determine your wealth.' This differs from siman 375:8, where if a person says, 'Let the judges estimate my expenditures and I will take that amount without an oath,' they listen, because labor costs can be estimated by comparing other workers who performed similar work.
If they assessed the tax upon each person and began collecting, etc. Rashba, responsum 887, writes that once the appointed assessors fixed the tax upon the community and collection began, Reuven became obligated for his entire assigned share even if nothing had yet been collected from him and he became poor before payment. It is then like any other debt. This implies specifically that both assessment and actual commencement of collection are required. Only that collection already begun becomes a debt fixed while he was wealthy. Later tax collections during the year need not necessarily follow the earlier assessment after his circumstances change. Even if one argues that Rashba spoke in a place where each collection had a fresh assessment, whereas today one annual assessment may cover all collections, that cannot be decisive, because Rashba deliberately requires both assessment and beginning collection. Thus both are needed.
Likewise, if he was poor and became wealthy, we always follow the time of collection. Rashba, responsum 797, discusses a communal debt established ten years earlier and payable now, where assessment practices changed in the interim. Where local custom clearly follows the current assessment, custom governs. Strict law, however, would follow the original assessment, as partners who jointly borrowed and must repay according to their shares at the time of borrowing. Darkei Moshe presents Rashba's words in the reverse order, first strict law and then custom, apparently to indicate that present practice follows the custom. Rama's statement here therefore means that when a debt already exists but actual collection begins only after the person became wealthy, the current collection standard governs. It does not merely refer to someone becoming poorer or richer after collection already began, for then Rama's later clause concerning one who came to the city would not connect. The wording 'he was poor and became wealthy' teaches an additional point: even though he was poor initially, if collection begins only after he becomes wealthy he pays as wealthy. The earlier clause taught the opposite novelty: when collection began while he was wealthy, he remains liable at that level even after becoming poor.
Even though it is an inferior custom, tax matters are not scrutinized so strictly. This is unlike ordinary matters, as I wrote above in Sema to siman 157. Darkei Moshe discusses it at length here.
The correct reading is: in tax matters, and afterward he refused, died, they despaired of the debt, and afterward his son arose in his place and deducted, etc. This is the explicit wording in the source and is cited by Darkei Moshe.
If they are collectible, etc. But one who possesses a paid or false promissory note and keeps it only until an opportunity arises to make a claim, or one who can extract money from a non-Jew only through claims and litigation, need not include such amounts in the tax assessment. So writes Terumat HaDeshen, siman 342. Mahari Weil, siman 124, writes that assessors are believed concerning what a person admitted before them even after they have left office. Mordechai appears to imply that townspeople are not believed concerning another's admission while they remain interested. Since the community is in possession, as explained above in siman 4, a doubt of law is resolved in favor of the possessor.
But if he benefits from the profit, etc. Terumat HaDeshen writes that money donated for a mitzvah is exempt. If, however, only part of its profit is spent on the mitzvah, such as where a large sum was set aside so that a perpetual lamp would be funded from the profits, while the money earns twice what is needed and the donor retains a right that if he or his descendants become poor they may take back the fund for themselves, the money is not exempt.
One who has a deposit in another's hands, etc.; some say he must pay tax on it, etc., and some disagree. Terumat HaDeshen cites both views. According to the lenient view, one who sealed his money and placed it with a trusted custodian need not pay tax on it unless he is exceptionally wealthy, in which case the ruler's eye is on him regardless. Terumat HaDeshen concludes that practice should follow the first view and tax even deposits, because the ruler does not care whether the owner profited from the money; whatever wealth he owns is taxed even if it is outside the country or never physically came into his hand.
According to all opinions, if he owned land, etc. The reason is that land certainly cannot be brought here, unlike a deposit. See Darkei Moshe.
Where taxation is based on assessment, he cannot say the assessors erred, etc. Those assessed must pay the full amount assigned and cannot claim the assessors were mistaken. Likewise the community cannot claim the assessors erred. The enactment itself necessarily contemplated that assessors would estimate each person's property according to their judgment, and their assessment stands even though they are not prophets and may sometimes err upward or downward. This differs where taxation is based on each person's oath concerning his wealth. There, because the amount depends on the taxpayer's truthful sworn declaration, if he later proves an error even after swearing, we listen and deduct it. Although the Tur and Mechaber in siman 81:31 say that after an oath we do not hear a claim of mistake because a person is presumed not to swear without certainty, where he substantiates his explanation and gives a credible reason we do listen. The novelty is that the tax enactment does not eliminate correction of a proven error where the system itself depends on sworn self-reporting.
Or he trades in real estate. Darkei Moshe cites Mahari Weil, siman 84, that houses held for sale are like other merchandise and are taxed. Maharam of Rothenburg says they are exempt because mere intention to sell does not create liability. Darkei Moshe distinguishes: if he bought the house specifically for resale at a profit, it is taxable merchandise; not so if he merely later wishes to sell his residence or an inherited house.
But jewelry. Maharik writes that coins are considered converted into jewelry only if their form as currency has been genuinely removed, not where during tax assessment people temporarily fashion gold coins as ornaments or cast silver into a cup-like ingot. Darkei Moshe cites this.
All communal needs, even though some individuals do not need them, such as a wedding hall, etc. This appears limited to needs that generally serve all Jews, such as a wedding facility or mikveh. Even if one individual happens not to need it because of age or another reason, he must contribute. This differs from an expense to secure governmental assistance concerning a private promissory-note matter that is not communal. Therefore Rama writes at the end of this siman, from Responsa Rosh, that others need not contribute to such assistance.
