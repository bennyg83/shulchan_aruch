They are considered the majority. This means only that the rest of the community cannot impose a ban upon them without the consent of the wealthy, because the wealthy control the majority of the money. It does not mean that a small number of wealthy people are themselves considered a numerical majority with power to impose a ban upon the majority of the community. Perhaps they are treated as equal to the other half, so both groups must compromise in matters of this kind. The matter requires study. Sema:
Five people. The Rama abbreviates the case. The community needed to appoint five people to assess taxes. Two brothers in the community were powerful and wealthy and wanted two of the five assessors to be people connected to them. In that case the law favors the wealthy brothers, since the majority of the assessors would still have no connection to them and the assessment would therefore more likely reach the truth. This applies specifically to powerful wealthy people where there is concern that the procedure will otherwise be disrupted. With ordinary people there is no need to be particular. It also applies only if they wish to select honest people. If they want to select schemers or dishonest people, there is no basis for their demand at all. The word "therefore" used by the Rama is imprecise. The ruling does not follow because the wealthy count as a majority, but because the process should not be disrupted. Sema:

When there is war. In peacetime the wall protects chiefly against bandits, who ordinarily plunder the houses nearest the perimeter first. When kings are at war, however, a victorious army enters the entire city and plunders throughout it, so the levy is based only on the value of property. If the danger also threatens life, the levy is based on both wealth and persons. Sema:

Sema. The Taz disagrees and holds that the tax assessment alone fixes the obligation even if collection has not yet begun:
He was poor and became wealthy. This does not refer back to the preceding rule as though he had been poor when collection began. Rather, he was poor when the tax was assessed and became wealthy by the time collection began. Therefore we follow his status when collection actually began. This also explains the Rama's later phrase, "However, one who came," meaning that even though we generally follow the time of collection, one who arrived in the city before collection began still need not necessarily pay. One who pays according to an assessment. Meaning that those whose tax is fixed by assessors cannot later say that the assessors erred. Likewise, the community cannot say that the assessors erred. The original enactment necessarily intended that the assessors determine liability according to their judgment, and their assessment stands even if they sometimes err, since they are not prophets. By contrast, where tax liability is based on what each person swears that he owns, if he can later prove that his oath rested on a mistake, we listen to him even after he swore. Although siman 81, seif 31, states a presumption that a person does not swear unless he has clarified the truth, when he later clarifies his words and offers a valid explanation, we listen to him.
