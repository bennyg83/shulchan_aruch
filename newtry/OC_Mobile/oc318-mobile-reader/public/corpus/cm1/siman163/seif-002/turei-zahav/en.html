There, in the Rama's gloss: 'and they despaired of the debt,' etc. Maharik writes there that although the individuals claimed that they had never despaired, but had placed their burden upon Hashem in the hope that He would turn the lord's heart favorably toward the Jews and that in future assessments he would deduct the amount for them, even though two tax collections had already passed without his making any deduction, in Maharik's view this claim had no substance. Rather, one must determine objectively whether it was reasonable to despair. He concludes that there was despair because of the lord's wickedness, since he had repeatedly acted very badly, and he discusses this at length. I am astonished. In siman 101, seif 3, we find that where a violent ruler robbed unencumbered land, the creditor does not collect from encumbered property because violent rulers are liable to fall and ultimately the creditor may recover his debt. That possibility is strong enough that we delay payment to a creditor who holds a promissory note. All the more so should it prevent us from confiscating the money of individuals who lent their funds, since perhaps they will eventually receive payment. Why should we say that they receive nothing merely because they despaired? Moreover, according to this, if someone has a debt owed by another person who then becomes poor, should we likewise say that the debt is lost through despair because the debtor is presently unable to pay? If one answers that a robber is worse, that is incorrect. In Bava Basra, daf 44, it is evident that it is more common to recover stolen property from a robber than to recover a debt from someone who became poor and may later become wealthy. There the Gemara asks why the victim should not be established as a witness for the robber, since perhaps he intends later to recover the stolen property from him; whereas on daf 45 Tosafos write that where the debtor became poor he may testify for him and we do not fear that the debtor may later become wealthy. Moreover, even in a case of complete despair, siman 259 states that under dina d'malchusa it may be proper to return the property. All the more so here, in practical law, there is no basis to cause the individual lenders to lose their money.
The editor says: In my humble opinion there is no difficulty from siman 111, because that case concerns land, and land cannot be stolen in the halachic sense, so despair does not operate upon it at all. On the contrary, I wonder at the Taz: before challenging Maharik, he should have confronted an explicit Gemara in the final chapter of Bava Kamma. If robbers took a person's garment and gave him another garment, the replacement belongs to him because the owners despair; similarly regarding one who rescues property from robbers. The Gemara distinguishes between non-Jewish and Jewish robbers only with respect to the presumption of despair. Where we actually know that the owner despaired, everyone agrees that despair is effective, even the Sages who disagree with R' Shimon regarding an ordinary robber. This is clear from the Gemara. The second objection likewise falls away because that case too concerns land. With a cow or garment that was sold, despair does operate, and the sugya itself demonstrates a difference between land and movable property. As for the question from siman 259, I do not understand it. There the ruler decreed that the property be returned; here the ruler himself is the robber. Nevertheless, I have a much greater difficulty with Maharik's basic ruling. In my humble opinion, despair does not extinguish a debt at all, consistent with the broad wording of Rabbeinu Binyamin cited by Maharik. Although he says, 'this is not despair regarding a debt,' the word 'this' is not limiting. Maharik himself builds a distinction upon it, yet his own hold on that inference is weak, as is apparent in his responsum. His attempted kal vachomer from a lost object is, in my humble opinion, refutable. Concerning a lost object itself, the Gemara in the second chapter of Bava Metzia states that if one picked it up before the owner's despair intending to return it, and only after the owner despaired did he intend to steal it, he violates the commandment 'you shall surely return them.' Thus, even though it came into his hand permissibly and the owner afterward despaired, he must return it because the mitzvah of return attached from the moment he took it until he restores it. So too, repayment of a creditor is a positive mitzvah like sukkah and lulav, and that obligation rests upon the debtor from the time of the loan until payment. Likewise, although the victim despairs of stolen property, the robber himself is not thereby exempt from repayment. Even according to the authorities cited by the Tur in simanim 353, 368, and 369, who hold that a purchaser from the robber need not return the object, no one says that the robber himself is exempt. Bava Basra 44 explicitly says: even if he despairs of the object itself, does he despair of its value? Tosafos in the second chapter of Bava Metzia likewise indicate that after despair the robber remains obligated by 'he shall return the stolen object.' Accordingly, certainly in Maharik's case, where the lord held the money as stolen property, and even according to Rashi who limits the formal category of theft to the moment of taking, Tosafos show that the positive obligation of return is rectified by actual restitution even where the object was originally taken with intent to return it and the owner despaired afterward. The same is true of a loan. Since the lord remained obligated to pay even after despair, his property remained liable for the debt. Therefore, what the duchess repaid after her husband's death by deducting the five thousand on account of his debt was lawful payment, not an acquisition from ownerless property. This is not comparable to rescuing property from robbers, where the rescuer may keep the actual object after the original owner despaired. Once the owner despaired of that object, it became like any other property of the robber, so the rescuer acquires the robber's property; nevertheless, the robber remains obligated to compensate the original owner. Otherwise, according to the opinion that ordinary theft by a Jew is presumed to involve despair, why was it necessary for Rebbe to enact that those who repent from robbery should not have their repayments accepted? If despair itself exempted them, there would be no debt to return. It cannot be answered that Rebbe's enactment applied only when they voluntarily repented, whereas otherwise beit din would compel them. If there were no legal obligation after despair, there would be nothing to compel. And there are many cases where beit din cannot break the teeth of the wicked. The Mishnah itself says that all robbers pay according to the value at the time of the robbery. The matter is therefore as clear as the sun: even if despair effects acquisition of the object and ordinary robbery is deemed subject to despair, that concerns only whether the identical object must be returned; its value remains payable. The same applies to a debt: no form of despair, whether an explicit cry of 'woe for my monetary loss' or any other evidence, removes the positive obligation of repayment. Even with robbers, if the robber gives the object to another person and says, 'Receive this for so-and-so, from whom I robbed it,' it is obvious that the victim acquires it. Maharik distinguishes between one despair and another, reasoning that with a debt every extension of credit is uncertain and sometimes a creditor thinks the debtor is poor or violent and will not pay when this is not truly so, whereas in a category where despair is presumed without any declaration, despair certainly operates. But the final chapter of Bava Kamma refutes this. We rule like Ulla that the dispute is only in an ordinary case, but where despair is known everyone agrees that it effects acquisition. Rashi explains that known despair means that the owner says, 'Woe for my monetary loss.' Yet here, according to the view that ordinary robbery is not presumed to create despair, one still needs such an express declaration, even though every robbery is at least as doubtful of recovery as a debt and the robber is violent. Nevertheless, that declaration suffices. One cannot say that it suffices because the robber truly is violent, for that very circumstance is insufficient to create presumed despair according to this view. Maharik's reasoning therefore turns against him: if in an ordinary debt there are fewer reasons for saying 'woe for my monetary loss,' yet he would attribute the statement to temporary circumstances and deny its effect, all the more so in robbery, where those circumstances are plainly present, the statement should not prove final despair. This is a powerful objection. Furthermore, even where a purchaser acquires fully through despair and change of possession, the Gemara says that one who wishes to act piously may return the object to the original owner. If he delivered it through an agent for the original owner, surely the original owner would acquire it. What difference does it make whether the giver is a non-Jew or a Jew? Here, moreover, the duchess deducted the five thousand specifically as repayment of her husband's debt, not as a gift or abandonment. On what basis should persons who never lent the lord anything acquire this repayment? This also disposes of Maharik's lengthy discussion of the dispute between R' Ephraim and R' Meir concerning one who gives money to another to pay a non-Jew and then forgets about it, cited by the Tur at the end of siman 183, and whether one can say that the money was abandoned only with respect to the duke. In my poverty I do not understand the analogy. Every lender lends on the understanding that the borrower may use the money however he wishes. If later the lenders despaired and, according to Maharik, the duke thereby acquired the money, then a purchaser from the duke would indeed acquire according to that view. If the dispute of R' Ephraim and R' Meir is relevant at all, it is to the latter stage, the duchess's repayment. She repaid the five thousand as her husband's debt, not as a gift or ownerless money. Certainly according to R' Meir the individuals must receive their original shares, because that is the purpose for which she paid and deducted the tax. Even R' Ephraim and Maharik should agree, since the individual lenders stand and demand their own property. The moment the duchess paid, the community received it on behalf of the individuals, for one Jew may serve as another Jew's agent to receive his money from a non-Jew. This is simple. It is comparable to a Jew who wishes to act piously and restore property after despair and change of possession, and sends it through an agent saying, 'Acquire this for so-and-so'; the original owner acquires it. There is no basis to distinguish because the duchess did not literally say, 'Acquire for the individuals,' because all expressions of repayment are effective, as explained at the beginning of siman 125, aside from the particular expression 'return,' about which there is discussion. Maharik's words here are therefore exceedingly difficult. I have an additional powerful objection to his final proof from Semag, Hilchos Mekach U'Memkar, who explains that the exclusion of documents from the law of ona'ah is needed for one who finds loan documents after the owner despaired, acquires the documents, and then sells them back to the original lender; the sale is effective by Torah law because the document is written in his name. Maharik writes: behold, it is explicit that despair is effective regarding debts. I am astonished that such words could be said. What connection is there between despair of the physical documents and despair of the debt? Say rather that despair does not affect the debt at all, for the reasons stated above, but if the lender lost his documents and despaired of the documents, and another person found them after despair, the finder certainly acquires the paper because it came to him permissibly. Although he cannot collect the debt through them on his own, the documents belong to him and he may use them as he wishes. The lender then buys them from him as evidence enabling collection from purchasers. Far from proving Maharik's position, this source proves the opposite. If Maharik were correct that the lender despaired of the debt itself, why would the lender buy the documents in order to collect with them? The lender could not lawfully collect a debt that had ceased to be his. Surely Semag is not discussing a wicked person attempting to collect what no longer belongs to him. Necessarily, despair applies only to the documents and not to the debt. Maharik's ruling is therefore extremely difficult to sustain. Although I am young and lacking human understanding, it appears to me that one should not rule in accordance with these words of Maharik. With due reverence for the holy remains of Rama, he too did not rule correctly in bringing Maharik's position as halacha in siman 262, seif 5. It is astonishing that the later authorities did not note this. Heaven has left room for me. In conclusion, the Taz's practical ruling is correct, though not for the reason he gave. End of the editor's words.
