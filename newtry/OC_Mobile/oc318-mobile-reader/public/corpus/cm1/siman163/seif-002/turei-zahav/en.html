(Ibid., in the gloss: They despaired of recovering the debt, etc.) The Maharik writes that although the private lenders claimed that they had never despaired, but had placed their burden upon Hashem in the hope that the ruler would eventually favor the Jews and deduct the amount for them, their claim is not accepted merely on that basis. One must determine objectively whether the circumstances were such that a reasonable person would have despaired. He concludes that there was despair because of the ruler’s persistent wickedness and his repeated harsh conduct. I find this difficult. In siman 101, seif 3, we find that where a violent robber seized unencumbered property, a creditor may not collect from encumbered property, because violent men are liable to fall and the owner may ultimately recover his property. That possibility is strong enough to postpone payment to a creditor who holds a valid document. All the more so should it prevent us from confiscating the money of private individuals who advanced their own funds and may yet receive repayment. Why should they receive nothing merely because they supposedly despaired? Moreover, if a debtor became poor, would we likewise say that the creditor despaired because the debtor cannot presently pay? One cannot distinguish by saying that a robber is worse, for Bava Batra 44 shows that recovery from a robber is more likely than a poor debtor’s later becoming wealthy. The Gemara is concerned that a robbed owner may testify for the robber in order later to recover the stolen property, whereas Tosafot on 45 state that one may testify for a person who became poor, without concern that he may later become wealthy. Furthermore, even where there was complete despair, siman 259 states that under the law of the kingdom it is proper to return the property. Certainly here we should not rely upon despair to cause the private lenders an actual loss. The annotator responds that the case in siman 111 concerns land. Land cannot be stolen in the full legal sense, and despair does not apply to it. He therefore questions the Taz’s proof from that case. The Gemara in the final chapter of Bava Kamma expressly teaches that if robbers took a person’s garment and gave him another garment, he may keep the garment received because the original owners despaired. The Gemara distinguishes, regarding presumed despair, between non-Jewish and Jewish robbers, but where actual despair was heard, all agree that it is effective, even the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Shimon regarding an ordinary robber. The second question likewise falls away, because the cited case concerned land; with a cow or garment that was sold, despair may indeed be effective. Nor is the proof from siman 259 compelling, because there the ruler ordered restitution, whereas here the ruler himself was the robber. Nevertheless, the annotator raises a more fundamental objection to the Maharik. In his view, despair is wholly ineffective with respect to a debt. This follows from the unqualified language of Rabbeinu Binyamin cited by the Maharik. Even though Rabbeinu Binyamin says, “This is not despair with respect to a debt,” the word “this” is not necessarily limiting, and the Maharik himself acknowledges that his inference is weak. The Maharik’s a fortiori argument from a lost object is also flawed. With a lost object, if one took it before the owner despaired in order to return it, and only after the owner despaired did the finder decide to keep it, he violates the commandment to return it. Although it came lawfully into his hand and the owner later despaired, the obligation of return had already attached from the moment he took it and continues until he returns it. The same is true of repayment of a debt, which is a positive commandment like sukkah and lulav. The obligation attaches at the time of the loan and continues until payment. Similarly, although the victim despaired, the robber himself is not exempt from restitution. The authorities who exempt a purchaser from returning the object after despair and transfer of possession do not exempt the robber himself. Bava Batra 44 states explicitly: “Granted that he despairs of the object itself, does he despair of its value?” Rashbam explains this there, and Tosafot in Bava Metzia 26 likewise indicate that after despair the robber still must restore value under the commandment, “He shall return the stolen item.” Even where an object was initially taken for the purpose of return, subsequent despair does not remove the obligation to return it. The same principle applies to a loan. Since the ruler remained obligated to repay even after despair, and his property remained liable, the duchess’s payment after her husband’s death was a lawful payment of his debt, not an acquisition from ownerless property. This is not comparable to one who rescues property from robbers after the owners despaired. In that case, once the owners despaired of the particular object, it became no different from the robber’s other property, and the rescuer acquires it from the robber. Yet the robber himself remains obligated to pay its value. Otherwise, why was Rabbi’s enactment needed that those who stole and then returned property in repentance should not have the return accepted from them? If an ordinary robbery were presumed subject to despair and the robber were thereby wholly exempt, no enactment would have been needed. Nor can one answer that the enactment applied only when they returned voluntarily as part of repentance. Tosafot show that Rabbi enacted the rule in both contexts. It also cannot be argued that absent voluntary return the court could compel them, and therefore Rabbi needed to discourage only voluntary restitution. Courts are often unable to break the teeth of violent wrongdoers. The Gemara’s case of one who stole a pair of oxen and plowed with them supports this. Moreover, the Mishna states plainly that all robbers pay according to the value at the time of the robbery. The matter is therefore clear: even if despair acquires the object, and even if an ordinary robbery is presumed to produce despair, that affects only the duty to return the identical object. The robber remains obligated to pay its value. The same necessarily applies to a debt. No form of despair, whether an explicit cry of financial loss or any other proof, removes the positive commandment to repay a debt. Even where despair was objectively reasonable, it does not extinguish the debt. If robbers returned the object to an intermediary and told him to receive it on behalf of the person from whom it was stolen, it is obvious that the victim acquires it. The Maharik attempts to distinguish between different kinds of despair, reasoning that credit is always uncertain, and a lender may mistakenly believe the borrower poor or violent when he is not. Yet regarding an ordinary robbery, where presumed despair is not automatic and an expression of despair is needed, the Gemara still treats an explicit statement of financial loss as effective even though recovery is uncertain and the robber may be violent. If those circumstances do not themselves establish despair, it is difficult to say that the owner’s expression should be weakened precisely because those circumstances explain it. This is a powerful objection to the Maharik’s distinction. There is a further difficulty. Even where one legally acquires an object through despair and a subsequent change of possession, the Gemara says that one who wishes to act beyond the strict law should return it to the original owner. Suppose he gives it to an agent to return to that owner. Surely the agent does not acquire it personally. The same applies whether the intermediary is Jewish or non-Jewish. Here, the duchess did not deduct the five thousand as a gift or as ownerless property, but as payment of her husband’s debt. By what right could members of the community who had never lent the ruler anything acquire that payment? This also disposes of the Maharik’s lengthy discussion of the dispute concerning one who gave money to another to pay a non-Jew and the recipient forgot to do so. The relevant comparison is not to the original loan, for every lender advances funds knowing that the borrower may use them as he wishes. The relevant point is the duchess’s later repayment. She paid the five thousand specifically as her husband’s debt, not as a gift. Therefore, even according to the more restrictive opinion, the private lenders are entitled to their shares. They stood and demanded their money, and when the duchess paid, the community received it for them. The community acted as their agents in receiving payment from the non-Jew. All legally effective formulations of repayment are equivalent, aside from a particular formulation discussed at the beginning of siman 125. A further decisive difficulty arises from the Semag’s statement in the laws of sale that the exclusion of documents from the laws of overreaching is needed for a case in which a finder discovered documents after the owner despaired, acquired the physical documents, and then sold them back to the original owner. The Maharik adduces this as explicit proof that despair applies to debts. This is astonishing. Despair of the physical documents has no necessary relation to despair of the debt. Even if despair cannot extinguish the debt, the finder who receives the lost documents after despair acquires the paper itself, which came lawfully into his hand. Though he cannot collect the debt with it, he owns the paper and may use it for any lawful purpose, such as covering a small vessel. The creditor purchases it back because he needs it as evidence and to collect from purchasers of encumbered property. Indeed, the Semag proves the opposite of the Maharik’s position. If despair had extinguished the debt itself, the original creditor would be acting wrongfully in buying the document in order to collect a debt no longer his. Why would he purchase it to collect from the borrower or encumbered property if the debt had already been lost? The only coherent explanation is that despair affects the document but not the underlying debt. Accordingly, the Maharik’s ruling is exceedingly difficult to sustain. Although I am young and of limited understanding, it appears to me that one should not rule in accordance with it. With due reverence, the Rama also did not rule correctly when he cited the Maharik’s position as law in siman 262, seif 5, and it is surprising that the later authorities did not address these objections. Heaven has left room for this clarification. In conclusion, the Taz’s practical ruling is correct, although not for the reason he originally gave.
