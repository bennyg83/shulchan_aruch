If it came through his request, etc. Meaning, even if the ruler will not reduce the community's assessment by the full amount that would have fallen upon him. The reason is that an ordinary partner does not separate himself from his fellow partners, and the whole community are partners in taxation. The Tur and Mechaber write similarly below at the beginning of siman 178 regarding a partner exempted from customs duty, and in siman 181:2 concerning a partner who saved property from marauders.
He must give the community his share. Meaning, the full amount he would have owed had the ruler not exempted him. The community may use it for the tax or other communal benefits, from which he too will benefit. This is the meaning of Mordechai's expression that he must 'divide with the community.' The opposing view holds that once the tax was already fixed on the community and the ruler reduces it by this person's share, that amount belongs to him. If the ruler exempted him before the community's assessment was fixed, however, perhaps the ruler would simply increase the community's burden by the same amount. With an idle person the rule is reversed: if exempted after the tax was apportioned, he remains liable because the ruler will not necessarily reduce the community's burden, saying that he had not intended that person's share because he was idle and under strict law liable. But if the ruler exempted him voluntarily before the assessment, there is room to say the exemption is effective.
An individual who pays a levy, etc. Responsa Rosh, klal 6, siman 10, writes that one who must pay a ruler because of his occupation must nevertheless share in communal taxes if the community's payment to the ruler is for protection and security, because he too benefits from it. Darkei Moshe cites this. Maharam of Rothenburg writes that if a wealthy person is singled out by the ruler for payment while the community refuses to allow him to separate from them, he should set aside his own tax, go to the ruler, and say, 'Under our law I may not separate from my fellows. I cannot pay you separately and also pay with them.' If the ruler then says, 'I want them to exempt you,' he is exempt, provided he accepts a ban that neither he nor his agent first suggested this arrangement to the ruler.
A community that exempted one person, etc. Beit Yosef cites Ritva that if a community agreed to exempt a person from taxes for a fixed period so that he would settle among them, or because of some other benefit he provided, and he dies during the period, his heirs must pay tax on the inherited property. Maharam of Rothenburg likewise writes that if the community gave one person permission to pay taxes separately and he died, his heirs cannot remain separate, because permission was granted to him, not to them.
However, they can say they exempted him only for one year, etc. Rama similarly rules above in siman 60:3 concerning one who undertook generally to support another or alternatively to pay him one hundred gold coins per year, that one year satisfies the unspecified undertaking. Both rulings come from Responsa Rosh, klal 6, ruling 17. There I proved that if multiple tax collections occur in one year, even one full year of all collections is not necessarily included in an unspecified exemption. Since the individual was otherwise liable and seeks exemption based on general language, the community has the stronger hand and can say, 'We exempted you only from one collection.' The support case is different because by offering the alternative of one hundred gold coins 'per year,' he revealed at least an intent to support for a year. This resembles Rama below at the beginning of siman 212, where one who says, 'So-and-so may live in my house,' without fixing a period can satisfy it even with a brief stay. Rama's statement here of a one-year exemption therefore concerns a tax collected only once annually, such as a per-capita levy. The responsum itself likewise discusses that type. Rama should have explained this rather than writing generally.
The community, etc. Responsa Rosh, klal 6, siman 10, writes that an enactment saying anyone who owns one hundred gold coins must pay tax includes one who owns land worth that amount. He further writes there, siman 17, that if the enactment says anyone who collects one hundred gold coins from debts must pay, selling one's debts to another counts as collection. He also writes in siman 28 that when tax collectors came to place Reuven under a ban for nonpayment and Shimon gave collateral for him, Shimon cannot later demand return of his collateral and require the community to impose the ban upon Reuven instead. Darkei Moshe cites these rulings.
One who does not bear the communal burden can compel, etc. This is the correct reading. Kol Bo writes likewise, and Darkei Moshe cites it. It appears to mean that although he himself does not contribute, he may compel others to pay taxes, communal assessments, or tzedakah. He should not, however, serve as the actual collector of those funds, so as to avoid suspicion because he himself is exempt.
