(One who causes damage to others, see Shach, seif katan 18.) Elsewhere I discussed Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Batra, who writes that once a communal tax has already taken effect, a person may not exempt himself in a way that shifts the burden and causes loss to his fellow. I suggested that perhaps one should distinguish in this matter between monetary loss and danger to life. I have now seen that at the end of siman 163 Shach, citing Mahari ben Lev, brings a proof from the episode in which Mephiboshet was spared. On its face, that source suggests that he does not distinguish between money and danger. It is possible, however, that there is no disagreement with the distinction. In Yevamot, regarding Mephiboshet, the Gemara asks how favoritism could have been shown. Mephiboshet did not save himself, so the principle that one's own life takes precedence was not directly applicable to his act. Once the Ark had accepted him among those subject to the decree, David's prayer to release him raised the issue of favoritism. Mahari ben Lev uses the episode only to prove that once the king's decree has attached to a person, it is regarded as having taken effect even though the king can later be appeased. The Ark's selection was likewise not irrevocable, for the Gemara says that it accepted and then rejected, yet once it had accepted him, saving him was treated as saving oneself at another's expense. Mahari ben Lev's case concerns money. Saving oneself from danger, however, should still be governed by the principle that one's own life takes precedence, as established by the case of the single jug of water. A similar idea appears at the end of Terumot regarding Sheva ben Bichri: because he had been specifically demanded by the government, others could not be sacrificed in his place. If the king does not specify a particular person, the source there implies that each person may seek to save himself. Nimukei Yosef's case can be distinguished because the tax had been imposed upon all townspeople. Those who fled were certainly included in the original burden whether or not scholars were ultimately required to pay, and by fleeing they necessarily shifted their proportional share onto those who remained. In that sense they had been specifically included. Mahari ben Lev may instead discuss a king who said only that he would take money from two wealthy men of the city without specifying which two. In such a case each wealthy person may attempt to exempt himself before the decree becomes attached specifically to him. This parallels the rule in Terumot and the episode of Mephiboshet, where the unresolved danger initially rested upon a class of persons and not one designated individual. Shach writes that a monetary levy can sometimes be comparable to danger because financial coercion can itself threaten a person. Perhaps that point does not apply to an ordinary royal tax, where it is known that those who remain will simply pay without danger. A lawful tax imposed by a king is not equivalent to an informer or violent extortionist. Thus those subject to the levy may not flee merely because they have the means to pay. Shach may have made the danger comparison only to strengthen his proof from Mephiboshet: one might otherwise argue that money is more lenient than life and therefore one may cause another monetary loss in order to save oneself. Shach responds that monetary coercion too can at times carry an element of danger. This requires further study. It is also possible that the particular levy discussed there was not an ordinary fixed royal tax but a loss caused by an accusation or arbitrary decree, in which case a person may sometimes save himself even though another is thereby exposed, as the authorities distinguish in siman 163. Maharam appears to hold that before a decree has attached the prohibition can in some respects be even stronger, as cited by Taz there, perhaps because he interpreted the earlier sources differently. The entire distinction requires careful study. Townspeople are obligated to assist one another with expenses necessary for the functioning and repair of the settlement. I have not found, however, a basis to compel one another to fund every measure that merely improves commerce, even though commerce is a central element of communal prosperity.
