(Seif 6: One whom the ruler exempted, etc.) This ruling requires explanation in light of the preceding law concerning an idle person. Tosafot state that when partners were granted remission of a customs charge, the remission belongs to all of them jointly. If the officials said, “We remitted it because of so-and-so,” that remission belongs to that individual. Rabbeinu Simcha asks what the first clause teaches and answers that it concerns a case in which one partner requested remission. Whatever was remitted belongs to all, because he is regarded as their agent. The second clause means that the officials themselves said, without any solicitation, that they remitted it because of a particular person, in which case the remission is his. Maharam writes that one may reject this explanation. The first clause may concern a case in which one person requested a general remission. Even if no one else petitioned, a remission stated generally belongs to all. But if the officials expressly say that they remitted it for a particular person, it belongs to him even when granted in response to his own request. The Mordechai further cites Maharam that if the community had already reached a settlement with the ruler and one member then sought exemption from his share, he rescued only himself and is exempt. If, however, he sought exemption before the settlement, he remains liable, for he may not lighten his own burden by increasing that of others. If the ruler independently declares that one person will pay no tax and specified others will bear it, the exempted person is free of liability even if they had previously been accustomed to pay together. Maharam then records another approach, under which fully integrated partners remain jointly liable even where the king independently says that one of them need pay nothing. Thus, Rabbeinu Simcha and Maharam disagree. Rabbeinu Simcha maintains that even if the king visibly deducts a particular person’s share from the total, that person is still treated as acting for all when the remission resulted from his request. Only a remission initiated by the king belongs exclusively to him. Maharam maintains that whenever the king expressly identifies the individual whose share is remitted, the remission is his even if he requested it, provided the king’s overall settlement with the community had already been determined. Before the settlement, we cannot know that the king actually reduced the total by that person’s share; perhaps he merely shifted the burden to the others. The person therefore may not secure relief for himself at their expense. When the ruler independently exempts him before the assessment is fixed, Maharam records two versions: the first exempts him and the second holds him liable. The Rama cites Rabbeinu Simcha first and then, as “some say,” Maharam’s latter version. The Maharshal rules like Maharam’s first version. Since a partner may divide without his fellow’s consent to avoid a loss, as stated later in siman 181, all the more so here, where the king himself separated them before the settlement. This appears correct to me. The law in the preceding seif concerning an idle person likewise supports Maharam’s first version. Nimukei Yosef rules that before a tax is fixed, the king may exempt such a person, and the Rashbash rules that one whom the officials omitted is exempt. We do not say that he may not lighten his own burden by increasing that of others. Evidently, an explicit exemption initiated by the ruler before the tax is fixed is effective. The Sema attempts to reconcile the law of the idle person with the contrary opinion cited here, but his distinction is difficult, for under that opinion there should be no difference between an idle person and one whom the ruler forgot. The matter is therefore as I have explained.
