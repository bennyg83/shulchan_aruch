Tzemach Tzedek, siman 2, discusses a community whose longstanding practice was that acceptance of a rav, chazzan, and shamash, and selection of communal officers, gabbaim, and dayanim, required the participation of all taxpayers. Some prominent citizens wished to change the system so that only persons of wealth, substantial tax contribution, or recognized Torah standing would have a vote. They argued that most communal business concerns money and that the opinion of a poor man should not equal that of a wealthy man, nor that of an unlearned man the opinion of a scholar. The poorer taxpayers protested that they too bear the communal burden, and the small sum they pay may be harder for them than the wealthy man's larger payment, and that the old custom gave them equal participation. Tzemach Tzedek rules that the poor are correct. The Rosh's statement that in monetary levies one follows the majority of wealth means, as the Sema explains, that a poor numerical majority cannot impose matters on the wealthy minority without their consent. It does not mean that a few wealthy people are themselves deemed a numerical majority entitled to decree upon most of the community. Rather, the two majorities, majority of persons and majority of wealth, balance one another. Thus many communities properly require both rov minyan and rov binyan, a majority by number and a majority by financial contribution. To exclude the poor entirely is improper, and their lack of scholarship is also not a sufficient ground. Nor can one prove anything from another city's practice, which may have been established there by the agreement of all taxpayers. The proper course is for a competent rav to arrange a peaceful regulation accepted by all, or at least by the appropriate majority of all taxpayers. In siman 1 he discusses a city of fifty taxpayers, five of whom, one man, his two sons, and his two sons-in-law, paid three fifths of the total assessment while the remaining forty-five paid only two fifths. A regulation required both majority by number and majority by wealth for appointing a rav, chazzan, or shamash. The public argued that the five relatives could always block everyone else because they controlled the majority of the wealth. Tzemach Tzedek explains that the regulation is sound in principle, but the calculation must be made according to the manner in which that particular expense is actually collected. Since the salaries of the rav, chazzan, and shamash were collected half per capita and half according to wealth, the forty-five householders in fact carried the majority of the total cost and therefore possessed both rov minyan and rov binyan. They could appoint an officer despite the five wealthy relatives' objection. His son adds that even if a rav or chazzan were willing to serve without salary, one cannot simply say that the wealthy taxpayers' consent is irrelevant, because there remain broader communal and religious interests in the appointment, including the principle that communal worship and Torah teaching should be by a representative accepted by the community. Tzemach Tzedek, siman 16, also considers a community that voted for three rabbinic candidates in ranked order: the first if he came, the second if the first did not come, and the third if neither of the first two came. The first candidate arrived, remained only five days, and left. Some argued that the community must now turn to the second candidate under the prior resolution, while others contended that the first candidate's arrival exhausted the original arrangement. He rules that the original resolution ended when the first candidate actually came. The agreement was conditioned on his coming, not on how long he would remain. Once the condition occurred, the contingent obligation toward the second candidate ceased and does not revive merely because the first later departed. He compares this to a kinyan whose obligation lapses once the specified contingency no longer applies. The community may therefore make a fresh choice of rav.
