Even if it is known that he consumed them only by his own admission, etc. The reason is that he did not claim that he purchased the field such that his consumption would serve as evidence for his claim. Rather, he claims that he inherited the land, and the land is removed from his possession by these witnesses. Since he has no migo against them, he must consequently also pay for the produce. This is the Sema's language, based on Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri. Ramban's Chiddushim appears to disagree; see there. It is also possible to explain the Tur and the Mechaber as referring to a case that is uncertain even to the claimant, following Ramban's explanation, in which case there is no migo here. Examine carefully. However, below in siman 145, seif 3, the Tur's view appears to follow Tosafot. The Rivash, responsum 336, appears to follow Ramban, as does Nimukei Yosef. This also appears from Rashbam on 33b, s.v. Amar Rav Nachman, and on 34a, s.v. Ela i dami, and likewise from Baal HaMaor. This appears primary. See Tosafot and examine carefully, and see below siman 396 and the Semag.
Whoever is stronger prevails. See Sema, seif katan 10, through his statement that the objection of the Ir Shushan is thereby removed. The Sema improperly attacks the Ir Shushan, for the Ir Shushan's objection is correct, and he knew the source in Terumat HaDeshen. Terumat HaDeshen writes that no proof can be brought from the Gemara in the chapter HaNizakin, because there no other arrangement was possible. If beit din were to say that the item should be held by beit din until the matter was clarified, who could gather the water in his hands? If they said that it should remain in place and no one should touch it, the water would flow away and be lost, or others would come and take it, and it could not be guarded. They therefore found no remedy except that whoever is stronger prevails. This does not apply to other matters in which the authorities disagree, because the object can be held by beit din, and beit din can sell it and divide the proceeds, or apply the rule of gud o igud. At the beginning of this siman we apply whoever is stronger prevails only because the true owner will exert himself for what is his. That rationale applies only when the litigant himself knows the truth and beit din does not. Here, however, why should this litigant exert himself more than the other? The law is no clearer to the litigant than to the judge or to his opponent. This is what the Ir Shushan writes here and at the end of siman 170, and his words are very correct. This also resolves the explanation of Maharam's ruling in the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Batra, because division was impossible there. Rashbam's explanation of the case of the ship is also well understood. He writes that we rule whoever is stronger prevails because witnesses may later come and establish ownership, making beit din's earlier ruling of division overturned and contradicted. Therefore beit din does not intervene and whoever is stronger prevails. The Sema's reference there to the Mordechai in Bava Metzia and at the beginning of HaMokher Et HaBayit is inaccurate; it appears only in the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Batra, and so too in Terumat HaDeshen. It follows that this rationale does not apply to an unresolved dispute among authorities, and beit din may properly intervene and rule that the property be divided. It is possible that Terumat HaDeshen and the Rama, who wrote that this applies where the rule of division is inapplicable, mean where division cannot in practice be carried out, as in the case at the end of siman 170 and Maharam's responsum there, and they were not precise in their wording. Examine carefully. All this concerns the initial ruling. But if one of them has already seized the property, we do not remove it from him. Even if he seized it from the other's possession, we do not remove it in a dispute among authorities. See my work Tekfo Kohen, where I discuss these laws at length.
