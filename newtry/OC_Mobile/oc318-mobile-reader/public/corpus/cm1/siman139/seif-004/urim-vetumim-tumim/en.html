Division is not applicable, rather kol d'alim gevar. The Shach in seif katan 6 writes that this refers to a case where physical division is impossible. No proof is needed, for Terumat HaDeshen says so explicitly. His proof is that where the truth can never be clarified, the Gemara does not apply kol d'alim gevar, while an unresolved dispute among authorities can never be factually clarified. The Shach offers another argument: the Rosh's reason for kol d'alim gevar is that the party who knows the truth will exert himself more strongly over his own property, whereas the false claimant will not. That rationale does not apply to a pure legal doubt among authorities. But according to the Shach, whenever both parties claim only uncertainty, kol d'alim gevar should likewise never apply. This is difficult. The Gemara challenges from the case of one who exchanges a cow for a donkey, and according to one opinion that case involves uncertain claims on both sides. If kol d'alim gevar does not apply to uncertain claims, there would be no difficulty. Nor can one say the challenge follows only the view that Sumchus applies even where both claim certainty, because the Gemara should then have said, This works according to one view. Moreover, Rif and Rosh explain the cow and donkey case as one of uncertainty on both sides and apparently regard that as the principal reading, yet the Gemara's unqualified challenge would then be inexplicable if kol d'alim gevar required certain claims. The Tur likewise lists cases where kol d'alim gevar does not apply but does not say that uncertain claims are excluded when neither party has possession or a prior ownership presumption. It is therefore clear that kol d'alim gevar can apply even to uncertain claims. The rationale is that where the truth can ultimately be clarified, even a person who presently lacks certainty will hesitate before violently seizing property because tomorrow the facts may become known and his seizure exposed as wrongful. He will first investigate, and only once the surrounding circumstances and strong indications lead him to believe the property is truly his will he exert himself to seize it. The Sages deliberately preferred this mechanism as one likely to bring the property to the person who has the stronger factual basis. Thus there is no difficulty. More precisely, the Rosh needed this special rationale only where the truth can be clarified and division is physically possible, yet the law still prefers kol d'alim gevar over division because the seizure mechanism may lead to the truth. Where the truth cannot ever be clarified and division is possible, the Gemara explicitly rules that the parties divide, not kol d'alim gevar. Where the truth cannot be clarified and division is impossible, however, what alternative is there? Kol d'alim gevar may still be used from necessity. Terumat HaDeshen says this explicitly, and the Shach's lengthy discussion is therefore unnecessary. The Mishneh LaMelech on Rambam, chapter 15 of Hilchot To'en, cites Rambam in Hilchot Arakhin chapter 4 concerning a woman who consecrated an ancestral field, after which her husband redeemed it from hekdesh, and the Jubilee arrived while it remained in his possession. Rambam writes that it is doubtful whether it returns to the woman or goes to the kohanim. Therefore, if the woman first takes possession, the kohanim cannot remove it from her, and if the kohanim first take possession, she cannot remove it from them. Rambam does not order division. This appears to show that even in a pure legal doubt Rambam applies a form of kol d'alim gevar. We must then say that Rambam understands the Gemara's question from two documents not as directed against Rav, who says divide, because according to Rav division itself may reflect the truth since he follows Rabbi Meir and both acquisitions may take effect simultaneously. The question is only against Shmuel, who says shuda d'dayni rather than kol d'alim gevar. The Gemara's distinction between matters that can and cannot be clarified therefore limits only the preference between shuda and kol d'alim gevar, not between division and kol d'alim gevar. According to this, Terumat HaDeshen's ruling is itself disputed. One must still reconcile Rambam's ruling in the unresolved question in HaMafkid whether a custodian acquires the double payment, where Rambam follows Rif and says divide when neither is in possession. Perhaps Rambam agrees with Tosafot in Bava Batra 34 that kol d'alim gevar is not used while property remains in the hands of a third party. There the double payment remains with the thief, effectively a third party. Further study is required.
