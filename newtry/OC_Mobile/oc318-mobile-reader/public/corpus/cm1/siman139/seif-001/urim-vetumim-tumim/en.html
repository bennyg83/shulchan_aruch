Even if his hand later becomes stronger, etc. The Shach in seif katan 2 cites Tosafot in Bava Metzia concerning the disputed bathhouse, who write explicitly that under the rule kol d'alim gevar, if the other litigant later overpowers the first and removes it from him, the second acquires it. Hagahot Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot write likewise. It is difficult for me to say that the Rosh would rule otherwise without noting that Tosafot disagree. It appears that kol d'alim gevar can rest on two different ideas. The first is simple possession: since we do not know to whom the property should be given, if one party seizes it he becomes the possessor, and the burden of proof rests upon anyone seeking to remove it from him. This resembles other unresolved legal doubts where seizure is effective if the first party has no prior ownership presumption. According to that rationale, if the second party later seizes it from the first, we likewise do not remove it from him, because he is now the possessor. This is how Rambam, Mechaber, and Nimukei Yosef rule above regarding the unresolved question where one was initially silent and later protested: so long as the first possessor holds it, he prevails, but if the other later seizes it, the prior possession is lost and the uncertainty returns. The Rosh, however, gives another rationale for kol d'alim gevar. The Sages deliberately established this rule because ordinarily the one who knows the truth is his will exert himself more strongly over his own property, whereas the false claimant will not. The Rosh explains this at length. According to that rationale, once the first party seized, a later seizure by the second should not help. Since the Sages intentionally relied on the first successful seizure as an evidentiary proxy, they would not want the parties to remain in endless conflict. The Rosh says this almost explicitly. Now, if we assume at the initial stage that merely consecrating the property is equivalent to physical seizure, that premise itself cannot fit the Rosh's rationale. What self sacrifice or physical assertion is shown by consecration? A few words of declaration accomplish it. Therefore, at that initial stage, the rule must rest not on the Rosh's special rationale but on ordinary possession, with consecration treated as placing the property in the possession of hekdesh through the principle that one's statement to consecrated property is like delivery. Under that approach, of course, later seizure by the other party would be effective. But once the Gemara concludes that mere consecration is ineffective, we can return to the Rosh's rationale for physical seizure, and then the second party's later seizure is ineffective after the first seized lawfully. This initially seems correct. There is, however, a further issue. The commentators already note an apparent contradiction within Tosafot. In one passage Tosafot explain the bathhouse case as one where one party consecrated and the other was silent, so the uncertainty concerned whether silence amounted to admission. In the following passage Tosafot give a different explanation: the uncertainty was whether consecration itself has the force of physical seizure under kol d'alim gevar, since once a person first seizes and then consecrates, the other can no longer seize it, whereas without consecration the other could. It therefore appears that Tosafot found Rashi's explanation of the parallel case of a kohen seizing a doubtful firstborn extremely difficult. Rashi treats the owner's silence as admission, but a doubtful firstborn is a case where even the owner himself does not know the truth. What admission can his silence represent? The difficulty is obvious. If the bathhouse question concerned silence as admission, it would have no connection to the case of the doubtful firstborn and the kohen's seizure. But if Rabbi Hamnuna holds that when a kohen seizes a doubtful firstborn we do not remove it from him despite the owner's prior possession, then certainly the Rosh's special rule that the first litigant's seizure is final cannot apply, for the first litigant cannot be stronger than an original owner who had a genuine prior ownership presumption. The Rosh's explanation that the one who seizes probably knows the truth is only an additional supporting consideration. The primary force is that the Sages legally allowed seizure. Even so, a first successful seizer cannot have greater standing than an original owner. Accordingly, for Rabbi Hamnuna, who holds that seizure by the kohen is effective, the bathhouse problem must be whether consecration itself counts as seizure, not whether silence is admission. Tosafot's second explanation is therefore directed specifically to Rabbi Hamnuna. They note that hekdesh may be stronger than ordinary seizure because after one party seizes and consecrates, the other cannot seize it back, whereas if seizure by a kohen is effective, ordinary possession may still be displaced by later seizure. But according to Rabbah's conclusion that seizure by the kohen does not help, the Rosh's rationale becomes possible: the Sages can strengthen the first litigant's lawful seizure as though it created a prior ownership presumption, so that a second seizure does not displace it. Under that conclusion the bathhouse uncertainty can once again be explained as Tosafot first said, namely whether silence in response to consecration amounts to admission. This resolves the apparent contradiction between the two Tosafot passages. It also explains why Tosafot place their discussion of the second explanation specifically at the Gemara's comparison to a kohen's seizure rather than earlier, where the bathhouse itself was introduced. The second explanation is needed only according to Rabbi Hamnuna, while the ordinary conclusion follows the first explanation. Thus Tosafot and the Rosh can be reconciled. It is true that Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot do not seem to read them this way, but in my view the analysis itself indicates that this was the Rosh's intention. According to the Rosh, who holds that post uncertainty seizure generally does not help, his rule that the second party cannot later seize is consistent. According to the Mechaber, however, who holds that seizure does help in unresolved monetary law, this special finality of the first seizure cannot be maintained. Sefer Tekfo Kohen, law 19, section 8, records that one authority understood the matter this way, and in my view he was correct. Thus the Mechaber cannot adopt the Rosh's special rule here. These points appear clear.
