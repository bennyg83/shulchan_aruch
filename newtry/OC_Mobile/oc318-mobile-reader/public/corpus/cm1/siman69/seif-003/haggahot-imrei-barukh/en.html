(Shach, same passage) Therefore he does not collect from encumbered property, since the guardian can claim, etc. Note: This requires study. If so, the Gemara in Shevuot 45b becomes difficult. It asks why the Torah needed to impose the guardians' oath, and answers that the depositor could have delivered the item by written document, implying that a depositor who deposits by document must be repaid before witnesses. Tosafot explain that if the guardian wishes exemption without an oath he must return before witnesses. But if the guardian remains exempt upon taking an oath, then there should be no lien on land, and the argument "why is your document in my hand" should not apply. He should therefore be believed by Torah law to say "I returned it" without any oath, and then he would have a migo to claim unavoidable accident. The Gemara's question would return. More fundamentally, the Rashba's intent seems plainly to be that even apart from the claim of return there is no lien in a deposit document. So long as the deposit exists, there is no monetary obligation upon the guardian; even when it is lost, liability begins only at the time of the loss, not at the time of receiving the deposit. I found the same implication in Responsa Maharit, Yoreh Deah, siman 21. I later found in the special responsa, siman 76, that the Rashba first gives the proof cited by the Shach and then adds that even according to us, who hold that the guardian can say "I returned it," that is only because he could have claimed unavoidable accident, which requires a severe oath. If every document that cannot be collected from encumbered property allowed a simple claim of payment, then here too "I returned it" should be believed without an oath. This explicitly follows the earlier analysis. Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Batra 70a, passage beginning "let him say to him," cites the Ran that Rav Amram initially understood Rav Chisda's statement that the guardian is believed to mean believed even without an oath, because the principle "why is your document in my hand" applies only to a document that cannot be evaded through a claim of accident, and a document that does not collect from purchasers is not guarded so carefully. Rav Amram challenged that even so a person does not ordinarily leave his document in another's hand. Rav Chisda therefore explained that belief rests on a migo, and the guardian is believed only with an oath, just as he would have sworn had he claimed unavoidable accident. This explicitly shows that even a document not collectible from purchasers is not ordinarily left in another's possession, and that the guardian is believed only because he has the migo of unavoidable accident.
