Even if the debtor's handwriting has been authenticated by beit din, the document has only the law of an oral loan established by witnesses. It cannot be collected from heirs or purchasers, but only from the debtor himself if he admits that he did not pay. If he claims, "I paid," he is believed, takes a heset oath, and is exempt. He cannot be answered, "Why is your document still in my hand?" because he is not concerned about leaving it with the creditor since it is not a full promissory note. If he admits part of the debt, he takes a Torah oath on the remainder. If he denies that it is his handwriting and the handwriting was authenticated by beit din or witnesses testify that it is his, he is established as one who falsely denied and must pay. If it was not authenticated, he takes a heset oath and is exempt. Rama: Some say that one cannot claim "I paid" against an obligation in his own handwriting. A judge has only what his eyes see in deciding this matter. If he says, "It is true that this is my signature, but I never signed an admission of a loan. I forgot and signed my name at the bottom of a blank sheet, and perhaps this person found it and wrote the admission above my signature," Rama: this claim is effective only if he claims definitely that he never signed his name on such an admission. A doubtful claim does not suffice. Alternatively, if he claims, "This admission was merely a trust document between us, but I never actually borrowed this loan," he is believed with a heset oath through the migo that he could have said, "I paid." If the document contains a clause of trustworthiness and his signature is authenticated, he is not believed to say, "I paid," because had he paid he would not have left the document in the creditor's hands when it contains such a clause. If his signature is not authenticated and he claims, "I paid," he is believed with a heset oath. See below, siman 126, seifim 13 and 14, regarding whether one is believed to say, "I erred," through a migo that he could have said, "I paid."
