Some say he cannot claim payment. Rashba proves that one cannot claim payment against a deposit document merely because it does not collect from encumbered property. The Shach rejects the proof, saying that the deposit document does not collect from purchasers precisely because the custodian can claim return through a migo of unavoidable accident. Without that migo, it might collect from encumbered property. But Rashba's proof is stronger than that. The Gemara says the custodian is believed to say, "I returned it," only through the migo of unavoidable accident, and even then he must swear. If the inability to collect from purchasers itself transformed "I returned it" into an independent claim, then once we know the document does not collect from encumbered property, the custodian should be believed to say return even without a migo and without the severe oath. Yet he is not. This shows that an instrument can fail against purchasers and still retain enough documentary force that the possessor does not ordinarily leave it after payment or return. There is also a further point. According to the Shach in siman 108, beit din advances for heirs even an uncommon claim of unavoidable accident. If so, deposit documents would never collect from heirs or purchasers independently of the custodian's personal migo. Yet the original custodian himself would still need the migo and oath to claim return. Again this proves that lack of collection from encumbered property does not by itself make return an ordinary unsupported defense. The Shach later distinguishes formal documents from personal handwriting and this distinction is correct. A true document, even if for technical reasons it does not collect from purchasers, is not ordinarily left in the creditor's hands after payment. Rivash, responsum 478, makes this distinction explicitly. The Raavad cited as saying otherwise regarding a gentile court document may therefore represent a different authority, or a different reading of the case.

A credibility clause is written in it. The Tur, citing Baal HaTerumot, implies that such a clause is effective without kinyan, even when added after the loan. In siman 71 the Tur appears to require a kinyan if the credibility was given after the loan. The Shach, following Bach, distinguishes ordinary credibility from credibility equal to two witnesses. The Tur's language does not support that distinction. The correct explanation is that here the credibility is incorporated into a written instrument. The document itself creates the obligation just as a kinyan would. The requirement of kinyan in siman 71 concerns an oral grant of credibility made after the loan.
