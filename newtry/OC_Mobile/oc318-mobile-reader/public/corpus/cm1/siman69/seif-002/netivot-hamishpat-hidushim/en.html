If he claims, "I repaid," he is believed. Even if the handwriting was previously authenticated and beit din already read the document, he may still say that he later repaid it. Likewise, the date written in a handwritten obligation proves nothing for purposes of priority, because perhaps the writer backdated it as part of a collusive scheme. Even seizure does not help. See Beurim. Regarding the negotiable bills commonly used today, Urim VeTumim holds that one cannot claim payment. Therefore, under the enactment concerning a fleeing debtor, those bills are treated like ordinary debt documents, but only where there is no concern that the date was advanced. For example, if witnesses saw the bill in the creditor's possession before the debtor fled, that concern is absent. Otherwise, perhaps the debtor gave him the handwritten bill only after fleeing and wrote an earlier date. With other handwritten obligations not drafted in the form of such a negotiable bill, even a credibility clause does not help against a later creditor. A document of trust. The Sema cites a responsum of the Rashba concerning one who produced his fellow's handwriting. The writer is not believed to claim that it fell from him or that he wrote it merely for practice, because we do not ordinarily suspect loss, and a person does not write something merely for practice in a manner that exposes himself to monetary loss. The Shach objects that the Rashba follows his own opinion that a person cannot claim payment against his handwriting. According to our ruling that he may claim payment, he should likewise be believed to claim loss or practice through a migo that he could have claimed payment. See Beurim. This applies specifically where the body of the obligation is written in another person's handwriting and the debtor merely signed it. He may then claim that the signature was written for practice and later lost. But where the entire obligation is in the borrower's own handwriting, we do not suspect that it fell from the borrower, and all agree that such loss is not ordinarily suspected. Nor do we assume that a person writes an entire debt obligation against himself merely for practice. Even with a migo he is not believed on those claims. He is, however, believed to claim that it was an accommodation document through a migo. The same applies to an oral admission before witnesses. He may say, "I admitted merely as an accommodation," through a migo that he could have claimed payment. See Beurim. With our ordinary iska document, he is not believed to claim that the principal was lost in the business even if he has a migo that he could have claimed payment or denied the event. And if it contains a credibility clause. Even an ordinary credibility clause makes the creditor believed without an oath. If it says merely that the borrower shall not be believed, without expressly granting credibility to the lender, or according to the authority who holds that a borrower cannot claim payment against handwriting even without a credibility clause, then in the present case the borrower is believed without an oath. According to our ruling that a borrower may ordinarily claim payment against his handwriting, however, when the writing says that the borrower shall not be believed, the lender must swear before collecting. He must swear even if the borrower does not expressly demand an oath, and in this respect handwriting is weaker than a formal debt document. Whether a kinyan is required for the credibility clause will be explained in siman 71, Sema and Shach. If his signature was authenticated. If one produces merely a blank signature with nothing written above it, see the Shach. Strictly according to law, nothing can be collected with such a signature. The custom, however, is to give it all the force of a debt document containing a credibility clause. It appears that even if two people signed it, so that technically it is a document susceptible to fabrication because someone could write an obligation against another person above the signatures and use the two signers as witnesses, a document susceptible to fabrication that contains a credibility clause still prevents a claim of payment, as explained in siman 45. It therefore is no worse than an oral loan supported by a credibility clause. The Sema further writes in siman 48 concerning negotiable handwritten instruments that where the signature is on one side and the amount is written on the other, the amount should be written in a manner that prevents cutting away the writing above it, with additional words written across or adjacent to the signature. Otherwise it would be a document susceptible to fabrication and invalid. This applies where additional witnesses sign after the debtor's signature to attest to the substance, creating a formal document susceptible to fabrication. If there is only the debtor's own signature, it is not invalid merely because he himself failed to protect against fabrication, since a person may choose not to guard himself against his own exposure. See Beurim. Where he gives a blank signature, it is forbidden to write above it anything beyond the standard favorable documentary formulas customarily used.
