It was authenticated. Rav Sherira Gaon wrote in a responsum that a handwritten acknowledgment is not authenticated by comparison to two other documents or two ketubot, because document authentication is rabbinic, and the leniencies stated by the Sages apply only where they were specifically stated. Baal HaTerumot and the Tur conclude that there appears to be no distinction between authentication here and other forms of authentication. The Beit Yosef expresses surprise, arguing that since biblical law does not require authentication, one should be lenient. The Shach explains that there is no difficulty: Rav Sherira means that, biblically, signed witnesses are regarded as though their testimony had already been examined, and because the need for authentication is only rabbinic, the Sages were lenient to authenticate from two documents. That rationale applies to a signed document, but a handwritten obligation requires authentication by Torah law because the debtor obligates himself and the rule that signed witnesses are regarded as examined does not apply. The Bach explains similarly. From the Rif, Rosh, Rambam, and Semag it appears that authentication of handwriting has the same laws as authentication of a document. Nevertheless, in all cases where we rely specifically on the rabbinic leniency of document authentication, such as authentication outside the litigant's presence or testimony by one who recognizes a signature from when he was a minor, we do not apply that leniency to handwriting. When Baal HaTerumot and the Tur say there is no distinction, they refer to authentication from two documents, where the Gemara did not expressly state that the reason is the rabbinic nature of authentication. This is unlike Responsa Maharashdam, siman 146. Even if one says there is a dispute here too, the primary ruling appears to follow Rav Sherira Gaon, for it is well known that one does not disagree with a Gaon without clear proof, particularly here where his reasoning is persuasive. Shach.
A proven denier. The Shach writes that this appears to apply even if he does not say, 'I never borrowed,' but merely claims, 'This is not my handwriting,' for that is treated as though he said, 'I never borrowed,' and he becomes established as a proven denier. This makes the Rama difficult, for here he rules without qualification like the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, while at the beginning of siman 82 he cites two views and does not decide. Perhaps the Tur and Shulchan Aruch mean that he denied the loan itself and said, 'I did not borrow, and this is not my handwriting.' According to this, if he merely claims it is not his handwriting, he is not established as a proven denier according to the dissenting opinion at the beginning of siman 82. But if he says only, 'I did not borrow,' then certainly, once the writing is authenticated, he is established as a proven denier, as the Beit Yosef writes here in the name of the Tashbetz. See below siman 79, seif 7. The Baer Heitev adds that this interpretation of the Shach is itself difficult, since the Shach concludes that saying only 'I did not borrow' is enough. The Shach further cites Responsa Maharashdam, siman 6, that an heir who says, 'This is not my predecessor's handwriting,' is exempt, for perhaps he simply does not recognize the signature.
I paid. The Shach discusses this at length and cites many early and later authorities, virtually all of whom hold that one is believed to claim payment against a handwritten acknowledgment. He concludes that this is how one should rule. The plaintiff cannot even say 'I rely upon the authorities who disagree' if he seized property in the presence of witnesses, although such a claim is generally effective in other disputes among authorities. All the more so today, when the accepted custom is to rule that one may claim payment against handwriting. Even if the earlier custom was otherwise, present custom is decisive. Therefore no adverse inference is drawn from the fact that he did not demand return of his handwriting, for he may have relied on the prevailing law. This applies ordinarily, but if the dayan sees circumstances indicating that the debtor would not have left the handwriting in the creditor's possession had he paid, everything follows the dayan's assessment. The Taz writes that according to this, he is not believed to claim, 'I forgot,' through a migo that he could have claimed payment, because this is not a valid migo: he fears that the dayan may see reason not to believe a claim of payment. He must swear a shevuat hesset. We do not say that he can alter his signature before beit din, for since he can certainly employ such a ruse, he would indeed alter his handwriting. Baal HaTerumot writes this, and the later authorities cite it. The Shach further writes that where the handwriting contains a clause of credibility, the debtor does not take a Torah oath when admitting part of the claim. Even though there is no lien on land, because he cannot deny the writing it is treated as though the admitted amount is immediately tendered. See below siman 88, seif 29. See also Minorat Zechariah, siman 96, where these laws are discussed at length.
And perhaps. The Sema explains that the word 'perhaps' refers to the phrase, 'and this person found it.' The signer cannot know with certainty that this person found it, because perhaps someone else found it and gave it to him. At minimum, however, he must assert with certainty that he never signed his name for any debt, for otherwise the signature is certain and the loan is doubtful, and a doubt cannot displace a certainty. The Shach writes that in Ir Shushan, siman 48, it is stated that one who produces another person's blank signature with no text above it has nothing, and the signer may claim, 'It fell from me and you found it,' or 'I paid,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I never borrowed.' The Sema there disagrees because we ordinarily do not suspect loss. The Bach there and here agrees with Ir Shushan, and the Shach rules likewise. With what can the holder sue him, when nothing is written above the signature? The possibility that the holder could write whatever he wishes does not obligate the signer, because that would be a migo used to extract money. The Shach's teacher likewise proves this from the Gemara in Ketubot, which says perhaps one will find a signature and write whatever he wishes above it. If the blank signature itself imposed liability, there would be no need to speak of writing anything above it. This is the law in principle. The Bach writes, however, that in these lands there is an established enactment to collect upon such a signature according to local regulations. Where there is no known custom, the primary ruling is as stated. See further the laws of such handwritten notes in siman 48. Shach.
An accommodation document. The same applies to other claims. See Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 73. The Sema cites the Rashba that the signer cannot claim, 'I wrote it only in order to practice writing,' but this requires study. The Rashba follows his own view that one cannot claim payment or that the document was one of accommodation against handwriting. According to the Mechaber and our ruling, however, that one may claim payment or accommodation, he should likewise be able to say, 'I wrote it to practice,' or advance other claims through a migo that he could have claimed payment. Thus the Sema's citation of the Rashba as practical law requires study. Shach. The Rama did not gloss here, even though he holds that one cannot claim payment against handwriting. It is strained to say the case is one of unauthenticated handwriting, for then he would be believed through a migo that he could have claimed forgery. Perhaps the Rama relied on what he wrote above. See the Taz regarding a case in which one gave another money as an iska and stipulated that any loss not proven by witnesses would have to be paid from the recipient's own funds. The recipient later claimed a loss. The Taz ruled that his migo that he could have denied the transaction is effective to exempt him from the condition, so he need not prove the loss with witnesses, but must swear to it. He brought proof from the Rosh in chapter HaNishba'in in the name of Ri HaLevi. The Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses, challenged the proof and concluded that the practical law requires study.
Credibility. The Sema writes that even if the writing does not state that the lender is believed without an oath, he nevertheless collects without an oath, as in the law of a credibility clause in an oral loan established by witnesses, as explained at the beginning of siman 71. If the document does not say that the lender is believed but only that the borrower is not believed to claim payment, and the borrower later claims that he paid, the lender swears that he was not paid and collects. The Shach explains that this applies even if the borrower does not say, 'Swear to me,' unlike a formal debt document at the beginning of siman 82. The Sema further writes that according to the Rama, who holds that one cannot claim payment against any handwriting even without a credibility clause, language stating that the borrower will not be believed has the added effect that the lender collects without an oath. The same is true according to all opinions when such language appears in a witnessed document, as explained in siman 71. The Shach adds that no kinyan is required for an ordinary credibility clause, as written by Baal HaTerumot and the Tur. This implies even if the clause was granted after the loan. But where the lender is made credible like two witnesses, a kinyan is required if it was granted after the loan, as stated in siman 71, seif 2, and the Bach likewise writes.
