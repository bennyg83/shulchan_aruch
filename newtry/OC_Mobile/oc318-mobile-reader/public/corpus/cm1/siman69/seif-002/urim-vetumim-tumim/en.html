And not from purchasers. Sema writes that even if the borrower admits he did not pay, the handwritten note cannot collect from purchasers because it lacks publicity, and we also fear collusion. Shach asks what collusion is relevant. If we do not fear that a handwritten note was returned to the borrower and later somehow came back to the lender, why should this case differ from an ordinary formal note? Sema's point is simpler. Against purchasers, beit din itself raises the defense of payment. A handwritten obligation ordinarily allows the borrower to say "I paid." The borrower's present admission that he did not pay cannot harm purchasers who acquired property relying on the ordinary legal defenses. That is what Sema means by collusion: the borrower and lender may now cooperate and say the debt remains unpaid in order to collect from purchasers, even though the borrower may actually have paid. Shach himself applies this principle in many places: where a borrower has a valid payment defense against a type of instrument, purchasers can invoke that defense even if the borrower presently admits liability. Sema's additional collusion reason matters even beyond the obvious lack of publicity. For example, where an earlier handwritten debt competes with a later formal creditor over the borrower's free assets, the earlier handwritten creditor may ordinarily claim priority if the writing has been judicially established. The later formal creditor can nevertheless argue that perhaps the handwritten debt was already paid, and the borrower's admission cannot prejudice him. One might object from Bava Batra, where the Gemara discusses a handwritten note authenticated in beit din and asks why it should not collect from purchasers. If payment can always be pleaded, what was the question? The answer is that had judicial authentication itself created the publicity necessary for purchaser collection, the borrower would likewise have been expected to guard himself and recover the document upon payment. A document publicly established as a collectible lien is no longer treated like an ordinary private handwritten note. Especially according to Rosh's reason that a borrower sometimes simply forgets a private handwritten paper, once public judicial authentication gives it the force of a collectible document, that excuse weakens. Therefore the Gemara could ask whether authentication changes the instrument's status. Under our practical rule, however, a mere handwritten note does not acquire such purchaser-facing force, and purchasers may still plead payment despite the borrower's admission.
From purchasers. Suppose a borrower writes a handwritten obligation intending merely to borrow with it later, and afterward actually delivers it before valid delivery witnesses. The sugya in Bava Batra 151 indicates that it still does not collect from encumbered property. The Gemara distinguishes a handwritten note authenticated in beit din from a document delivered before witnesses by saying that in the latter case "from the time of writing he obligated himself." Rashbam explains that the document was originally written for the purpose of being delivered before witnesses and therefore became a formal instrument. But an ordinary handwritten paper was initially no more than an oral-loan memorial. Later judicial authentication cannot retroactively transform it into a purchaser-facing lien because it was not written for that purpose. That supports the distinction stated here. Shach objects based on Baal HaTerumot's reason that judicial authentication lacks publicity because judges may certify a signature without reading the underlying document. If that is the whole issue, says Shach, the Gemara's question from delivery witnesses is easy: delivery witnesses must know what they are witnessing, whereas judges authenticating a signature need not read the paper. Shach therefore interprets the Gemara's phrase "from the time of writing he obligated himself" merely as meaning the document was written in a setting where the witnesses were expected to read it. That interpretation is strained and does not fit the wording of the Gemara or the other commentators.
Therefore, Baal HaTerumot's point should be understood as applying after the Gemara's conclusion. Tosafot Ketubot 109 say that although judges normally may authenticate a signature without reading the document, where the legal consequence actually depends on the document's content, they must apply themselves and read it. Thus, at the stage where the Gemara entertained the possibility that judicial authentication would turn the handwritten paper into a formal note collectible from purchasers, the judges would have to read it. Their act would then create some publicity. After the Gemara concludes that authentication does not transform the instrument into a purchaser-facing note, the judges no longer need to inspect the underlying debt in the same way. Baal HaTerumot can therefore say that practical authentication lacks the publicity of a formal witnessed instrument. There is an additional consequence. Rashba infers from the rule that judges may authenticate without reading that relatives can sometimes participate in signature authentication because the process is only rabbinic and does not adjudicate the substance. But according to Shach and Bach, authentication of a handwritten obligation can be of Torah significance. If so, relatives should be disqualified and the judges may indeed need to know what they are validating. That weakens Baal HaTerumot's practical rationale in such a case. The broader point remains: later authentication does not ordinarily convert a paper that was not originally created as a formal witnessed obligation into one carrying purchaser-facing lien rights.
If it was authenticated, and so forth, or witnesses testify, and so forth. The wording implies that "authentication" here need not mean live witnesses who identify the handwriting. It can be accomplished by comparison with two established documents or the other methods of signature authentication described above in siman 46. This rejects R. Sherira Gaon's view that a handwritten obligation can be authenticated only through actual witnesses who testify that this is the debtor's handwriting, because authentication of a handwritten debt is of Torah force. Shach explains that even if R. Sherira is correct that the need for authentication is Torah-level, it does not follow that the ordinary methods of authentication are unavailable. Once halachah recognizes reliable document comparison, the same mechanism can prove handwriting even in a Torah matter. Still, R. Sherira likely derived his position from the sugya of a found loan document. Shmuel says that where a note with property warranty is found and the borrower does not admit it, it is not returned. The relevant denial is that the note is forged, because according to Shmuel the borrower cannot simply plead payment against a valid note. Tosafot ask: return the note. If the lender later succeeds in authenticating it, then the borrower's forgery claim was false; if he cannot authenticate it, he cannot collect anyway. Tosafot answer that a fallen document is uniquely weakened. Even if the signatures later resemble established signatures, perhaps the forgery was unusually skilled and the fall adds suspicion. R. Sherira may resolve the passage differently. Ordinary rabbinic authentication through comparison with other documents works only because a formal note ordinarily carries a Torah presumption against forgery. When a fallen document loses that presumption and the issue returns to a Torah-level need for proof, mere comparison is not enough. Live testimony identifying the handwriting is required. That would explain why returning the note could create an improper opportunity for later lower-grade authentication. This conceptual distinction also fits the debate whether handwritten obligations themselves require Torah-level authentication. Rama above allows a single rabbi to perform contemporary document authentication and treats common handwritten commercial instruments similarly, reflecting the practical view that the authentication framework is largely rabbinic. Therefore the matter depends significantly on the judge's assessment of the particular instrument and the strength of the evidence. Even R. Sherira's stringency should be limited to a case where the debtor expressly alleges forgery and the authenticity question is genuinely contested. If the debtor does not specifically claim forgery, we need not independently suspect elaborate falsification. In such a case ordinary document comparison should suffice even for a handwritten obligation. This also answers the attempted proof from vows, where Ramban allows reliance on a person's recognized handwriting to establish permission or authorization in a Torah matter. There no litigant is affirmatively claiming the writing was forged, so a lower level of authentication can be enough. Practical authentication therefore depends on the nature of the denial and the strength of the writing comparison.
And if he denied and said it is not his handwriting, and so forth. Shach asks from Rama below in siman 82. There Rama cites Rashba that if a defendant says a formal note is forged and it is later authenticated, he is not necessarily established as a liar. His statement can be understood as: "I will litigate only after you authenticate the note." Rashba himself said this regarding handwriting. Why then does Mechaber here appear to treat the denial as a binding falsehood once the handwriting is authenticated? The distinction is straightforward. With an ordinary formal note, the borrower cannot normally say "I paid" while the note remains in the lender's hand. If he believes he paid but lacks the ability to use that defense, he may challenge the creditor first: "Authenticate your document." Calling it "forged" can function as procedural shorthand for demanding authentication. Even if he later produces payment witnesses, one can say he did not know those witnesses were available when he first challenged the note. Therefore the forgery claim need not make him a confirmed liar. A handwritten obligation is different under Mechaber's ruling because the debtor is always believed to say "I paid." If he truly had paid, why would he need to fabricate or even strategically use a forgery claim and force the creditor to search for authentication? He could simply say "I paid" and be believed. His deliberate choice to say "this is not my handwriting" is therefore much more probative. Once the writing is authenticated, the false denial can establish him as a liar with respect to the obligation. Rashba himself could apply his more lenient procedural explanation to handwriting because Rashba holds, unlike Mechaber, that a debtor is not always believed to say "I paid" against a handwritten note. In Rashba's system, handwritten and formal notes are more similar. But once we rule against Rashba on the payment issue, the cases must be separated. That is why Rama cites Rashba's leniency below in the ordinary formal-note context but does not apply it here to the standard handwritten obligation. The distinction is clear and resolves the apparent contradiction.
And some say he cannot say, "I paid." Rashba brings a proof from the rule that one who deposits property under a written document is believed to say, "I returned it," only because he has a migo that he could have said, "It was lost through unavoidable circumstances." This implies that without the migo he would not be believed to say he returned it, even though the deposit document has no property warranty. Rashba applies the same reasoning to a handwritten debt. Shach rejects the proof. He says that if the depositary did not have the return-or-accident migo, then the written deposit document would itself create a lien capable of reaching purchasers. The only reason it does not do so in practice is that purchasers can raise the defenses of return or unavoidable loss. Thus the deposit case does not prove that every handwritten paper blocks a payment defense. That answer is persuasive. R. Gershon in Chiddushei Gershuni questions whether a deposit obligation can really create a purchaser-facing lien merely because the custodian lacks an accident defense. But Shach's position is supported by Bava Batra 70. The "judges of the exile," meaning Shmuel and Karna, rule that where a business-account document emerges against heirs, the claimant can recover the whole amount. Yet Shmuel ordinarily holds an oral loan does not collect from heirs. The business arrangement must therefore have become a written obligation against the estate once the usual defenses of return or loss are unavailable. If the property itself were still intact, there would be no issue of monetary collection from heirs. The case concerns an obligation to replace value, and nevertheless the document reaches the estate. This supports Shach: where the factual defenses are unavailable, the written deposit instrument can function like a formal debt. Still, Rashba's underlying concern deserves explanation. Why did Rashba not simply prove his rule from the ordinary statement in Bava Metzia that a note without property warranty still does not permit the borrower to say "I paid"? Probably because Rashba distinguishes a formal note from mere handwriting. When a borrower goes to the trouble of issuing a formal document, that conduct itself shows he did not trust the creditor-borrower relationship to remain informal. Therefore the borrower is expected to recover the note upon payment. Tosafot similarly distinguish between an ordinary witnessed loan and an informal admission when discussing whether payment before maturity is expected. A handwritten note may lack that same significance. Rashba therefore needed the deposit-document analogy, where the writing itself was created for evidentiary protection. Even there, however, the proof depends on how we understand the lien effect. Shach's objection therefore remains substantial.
To say, "I paid." Shach rules like the authorities who allow a debtor to say "I paid" against his own handwritten obligation, and adds that even if the creditor seized property in the presence of witnesses, the seizure must be returned. In his view the creditor cannot say kim li like the stricter authorities. That goes too far. Rosh himself, in responsum klal 67, says this is a dispute among major authorities and that if the creditor seized movables during the debtor's lifetime, the property is not removed from him. This plainly refers to seizure in the presence of witnesses. If the seizure were secret, the creditor could be believed even without the handwritten note through an ordinary migo. And after death, secret seizure can also create a migo in certain types of property. Thus Rosh clearly treats the dispute as substantial enough for possession to matter. Who can rule categorically against Rosh? The principal proof for the lenient side is not overwhelming. Tosafot Ketubot infer from the story where Ravina was told to obtain witnesses and a written document that a mere handwritten note was insufficient. But even that can be answered: perhaps the document should also have contained a credibility clause, or perhaps Ravina avoided personally writing close to Shabbat and therefore preferred witnesses who could record the transaction. There is, however, another question. After the Geonic enactment allowing collection from heirs' movables, why does Rosh require seizure specifically during the debtor's lifetime? If heirs' movables are now liable, seizure after death should also help. The answer appears from Mordechai in Perek Almanah Nizonet. Where a claim against heirs requires an oath before collection, even after the claimant swears the inherited assets remain presumptively in the heirs' possession. Seizure after death does not necessarily alter that presumption. R. Yoel and Maharam Mintz likewise treat post-death seizure from heirs as weaker even after the enactment exposing movables to creditor claims. Rosh therefore requires possession obtained during the debtor's lifetime, before the heir-facing oath and estate presumption arise. The practical custom regarding contemporary handwritten commercial obligations is also important. Jewish batei din generally do not allow a debtor to say "I paid" against the common transferable handwritten note used in commerce. Some explain this through dina demalkhuta dina. That is not the correct reason. Civil law generally protects much more than this specific instrument. If we imported every secular evidentiary rule, much of Choshen Mishpat would disappear. Dina demalkhuta applies only in its proper sphere, such as where the sovereign makes a particular official form exclusively controlling. The true reason is rooted in the instrument's commercial nature. The ordinary reason a debtor is believed to say "I paid" against simple handwriting is that he may not be meticulous about recovering a private paper that has no purchaser-facing effect. But a modern transferable handwritten obligation is specifically designed to pass from hand to hand. The debtor knows the creditor may transfer it to others, including people far away, and that a later holder may say, "I am not your original creditor. Your private receipt or oral payment claim does not bind me in the same way." A prudent debtor is therefore extremely careful to recover or cancel such an instrument upon payment. This restores the classic presumption "your document in my hand, what is it doing there?" Ramban in responsum siman 23 says explicitly that where royal or commercial law gives an instrument such force that the debtor knows it may later be enforced, he is presumed not to leave it outstanding after payment. That is not because foreign law replaces Torah law, but because the external legal reality changes the factual chazakah of ordinary human conduct. Thus the established beit din custom is well founded. Ordinary private handwriting and a widely transferable commercial instrument need not have the same payment-defense rule.
And it does not help, and so forth. The discussion concerns the warning not to sign a blank sheet. One might ask: why is such a signature dangerous? If someone later writes a debt above it, the signer can say, "I forgot that I signed a blank page and you found it." Or, under the lenient view of handwritten obligations, he can simply say, "I paid." Baal HaTerumot answers that beit din does not raise the unusual lost-signature claim for him automatically, and the signer himself may fail to make it. Rosh gives a similar answer regarding payment: the fraudulent holder may frame the case so that the signer denies the transaction entirely, becomes contradicted by his own signature, and loses the more useful defense. Later authorities ask a stronger question. Why not write a debt with a future due date above the signature? Then the signer cannot say "I paid" because the debt is still within its term, and the other unusual claim is also supported only through the payment migo. There is no difficulty because the original Talmudic warning is stated by Abaye, and Abaye holds in the relevant sugyot that a person may pay even within the term. Thus the within-term device does not defeat the payment defense under Abaye. A more serious question remains: the fraudster could write a strong credibility clause above the signature, eliminating the payment defense entirely. The answer is that the warning concerns a signature placed at the very top of the page. As Tosafot note, the precise rule is that one should not sign in a position leaving substantial writable space above. At the absolute top edge, it is difficult to fit a full professionally drafted clause. At most a fraudster may squeeze in a short statement such as "so-and-so borrowed one hundred from so-and-so." This connects with Ribam's view discussed above: a same-line debt at the very top may itself be too irregular to collect. The safest advice is therefore to sign only where essentially no usable document space remains above the signature. I later found Derishah making a similar point, though without fully developing the connection between the top-of-page requirement and the limited space available for a credibility clause. Thus the Talmudic warning remains practically meaningful.
If a credibility clause is written in it, and so forth. Shach cites Ir Shushan regarding a person who produces another's signature on an otherwise blank sheet and claims that the signer owed him money. Ir Shushan says the paper proves nothing, because the signer can say, "It fell from me and you found it." Sema disagrees, reasoning that we do not generally suspect loss. Shach sides with Ir Shushan for a different reason: there is no debt text on the paper at all. The claimant's only argument is that he could have written a debt above the signature. That is a migo used to extract money, which ordinarily does not work. This is correct. Below in siman 82, however, there are circumstances where a migo can extract against a defendant who only has a doubtful claim, or can preserve property already seized. Therefore if the signer says merely "I do not know," while the holder makes a definite claim, or if the holder already seized property in a manner that gives him possession, the blank signature may have greater evidentiary force. Still, we should not treat the blank signature as though it automatically contained a credibility clause. A waiver of the payment defense requires express language or an established undertaking. Nothing on the blank paper states such a waiver. Bach suggests the signer might say, "I forgot that I signed it." That is strained. A person is normally careful with a bare signature precisely because anyone can write above it. More important is the case where the holder actually writes the debt text above the signature before coming to beit din, and it is known that the writing was added after the signature. In my opinion, that document can be valid. By handing over his signed blank paper, the debtor trusted the recipient to fill in an appropriate obligation. This resembles the rule above concerning one who signs a document he cannot read: by signing, he relies on the other party's completion of the instrument. It also resembles the husband's statement "I gave it only as a deposit" where a trusted third party later says it was intended for divorce. The original party created the other person's authority to determine the document's use. Thus if the holder fills in the debt before litigation, the signer has effectively authorized the writing. This is all the more so according to Bach's own concern that the blank signature is dangerous because it is capable of later completion. A responsum of Shevut Yaakov holds that if it is visibly apparent the debt text was added after the signature, the paper is invalid even though the addition preceded litigation. I do not see a sound basis for that. The strongest objection would be the Tosafot rule that witnesses who sign before the document is written cannot later have a debt text placed above their signatures. But witnesses are different. They cannot truthfully testify in advance to whatever unknown content another person may later insert. That would be false testimony. A debtor, however, can obligate himself voluntarily to whatever amount or terms he authorizes the holder to write, just as a person can sign an unread undertaking. Thus witness signatures and debtor signatures are fundamentally different. There is a related rule where two people sign in a manner that allows the writing on the reverse or above to be altered. If the two signers can serve as witnesses against one another, then the resulting paper may indeed be a document susceptible to forgery and can be invalid. But where the only person exposed is the signer himself, and he knowingly handed over a format that can be completed, why should beit din protect him from the risk he voluntarily created? The Talmudic invalidity of an alterable document is designed to protect innocent third parties and borrowers from fraud by witnesses or holders. It is not necessarily a rule that a debtor may never authorize a flexible instrument against himself. Therefore the broad invalidation stated by some later authorities requires study. At minimum, where the debtor intentionally delivered the signed blank to the creditor for completion and the creditor filled it in before litigation, there is strong reason to enforce it.
