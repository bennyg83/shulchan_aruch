And not from purchasers. Because it has no publicity. If there is also a later loan evidenced by a signed document, although we rule that an earlier oral loan has priority over a later documented loan, one can nevertheless argue that a handwritten acknowledgment has no priority. The holder of the later document can say that perhaps the handwritten debt was paid. Even if the borrower admits that he did not pay it, he is not believed to say that it remains unpaid so as to impose liability upon others.
From purchasers. If he first wrote a handwritten acknowledgment and afterward decided to deliver it in the presence of witnesses, but at the time of writing he did not intend to create a document of that sort, see Tumim for what I derived from the Gemara, that it does not permit seizure from purchasers. See also above, siman 51, where in any event many authorities hold that the rule that witnesses to delivery effect validity does not apply to other monetary documents.
He is believed. Even if he claims that he paid after the handwriting was authenticated in beit din and the judges had read the writing at the time of authentication, he is nevertheless believed to say that he subsequently paid. So writes the Shach.
He cannot be challenged, "Why is your document in his possession?" etc. With a document signed by witnesses, even one without a property guarantee, or a document of a recognized non Jewish court, the debtor cannot claim payment, because it has the legal status of a document. So writes Rivash. Perhaps the reason is that since the debtor saw that the lender took the trouble to obtain a witnessed document, he understood that the lender did not trust him, and therefore the borrower likewise would not trust the lender to return it after payment without receiving it back. By contrast, a handwritten acknowledgment may have been taken merely as a reminder so that the lender not forget the loan, as the Gemara says that one who lends without witnesses brings a curse upon himself. The Rosh gives another reason: since a handwritten acknowledgment has no publicity, the debtor can say, "I forgot that I had given you this writing." With a witnessed document, however, even one that has no publicity of its own in the sense discussed above, the borrower can inquire of the witnesses and they will remind him that they wrote a document for the lender.
He swears a Torah oath. Whether a Torah oath can apply to a debt evidenced by a document, or even to an oral debt according to the opinion that a lien exists by Torah law, so that the claim concerns a lien on land, see what I wrote below in siman 87 at length.
If it was authenticated in beit din. See Tumim. I concluded that there are many opinions whether a handwritten acknowledgment may be authenticated by comparison with two other writings or two other documents, and likewise whether the other leniencies stated above in siman 46 apply. Since ordinary document authentication is rabbinic, those leniencies generally apply, but the Shach holds that authentication of a debtor's handwriting here is required by Torah law and therefore they do not. See Tumim, where I noted that Rav Sherira Gaon has proof from the Gemara. In practice everything depends upon the judge's careful assessment for the sake of Heaven. This dispute concerns a debtor who expressly claims forgery, for there is room to say that in such a case we do not rely on the usual leniencies or on mere comparison of handwriting. But if he does not expressly allege forgery, for example because he died or went abroad, all agree that we do not suspect forgery and may rely upon recognition and comparison of handwriting just as in ordinary document authentication, without distinction. See there.
He is established as one who falsely denied. See Tumim for how I reconciled this so that it does not conflict with siman 82.
And some say that he cannot say, etc. The Sema writes that Maharam ruled this way, while the Shach decides in accordance with the Mechaber. Accordingly, even if the creditor seized property in the presence of witnesses, we remove it from him. See Tumim. The principal view is as the Rosh wrote in a responsum: if the creditor seized during the debtor's lifetime, we do not remove it from him. If, however, he seized after the debtor's death from the heirs, the Rosh holds that seizure is ineffective. This is disputed among the great authorities. See below, siman 107.
And the judge should not, etc. Accordingly, where the prevailing custom today is to treat such handwriting as a formal obligation, the debtor cannot claim payment. See Tumim for the reason.
And it is possible that he found it, etc. Meaning that the one producing the writing may possibly have found it, while the alleged debtor claims with certainty that he never became obligated. If he does not claim with certainty that he never became obligated, he must pay, because a mere possibility cannot displace a definite obligation.
Or he claims that it was written as an accommodation, etc. The Sema cites a responsum of Rashba. Reuven produced against Shimon a handwritten account listing many maneh of merchandise that had belonged to their partnership and the amount of profit. Shimon replied that he had written it only in jest and as a casual exercise, and that it fell from him or was stolen and came into Reuven's possession. Rashba ruled that he is not believed. We do not suspect such loss or theft, and people do not ordinarily write such accounts as a joke. The Shach writes that Rashba follows his own view that one cannot claim payment against a handwritten acknowledgment, so Shimon has no migo. According to our ruling that he may claim payment, the Shach understands that he would have a migo. See what I wrote above in siman 50, Tumim seif katan 4, where I proved from Baal HaTerumot that regarding a loss that could have occurred only to one particular person we do not say a migo, because it is a migo against a powerful presumption. Here too the alleged loss is of that type, because the writing came specifically into the hands of Reuven, his partner, as the Shach himself notes. Therefore he is not believed by virtue of a migo, and the Sema's ruling is correct.
And if he wrote a credibility clause in it. The Sema writes that if he wrote generally that the lender is believed, even without expressly stating that the lender need not swear, the lender collects without an oath, because the debtor granted him credibility. But if he wrote only that the debtor shall not be believed to claim payment, the matter depends upon the dispute above. According to the opinion that payment may never be claimed against a handwritten acknowledgment, the credibility clause must add that the lender need not even swear. According to the opinion that the debtor normally may claim payment, the clause has the effect that he cannot make that claim, but the lender must still swear and collect. The Shach writes that we impose this oath even if the debtor did not specifically demand, "Swear to me," which makes this inferior to an ordinary documented debt, where the borrower must request the oath.
Credibility. No kinyan is required, even if it was granted after the time of the loan. So writes the Shach. See below, siman 71. If the debtor writes in his own handwriting that the writing shall have the status of a formal document, Rabbi Eliyahu ibn Chaim holds that this is equivalent to granting the lender credibility. The Shach disputes this, and his words are more reasonable, because the holder of a document has the lower hand in interpreting uncertain wording. It is possible, however, that if the writing expressly says that ambiguities shall be interpreted in favor of the holder, even the Shach would agree that it is effective, since the language can bear both meanings. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 79, in the gloss, who likewise treats the phrase, "You shall be believed like one hundred witnesses," as dependent upon interpretation of uncertain contractual language. This is certainly true here, and so is correct.
If it was authenticated. If the lender possesses the borrower's signature on a blank sheet and nothing is written above it, while the lender claims that a particular sum is owed to him and that the borrower granted him credibility but simply did not write it above the signature, see Tumim. I concluded like the Shach that he cannot collect on that basis even though he has a migo, because it is a migo used to extract money. Therefore, if he already seized the money, even in the presence of witnesses, or if the borrower does not assert a definite contrary claim, in which cases we do accept a migo to extract money as explained below in siman 82 in the rules of migo, the lender is believed. If, before coming to beit din, the amount of the debt and the credibility clause were written above the signature, then even if we know that the writing was added many days after the signature, I concluded in Tumim that since the lender comes to beit din holding a properly completed handwritten acknowledgment, collection is made with it. See there. If a person signed on one side of a sheet and wrote on the reverse that the handwriting on the reverse acknowledges a debt of a specified amount, the Sema and Shach write that the text must be positioned directly opposite the signature, or at most one line above or below it close to the signature, so that if the text were cut away and something else written above the signature, the forgery would be evident. Otherwise it is invalid, since one could cut away what was written on the reverse and write whatever he wished above the signature. See above, siman 48. I wrote in Tumim that it makes no difference even if forgery is physically possible. Since the borrower himself created the opportunity and knew what he was doing, he accepted that risk. The exception is where two people signed and are valid witnesses, because then the possibility of alteration affects third parties, since one could write above their signatures a document obligating others and use them as witnesses. See there.
To say, "I paid." The same rule means that the creditor may collect from encumbered property, because witnesses create publicity. So writes the Shach, and it is clear.
