(Seif 2: “I forgot and signed.”) The same applies to other explanations, such as: “I wrote it merely as a memorandum,” “I wrote it so that people would not think me wealthy,” “I kept it in my house, it was lost, and this person found it,” or “I wrote it merely as a specimen without intent to create an obligation.” He is believed in all these claims through the migo that he could have said, “I paid.” This is included in the Beit Yosef’s citation from Sefer HaTerumot: since he does not deny the original debt or his handwriting, he is believed through the migo available to him. There is no distinction between a writing that bears his signature and one that does not. The Beit Yosef cites many objections raised by Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi against the Tur, but none is compelling. The governing principle is that only while the debtor possesses a migo of payment may he claim that he forgot and signed. Thus the warning not to sign at the bottom of a scroll, lest another find it and write above the signature, remains necessary. The holder may first demand payment orally, before the signer knows that a written instrument exists. The signer may then deny the loan entirely. When the writing is later produced, he can no longer claim payment, because his denial established that no loan occurred and hence that he could not have repaid it. Certainly he can no longer claim that he forgot and signed, since that is a weak explanation accepted only through the migo of payment. Rabbi Mizrachi himself cites this principle from the Rosh and Mordechai in answering another question. The Tur’s present case is where the debtor immediately says both, “There was no loan,” and “I forgot and signed,” while a migo of payment is still available. If he first denied the transaction and only afterward saw the writing, a retroactive migo is not recognized. This also answers the objection from an oral acknowledgment before witnesses. A person who acknowledged owing one hundred may later explain that it was an accommodation acknowledgment, made because he trusted the other to lend him money later, provided he still has a migo of payment. We likewise find that one who does not authenticate a document may call it an accommodation document through the migo that it is forged. Siman 79, where one first admits and later denies the debt, concerns a bare denial that contradicts the witnesses and offers no explanation. An accommodation claim, by contrast, supplies an explanation and is accepted when supported by a migo. A claim that the acknowledgment was made merely to avoid appearing wealthy is different when made in the creditor’s presence, because it conflicts with the witnessed circumstances. The Beit Yosef reports that Rabbi Mizrachi later retracted his objections, perhaps for these reasons.
