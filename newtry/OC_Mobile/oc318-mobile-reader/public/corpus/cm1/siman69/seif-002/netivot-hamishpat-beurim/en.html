And not from purchasers. See Shach, seif katan 6, who questions the Sema: what concern of collusion is relevant here? At first glance the Shach's words are themselves difficult, because the debtor is believed to claim payment only where he admits the obligation. There would therefore seem to be a concern that debtor and creditor colluded, with the debtor admitting the handwriting in order to enable collection from purchasers. It appears that the Shach's intent is to question the Sema according to the Rama's view in the gloss, that the debtor is not believed to claim payment. The rule that one nevertheless cannot collect from purchasers is undisputed and accepted by all. Accordingly, the reason cannot be concern for collusion, and it must instead be that a handwritten obligation lacks publicity. The Shach's further objection, that elsewhere we do not suspect collusion unless the instrument was lost and thereby impaired, is directed only against the Sema's own position, for the Sema consistently holds that collusion is not suspected unless the document was lost and impaired, as explained above in siman 39, seif katan 29, and siman 79, seif katan 12. The Shach himself, however, holds generally that collusion may be suspected even when the document was not lost. His point is therefore only that the Sema contradicts himself. The Ketzot HaChoshen proves from Tosafot in Gittin 3a, who write that immediately after the writing she may seize the produce and claim that she had already been divorced as of the date written in the get, that seizure supported by witnesses is effective regarding a handwritten document to establish that it was written on its stated date. This proof is not compelling. Why should she be believed merely because of seizure? Moreover, even if seizure were effective, once the husband sold the produce we should raise on behalf of the purchasers the claim that the husband had acquired it from her, for even where one sells an item known to belong to another, we raise for the purchaser a claim that the seller had acquired it. Rather, Tosafot should be understood as follows. Sometimes the husband sells the produce of the land to purchasers through a kinyan sudar. After the get is written, the woman then seizes the produce and refuses to deliver it to the purchasers. Since she is now in possession, the burden of proof lies on the purchasers. This resembles two persons disputing an object, each claiming that it was sold to him first, where the possessor is certainly believed. The same applies here, because she is in possession.
If he claims that he paid. See Shach, seif katan 7, through his statement that judges sign their names even if they have not read the document. The Tumim asks that since the Shach writes in seif katan 12 that an authenticated handwritten obligation is effective d'oraisa, familial disqualification applies, and the judges must therefore read it to ensure that there is no issue of kinship. This is no difficulty, because they need read only the names of the creditor and debtor, not the amount or the substance of the obligation. Regarding the promissory notes known as vekslach that are commonly used, all batei din in the Jewish people have accepted the practice that the debtor cannot claim payment. This cannot be because of a ne'emanut clause written in the note. In proceedings under the enactments concerning an absconding debtor, they adjudicate on the basis of instruments that are treated as prior to an oral loan, even where the only formal element is authentication of the signature, although the debtor would ordinarily be believed to claim payment. A mere ne'emanut clause does not prevail against a later creditor, as explained in siman 71. The reason must therefore be as the Urim VeTumim writes in seif katan 5, that even without a ne'emanut clause the debtor is not believed to claim payment in such instruments.
And if he denies it and says that it is not his handwriting. See Shach, seif katan 10, who questions the Rama. Here the Rama rules simply in accordance with the Tur and Mechaber, whereas in siman 82 he cites two opinions and does not decide between them. This is no difficulty. Only with regard to a formal debt instrument, where the debtor would not be believed to claim payment, can we interpret his denial as not necessarily denying the underlying loan. He may merely be denying the instrument so that the debt should not carry the legal status of a loan in writing, thereby preventing collection from purchasers and preserving his ability to claim payment if the document cannot later be authenticated. By contrast, regarding a handwritten obligation, according to the opinion that even when authenticated the debtor is believed to claim payment, if he says that the writing is forged he plainly intends to deny the underlying loan itself. Otherwise there would be no practical purpose to denying the handwriting. The Tumim gives the same answer, and I independently reached this reasoning.

Or he claims that it was an amanah document. See Shach, seif katan 16, where he writes that above in siman 3 the Tur and Mechaber rule that one is believed to say, “It fell from another person and I found it.” I find this very difficult. On the contrary, that source proves the reverse. One may claim that it fell from another person specifically because the creditor's name is not written in the document. But he cannot claim, “It fell from me and you found it.” Otherwise, why did they not simply state the broader claim, “It fell from me,” which would work even when the creditor's name was written in the instrument? Their choice of the claim that it fell from someone else, which is useful only when the creditor's name is absent, shows that a claim that it fell from the debtor is ineffective. Moreover, according to Abaye, who in Bava Batra 173a is concerned even for the loss of an instrument by a single person and therefore challenges the rule that two persons of identical names may each collect instruments from others, why does the Gemara not raise the same concern regarding every instrument presented before us, namely that perhaps it fell from the debtor? It must be that there is no basis to claim that an executed loan instrument fell from the debtor. We apply a chazakah that immediately after an instrument is written it is delivered, which is why this concern is not raised regarding ordinary instruments, as Tosafot write in Gittin 18a. Maharam of Lublin likewise writes regarding a handwritten obligation that we presume it was delivered immediately upon being written. We are concerned only that it may have fallen from a different creditor when the creditor's name was omitted, or, according to Abaye in Bava Batra, even when the creditor's name was written if two persons share the same name. But everyone agrees that we do not suspect that it fell from the debtor. The Sema therefore correctly cites the Rashba that we do not suspect a loss from the creditor's hand in this manner. The rule allowing a person to say, “I signed my name at the bottom of a blank sheet and it fell from me,” applies specifically when the body of the document is in another person's handwriting and only the signature is his. He may then say that the signature was written for an unrelated purpose. Here, however, the entire text is in his own handwriting. We do not suspect that a person would write against himself, “I borrowed,” other than at the time of an actual loan, nor would he write such words merely as an exercise, since a person does not ordinarily create a writing that could cause him loss merely for practice. Likewise, we do not suspect that a complete loan instrument fell from the debtor after being fully written. A claim of amanah is different and may be believed by virtue of a migo. See Tumim, siman 46, who notes an apparent contradiction in the Sema. The Sema explains that when one says, “I wrote it in order to borrow but did not borrow,” he means that the writing fell from him, and he is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed forgery. Yet in Even HaEzer, siman 132, one is not believed to claim that a get fell from him by virtue of such a migo. The Tumim is correct. Even if we say that one may claim, “It fell from me,” with a migo of forgery, it nevertheless appears that he is not believed with a migo of payment. A claim of forgery is different, because he can say that he did not guard the writing carefully since it could not be authenticated, and he relied on being able to deny the loan and claim that the handwriting was forged. He could not similarly rely on a future claim of payment. The Rosh in Ketubot writes that perhaps an unscrupulous person would first sue him orally, and he would truthfully answer that the event never occurred because he would not imagine that this person had found his instrument. The claimant could then produce the handwritten obligation and establish him as one who falsely denied the debt. This concern does not apply to an unauthenticated writing where he would not become a proven denier. See further at the beginning of siman 46. There is another reason. A migo of forgery is a case where the document requires his admission that he wrote it, because absent that admission we ourselves raise the claim of forgery. It therefore has the force of ha'peh she'asar hu ha'peh she'hitir, which is stronger than an ordinary migo, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 34b. The poskim likewise explain that such a claim may be accepted even against a strong chazakah. The chazakah that a document is delivered immediately when written is exceedingly strong, since in matters of kiddushin and divorce we may even impose capital consequences on the basis of the presumption that delivery occurred on the date written. Nevertheless, because a migo of forgery here is really ha'peh she'asar, it is accepted even against that chazakah, just as Tosafot write that ha'peh she'asar may operate even in the face of witnesses. A migo of payment, however, is merely an ordinary migo, and we do not apply a migo against a chazakah. This also resolves the case in Even HaEzer, where one is not believed to claim that the get fell from him with a migo of forgery. In a get, when the husband does not assert forgery, we do not raise that claim on his behalf. Since he must affirmatively assert it, the claim is merely a migo and not ha'peh she'asar, and an ordinary migo is not accepted against a chazakah. See Taz, who writes that even regarding an oral acknowledgment made before witnesses, one may claim, “I acknowledged it only as an amanah,” with a migo that he could have claimed payment.
For had he paid it, he would not have left it in the creditor's possession. This proves the view of the Taz below in seif 5, that even ordinary ne'emanut allows collection from the heirs. The reason is that since a debtor would not ordinarily leave such a document in the creditor's hands after payment, it has the full legal force of an instrument. This is analogous to the opinions that hold that regarding any handwritten obligation the debtor is not believed to claim payment, according to whom collection may certainly be made even from heirs. The rule that ordinary ne'emanut does not operate against heirs concerns only the creditor's ability to collect without taking the oath ordinarily imposed on one who collects from heirs. Here, however, as to the debtor's inability to assert payment, even ordinary ne'emanut is effective, as the Taz writes there. According to the Sema and Shach there, by contrast, the Mechaber's reason that the debtor would not have left the writing in the creditor's hands is difficult. Even for an oral loan containing ne'emanut, where that reason does not apply, the creditor is believed. The primary ruling therefore follows the Taz. Indeed, even regarding a handwritten obligation without ne'emanut, the Rama rules that the debtor is not believed to claim payment where there is a compelling circumstantial presumption, and there is no stronger presumption than this one.
And if his signature was not authenticated. The Taz discusses an iska in which the parties stipulated that if the recipient could not prove that a loss was possible, he would have to pay. See the gloss there, where the ruling is that even though he has a migo, he must pay. At first glance this is difficult. What difference is there between a condition that if he cannot prove payment he must pay, regarding which it is nevertheless established in siman 71 that he is believed where he has a migo concerning the ne'emanut itself, and a condition that he will not be believed to say that the funds were lost through accident? In both cases it should follow that where he has a migo concerning the very matter of the ne'emanut, he should be believed. Yet all batei din in the Jewish people rule concerning our standard iska that the recipient is not believed to say that he suffered a business loss even where the agreement was oral and he has a migo that he could deny the transaction entirely. It therefore appears that Maharam's enactment regarding our standard iska was that so long as the iska dealings were not conducted in the presence of witnesses, in a manner that would have enabled the recipient to prove the loss through witnesses, he would be liable even for unavoidable loss. This is a condition defining the underlying liability, comparable to a shomer chinam who stipulates that he shall have the liability of a borrower. By contrast, where ordinary partners stipulate merely that one partner will not be believed to claim a loss, it appears that if he has a migo concerning the ne'emanut itself he is believed, just as in ne'emanut concerning payment. There is no distinction between the two. See Shach, who cites the law of a blank signature. The Tumim writes that it nevertheless does not have the legal force of ne'emanut because it requires a kinyan. I do not understand this, because ne'emanut stipulated at the time of the loan does not require a kinyan. It appears, however, that if a person delivers a blank signature on which the recipient is permitted to write only the standard favorable clauses customarily written in such instruments and no more, then if the recipient admits that he later wrote more than was permitted, the excess writing is void. This resembles the rule stated by the Shach in siman 41, seif katan 9.
