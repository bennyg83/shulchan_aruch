But not from purchasers. Meaning, even if the borrower admits that he did not pay, because such a handwritten instrument does not generate publicity, and we also suspect collusion, as the Tur and Mechaber explain below in siman 71.
If he admits to him partially, etc. This is specifically with the debtor's handwriting. But concerning a document signed by witnesses, a Torah oath is not imposed, as the Tur and Mechaber write below at the end of siman 88, seif 29. See the Tur there, seif 38, and my Perishah, Derishah, and Sema for the view of Tosafot, Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber that even with an oral loan no Torah oath of partial admission or one witness is imposed in such a situation; all the more so with handwriting according to them.
Or witnesses testify, etc. We do not say that beit din should inspect his signature and compare it, because since he can act deceptively he will surely alter his signature. Baal HaTerumot cites an opinion to this effect; see Darkei Moshe, seif 12.
That he cannot say, 'I paid,' etc. Rama rules accordingly. See Hagahot Darkei Moshe.
Rather, 'I forgot.' Meaning, I did not think about the possibility that harm could result from leaving the signed paper.
And perhaps this person found it. In Perishah I wrote that 'perhaps' modifies only 'this person found it.' The debtor cannot know with certainty that this particular claimant found it; perhaps someone else found it and gave it to him. But the debtor must assert with certainty, 'I never signed any such acknowledgment of a loan,' as Rama writes in the gloss. Otherwise the signature is certain and the loan uncertain, and a doubt cannot displace certainty.
The correct reading is: 'This claim is effective only if he asserts it with certainty, but if he claims,' etc. This refers to the Mechaber's statement, 'I never signed an acknowledgment of a loan.' He must make that assertion definitely.
Or he claims that it was an accommodation writing, etc. Beit Yosef, seif 5, cites Rashba: Reuven claimed against Shimon, 'Here is your handwriting recording how much you sold my merchandise for and how much I purchased, and it exceeds what you admitted.' Shimon answered, 'I wrote it only as a working memorandum, not as evidence for you. I did not sign my name, and it never came from my hand to yours. It was stolen, lost, or fell from me and you found it.' Rashba ruled that Shimon's claims are of no effect. We do not suspect that it fell, as stated at the end of chapter Get Pashut, and we likewise do not suspect theft. His claim that he wrote it merely for working purposes is also ineffective, because a person does not ordinarily make a memorandum in a way that causes himself a loss.
If it contains a credibility clause, etc. In Hagahot Darkei Moshe I wrote that even if the document does not expressly say that the lender is believed without an oath, it nevertheless appears that he collects without an oath, following the law of a credibility clause concerning an oral loan before witnesses, as the Mechaber writes at the beginning of siman 71. I explain the reason there. I also wrote that if it does not say the lender is believed but instead says that the borrower will not be believed to say, 'I paid,' and he later claims payment, then the lender swears that he was not paid and collects. According to the 'some say' cited by Rama above, that any handwritten obligation prevents the debtor from claiming payment even without any special clause, it appears that if the document expressly says the borrower will not be believed, the lender collects without an oath. The same is true according to everyone with a document signed by witnesses, as explained below in siman 71.
He is believed with a hesset oath. Although the writing says that the lender is trusted, the borrower can say that he paid and nevertheless left the document with the lender because he relied on the fact that the lender could not authenticate it without him. He therefore has a migo: he could have said that the document, including its credibility clause, was forged. So writes the Tur.
