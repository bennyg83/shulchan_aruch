But not from purchasers. A handwritten obligation does not collect from purchasers, even if the debtor admits it, because its primary deficiency is that it creates no public notice, not merely the concern for collusion.
But if he claims, "I paid," etc. The debtor is believed to say that he paid even after his handwriting was authenticated in beit din. The judges may have authenticated only the handwriting without reading the contents, and in any event such a writing creates no public notice. Even if the judges read it, it appears that he may still claim that he paid.
If he admits part of the claim, he takes a Torah oath, etc. See above, siman 88, seif 29. Where a handwritten obligation contains a credibility clause in favor of the creditor, the partial admission is considered like heilech, and there is no Torah oath of partial admission, even though the obligation does not create a lien on land.
He takes a Torah oath, etc. Where one witness signed the document, there is likewise no Torah oath of partial admission. The Sema cites Tosafos, the Rosh, and the Tur as holding that even an oral loan does not generate such an oath in this case, but in my view their words do not establish that conclusion.
If he denied it and said that it was not his handwriting, etc. The Rif, Rivash, Rosh, Rambam, and Semag discuss a case where he said both, "I did not borrow," and, "This is not my handwriting." One must therefore distinguish between a denial of the handwriting alone and a denial of the underlying loan. The Rashba, cited in siman 80, indicates that saying only, "This is not my handwriting," does not necessarily mean, "I never borrowed." The Tur and Mechaber omitted the additional words, which may imply otherwise. The Rama's ruling here also appears inconsistent with siman 82. Accordingly, if he said only that it was not his handwriting, under the dissenting view he is not necessarily established as a liar when the handwriting is later authenticated. If, however, he said only, "I did not borrow," then once his handwriting is authenticated he is certainly established as a liar.
That it is not his handwriting, etc. An heir who says that the writing is not his father's handwriting is exempt, because he may simply not recognize his father's signature. This is stated in the responsa of Maharashdam.
If it was authenticated in beit din. Baal HaTerumos and the Tur cite Rav Sherira Gaon that a person's handwriting cannot be authenticated by comparison with two other documents or two ketubot in the same manner as an ordinary document. The reason is that the leniencies governing rabbinic document authentication were not extended to a debtor's own handwriting, whose verification requires proof on the Torah level. The Beit Yosef questions this ruling, and the Bach explains Rav Sherira's position. It appears to me that Baal HaTerumos and the Tur disagree with Rav Sherira only regarding certain neutral methods of comparison, but they clearly agree that distinct rabbinic leniencies in document authentication do not apply to a handwritten obligation. Thus it may not be authenticated in the debtor's absence merely because ordinary documents may be authenticated in that manner; nor do the rules permitting one who recognized a signature in childhood to testify after reaching adulthood, or permitting a witness to become a judge, necessarily apply here. Even if one understands them as disagreeing more broadly, Rav Sherira's ruling is primary. See what I wrote in siman 46.
Or witnesses, etc. The writing presently shown in beit din cannot simply be compared with another sample of the debtor's handwriting, because he may intentionally alter his writing. This is stated by Baal HaTerumos.
Some say that he cannot, etc. The Sema understands the Rama to rule that the debtor cannot claim that he paid. In my view, however, the ruling of the Mechaber and the great majority of authorities is primary, namely that the debtor may claim payment against his own handwritten obligation. Such a writing creates no public notice, and a debtor may therefore pay and forget to retrieve it, or may not be careful to retrieve it. The Rashba questions this from the law of a deposit document, but there the document and the presence of witnesses create greater publicity. The Rosh and numerous responsa likewise support the debtor's ability to claim payment here. Consequently, if the creditor seized property in the presence of witnesses, he cannot retain it merely by asserting that he follows the minority view, because the majority rules that the debtor is believed. If he seized without witnesses and has a migo, however, his seizure is effective. Established custom also supports the debtor's claim of payment. Nevertheless, if the judge sees powerful circumstances showing that a debtor would not reasonably have left this particular handwritten obligation in the creditor's possession after payment, the circumstances may affect the ruling.
Rather, when he claims. The debtor is believed only when he makes a definite claim concerning the loan. If he says, "I do not know this person," or, "I do not remember whether I borrowed," the signature establishes the loan with certainty while his defense is doubtful, and doubt cannot displace certainty. He must therefore pay.
Or he claims that it was written as an accommodation, etc. The same applies to comparable defenses. The Sema cites the Rashba as saying that the debtor cannot claim, "I wrote it merely for practice, I did not sign my name as an obligation, and it fell from me, was lost, or was stolen, and this claimant found it." The Rashba follows his own view that payment or an accommodation writing cannot be claimed against one's handwriting. According to the Mechaber and my ruling that payment and accommodation may be claimed, the debtor may likewise advance these defenses by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed payment. The Tur and Mechaber in siman 50 already rule that one may claim that the writing fell from another person by virtue of a migo of payment. There is no basis to distinguish between loss in a private place and loss in a public place, for Rava made no such distinction.
If it contains a credibility clause, etc. Mahara ibn Chaim considers whether, if the handwritten obligation states that it shall have the legal force of a formal document, the creditor may perhaps swear and collect. This does not appear correct to me. Such language may be standard boilerplate, and a handwritten writing still lacks witnesses and publicity. This differs from an explicit credibility clause, where the debtor voluntarily accepted the creditor as credible. If the clause does not affirmatively state that the creditor is believed, but merely states that the debtor will not be believed to say that he paid, then when the debtor claims payment the creditor must swear and then collect. As for a blank signed paper, the Ir Shushan rules that the signature alone has no effect, and the signer may claim that it was lost and found or may rely on a migo of payment. The Sema disagrees because there should be no concern for loss. In my view the Ir Shushan is correct. There is no substantive obligation written above the signature, and a migo does not itself permit extracting money. My teacher brought proof from Ketubot 21, where the concern that an unscrupulous person might write an obligation above a blank signature implies that the blank signature itself has no operative force. Local commercial custom, however, may establish a different practice.
It contains a credibility clause, etc. The Sema writes that even if the clause does not expressly say, "without an oath," the creditor collects without an oath, just as with a credibility clause in an oral loan. If the writing does not say that the creditor is believed, but only that the debtor is not believed to say that he paid, then if the debtor claims payment the creditor swears and collects. In my view the creditor must swear even if the debtor does not expressly demand, "Swear to me," unlike an ordinary document under siman 82.
Credibility, etc. No kinyan is required for an ordinary credibility clause, as stated by Baal HaTerumos and the Tur, even when it is granted after the loan. If, however, the debtor grants the creditor credibility equivalent to two witnesses after the loan, a kinyan is required, as explained in siman 71, seif 2, and by the Bach.
