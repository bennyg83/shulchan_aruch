And it is possible. See Baer Heitev concerning one who produced another person's blank signature. The Shach, in the name of his revered teacher, cites a proof for the position of the Olat Shabbat from the Gemara in Ketubot. This proof is also brought in Korban Netanel on chapter 2 of Ketubot, letter 48, in the name of Rabbi Heshel. See there. Responsa Shevut Yaakov I, siman 149, rejects that proof, yet rules in practice in accordance with Olat Shabbat, Bach, and Shach that one may not extract money from a person in possession on the strength of a blank signature, except where local enactment and accepted commercial practice make such an instrument collectible. Shevut Yaakov further discusses a case in which Shimon demanded one hundred gold coins from Reuven on the basis of a customary commercial bill bearing Reuven's signature. Reuven answered that he had merely signed at the bottom of a blank sheet, which Shimon later found and filled in, and that he had never borrowed from him. He pointed to the difference in age between the writing and the signature. Shevut Yaakov ruled that where there is a real defect, visibly showing that the obligation was written a substantial time after the signature, the case is treated as though the signature still stood on blank paper, and money cannot be extracted from the possessor. This is only where the difference is visually clear and results from the signature having aged before the body of the obligation was written. If the difference can be attributed to the inks themselves, which commonly differ in appearance, the instrument is not invalidated if properly authenticated. If the judge sees circumstantial indications of deceit, he may impose a stringent oath on the opposing party, as explained in siman 15. Tumim cites this Shevut Yaakov and disagrees. Although Responsa Bach somewhat implies the same view, Tumim writes that there is no foundation for invalidating the instrument merely because the body was written later. The Sema, citing Migdal David, even validates collection when nothing at all was written above the signature, so there is certainly no reason to invalidate it merely because text was later added. Beit Shmuel Acharon, siman 6, writes that the possessor may certainly plead kim li in accordance with Bach and Shevut Yaakov. See there further. The Taz in siman 48 likewise agrees with Olat Shabbat but gives another reason. Regarding a memorandum containing no text at all and only a signature, he writes that nothing may be collected on it when it is presented to beit din in that defective state, because the holder could forge any obligation he wishes above the signature. Even if the debtor personally trusts him, he may owe other creditors on similar instruments and have insufficient assets, thereby harming them. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 65, questions this reasoning. A debtor who already owes others may nevertheless undertake a new genuine obligation to anyone he wishes, binding his person and property even if he receives no new money. He therefore explains that the Taz must mean that the holder could date the new obligation earlier and thereby prejudice other creditors, since an earlier oral loan has priority over a later one with respect to unencumbered property, and according to the Mechaber in siman 104, seif 13, even an earlier documented loan may have such priority. Yet this too is difficult, because one who seeks priority for an oral loan must prove its earlier date through witnesses. The debtor's handwriting alone would not establish priority because of concern for collusion, as stated there and in Shach, siman 48, seif katan 2. Moreover, even if one grants the Taz that other creditors could be harmed as to priority, that would not justify invalidating the entire document. A genuinely antedated document is, according to many major authorities, valid against the debtor's unencumbered assets, and he cannot claim that the loan never occurred or that he paid, as the Mechaber rules in siman 43, seif 7. Even the Rama's view there that such a document is entirely invalid applies where the date was certainly advanced in order to collect from that earlier date. Here, who says that occurred? Noda BiYehudah therefore offers another reading of the Taz. The Taz was combining his point with the Levush, who reasoned that people do not ordinarily entrust a creditor with such a blank signed paper, and therefore the debtor is believed to claim that the paper fell from him and was found by the holder. The Sema answered that when one needs money he may indeed trust the creditor. The Taz then adds that although a debtor may personally choose to trust the creditor, he is not authorized to trust him in a manner that prejudices other creditors. Since doing so would be improper, people do not ordinarily act that way, and we therefore accept the claim that the paper fell from the debtor and was found by the holder. Noda BiYehudah further discusses a wealthy man who died. A merchant claimed that the deceased owed him a stated sum for merchandise sold on credit and produced his ledger, listing the merchandise item by item, with the deceased's signature below. Between the list and the signature were four or five blank lines, within which appeared a notation of a payment of fifty gold coins from the deceased. The question was whether the heirs could be made to pay on the strength of this writing. Noda BiYehudah writes that even had the father been alive there would have been room to discuss whether he could claim payment. The blank lines between text and signature are not directly comparable to the rule that a witnessed document is invalid if the witnesses left two blank lines, because there the witnesses improperly created a document susceptible to forgery. In a handwritten obligation the signer himself left the space and apparently trusted the holder, so there is no obvious basis to invalidate it. The matter instead depends on the dispute of Levush, Sema, Shach, and Taz concerning a blank memorandum. According to the Sema there is no defect at all. Indeed, the instrument is stronger, as though it contained an express clause of credibility, since the holder could have inserted such a clause. According to the Shach, although a wholly blank signature is invalid because the holder would need a migo to create the debt and a migo cannot extract money, that reason does not apply here because the debt itself is already written. According to the Levush, however, one might still say that people do not ordinarily extend such trust and that the paper fell from the signer and was found by the claimant. Yet in this case the deceased signed in the claimant's own ledger, so the Levush's concern that the page fell from the signer is not readily applicable. If the watermark or other identifying paper mark shows that the signed page belongs to the same batch as the rest of the ledger, the remaining possibility, that the claimant found a separate signed sheet of matching paper and then fabricated an entire ledger around it, is exceedingly remote. One should also examine earlier pages to determine whether they demonstrably contain entries written long ago. If so, the concern of accidental loss is removed, and so too is the Taz's concern for later forgery. If these concerns are excluded, then had the father been alive he could not have claimed payment. This is clear according to the authorities who generally reject a payment claim against one's handwriting. Even according to the Mechaber, who ordinarily permits such a claim, here the Sema would treat the blank space as though an express credibility clause had been inserted, since it could have been inserted. According to the Levush as well, the debtor would not be believed to claim payment, because the Levush permits that claim only through a migo that the debtor could deny the original loan, and here he could not deny it. The Shach's rule against using a migo to extract money also does not apply once the Levush's concern has been eliminated, and the blank space is then treated as though it contained a credibility clause. That, however, addresses only collection from the debtor himself. Against heirs, Noda BiYehudah had explained elsewhere that ordinary credibility granted to a creditor does not suffice because the debtor may have trusted the creditor not to deny repayment to his face while alive, without contemplating the uncommon event of death. Thus ordinary credibility does not authorize collection from heirs unless the debtor expressly made it effective against them. Here there is no express credibility clause at all. We infer trust only from the manner in which the document was delivered, and that inference naturally relates to the debtor himself, not to his heirs. The claimant nevertheless has a migo, since he could have written an express clause of credibility against heirs into the blank space. Although the Shach is concerned that a migo cannot extract money, Noda BiYehudah concludes that where the claimant asserts certainty and the heirs have only a doubtful claim, a migo may be used even to extract money, as explained in the Shach's rules of migo. This is especially so because the general rule that a migo cannot extract money is itself disputed. He therefore concludes that if inspection of the ledger removes the concerns described above, the claimant may collect after taking a stringent oath that he was not paid. Even so, because one should not hastily extract money from heirs on the basis of analogies, it is proper to seek a compromise. See there at length.
