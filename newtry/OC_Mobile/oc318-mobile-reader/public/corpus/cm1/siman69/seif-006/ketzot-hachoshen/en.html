I was uncertain about a father who had already been judicially ordered to take a Torah oath, then died before swearing. Must the heirs pay because their father had already reached the stage of "swear or pay"? The verse, "The oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them, but not between the heirs," does not directly resolve this. That verse concerns heirs who themselves say, "Fifty we know and fifty we do not know," where the father had never incurred an oath. Here the father himself had already been ordered to swear. The matter appears to depend upon the early dispute concerning one obligated to swear but unable to swear when he had no reason to know the facts. Baal HaMaor cites Raavad, who limits the rule of inability to swear to a case where the defendant's claim is itself impaired, such as suspicion of dishonesty or a partial lack of knowledge where he should have known. Where the father could have sworn and there was no defect in his claim, the heirs' later inability should not convert the case into payment. This is also the rationale of Tosafot and Rosh, who exempt one who genuinely could not have known. Under that approach, if the father died after being ordered to swear, the heirs remain exempt because no defect existed in the father's claim. The same may apply if he later became disqualified from swearing. Rambam and those who follow him apply "unable to swear, therefore pays" even where the party had no reason to know. They exempt heirs for a different reason: heirs are not the original litigants and beit din advances their father's possible claims for them. Under that approach, if the father himself had already become obligated to swear and afterward became disqualified, the opposing party may swear and collect. Likewise, if he died after the oath obligation was fixed, there is room to say the estate must pay. Maharik, shoresh 120, writes explicitly that even if the father had said he was willing to pay rather than swear, the heirs can still say they do not wish to pay. He proves this from Bava Metzia, where the Gemara asks about a custodian who said, "I will pay," then died, and the sons said, "We will not pay." But this proof is not conclusive. Tosafot and Rosh explain that once the custodian said, "I will pay," he cannot really retract. The Gemara asks only whether the later refusal prevents him from receiving the double payment because he troubled beit din. The same should apply to the heirs. If their father already made a binding commitment to pay, they cannot undo it, though the question of the double payment remains. Indeed, if they were entirely exempt from payment, they certainly could not acquire the double payment, because the transfer of the double payment depends on bearing the custodial burden. Thus Maharik's proof does not establish his conclusion. Accordingly, where the father was already obligated in a Torah oath and died before taking it, the case remains an unresolved legal doubt tied to the broader dispute over inability to swear where there was no defect in the original claim. The same applies where he became a suspected swearer only after judgment. By contrast, if he was already suspected at the time the oath obligation arose and later repented, the law does not reopen. At the time of judgment his claim was already impaired and the opposing party acquired the right to swear and collect. Later repentance does not undo a completed ruling. See siman 34.
