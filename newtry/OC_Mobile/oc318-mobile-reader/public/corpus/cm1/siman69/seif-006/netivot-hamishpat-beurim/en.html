They are exempt without an oath. The Ketzot HaChoshen writes that where a person became obligated in a d'oraisa oath and then died, according to Tosafot, who hold that when a litigant had no reason to know the matter he is exempt because his claim is not impaired, the heirs are likewise exempt even if their father had already become obligated in a d'oraisa oath, since his claim had not been impaired because he had asserted a definite claim. Similarly, if one became obligated in a d'oraisa oath and then became disqualified from taking an oath, since his claim was not impaired when asserted, according to Tosafot we do not apply the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. According to the Rambam, however, who holds that even where the litigant had no reason to know and his claim was not impaired he must pay, while heirs are exempt for the reason given by the Shach in siman 72, namely that they are not the original litigants and we raise for them their father's claim, if the father became obligated in a d'oraisa oath and then became disqualified or died, they would be liable under the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. According to the Ketzot, under the Rambam this would apply even if the father merely admitted part of the claim and beit din had not yet formally imposed the oath. These words are very difficult. His statement that if one admitted part and then became disqualified he is exempt according to Tosafot is astonishing. The claimant is now suing him as well, and beit din now imposes an oath upon him while there is a present defect arising from his disqualification. Why should he be exempt? Is it because he was once sued earlier, before becoming disqualified, and became obligated in an oath, that he should now be exempt from the present claim? Certainly, when he cannot swear because of his own present defect, he must pay. His statement that according to the Rambam the heirs are liable is likewise difficult. If so, even where the heirs themselves say, “We know about fifty and do not know about fifty,” they should be liable. The rationale that we raise for them their father's claim does not apply, because once the heirs know that their father owed fifty, their father himself could no longer have exempted them merely by denial. Indeed, even if he had definitely denied the claim and then died, the heirs would have to pay if he had not sworn. We must therefore say that the heirs are exempt because we say that, had the father lived, perhaps he would have sworn and been exempt. If so, even where the father had already incurred an oath, we should likewise say that perhaps he would have sworn and been exempt. The correct distinction is as I wrote in siman 108. If beit din had already formally obligated him to take an oath, then when he stood in judgment his property became subject during his lifetime to payment if he failed to swear. Therefore collection may be made from the heirs, both according to Tosafot and according to the Rambam. But if he did not stand in judgment during his lifetime, his property did not become subject during his lifetime, and collection cannot be made from the property by virtue of the father's liability. Nor are the heirs personally liable, because we do not apply to heirs the rule that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay. A final judgment during the father's lifetime is specifically required, much as with a fine, which does not become monetary liability until final judgment, as explained in Bava Kamma, chapter Merubah.
