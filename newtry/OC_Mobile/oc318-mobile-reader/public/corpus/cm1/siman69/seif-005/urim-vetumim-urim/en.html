We plead on behalf of the heirs, etc. Even if the father wrote a credibility clause, unless it expressly grants credibility against him and against his heirs, the lender has no special credibility against the heirs. See below, siman 71.
From the heirs' oath. This means the oath that their father did not instruct them that the debt had been paid. That oath applies when they seek to collect their father's document from another person and the debtor claims that it was paid, not in the reverse situation. If the claimant asserts with certainty that an heir knows his father owed him, for example because the father instructed the heir concerning it, the heir must take a hesset oath like any other person.
And if it is within the due date, etc. The Shach asks: if the writing was not authenticated and the heirs merely admit that it is their father's handwriting, why do we not plead for the heirs that had their father been alive he could have claimed payment by virtue of a migo that he could have alleged forgery? The Shach writes that this depends upon the dispute below in siman 108 whether we plead on behalf of heirs that property entrusted to their father was lost through unavoidable accident, or whether we do not raise such an uncommon claim. If we do not raise uncommon claims, perhaps payment before the due date is likewise uncommon. See Tumim. I clarified that even Tosafot, who hold that we do not plead unavoidable accident for heirs, nevertheless appear to hold that we do plead payment within the due date where the father would have had a migo of forgery. Therefore this ruling requires study.
Or the debtor admitted, etc. If an entry is found in the deceased's ledger, that is no better than his handwriting alone. But if the ledger states that the handwritten acknowledgment held by the other person remained unpaid, that is effective like an admission made during his final illness. So writes the Shach, and this is correct.
And if they do not admit, etc. The wording implies that authentication is required specifically in the case of payment within the due date. In the other cases, why should authentication be necessary when the father admitted that he owed? The Rama and Sema hold that even where he admitted, as long as the handwriting is not authenticated we may say that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy. See Tumim for how I reconciled the Shach's difficulty. In truth, I explained in Tumim that the case is one where the father admitted generally that any handwritten acknowledgment of his found in another person's possession was unpaid, because he was always careful not to pay until the writing was returned to him. He did not expressly admit that he presently owed this particular debt. Therefore, if the handwriting is authenticated, collection is made because he admitted that he had not paid it; if not, we plead forgery on behalf of the heirs. This is clear. See Tumim.
However, if he was placed under ban. The later authorities struggled to explain the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, who require authentication even here. See Tumim. The case is where he was placed under ban because he refused to comply with beit din. It is therefore possible that during his lifetime he could not have claimed forgery because the lender had one witness supporting the writing, whereas against the heirs that single witness does not suffice. See there.
