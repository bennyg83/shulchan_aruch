Since they admit it is their father's handwriting, and so forth. Shach raises a powerful objection. If the father were alive and the debt were still within its term, he could theoretically say "I paid" through a migo that he could have said the handwriting was forged. Why should his heirs not receive the same claim? We often plead for heirs any defense the father could have made, even an uncommon defense, such as "I returned the business property" through a migo of unavoidable loss. Shach says Tur follows the authorities who do not plead uncommon claims for heirs. Payment within the term is uncommon, so the heirs do not receive it. But Mechaber elsewhere does plead uncommon defenses for heirs. The difficulty therefore remains. Indeed, even the authorities who generally reject uncommon heir-claims may admit this particular defense. Tosafot at the beginning of Gittin discuss an unverified note presented against orphans. They conclude that we must raise forgery for the heirs; otherwise anyone could fabricate a document after a person's death and take the estate. Tosafot consider whether perhaps we need not plead forgery itself because we can plead payment through the father's migo of forgery. Their discussion implies that even a payment claim supported by a forgery migo is a serious heir-defense. This seems to include payment within the term as well. Otherwise Tosafot could have made their "no life would remain" argument with the simple case of a fabricated note whose due date lies after the debtor's death. There would be no need to shift to sale or gift documents. Maharam Lublin argues that this omission proves the opposite: within-term payment is not pleaded, and Tosafot therefore needed a sale or gift example where payment is irrelevant. His reading can be defended through a more elaborate distinction. We do not ordinarily suspect someone of fabricating a completely imaginary debt from nothing, because falsehood has no legs. Witnesses, acquaintances, and surrounding facts may expose the fraud. The real danger is where a genuine old debt existed and was paid, and after the debtor dies the creditor fabricates a replacement document matching the original transaction. Then the general facts support rather than refute him. In that scenario, if the original debt had been within its term, the strong chazakah against early payment reduces the concern. Thus Tosafot could reasonably move to sale and gift instruments for their strongest example of unrecoverable fabricated claims. This is ingenious, but it is not the plainest reading. There are also practical differences between pleading "forged" and pleading "paid through a migo of forged." According to the opinion that one who admits writing the note need not authenticate it, forgery may be available while payment is not. Likewise, a forged receipt presented against heirs raises different questions from a payment plea, and the creditor may have protected himself by requiring special language against receipts. These differences explain why Tosafot had reason to discuss forgery directly rather than rely solely on a payment migo. At first I thought one could distinguish between a note whose authenticity is unknown and one whose handwriting the heirs themselves admit. If authenticity is uncertain, we have two cumulative possibilities: perhaps it is forged, and even if genuine perhaps it was paid within the term. Two individually weak possibilities together can prevent extraction. But where the heirs admit the handwriting, only the uncommon early-payment concern remains. However, Tosafot's discussion of Shmuel and authentication shows they do not readily build the law through such cumulative weak suspicions. The distinction is therefore uncertain. The practical conclusion is difficult. Tur clearly does not plead early payment once the heirs admit the father's handwriting. Mechaber follows him. Tosafot nevertheless provide substantial conceptual basis for a broader heir-defense through the father's forgery migo. The issue therefore requires study, and Shach's objection should not be dismissed.
And if they do not admit, and so forth. Sema seeks to explain Tur and Baal HaTerumot, who require authentication even where the dying father acknowledged the obligation during his illness. Without authentication, Sema says, perhaps the father acknowledged only in order not to appear wealthy, and the note itself is forged. Rama in Darkei Moshe gives a similar explanation. Shach objects: even after authentication, perhaps the debt had already been paid and the father acknowledged it merely in order not to appear wealthy. Why should authentication eliminate that concern? There is a strong proof for Sema's distinction from the end of Perek Get Pashut. The Gemara says: if the dying person said "give," authenticate the note; if he did not say "give," do not authenticate it. In the authenticated case, his statement can be relied upon. Tosafot ask why his deathbed admission should remove the normal heir-facing oath. Perhaps he had already been paid and admitted only so as not to appear wealthy. They answer that a person does not ordinarily make a false poverty-motivated admission about a formally authenticated note. Outsiders would see an established valid document in the creditor's possession and reasonably think the debt remained outstanding. Thus once a note is authenticated, the theory "he acknowledged only so as not to appear wealthy" weakens substantially. The same can apply to handwritten obligations. One might distinguish that a formal note blocks the payment defense while handwriting does not. But the relevant point is not the payment rule itself. It is the deceased's behavior in making a false public acknowledgment about an authenticated instrument. Ramban and other commentators even apply the Talmudic rule to deposits, where heirs can ordinarily plead return through a migo of accident. Yet a deathbed acknowledgment tied to an authenticated document is still treated seriously. Therefore Sema has support. Shach offers another answer: perhaps the father said only, "He has a handwritten obligation from me," without identifying its amount. A creditor could then fabricate a paper for one hundred even if the father acknowledged only a dinar. Authentication is needed to establish the actual writing and amount. That answer is also sound, though it does not fit every wording. A further possibility is even cleaner. The dying person may have said generally, "Any handwritten obligations of mine in Shimon's possession are not paid." He does not admit a definite current debt; he states his established practice that he never pays without recovering the paper. If the writing is authenticated, the heirs cannot say "paid," because the father expressly said he did not leave paid handwritten obligations outstanding. If it is not authenticated, however, they can say the paper itself is forged. The father never admitted that this particular writing was his. This reading harmonizes the law naturally. The case of a debtor who was placed under nidui is more difficult. If the nidui was a final adjudication requiring payment, the note would be unnecessary. Therefore Bach and Derishah explain it as a nidui for refusing to appear before beit din, not a final judgment on the debt. If the writing is authenticated, his continued refusal to appear supports the debt: had he paid, he could simply have come and said so. If the writing is not authenticated, however, the heirs can still allege forgery. Perhaps witnesses who could have authenticated during his lifetime were then available but later became disqualified relative to the heirs, or only one identifying witness remained, creating a situation the father reasonably preferred not to litigate. Since every reasonable doubt is raised for heirs, the unverified writing cannot collect. Thus the requirement of authentication can be defended.
