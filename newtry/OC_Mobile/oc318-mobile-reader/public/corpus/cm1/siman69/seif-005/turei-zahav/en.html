I was asked about one who gave funds to another as an investment and privately stipulated that any loss not proved by witnesses would be borne by the recipient. The recipient now claims that a loss occurred and seeks belief through a migo of complete denial, while the investor denies the loss. At first glance this differs from our case. A migo ordinarily lends credibility to the present factual claim, whereas here the parties do not contradict one another about the existence of the stipulation; the investor merely demands its enforcement. Why should this condition be weaker than any other valid monetary condition? Nevertheless, it appears that the recipient does possess a migo. Since he maintains that an actual loss occurred, he may say: “When I promised to prove any loss by witnesses, I never finally intended to bind myself, because I relied on my ability to deny the entire arrangement.” The Rosh, citing Rabbi Yosef Halevi, discusses a former partner who admits the partnership and the stipulation that he would swear after division, but says he never misappropriated anything. Although no witnesses establish either the partnership or the stipulation, the migo does not exempt him from the oath, because a migo does not ordinarily remove an oath. The case necessarily assumes that he admits the stipulation; otherwise no oath would arise after division. Yet his migo would have defeated the stipulation but for the special rule concerning oaths, since from the outset he relied on his ability to deny the partnership. All the more so here, where no witnesses establish either the investment or the condition, the migo should defeat the condition requiring proof by witnesses. He need only swear that a loss occurred. The glossator objects that the partnership condition merely preserved an oath already imposed by law and prevented its waiver upon division; it did not create a new monetary liability. Here, by contrast, the condition itself creates liability for any unproved loss, and every monetary condition is valid. The glossator therefore leaves the practical ruling unresolved.
