If a creditor produces an obligation in the debtor's handwriting against the debtor's heirs, even if they admit that it is their father's handwriting, we advance for them the claim that it was paid, and they are exempt even from the heirs' oath. A general cherem is nevertheless proclaimed in their presence against anyone who knows that their father did not pay it. Rama: This is the primary ruling, unlike those who say the debtor's heirs must swear that they do not know that their father remained indebted. Nevertheless, in later generations the practice has been to impose an oath upon heirs where there is some basis for suspicion and it is doubtful whether he remained indebted. If the debt is still within its fixed term, or the debtor admitted and instructed during his illness that he owed this amount in his own handwriting, or he was placed under nidui until he paid and died while still under nidui without paying, then since the heirs admit that it is their father's handwriting, or the handwriting is authenticated, the creditor collects from them. See the Tur and Beit Yosef. If they do not admit that it is their father's handwriting and it has not been authenticated, they are exempt even where the term has not yet expired. This is the Rosh's view as cited by the Tur. A general cherem is nevertheless proclaimed in their presence against anyone who knows that their father owed this debt and did not pay it. Rama: If he was placed under nidui and died while still under nidui, there is no distinction, and in every case they must pay.
