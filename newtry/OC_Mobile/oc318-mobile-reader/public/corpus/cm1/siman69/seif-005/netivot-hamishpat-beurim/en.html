We raise for them the claim that it was paid. According to those who hold above that even their father himself could not have claimed payment against his own handwritten obligation, collection may likewise be made from the heirs, as is explicit in responsa of the Rosh and Rivash. This is straightforward.
And if it is still within its due date. See Shach, seif katan 26, who questions the Mechaber. Below, in siman 108, seif 4, the Mechaber cites two opinions regarding a claim that property was lost through unavoidable accident, while here he rules categorically. The Mechaber and Rama nevertheless rule this way without qualification in many places, including here, siman 78, seif 4 in the gloss, siman 108, and siman 361. It therefore appears that even according to the view that we raise for heirs a claim of unavoidable accident, its rationale is as follows. The principle that we do not raise for heirs an uncommon claim means that whenever we do raise a claim for heirs, their position is treated as though they had asserted a definite claim, as the Rosh writes at the beginning of the chapter HaIshah Shenitarmelah. With an uncommon claim, however, we do not elevate their position to that of a definite claim, and it remains comparable to a case where their father were alive and said that he was uncertain. In a case of unavoidable accident, if their father were alive and claimed that he did not know whether an accident had occurred, he would not be liable except because he is one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, as explained in siman 251. That doctrine does not apply to heirs, because the verse says, “the oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them,” and not between the heirs, as explained in Shevuot. Therefore, even their uncertain claim of unavoidable accident is a sufficient claim. Here, by contrast, because the debt is still within its due date, the father himself would need a definite claim in order to be believed through a migo. We do not raise for heirs a claim in a manner that would transform their uncertainty into the definite claim required for that migo.
And if they do not admit it. See Shach, seif katan 28, who writes that even if the heirs admit that the handwriting is their father's, we raise for them the possibility that their father paid, and that his later acknowledgment was made merely so as not to appear wealthy. At first glance this is difficult, because when a person acknowledges in his own handwriting, we do not raise the claim that the acknowledgment was made so as not to appear wealthy, as explained in siman 81, seif 17. This is no difficulty. There, the written acknowledgment itself remains before us. Here, however, it is possible that the father forgot about the handwritten obligation, as the Shach writes in seif katan 14. He may therefore have made an oral acknowledgment merely so as not to appear wealthy, because he had forgotten the writing and believed that no writing of his remained in the other person's possession. This is comparable to the rule above in siman 40, that if one says, “I hereby owe you one hundred by instrument,” this is treated as though he said, “You are my witnesses,” and he cannot later claim that he spoke merely so as not to appear wealthy. That case is different because he is presently creating an obligation through that statement. But if he says by way of acknowledgment, “I admit that the handwriting in so-and-so's possession stating that I owe one hundred is genuine,” and that person in fact has no such handwriting at all, he certainly incurs no liability. He can explain that he made the acknowledgment precisely because it would later become clear that it was only to avoid appearing wealthy, since the other party could produce no instrument against him. So too here, because he had forgotten that his handwritten obligation was still in the other's possession, his acknowledgment may be explained as having been made so as not to appear wealthy, in accordance with the Shach. According to this reasoning, if he saw the handwritten obligation at the time of his acknowledgment and nevertheless admitted it, he cannot later claim that the acknowledgment was made so as not to appear wealthy.
