Since they admit. The Sema writes that even where the decedent instructed that he owed money in his handwriting, perhaps he made the statement only so as not to appear wealthy. The Shach objects that if so, even when the heirs admit the handwriting is their father's we should still say perhaps their father had already paid and merely admitted the debt so as not to appear wealthy. This is not difficult. Where an actual debt instrument exists, a person does not ordinarily make a false admission of indebtedness merely to avoid the appearance of wealth. Tosafot in Bava Batra states this explicitly: where the creditor holds a validated document, a debtor who has paid does not ordinarily say, "I still owe him the amount written there," merely so the public should think he remains in debt. The same can apply to personal handwriting. The real difficulty comes from siman 40. One who says before witnesses, "I owe you a maneh in a document," becomes obligated even without formally saying, "You are my witnesses," because the language of a document is strong enough to function like such designation. Why should saying, "I owe you a maneh in my handwriting," be weaker? The answer depends upon the Tur's view in siman 81. The Tur holds that even saying, "You are my witnesses," does not create a binding admission when the creditor is not present. The present case likewise concerns an admission made outside the creditor's presence. Baal HaTerumot appears to agree. According to our practical law, however, which accepts an admission before designated witnesses even when the creditor is absent, the same should apply to saying, "I owe you a maneh in my handwriting." The force of the written form excludes both joking and the claim that the statement was made merely for appearances. The Shach in siman 81 likewise writes that even Ramban and Rashba, who dispute the creation of a new obligation through such language, agree that for an actual admission the phrase is as strong as designation of witnesses. See siman 81 and siman 255.
