[We say yes. And even he wrote in his hand loyalty not to the faithful to the heirs unless it is written in the words that he will be faithful to his successors and so that he will not be violated and taken away from him and that he will not be faithful to him, and that he will not be given to him They believe in him and his heirs.”
They are exempt even from the heirs’ oath. Heirs take the heirs’ oath only when collecting upon a note, namely that their father did not instruct them that the note had been paid. To be exempt from liability, heirs who plead uncertainty need not even take a hesset oath. If they make a definite claim that their father paid, they must swear, as explained below in siman 75, seifim 16 and 20. See what I wrote there and Responsa Maharit, simanim 72 and 112. Nevertheless, a general ban is proclaimed, as the Sema explains in seif katan 19.
If it is still within the due date, etc. The ruling that the heirs are liable because they admit that it is their father’s handwriting requires study, although Baal HaTerumot and the Tur also state it. If their father were alive and admitted the handwriting, he could nevertheless plead payment within the term through a migo that he could have denied the handwriting. Why should we not advance for the heirs the claim that their father paid? Even where the father himself would have been believed only through a migo, we advance that defense for heirs, as I conclude below in siman 297. There is no difficulty where the father admitted on his deathbed or died while under a ban, because had he lived he could not have pleaded payment after already admitting nonpayment or after being placed under a ban for nonpayment. But where the only evidence is handwriting within its term and the father never admitted nonpayment, the ruling is difficult. The Tur follows his own view in siman 108 that we do not advance for heirs a claim of unavoidable loss because it is uncommon, and Baal HaTerumot may agree. The Mechaber, however, records two opinions there, yet rules here without qualification like Baal HaTerumot and the Tur. Perhaps he holds that the presumption that a person does not pay within the term is even stronger than the rule that an uncommon claim is not advanced for heirs. In truth, however, the authority who disagrees there should disagree here as well. Although nonpayment within the term is a stronger presumption than mere rarity, the reason of the dissenting view is that we always advance for heirs every claim their father could have made, treating the matter as though he were alive and asserting it. This is explicit in the Ramban’s Milhamot. That appears to be the primary ruling.
Or the obligor admitted. The Tashbetz, citing Baal HaTerumot, means that the obligor instructed during his illness that he still owed the debt, or that his ledger contained an entry that he still owed it, or that he had been placed under a ban. Bedek HaBayit explains that an entry in his ledger, even after the due date, is equivalent to an admission and is stronger than his handwriting. Responsa Mabit gives the same explanation. In my view, however, the ledger entry refers back to the immediately preceding matter, meaning that it states that this particular handwritten obligation is still unpaid. Otherwise, a ledger is no stronger than handwriting, as the Rashba expressly writes in the responsum cited in the Beit Yosef and in the Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 107. This also explains why Baal HaTerumot lists only three methods rather than four. The ledger is not an independent fourth method, but part of the admission concerning the existing handwriting.
If they do not admit, etc. This applies even within the term. The Sema initially suggests that the Mechaber mentioned the term only because the heirs may say that the handwriting is forged, so the date written in it has no significance. He then seeks to extend the exemption to a deathbed instruction and to death under a ban, offering various possibilities: perhaps the father admitted merely to avoid appearing wealthy before his sons, or perhaps the ban concerned a different authenticated writing, while the claimant now presents a forgery and may later collect again upon the authentic writing. Several later authorities offer similar answers. In my view these explanations are not persuasive. Money should not be withheld from a creditor because of such remote concerns, particularly since we rule that a receipt may be written. The analogy to a reconstructed erased note is misplaced, because there the debtor might innocently pay again without detecting the fraud, whereas here he knows of the concern and can demand a receipt. The Mechaber appears to follow his discussion in the Beit Yosef, where he was troubled by Baal HaTerumot and the Tur concerning a deathbed admission and death under a ban. The Rama, by contrast, follows a third view, as he states in Darkei Moshe, distinguishing death under a ban from a deathbed admission on the ground that the latter may have been made to avoid appearing wealthy. Yet that concern itself is difficult, for if so we should raise it even when the heirs admit the handwriting. Furthermore, siman 108 states generally that collection may be made from heirs in the relevant cases, evidently where no such concern exists. The correct explanation is that Baal HaTerumot and the Tur refer to an admission that the particular handwritten obligation held by the creditor remains unpaid, without independently specifying the amount. Similarly, the ban concerned that particular writing. The heirs may still claim that the writing presented is forged and that their father’s admission concerned another writing. This is why the Tur says that he admitted owing “such and such according to his handwriting.” The amount was identified only by reference to the writing. Thus the heirs’ defense is coherent, and the wording of Baal HaTerumot is properly understood.
“I don’t admit that he has written his father’s hand, nor will he be cut off.”
They are exempt. The Ir Shushan writes that although a person ordinarily does not pay within the term, this presumption applies only to a complete note and not to handwriting. His words are surprising. Even a complete note that has not been authenticated may be wholly forged, so the presumption does not apply. Conversely, authenticated handwriting is subject to the same presumption against payment within the term. Here the exemption rests solely upon the possibility that the entire handwriting is forged. Perhaps this is what the Ir Shushan meant by a “complete note,” namely one that has been authenticated, but his language is strained.
