And we advance for them the claim that it was paid. Even if the document contains an ordinary credibility clause, it does not help against heirs unless it expressly says that the lender is believed also against the borrower's heirs. So writes Darkei Moshe, and likewise the Tur and Mechaber in siman 71. There the lender swears on a formal promissory note that he was not paid and then collects; here, with mere handwriting, the heir is exempt.
And they are exempt even from the heirs' oath. The ordinary heirs' oath is a stringent oath with an object held in the hand, instituted where heirs seek to collect and swear, 'our father did not instruct us that it was paid,' as the Tur and Mechaber explain below in siman 108. Since the Mechaber concludes, however, 'but they impose a general ban,' it appears that he holds that even a hesset oath and even a ban directed personally at the heirs are not imposed. This follows the Rosh and Tur, who write that Chazal instituted an oath for heirs only when they seek to extract with a document their father left against another and it contains no credibility clause, while the borrower says he paid. They did not institute such an oath where another person produces a claim against the heirs. Baal HaTerumot and those who agree with him disagree and require a hesset oath. They are the 'some disagree' cited by Rama. See Perishah.
But they impose a general ban, etc. The Tur writes in the name of the Rosh: they impose a general ban in the heirs' presence that anyone who knows whether this money was paid should admit it, and if it was not paid should testify; the heirs likewise should say whether they know it remains unpaid. In Perishah I explained that as part of imposing the ban, they include the lender as well, requiring him to admit and return the document if it was paid. Because the lender himself is present before beit din, the ban is directed to him personally. The heirs, however, are not themselves the borrowers, so no personally directed ban is imposed on them to admit. Rather, a general ban is declared upon anyone who knows the matter to come testify, and the heirs are included within that general formulation. The Mechaber abbreviated this aspect of the ban.
Since they admit that it is their father's handwriting, etc., even though it is within the term, etc. At first glance, the Mechaber's emphasis 'even though it is within the term' might imply that only in such a case, where the document might be forged, do we disregard the apparent date showing that payment was not yet due, because the instrument itself may be false. But the Tur writes that the heirs are exempt also where their father instructed while ill that he owed the debt, or where he died while under a ban for refusing to pay. The Mechaber likewise begins with all three cases and then says that it must additionally be known to be the father's handwriting or admitted by the heirs, implying that without such authentication the heirs are exempt in all three. In Perishah I explained the reasons. If the father died within the term, he is exempt because perhaps the whole writing is forged. If he instructed while ill that he owed money, one can say he made the statement merely so that his children not appear wealthy, so long as he did not say, 'Give it to him.' Although we do not ordinarily say that a debt recorded in a document or handwriting was acknowledged merely to avoid appearing wealthy, perhaps this handwriting is itself forged; the oral statement may have been merely to avoid creating an impression of wealth. In the case where he died while under a ban, that explanation is unavailable. Yet there remains the present defect that the handwriting is unauthenticated, the heirs say they do not recognize it, and beit din did not see it during the father's lifetime. Had beit din seen it while he was under a ban and he did not claim forgery, it could have authenticated it then. We can therefore be concerned that the ban was imposed because of a different, authenticated handwritten obligation, while the claimant now presents a forgery and seeks to collect on this one first and later again on the genuine one. The rule that even an oral loan may be collected from heirs where the borrower died under a ban, as explained in siman 108, applies only where there is no present defect. In a situation containing a concrete defect, we are concerned and do not force heirs to take and safeguard a receipt indefinitely. Shiyarei Yosef and Rabbi Heshel's glosses write something similar. See Perishah for more. It appears that the Mechaber merely stated the first of the three cases here but intended the same law for the other two. Rama's conclusion, 'however, if they placed him under a ban,' represents a third view: he agrees that the heirs are exempt within the term and where the father gave an instruction during illness for the reasons above, but rejects my concern in the case where he died under a ban. Nevertheless Rama should not have written it as though he were merely explaining the Mechaber, for the Mechaber appears to follow the broader rule I described. Eir Shushan followed Beit Yosef's difficulty and therefore writes that both where he died under a ban and where he instructed while ill that payment be made, the heirs must pay in all circumstances. What I have written appears correct. Understand this well.
