He signed his name at the top of the sheet. Even if he does not raise the claim himself, beit din advances it for him. However, if the handwritten loan text is on one line and the signature on the next, or the text and signature are on one line and he claims that he signed as a witness upon another person's debt note and the paper was then cut and used to create this note, his claim is accepted, but beit din does not advance it for him. Likewise, if he signed in the middle of the sheet, nothing was written above his signature, and below it appears, "I borrowed one hundred from so and so," and he claims, "I signed merely as a sample and the paper fell from me and he found it," this is a valid claim, but beit din does not advance it for him. Even if he did not write his name. The Shach concludes that where such wording is not customary, he may claim, "I wrote it merely as a sample form." Only among partners does a writing without mention of the writer's name help, because that is customary among partners. But if one writes a handwritten obligation without mentioning his own name, the obligation is effective and he cannot say, "Such a writing does not obligate me."
