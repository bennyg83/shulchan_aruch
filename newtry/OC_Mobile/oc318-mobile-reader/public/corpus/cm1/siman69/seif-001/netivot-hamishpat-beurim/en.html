And he signed below. See Shach, seif katan 3, through his statement that this is a remote concern. There appears to be proof for the Sema's view that beit din does not raise such a claim on his behalf except where there is a reasonable concern that the beit din itself required him to act in that manner. Otherwise, when the text of the instrument occupies one line and his signature occupies the following line, the instrument should always be invalid, because we could claim for him that he merely signed his name while leaving one blank line between the writing and the signature, and that someone later cut away the original writing and wrote the present instrument in that blank line, as discussed in siman 45, seif 18. It must therefore be that we do not raise such a claim on his behalf except where beit din may have required him to do so. For this reason the Mechaber mentioned only a case invalid without any affirmative claim, and did not mention an instrument where the text occupies one line and the signature a second line. Such an instrument is invalid only if the signer himself makes the claim, but we do not make it for him. It therefore appears that if his signature is in the middle of the sheet, nothing is written above it, and below his signature are the words, “I borrowed one hundred from so-and-so,” neither the Sema's reason nor the Shach's reason applies. Only the reason given by the Ir Shushan, that a person might sign his name without purpose, could apply, and he would have to assert that claim himself. If he does not, we do not raise it for him. It is even possible that the Ir Shushan's reason does not apply here, because a person does not ordinarily sign his name without purpose in the middle of a sheet. He may sometimes do so only at the top of a sheet.
Even if he did not write his name. See Shach, seif katan 4. His words require some explanation. He writes that the Bach states that even the Ramban agrees that the writer is believed with a migo that he paid, for even regarding a signed handwritten obligation, according to the Ramban, he is believed to say that it was an amanah document. The Bach means that just as one is believed to say concerning his signed handwriting that the document was written as an amanah, by virtue of the migo that he could have claimed payment, so too one should be believed concerning an unsigned text in his own handwriting to say, “I wrote it merely as a draft,” by virtue of the migo that he could have claimed payment. The Shach responds that the Bach's words are not established, because the Ramban appears to regard a person's own handwriting exactly like his signature in all respects. Regarding a signed document he certainly is not believed to say that he wrote it merely as a draft, and the same should therefore apply to an unsigned text in his handwriting. The Shach then adds that the Bach's proof from a claim of amanah is no proof. This is a separate objection. Even if we accept the Kesef Mishneh's explanation that the claim of amanah is believed because of a migo, it follows that when the document contains a clause of ne'emanut and the migo is unavailable, he is not believed. Yet the Ramban and Ritva imply that one may claim “I wrote it merely as a draft” even without a migo, because he could make that claim even where the text itself contains ne'emanut, saying that he wrote the draft together with the ne'emanut clause. The Bach's equation of the Ramban with the Ritva therefore does not fit. The Shach further writes that the Ramban spoke only as a matter of strict law, and the practical difference is that one can obligate himself in writing without mentioning his name. The words “and the practical difference” belong after the ensuing explanation. The Ramban means that under Talmudic law a person's handwriting has the same legal status as his signature in all respects. Nowadays, however, when accepted practice is that a person does not obligate himself through handwriting without a signature, he may certainly say that he wrote it merely as a draft, because it is known that people do not ordinarily create personal obligations in that manner. The practical difference is that even today, despite the ordinary practice, a person who expressly wishes to obligate himself may do so in his own handwriting without mentioning his name.
