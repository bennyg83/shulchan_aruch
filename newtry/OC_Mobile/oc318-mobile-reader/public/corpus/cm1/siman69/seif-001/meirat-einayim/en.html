He collects from unencumbered property. Whenever no witnesses are signed on the instrument, the loan does not become publicly known and purchasers do not know to protect themselves. Therefore the lender cannot collect from encumbered property, even though we rule that the lien of a debt exists by Torah law even for an oral loan.
Perhaps he signed his name at the top of a blank sheet. This implies that even if he does not make this claim, beit din advances it for him. The reason is that people are sometimes required to sign in this manner. For example, if he and another witness signed a document, the other witness is unavailable to authenticate his own signature, and there is no one who recognizes that signature except this witness and one additional person, beit din requires this witness to write his signature at the top of a sheet in the presence of witnesses and leave it with beit din for comparison, as explained by the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 46, seif 13. The Ir Shushan writes that perhaps he casually signed his name at the top of the sheet. This does not appear correct to me; the explanation I gave is preferable.
And he is not believed to say, 'There never was such a debt.' If the handwriting is recognized as his, he is not believed to say that he never borrowed and that he wrote the admission merely in jest or in order not to appear wealthy. Such claims are accepted only regarding an oral admission, not regarding a written admission in his own hand, as the Tur and Mechaber explain in siman 81, seif 17.
