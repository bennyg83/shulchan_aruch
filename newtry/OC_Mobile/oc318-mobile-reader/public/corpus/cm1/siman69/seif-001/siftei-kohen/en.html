He collects from unencumbered property. Since no witnesses are signed upon it, the obligation creates no publicity and purchasers cannot know to beware. The creditor therefore cannot collect from encumbered property, even though we rule that a debtor's lien may arise by Torah law even for an oral loan. See what I wrote at the beginning of siman 39, seif katan 2, that perhaps such a lien is not of Torah origin.
And he signed below. This means that he signed on a separate line beneath the text. If the first line states, 'I borrowed one hundred from so-and-so,' and his signature appears at the end of that same line, the document has no effect, according to Hagahot Ashri in the name of Rivam. Yet Tosafot, the Rosh, Mordechai in the name of Ri, Nimukei Yosef in the name of Rabbenu Tam and the Ritva, and Rabbenu Yerucham all indicate otherwise. They explain that a witness's signature in the middle of a line does not invalidate the document merely because someone might write an obligation above the signature, since this cannot create a valid obligation against another person. Their words show that the signature itself can support an obligation against the signer, contrary to Hagahot Ashri in the name of Rivam.
Perhaps he signed his name at the top of the sheet. This implies that even if he does not claim this specifically, but merely says that he owes nothing, beit din advances the possibility for him. At times a witness is required to sign at the top of a blank sheet so that his signature may later be compared, such as where he and another witness signed a deed and the other witness is unavailable and no one recognizes his signature except this witness and one additional person. Beit din may then require him to write his signature before witnesses and retain it. My teacher added that a person may have signed some other matter and left blank space beneath, after which someone cut away the signature together with that space and wrote whatever he wished above it. The Ir Shushan's concern that he may simply have signed his name idly is also plausible. It is more likely than assuming that beit din once required this particular signature. His word 'idly' may mean only that the signature was not given for an obligation, in accordance with his express conclusion.
Even if he did not write his name, but wrote, 'I owe so-and-so,' etc. If the defendant claims that he wrote it merely as a sample form, the Ritva cites authorities who believe him, although partners are different when their practice is to keep accounts in this form. The Bach argues that even the Ramban agrees, because the defendant has a migo that he could have pleaded payment, just as one may plead that a signed note was given in trust. In my view the Rashba's responsum cited below appears otherwise, although one might distinguish between writing a sample and writing for practice. The plain words of the Ramban, Tur, Mechaber, Maharik, and other authorities indicate that a person's handwriting has the same legal force as his signature. The Bach's proof from a trust document is not decisive, especially where the writing contains a clause of credibility. Nevertheless, it appears that the Ramban speaks only of the abstract law, meaning that one can obligate himself through handwriting even without expressly naming himself. In a society such as ours, where no one ordinarily presents an unsigned handwritten obligation against another except between partners or in comparable settings, the writer may certainly claim that it was only a sample, even if it contains a credibility clause. The Ritva also rejects the Ramban's proof from Gittin, and the Ran writes that a get is different because the husband must write his name, so his handwritten name functions as a signature, while handwriting without any signature may not suffice. Baal HaTerumot also cites an opinion that 'his handwriting' means specifically his signature, and Rabbenu Yerucham reports Rivam's view that the body of the writing adds nothing, whether written by the debtor or another. Accordingly, the practical ruling follows the distinction stated above. See also Responsa Mabit, part II, siman 292.
