From free property. Maharam Lublin, responsum 35, writes that a debtor's own handwritten obligation is a complete document for priority even against land. He says its date proves when it was delivered and one need not bring separate witnesses of delivery, because there is a presumption it was delivered on its written date. Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 47, indicates otherwise: with personal handwriting the date is not enough where later purchasers may be harmed, because of collusion. Maharam Alshich likewise rules that no priority exists for personal handwriting. Nevertheless, it appears that if the creditor or purchaser has already seized property, we do not remove it merely because the handwriting's date could theoretically be collusive. Tosafot at the beginning of Gittin discusses a get written entirely in the husband's handwriting without witnesses and asks, according to the opinion that dates in gittin protect the husband's produce rights, why such a get is problematic. If one fears the husband antedated the get in collusion with the wife so that she could wrongfully collect produce from purchasers, then one should simply refuse to rely on the date against purchasers. Tosafot answers that even if the date would not support active collection, the wife might seize the produce immediately after writing and say she had already been divorced as of that date. This implies that seizure can be effective on the strength of the written date even though the date is insufficient for initial extraction from purchasers. Maharam Alshich discusses Reuven, who sold a house to Shimon. Shimon later moved out, and Reuven returned to the house. Shimon then borrowed from Levi and pledged to him the original sale deed. Levi demanded either the house or its rent from Reuven. Reuven produced Shimon's own handwritten document saying that Shimon had sold the house back to Reuven before Levi's loan. Alshich ruled that Reuven did not prevail because the handwritten date could be collusive, citing Rashba. In my view, once Reuven is in possession of the house, the result should differ. Reuven is certainly a purchaser, while Levi's lien is doubtful. A doubtful lien cannot remove property from a definite purchaser. This resembles the Mishnah where a house falls upon a father and son and it is uncertain who died first. Beit Hillel leaves the property in its present presumption because a doubtful lien cannot remove from a definite heir. So too here, the house should remain with Reuven. I later saw Maharam Lublin's own discussion of Tosafot. He rejects the common reading that seizure helps because the possessor can say the date is genuine. He instead interprets Tosafot as fearing that the wife will seize produce only from the present date onward, not retroactively. But that is difficult. If the husband has not yet actually divorced her, she still has no right to present produce. The husband retains produce until divorce even if the get has been written. Ramban and Ran state this explicitly. Moreover, if the husband's own handwriting were a valid signed act in itself, the date issue would return. Therefore the simple reading of Tosafot, accepted by most students, is stronger: after seizure, the possessor can say the document was written on its stated date, and the burden is on the one seeking to remove the property.
