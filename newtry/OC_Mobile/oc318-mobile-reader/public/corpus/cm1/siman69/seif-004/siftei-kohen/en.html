A ledger, etc. The Mechaber’s ruling requires study. According to the Ramban and many authorities cited above, the reason a debtor may plead payment against handwriting is that it cannot collect from encumbered property, so he was not careful to retrieve it. That reasoning applies to a jointly maintained ledger as well. The Rosh appears to rely primarily upon a different reason, namely that handwriting creates no publicity and the debtor may forget that he gave it. In a ledger specifically maintained by the parties, with one witness, in which they regularly record all their business, forgetfulness is less plausible. Yet the Mechaber himself wrote in seif 2 that because handwriting is not a complete note the debtor is not careful to retrieve it, which appears to follow the Ramban. Moreover, even the Rosh spoke only in the particular case before him. There, the ledger was plainly established to clarify the entire business relationship, and neither party was permitted to alter what appeared in it unless both signed every change. It therefore functioned like handwriting containing a credibility clause. The Rosh also did not impose liability solely because of the ledger, but because of many additional circumstances showing that Rabbi Yisrael was lying. He concluded that even without the ledger Rabbi Yisrael owed more than the amount awarded. Further, Rabbi Yisrael repeatedly evaded Rabbi Shlomo’s summons concerning the ledger, so he could not plausibly claim that he paid and forgot to demand its return. He clearly knew that the ledger remained in Rabbi Shlomo’s possession. In fact, one item in the ledger referred to a note held by Rabbi Shlomo, while the torn note itself was in Rabbi Yisrael’s hand, yet the Rosh still held Rabbi Yisrael liable because of the unusual facts and other proofs. Ordinarily, a ledger and one witness would not suffice to impose payment where the defendant has the torn note. Thus the Rosh’s ruling was fact specific. In an ordinary case, one may say that the debtor paid and forgot that the ledger remained with the other party. The Mechaber’s unqualified ruling that one cannot plead payment against a ledger and one witness therefore requires study. The matter should depend upon the judges’ careful inquiry and assessment. Where the facts cannot be clarified, money cannot be extracted.
He is not believed, etc. The Sema cites the Rosh’s explanation that if payment had been made, the debtor would not have left the ledger in the other party’s hands, because both had signed it and added one witness to clarify all their dealings. This differs from the Ir Shushan’s reason that the witness acted as a stakeholder between them, a reason not found in the Rosh. That reason is also incorrect, for as the Tur and Mechaber rule in siman 54, if the debtor pleads payment, a stakeholder is no more credible than if the document emerged from the creditor’s own hand. The Bach gives yet another explanation, based on the Rambam’s view that one witness in a note makes the defendant one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear. The Rosh rightly did not use this reason, since nearly all authorities reject the Rambam’s position, as explained in siman 51, and the Bach himself rules against it there. Moreover, even the Rambam referred to a note formally written at the debtor’s instruction, whereas an entry in a ledger is merely written testimony, which is excluded by the principle that testimony must come from the witnesses’ mouths and not from their writing. The Rashba and Rama state in siman 39 that even two witnesses who write without being told to write and sign produce no more than a memorandum. Certainly this is true of one witness and of an actual ledger. Although the Rama cites an opinion that an authenticated writing may be adjudicated, I disputed that ruling there. In any event, even the Rama appears to agree that a true ledger entry written merely as a memorandum cannot be adjudicated, especially with only one witness. The only possible basis is therefore the Rosh’s fact specific reasoning, and as explained in the preceding seif katan, the Mechaber’s general rule requires study.
