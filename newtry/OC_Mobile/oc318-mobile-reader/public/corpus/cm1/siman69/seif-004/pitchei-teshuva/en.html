To say, "I paid." See Baer Heitev and Tumim, seif katan 5, who writes that with the bills of exchange commonly used in our times, all batei din in Israel have adopted the ruling that the debtor is not believed to say that he paid. This is not merely because of dina de-malkhuta. Since under the law of the kingdom the debtor cannot make such a claim, the creditor could collect through the secular courts or transfer the bill to a non-Jew who would collect through those courts. A person therefore does not ordinarily leave such a paid instrument in the creditor's hands, and under Torah law as well he is not believed to say that he paid. Tumim found this in Responsa attributed to the Ramban, siman 22. See there. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 128, who writes that if collection is sought from orphans' property, it is obvious that the creditor may collect only after taking an oath. Such a bill is no stronger than a document signed by witnesses. There is no distinction whether the inheritance is in the heirs' own possession or deposited with others. See there further. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, concerning Reuven, who sold his large house to Shimon with the customary warranty and then died. A non-Jew came with Reuven's handwritten obligation. Seeing that the rent from the house remaining with the heirs was small, and that under their law the house itself could not be sold but only its income taken, while repairs would consume the income, he turned instead to Shimon, purchaser of the large house. Shimon paid the non-Jew the principal together with expenses, interest, and penalties accruing after Reuven's death under their law, and the non-Jew delivered Reuven's handwritten instrument to Shimon, from whom he had collected. Beit Ephraim discusses at great length whether Shimon may recover from the heirs by virtue of the warranty their father accepted in the deed of sale, but reaches no definite conclusion. See there, and see below, siman 92, seif 12, seif katan 13, and siman 225, seif 2, seif katan 3. See also Noda BiYehudah, siman 10, a responsum by the author's son, concerning one who lent money on a customary bill of exchange. The bill was postdated: it was written in Nisan but dated for the following Tishrei, for reasons between the parties, and it contained no separate due date. Before Tishrei the debtor demanded return of the bill, asserting that he had already paid. The creditor had earlier said that he would search among his papers the next day, but now denied receiving payment. The question was who is believed. The son writes that although contemporary batei din universally rule that a debtor is not believed to claim payment against a bill drafted according to the law of the country, because dina de-malkhuta so provides, here the postdated bill was completely invalid under that law and exposed the creditor to a penalty. The matter must therefore be judged under Torah law, and the instrument is no better than an ordinary handwritten obligation, against which the debtor may claim payment. There is also a stronger reason: normally, because the debtor has no defense against such a bill under secular law, we presume he would not pay while leaving it in the creditor's hands lest the creditor sue him in secular court. Here, however, the debtor had no fear in leaving it with the creditor, because he could invalidate it in secular court, forcing the creditor to deny that he possessed any such bill. For this reason even authorities who ordinarily hold that one cannot claim payment against a handwritten obligation would agree here that he may do so, and the creditor cannot answer, "If you paid, why is your document in my hand?" When the debtor can render the document entirely void, he has no reason to insist on taking it back. The son nevertheless questions whether another consideration prevents the payment claim. During the interval before the date written in the bill, perhaps the debtor was not yet obligated to pay. That may be equivalent to a fixed due date, before which a debtor is not believed to claim payment, as in siman 78, or perhaps it is comparable to an ordinary loan during its first thirty days. He argues at length that the presumption that a person does not pay before the due date applies from the loan until the later date written in a postdated instrument, making the later date equivalent to a fixed term. Accordingly, even here the debtor might not be believed because the date of the bill had not yet arrived. In siman 11, the Noda BiYehudah himself replies to his son. He agrees that against a handwritten instrument which has no validity under the law of the kingdom the debtor is certainly believed, according to all opinions, to say that he paid, and the argument "If you paid, why is your document in my hand?" does not apply, since the document is merely a shard. According to this, perhaps even after the date written in the bill the debtor could claim payment, though the matter still requires study because subsequent clarification may be difficult. As to whether a knowingly postdated instrument creates the presumption that one does not pay before the due date during the period between execution and the written date, he writes that according to the authorities cited by the Ran in chapter 2 of Ketubot and by the Shach in siman 39, seif katan 8, who apply that presumption even to an ordinary loan during the first thirty days, the same would be true here. According to our ruling at the end of siman 78 that the presumption applies specifically where a term was demanded and fixed, however, even if the postdating was done at the debtor's request there is no such presumption. A debtor who requests a fixed term ordinarily does so because he needs the money until that time. One cannot explain that he requested time merely so that he could sell land in the interim without frightening purchasers, because postponing the due date does not lessen the creditor's lien, which begins at the time of the loan. By contrast, where a document made in Nisan is dated from Tishrei, even if the debtor requested this, there is no proof that he needed the money until then. He may have wanted to sell land in the interval, because the creditor's lien under a postdated document does not begin until the date appearing in it. At most this resembles an ordinary loan during the first thirty days. Nevertheless, the son's ruling was correct in the present case because that analysis applies only to a witnessed document creating a lien on land. A handwritten obligation or bill of exchange creates no lien on land and no right to seize from purchasers. Therefore the only plausible reason for postdating it was that the debtor would not have to pay until that later date. It is thus a true fixed term, and he cannot claim payment before its arrival. See there at length.
