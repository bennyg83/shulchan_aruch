Sema explains that where the rescuer agreed on a wage with a fisherman who abandoned his fishing in order to rescue, the entire agreed wage is paid so long as it was possible that he might have caught fish worth that amount, even if there is no proof that he actually would have done so. From Sema's language one might infer that if it is visibly impossible for the fisherman to have earned so much, he receives only the amount he could actually have earned. Rosh indicates otherwise. He writes that a person is not required to abandon his work to return a lost object or unload another's burden for less than his loss. If the owner or beit din tells him, "Leave your work and we will pay your entire wage," they must pay the full stipulated amount even if it exceeds his probable loss. Rosh compares the case to one who says, "Take a dinar and ferry me across," where the promised wage is paid because the ferryman can say he lost fish worth a zuz. Rosh concludes that once the worker suffered some loss, he receives the entire agreed wage. Maadanei Yom Tov explains this through the rule that where one benefits and the other suffers no loss, payment can be exempt, but once even some loss exists the entire benefit can be charged. So too here, the rescuer's body and labor were used for the other party's benefit. Once he suffered any real loss from abandoning his work, the entire stipulated wage can be enforced, even if the actual lost fishing profit was much smaller. Although later authorities do not all read Rosh this way, his language and Maadanei Yom Tov are explicit.
