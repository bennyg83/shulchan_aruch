See Responsa Chavot Yair 154, which writes that the same applies to an intercessor, broker, or matchmaker. If one tells him, "Intercede for me regarding this matter before the authorities," "Arrange for me a match with this particular woman," or "Broker for me this particular house," and fixes a fee without saying that payment is contingent upon successful completion, then if the agent labors but does not succeed, he receives the amount fixed, provided it is not grossly excessive relative to the work. If it is excessive, whether he is an intercessor or a broker, he receives only the customary amount appropriate for such labor and for a person of his standing, and even if he succeeds he receives no more. If, however, the agreement expressly states, "when the matter is completed," he receives nothing if it does not succeed, not even expenses he incurred unless those were separately stipulated. Chavot Yair 207 discusses a communal officer sent by the residents to the governor concerning a levy of one thousand Reichsthalers, for which they fixed his compensation. He succeeded in reducing the levy by half, but afterward they sought to reduce his fee for three reasons: first, they learned that in his first written submission he had already agreed to five hundred Reichsthalers, and they claimed he should have sought a much larger reduction; second, they believed his expenses were inflated, suspecting that because he procured for himself a charter of freedom in various commercial matters he had spent communal funds to win favor for his private requests; third, he remained there for an extended period, increased his food expenses, and conducted profitable private trade in silver and gold. The intercessor answered that these accusations were baseless, that not a single additional coin was spent for his private affairs, and that his immediate willingness to offer five hundred followed the advice of counselors and ministers, lest the governor become angry if offered an absurdly small sum. Chavot Yair ruled for the communal officer. The objections were merely complaints born of jealousy, with no definite claim, and the complainants deserved rebuke, for otherwise competent people would be deterred from undertaking communal missions. Even if the intercessor obtained some personal benefit from expenses that the community would have borne anyway, this is a case of one benefiting while the other suffers no loss. One who sends an intercessor intends that he exercise his own judgment, especially after consulting persons familiar with the governor's disposition. Reason itself dictates concern that an improper demand may provoke anger, as illustrated by Bathsheba's request to Solomon concerning Abishag and Adonijah.
