See Sema, seif katan 12. The Sema writes that when the owners are not present, one who goes down to rescue is treated as though he stipulated for compensation even if he ultimately failed to save. It appears that this is so only where, at the time he undertook the rescue, the prospect of success was sufficiently near that the mitzvah of returning lost property would have required him to attempt the rescue despite the expenses he incurred. If, however, he was primarily saving his own property and, when he began, there was no indication at all that his act would save his fellow's property, and he did not even know that his fellow's property was endangered, it is obvious that the fellow need not pay. There is no proof that he intended to act for the other's benefit. Even if he knew that his act might possibly help the other, so long as he did not regard that benefit as certain, perhaps we cannot say that the absence of the owner automatically makes the case equivalent to an express stipulation. This requires further study. Yet perhaps an ordinary person who acts under such circumstances is presumed to intend all available benefit, including benefit to himself, and the act may therefore still be treated as if compensation had been stipulated.
