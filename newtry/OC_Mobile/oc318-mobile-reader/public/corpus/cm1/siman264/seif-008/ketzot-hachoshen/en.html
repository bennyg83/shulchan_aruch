The ordinary reason the excessive promise can sometimes be reduced is that the rescuer can be told, "I was merely playing with you." A binding oath removes that defense. If the promisor dies, his heirs can also be liable. It is true that heirs do not ordinarily inherit their father's vow or oath. But here the underlying employment obligation itself is acquired through the verbal agreement. The only defect was the possible defense of jesting. Once the oath removes the jesting defense, an actual monetary debt remains, and the heirs inherit that debt. The same applies if the promised wage was to a poor person. A promise to give to the poor becomes a charitable vow even without a formal kinyan. Responsa Maimoniyot records a case where a father in law promised two litra to his poor son in law to study with his child. Because the recipient was poor, the promise became a binding charitable vow. The fact that the payment was connected to study did not weaken it, because a bare verbal promise of money to a poor person would already bind. Accordingly, where the rescuer is poor, the entire agreed wage can be enforceable through the law of charity even when an ordinary wealthy rescuer might face the "I was merely playing with you" defense.
