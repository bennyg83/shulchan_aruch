Misgeret HaShulchan cites Bach, Responsa 28, who ruled that a matchmaker must receive everything promised even where there is a communal regulation fixing the usual fee, because the regulation governs only where no special agreement was made. My grandfather, Panim Meirot I:97, cites Bach at length but disagrees, concluding that one should not depart from Rama: where a regulation fixes the fee, a promise of more does not entitle the matchmaker to more than the regulated amount. If the promisor gave collateral or a debt instrument, however, the matchmaker may at least plead reliance upon the authorities who enforce the larger promise. Panim Meirot notes that an ordinary pledge or instrument is not itself acquired unless the promisor conveys the pledge itself, yet possession may still support a claim of kim li. Responsa Chacham Tzvi 57 similarly implies that a matchmaker in actual possession may rely upon the authorities who enforce the entire stipulated fee, but holding a mere written debt instrument does not make him the monetary possessor, since we do not rule that an instrument standing for collection is deemed collected. Noda BiYehudah 5 likewise explains that possession of an instrument does not itself make its holder the possessor of the money, though it may deprive the opposing party of an absolute presumption. Perhaps Panim Meirot referred to a bearer instrument that could be delivered to anyone for collection; yet if so, it resembles actual payment and the additional argument of kim li would seemingly be unnecessary. Shevut Yaakov II:157 also rejects Bach and rules like Rama that a matchmaker receives only the proper fee unless the excess was already paid. He adds that even where money was not paid but collateral was delivered, the matchmaker may retain it until paid, because the matter is a doubt of law and one does not remove the pledge from his possession. Responsa Rama 87 rules similarly. A mere handwritten obligation, however, is insufficient unless accompanied by a complete acquisition. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if the promisor swore or gave a formal handshake undertaking to pay the larger amount, he must pay it all, because the ordinary ground for reducing an extravagant promise is that it was made in jest, and an oath removes that contention. Even if the promisor dies, his heirs must pay, since employment is acquired by verbal agreement. The same applies where the promised wage was dedicated to a poor person. Shevut Yaakov further discusses Reuven, who asked Shimon to secure for him a right of residence and promised one hundred Reichsthalers. He ruled for Shimon, because Rama himself, Responsa 86, holds that intercession, which is more demanding than ordinary brokerage, requires payment of the full amount stipulated, and a valuable right of residence in those countries plainly falls in that category. If the parties dispute what was agreed, the relevant oath applies. See also Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 91, discusses Reuven, who gave Shimon a formal handshake promise of one thousand gold coins if Shimon would persuade the bride's father to provide a full male inheritance instrument at the signing of the tena'im. Shimon succeeded, and the first tena'im recorded such an undertaking worth sixteen thousand gold coins. He postponed demanding his fee until after the wedding. Reuven then denied ever promising that amount, admitted only that Shimon had interceded, and asserted that at the wedding his father-in-law withdrew and gave only the customary half male inheritance instrument. Chatam Sofer writes that if no special amount was promised, beit din must assess the proper fee. But if the large fee was promised, even without the formal handshake it must be paid in full, because large fees are customary for such intercession, and the Shach additionally cites Maharshal that the entire amount is generally payable. Reuven must therefore take at least a Torah oath, since some compensation is unquestionably due. This assumes that the full instrument was ultimately given. If the father-in-law simply refused by force, one cannot collect the agreed fee solely because of the initial reconciliation, though if the arrangement was secured by binding acquisitions or the father-in-law substituted another benefit by agreement, the full fee is due. Because Reuven concealed the later tena'im and produced only ambiguous documents that mentioned a half instrument rather than a full one, there were grounds to require him to swear that he did not in fact receive the full instrument and that the change occurred by compulsion rather than by settlement. If he refuses that oath, he must at least swear the first, heset oath that he never promised the extravagant fee, after which beit din assesses the ordinary fee. A Torah oath cannot be generated merely by his refusal to take the additional oath that arises from circumstantial suspicion rather than a definite monetary claim.
