And in matchmaking. See Shach, seif katan 14, who says the same applies to a broker. Regarding whether a kinyan or written undertaking helps, Tumim in siman 81, seif katan 5, notes that these cases are compared to "Take a dinar" and to the case of one who demands excessive payment to perform chalitzah. Even HaEzer siman 169 says that a kinyan does not help there, based on Rashba. Yet at the end of siman 129 concerning a guarantor, also compared to "Take a dinar," Rashba cites Rosh that a kinyan does help. Tumim therefore understands a dispute between Rashba and Rosh. In my view there is no disagreement. Mordechai holds that the right to say, "I was merely fooling with you," applies specifically in matters of mitzvah. Rosh, responsum kelal 64, siman 3, extends it to any demand greatly exceeding proper compensation in the absence of kinyan. Rashba's rule that even a kinyan does not help concerns a mitzvah context, such as one who demands money to perform a religious obligation, or a ketubah guarantor where many authorities treat the undertaking as connected to mitzvah and as arising under coercive circumstances. But where the matter is not a mitzvah and the only reason the oral promise fails is that a person did not truly obligate himself by mere speech, a valid kinyan can certainly create the obligation. Tashbetz says explicitly that a kinyan helps in non-mitzvah matters. The remaining question is why, according to authorities who apply the rule even to brokers and matchmakers, one may refuse the excess payment without a kinyan. These are not mitzvah cases. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Kamma gives the reason as ona'ah, and the responsum Panim Me'irot, part 1, siman 97, cites authorities who answer the laborer case by saying a laborer has no ona'ah. This implies that brokerage and matchmaking are treated through ona'ah. One might then ask: in the case "Take a dinar and ferry me across," if the excessive price is ona'ah, why can the ferryman retain the money once it was already paid? The service is a contractor-type service, which can have ona'ah. The answer is that where the amount is beyond any range in which a normal mistake is plausible, ordinary ona'ah law does not apply in the usual manner. Bava Batra 78 concerning a yoke and oxen treats such a disparity as a gift. Taz in siman 227 likewise indicates that where the buyer knew the disparity and the transaction was subject to cancellation, he cannot later cancel after knowingly proceeding; he is treated as having waived. With a service that cannot afterward be returned, however, the person can say he never accepted the excessive price and merely made the promise to get himself out of immediate difficulty. This is the doctrine of "I was fooling with you." Because the provider demanded an improper overcharge, the recipient can rely on that doctrine and then demand correction of the ona'ah. If the excessive amount was already paid, Rashba's reason that there is no "fooling" after actual payment applies. Ona'ah alone does not necessarily permit recovery where the payer knowingly accepted an extreme amount, like a disparity beyond ordinary mistake. That is why the money remains with the recipient. Ritva in Kiddushin 8 says that if an object is worth the price only to the pressured buyer, an agreement made under pressure is not a true price agreement, and he proves this from the ferry case. He too speaks where the transaction cannot later simply be undone. The mechanism is the same: the promisor can say he was merely saying what was necessary to escape the pressure. The opinion that allows recovery even after payment follows authorities cited in Shitah Mekubetzet who allow the "fooling" claim even after payment. By contrast, where the sale object remains intact and can simply be returned, a buyer who knowingly pays an absurd disparity cannot ordinarily claim he was merely fooling. Why buy it in the first place if the plan was immediately to undo the transaction? The Gemara in Bava Batra therefore treats the excess as a gift. This also resolves Ketzot HaChoshen's question in siman 227 from pearls, where a person can value them unusually highly and yet ona'ah applies. The decisive issue is whether the object remains subject to normal market valuation and whether the particular transaction can be undone. This also supports Sema's statement there that even with a price large enough to void a sale, a person who knew of the overcharge may still claim in the special settings where the transaction cannot later be restored, like labor or ferry service. The practical conclusions are therefore: In matters of mitzvah, one can claim he was merely fooling with the demander, and even a kinyan may not help. In non-mitzvah services such as a laborer, broker, or matchmaker, if there is a true overcharge, even one beyond ordinary mistake, he can claim he was merely fooling unless there was a valid kinyan. If the service is one in which unusually large compensation is sometimes customary and there is no fixed measure, the disparity is not ona'ah and the promisor cannot use this claim. Tashbetz writes that if the extra amount is close to proper compensation, it is owed; only an excessive addition may be rejected. In my view "close" means within one sixth. Panim Me'irot shows that wherever ona'ah law does not apply, the "fooling" claim does not apply either. Thus an excess less than one sixth cannot ordinarily be rejected on that basis.
