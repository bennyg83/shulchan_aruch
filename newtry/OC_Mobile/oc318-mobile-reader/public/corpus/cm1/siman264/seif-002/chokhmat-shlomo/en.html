Seif 7, gloss. In matchmaking he receives only the ordinary fee even if the other promised him much more. Note: It requires study how the Rama rules this so simply. Mordechai in HaGozel cites many authorities who hold that the full amount promised must be paid. He also cites a distinction: where the person was already obligated to perform the act, the excessive promise need not be paid, but where he had no prior duty to act, the full stipulated amount is due. The dissenting view should at least have been mentioned, particularly where the matchmaker has already seized payment. Rashi and the Rosh in Yevamot, chapter Mitzvat Chalitzah, likewise imply that the defense "I was merely jesting with you" applies because the other person had a duty to perform the chalitzah. Where there is no such preexisting duty, that defense should not apply. The Rosh's formulation shows that the woman is like one under compulsion because she cannot marry without the chalitzah and the other party suffers no loss. In an ordinary matchmaking case there is no comparable compulsion, so why should the full promised amount not be owed? Although a responsum of the Rosh says otherwise, we generally follow his rulings. I am also troubled by the rule concerning one fleeing prison who promises an excessive fee for a ferry and pays only the ordinary amount. Where a worker's fee has no fixed market rate, the authorities generally require payment of the amount stipulated. A hired worker is not subject to the law of overreaching. Although an independent contractor may be, the rationale is that he can perform whenever he wishes. A ferryman hired by an escaping prisoner must act immediately and is more like a day laborer. One might suggest that payment also includes rental of the boat, which is subject to overreaching, analogous to hiring a worker together with his horse. But if so, the Gemara and codifiers should have said so. Nor can one say that an unusually high promise itself permits the employer to say he was jesting, for then every worker could raise that defense despite the rule that workers are not subject to overreaching. The authorities do not limit the rule to cases where the fee was prepaid. The matter fits well according to those who restrict the "jesting" defense to a mitzvah or other preexisting obligation, but according to the Rama's broad ruling it remains very difficult. See my Yoreh Deah, fourth edition, Hilkhot Bikkur Cholim, siman 157.
