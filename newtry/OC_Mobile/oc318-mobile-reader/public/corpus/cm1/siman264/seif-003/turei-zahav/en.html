(Seif 4, specifically where he stipulated.) Maharam of Padua writes plainly that the Gemara’s rule, “he descended to save and did not save, he receives only his wages,” concerns a case where he stipulated. Without a stipulation, even if he saved the property he receives only his wages, not the value of his donkey. If he did not save anything, since the owner received no benefit, he need not pay even wages. I have not understood the Sema, seif katan 11, who equates a case without a stipulation to one with a stipulation. (Likewise, if his donkey came up on its own.) This means the donkey of the person whose property was being saved. Nimukei Yosef writes in HaOmanin in the name of Ramban, citing the Yerushalmi, that where the property was saved on its own, the law is like one who descended to save and did not save. Beit Yosef cites this in siman 334. Although the substance of the ruling is correct, it concerns a case where the owner could have saved only with difficulty. Otherwise he need not pay even wages, for the rescuer merely drove away a lion from another’s property. As stated at the beginning of HaKones, one does not pay even wages except where the loss was certain, like the case of property swept by a river. The Gemara explicitly establishes that case where the owner could save with difficulty. It resembles the case mentioned by the Sema, seif katan 12, of a government official who seized collateral, which is the very ruling later mentioned by the Rama: “If it would not have been released except through his efforts,” and so forth.
