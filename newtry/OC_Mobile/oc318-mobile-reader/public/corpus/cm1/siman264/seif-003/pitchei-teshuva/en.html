Even where he saved only his own property, etc. Responsa Beit Yaakov 148 questions this ruling from the Gemara in Bava Batra, which requires the townspeople to compel one another to build a wall, gates, and a bar, and even collects from orphans for measures that provide protection. There the other residents would have had to spend the same amount even without the orphans, yet the orphans must still contribute. He also cites Responsa Maharam 59 to this effect and argues that Rama should not have ruled simply against it. Responsa Shevut Yaakov I:158 rejects Beit Yaakov's objection and maintains that Rama's ruling here is correct, as Responsa Emunat Shmuel 36 also rules. The distinction is between a matter affecting a community as a whole and a private dispute between two individuals. Otherwise Rama would contradict his own ruling above, siman 163:10, concerning an accusation imposed upon an entire city. Shevut Yaakov therefore ruled in a case of a great fire in Prague where a building in the Jewish street had three stories. The owner of the upper story, seeing the fire approaching, broke apart the roof so that the house would not catch fire, thereby saving the entire building. He later demanded contribution from the lower owners toward replacing the roof. Shevut Yaakov ruled that his claim was unfounded, because the upper owner had been compelled to take this measure to save his own property, while the lower owners benefited incidentally without causing him additional loss. In Shevut Yaakov II:156, however, the authorities themselves ordered roofs of neighboring houses broken to halt a fire. There he ruled that the lower owners must contribute toward the repair, because the roofs were broken by governmental order for the rescue of all owners together, unlike the earlier case where the upper owner acted for himself and the lower one was saved incidentally. Since in ordinary circumstances the upper owner bears responsibility for roof repairs, the benefit he receives from now obtaining a new roof must first be assessed and deducted, and the remainder is shared equally. See also Chatam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, end of 239. Responsa Pri Tevuah 58 discusses Reuven, who claimed an oral debt of twenty gold coins from Shimon. Shimon admitted the debt but sought to deduct five coins. During a recent fire Shimon hired non-Jews for ten coins to rescue two chests from his house. When they arrived, his wife had already saved one chest, and they rescued only the other, but refused to return five coins. Shimon therefore told them to rescue a second chest from his neighbor Reuven's house. He informed Reuven that the remaining five coins were already in their hands and demanded that Reuven reimburse him. Reuven replied that the money would be lost to them regardless and that Shimon should therefore do him this favor without charge. Shimon insisted upon reimbursement and, relying on that demand, ordered the workers to rescue Reuven's chest. He now sought to deduct the five coins from his debt. Reuven responded that he had expressly refused to pay, and that the rescue was incomplete because when the fire later intensified he had to hire others to carry the chest to the river embankment. The questioner thought Reuven should prevail because the workers would not have returned the five coins in any event, making this a case of one benefiting while the other loses nothing, and because Reuven expressly declared that he did not wish to pay. Pri Tevuah rejected this. Since Shimon could have sent the workers into another person's house and earned those five coins for rescuing someone else's property, his opportunity had value. This resembles Rama, siman 363:6, citing Mordechai, and also the ruling of the Tur in the name of the Rosh in siman 174. Since Shimon is in possession, he may say that he relies upon those authorities. The questioner's reliance on Shach, siman 335:2, is mistaken. The Shach's case of teaching another man's son concerns something that is not regarded as a definite monetary benefit. Where there is certain benefit, the beneficiary's declaration that he does not wish to pay does not exempt him. Although Rama at the beginning of siman 336 might seem to imply that one who benefits another may collect only if the beneficiary knew and remained silent, that discussion concerns entitlement to the entire stipulated wage rather than merely the lowest market wage. Accordingly, even though Reuven later had to hire others to move the chest farther, Shimon is still entitled to compensation, comparable to siman 310:2 concerning one who hires a particular donkey and it fails midway, where the owner nevertheless receives the wage for the portion of the journey performed.
