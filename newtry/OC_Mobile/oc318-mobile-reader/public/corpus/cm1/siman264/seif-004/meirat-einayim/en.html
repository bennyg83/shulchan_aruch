Specifically where he stipulated with the owners. Meaning the condition stated above, that they pay him the value of his donkey if he allows his own donkey to be lost while rescuing theirs. It appears that even if he did not specify that exact amount and merely stipulated generally for payment, they must at least pay the normal rescue wage, though not necessarily the full value of his lost donkey. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who understands Rama to require an express condition that they pay his wage if the rescue fails. If that were the stipulation, then even if he failed to save their donkey they would owe him under the ordinary rule that monetary conditions are binding. Moreover, Rama's limitation is not stated explicitly in the Gemara but is inferred because the law under discussion continues the Mishnah's case in which the rescuer stipulated for payment of his donkey's value. That Mishnah speaks of a general stipulation. The point is to exclude one who simply descends to rescue without any stipulation. If he saves the other person's donkey but his own is lost because of the rescue, he receives only the ordinary rescue wage, like another rescuer who suffered no such loss. If he fails to save the other donkey or it rises by itself so that his efforts produce no benefit, he receives not even ordinary compensation unless there was a stipulation.
Likewise, if his companion's donkey rose by itself, etc. Meaning, even if he stipulated before descending that he would rescue it in exchange for payment of his own donkey's value, then descended, lost his own donkey, but the other donkey rose by itself, he receives only the ordinary hire of a rescuer, because the benefit did not result from his rescue efforts. If he made no condition with the owner or with beit din before acting, then even ordinary hire is not due where he did not actually save it. It appears, however, that in these cases of descending unsuccessfully or the other donkey rising by itself, if the owners were absent, his act should be treated as though he had stipulated with them and he should at least receive ordinary compensation, just as one would pay another rescuer. In my responsum I argued at length against the proof cited by Rama and Maharam Padua for the proposition that even after a condition, if the donkey rises by itself he receives only ordinary wages. In my view, where he conditioned and suffered actual loss because of the attempted rescue, he may be entitled to the full value of his donkey. See there.
Although his factual claim is true, nevertheless he is liable, etc. Darkei Moshe challenges Maharam Padua from Mordechai. Two partners held one pledge. A ruler associated with one partner seized the pledge from him, and that partner spent money to recover it. The second partner refused to contribute, saying that his own ruler was stronger and could have recovered the pledge for free. Mordechai rules that he need pay only the ordinary value of the service, implying that some compensation is due even if the second partner's claim was true. Rama answers that there the stronger ruler had not actually recovered it. Perhaps he could have, but the benefit had already been produced by the first partner's efforts. That differs from the present case, where the item actually came out or was saved on its own without the claimant's effort. This is Rama's meaning in saying, "if it did not come out except through his efforts, etc., even though the truth is with him."
