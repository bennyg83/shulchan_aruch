An armed band came to plunder it. Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Gezelah 12, note 9, records that the Yerushalmi does not read "to plunder it" as does our baraita. Or Zarua infers that because the Yerushalmi does not say "to plunder them," it speaks of a case where the band has already begun plundering. Chidushei Anshei Shem explains that once the plundering has begun it is clear that they came for money, so the loss is apportioned according to each person's wealth; while they merely stand poised to attack, the danger may still be to life and the expense cannot be allocated solely by wealth. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 100, calls this forced and suggests instead that the passage is not about a ransom paid to the attackers, but about travelers whose money was pooled and some was actually seized, in which case the loss is allocated in proportion to their money, as in siman 292:10. Maharshal, Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma 10:42, also cites Or Zarua and understands it as actual plunder. He struggles with the case: if the travelers could still rescue their goods with difficulty and do not do so, why should they divide the loss by wealth, since each owner seemingly despaired of what was taken; and if rescue is impossible, the goods are plainly ownerless. He therefore explains that they can rescue with difficulty. Their decision not to exert themselves to save each person's property separately is presumed to rest upon an understanding that the total loss will be shared in proportion to their respective wealth, as though nothing had yet been seized. Each person is obligated to assist in rescuing his companion, and this is the presumed basis on which they refrained from separate rescue. Chacham Tzvi 132 explains that Maharshal requires "rescue with difficulty" because if rescue were possible without difficulty it would be obvious that those who did not rescue are exempt: one cannot say they refrained because they intended to pay proportionately, for if that were their intention they would certainly have rescued rather than willingly lose their own money. Bach likewise cites Or Zarua and concludes that even if much was taken from one and little from another, or only from some travelers, they afterward calculate the loss according to their wealth. He distinguishes the case of a ruler imposing an accusation where some residents flee, because those who flee are no longer in danger, whereas every member of a caravan confronted by a band of robbers remains exposed to monetary danger and cannot simply escape. His language implies that he applies proportional contribution even where rescue is impossible. Chacham Tzvi 132 strongly rejects both Maharshal's extension and Bach's ruling and concludes that with the armed robbers common in his time, whom one cannot safely resist, even Maharshal would agree that those whose money was not taken need not contribute at all. Maharshal's reason depends entirely upon the possibility of rescue with difficulty; armed robbers create an unavoidable loss. Bach therefore stands alone, and even if his view were expressly stated in the Yerushalmi one would not rely upon a lone view against all other authorities in monetary law. Maharik already ruled that one cannot plead kim li like a single authority against the consensus of Israel's sages, and Radbaz added that even two authorities do not suffice in such circumstances. Thus even an original possessor could not rely upon Bach, all the more so one who seized only after the doubt arose. Chacham Tzvi therefore concludes that the other members of the caravan are entirely exempt from contributing to the person who was robbed.
