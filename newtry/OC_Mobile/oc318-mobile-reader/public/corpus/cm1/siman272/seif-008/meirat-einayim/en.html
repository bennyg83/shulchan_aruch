He is not obligated to attend to it. In the Perishah I explained that Rambam speaks here only of loading. Therefore he is not required to help, because the mitzvah of loading was not commanded regarding a non-Jew's animal. But regarding unloading, Rambam too holds that he is obligated by Torah law because of animal suffering, though he may accept payment. When Rambam continues, "but if not, he is obligated to unload and load, etc.," he means that if a Jew is also present, he must load just as he must unload. Unloading is obvious. Likewise, in the final clause where both the animal and load belong to a non-Jew and Rambam says he need attend to it only because of hostility, he speaks only of loading. The reason he says here "he is not obligated to attend to it" and does not require assistance because of hostility is that since the load belongs to a Jew and he nevertheless does not help load the Jew's own load, there is no hostility when he also does not help on account of the non-Jew's animal. The non-Jew is no better than the Jew whose load he sees being left without assistance. Where both animal and load belong to the non-Jew, however, hostility does arise. The Beit Yosef explains similarly. The Tur holds that hostility applies even here and therefore challenges Rambam. See the Derishah.
Because of the Jew's distress. Meaning, if he does not help with the loading, the Jew will have to remain there. Although "distress of a Jew" is not expressly mentioned elsewhere, Rambam holds that it is certainly no less significant than animal suffering. He may nevertheless accept payment. Rambam mentions the Jew's distress rather than animal suffering because loading itself does not involve animal suffering.
