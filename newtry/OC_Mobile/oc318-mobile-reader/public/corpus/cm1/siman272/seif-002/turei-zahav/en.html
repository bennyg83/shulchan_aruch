(Seif 9, likewise, if it was.) Rambam’s ruling may be defended against the Tur’s objection. Indeed, concern for hostility applies in every case. One cannot say that it applies only where both the animal and load belong to a gentile, but that where the load belongs to a Jew the gentile will not resent the refusal because he sees that the Jew does not assist even his fellow Jew, as Beit Yosef cites from the commentators. If that were the distinction, the latter clause, “but if the animal belongs to a gentile,” would teach the reverse of the first clause: where both belong to a gentile there is only concern for hostility, unlike the first clause. Rambam should then have written, “but if the animal and load belong to a gentile, he is obligated because of hostility.” Why does he instead write “he is not obligated,” which sounds like a leniency? It appears to me that the latter clause excludes the Torah obligation of the first clause. In the first clause Rambam distinguishes between Torah liability and exemption. One might object that unloading is nevertheless a Torah obligation because of animal suffering, so why mention the Jew’s distress? The case is where the gentile driver himself unloads and reloads, so there is no animal suffering. The Jew need not assist the gentile under Torah law. But if a Jew is also present and distressed, the observer must assist under Torah law because the Jew’s distress is included in the concern for animal suffering. Rambam’s later statement, “Likewise, if the animal belongs to a Jew,” rests on the Jew’s distress, not on the mitzvah of unloading, as the Tur objected. The mitzvah of unloading applies when the animal is crouching under its burden, whereas here the Jew is himself unloading and only requires assistance because of his own distress. In all these cases Rambam states a Torah obligation. When both animal and load belong to a gentile, however, there is no obligation except the rabbinic concern for hostility. This appears correct. The Sema explains differently.
