But if one of them contradicts the witness, there is no concern. This wording can be understood to mean that there is no concern at all, even for the party who does not contradict the witness. This would apply where the contradicting party does not say, 'it never happened,' but says, 'I was jesting and did not intend kiddushin.' He is believed through a migo, since he could have denied the witness altogether. If one party did not intend kiddushin, the second party likewise need not observe the kiddushin. But if one party claims that it never happened while the second claims that the kiddushin took place before one witness, why should that second party not be prohibited to the first party's relatives according to the view that takes one-witness kiddushin into account? Furthermore, even regarding a claim of jesting there is room to question whether, just as such a claim is ineffective before two witnesses because unexpressed intentions have no legal effect, the same should apply before one witness. What does it help that the party has a migo? We are not saying that we disbelieve the party. Rather, the reason is that unexpressed intentions have no legal effect, and even if we know that their intention was to jest, we nevertheless follow the spoken words and not the unexpressed intention. The Bach distinguishes between one witness and two witnesses regarding a claim of jesting and rules that before one witness the party is believed to say that it was merely jesting, and he reports having ruled this way in practice. His words are not compelling enough to permit the matter initially. See Responsa Maharam Padua, siman 32, where he tends toward stringency as to whether contradiction by a litigant is effective, though in siman 37 he retracts. See there and Darkei Moshe.
