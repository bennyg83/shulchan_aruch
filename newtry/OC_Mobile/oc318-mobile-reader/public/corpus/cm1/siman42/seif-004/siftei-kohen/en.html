From three through ten. When the subject of the document is grammatically feminine, one should initially write "thirteen" in the feminine form as well.
Gloss: If he wrote "three," etc. If so, the statement that one should not write numbers from three through ten cannot be literal, because with the number eight there is no concern that it can be altered. Alternatively, the authorities were not precise and merely followed the flow of the Gemara's language. The Gemara discusses Aramaic, where the word for eight can be altered into eighty. This answers the difficulty left by Tosefot Chachamim in chapter Get Pashut based on the number eight. Raavan, page 110b, however, explicitly writes that even in masculine language one should not write "three" or "eight," because a forger may erase the final letter and alter the word. Tosefot Chachamim there writes that Raavan's words are correct. See there.
If it happened to appear at the end of a line, etc. The Bach writes that even if the document first wrote "three" in the middle of a line, if it later repeated "three" at the end of a line, the document is invalid, because the latter occurrence can still be altered and the rule is that the lower text governs. This conclusion is not compelled. Examine it carefully.
For example, two or four. Ir Shushan writes that if the amount is written by numerical notation or initials, for example twelve or thirteen, the document is valid because it cannot be altered. We need not fear that it originally said two or three and the forger added a ten, because we presume that witnesses act properly and would not have signed if the original text had merely said two or three in a way that left room for such an addition. The Sema challenges this, asking why one could not write three, since that numeral cannot be transformed into another letter, and says Ir Shushan should simply have explained that the witnesses would not sign if sufficient blank space remained for an addition. This is no challenge for two reasons. First, that is itself Ir Shushan's intent: if two or three had been written with dangerous blank space, the witnesses would not have signed. Second, in ordinary handwriting the Hebrew numeral for three can be altered into the numeral for fifty. See Responsa Rama, siman 44, and Responsa Mabit, part 1, second set of questions, siman 155.
