The wording customarily used. See Responsa Maharshal, part III, simanim 46, 48, and 116, and Responsa Maharanach, end of siman 21.
Even if it was not written, it is judged as though written. The reason is that the party presumably intended the customary clause to be included. It follows plainly that this applies only when he is known to have been aware of the custom. If he did not know it, the clause is not treated as written merely because laypeople customarily include it. This explains the Rambam's ruling concerning the enactment of the Geonim that a woman may collect her ketubah from movable property. Where communities customarily write an express lien on land and movables, a ketubah written without that clause is collected from movables only if the husband knew of the enactment. When his knowledge is uncertain, the judges must deliberate carefully, because the enactment does not operate like an automatic condition of the ketubah. Where all documents omit the clause but everyone nevertheless collects from movables, a person cannot plausibly claim ignorance, since ordinary practice reveals the enactment. Where documents routinely include the clause, however, one may say that he believed collection from movables depended upon its express inclusion and deliberately omitted it. This is the Rambam's distinction and the Ribash's explanation. The Rashba indeed rules that a locally customary reservation is treated as written, like a condition imposed by beit din. Yet the Rashba also writes concerning a document designed to shield assets that even a standard formula may sometimes require proof that the woman knew the scribes used it, because one cannot assume that every woman knows technical documentary language. The matter therefore depends upon the judges' assessment. These sources show that customary wording is treated as included only when the party knew that it was ordinarily written. This is my considered view.
