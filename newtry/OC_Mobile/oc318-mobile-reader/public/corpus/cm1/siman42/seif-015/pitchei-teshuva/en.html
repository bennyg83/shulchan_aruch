Since he raised him. See Tumim, who cites Knesset HaGedolah as distinguishing that this applies only where the person who raised the child has no children of his own. Tumim writes that he does not know the source for this distinction, because the reason given in the responsa of Maimonides, that one who raises an orphan in his home is considered as though he fathered him, together with the element of affection, applies even where he has children. See there. Chatam Sofer, Even HaEzer, responsum 76, however, writes that the reasoning of Knesset HaGedolah is sound. Where a person has no children, it is natural that out of affection he might call the child “my son,” without concern that this wording could later cause damage to his heirs, since he has no children of his own and may even prefer that his beloved stepson inherit rather than his brothers or other relatives. Everyday experience supports this. But where he has biological children, he is very concerned that such wording not create a mistake that could deprive his own children of their inheritance. There is a strong presumption that a person does not abandon his own child and give to others. Therefore a document written in such terms is invalid. On this distinction Chatam Sofer also resolves Tosafot in Pesachim 54a. See there further.
