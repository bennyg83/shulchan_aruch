The wording of the Mechaber itself also indicates that he did not state the matter conclusively. He further discusses a case in which Reuven, at his son’s marriage, gave his son a deed of gift to one half of his house, reserving for himself the right to live there throughout his lifetime. The deed further stated that if the couple wished to give Reuven’s heirs a specified sum one half-hour before Reuven’s death in exchange for the second half of the house, and paid that sum then, the second half would also belong entirely to the couple. Reuven died leaving three heirs, the son who received the deed and two others. The other children argued that “Reuven’s heirs” in the deed meant the heirs other than that son, while the son argued that it meant all the heirs, including himself, and that he should receive one third of the payment. Knesset Yechezkel ruled that if the couple never exercised the option to buy the second half, that half remains part of the estate and the son shares equally with his siblings. Likewise, if the couple did not notify beit din before Reuven’s death that they were ready to pay, the half-house belongs to the estate and is divided into three shares according to its current value. But if they declared before beit din or witnesses during Reuven’s lifetime that they were prepared to pay, the doubt concerns division of the money. In that case one follows possession. The Gemara in Bava Batra 138 teaches that an unnecessary expression may be expounded to add something, and numerous responsa apply this principle. Here the words “to Reuven’s heirs” seem unnecessary, especially since payment was to be made before his death, when the money would ordinarily have been paid to Reuven himself. This suggests that the added phrase was intended to include the other heirs. The Beit Yosef, however, at the end of siman 61, cites the Rashba that we lack authority to expound an unnecessary expression beyond the examples stated in the Gemara, and many responsa follow him. The apparent contradiction within the Rashba’s own responsa is best resolved, as the Radbaz explains, by distinguishing between extracting money and supporting one who is already in possession. To extract money, one does not use an extra phrase to expand liability unless the Gemara expressly does so, but in favor of a possessor one may. We must therefore determine who is in possession here. During the father’s lifetime the house remained in his possession. Even if the son lived there, there is no presumption of ownership against a father under the circumstances described. If, after Reuven’s death, the son lived there for three years without protest and the other heirs were adults, he becomes the possessor and receives one third of the payment. If the other heirs were minors, who cannot effectively protest, his possession does not establish a chazakah against them, even if they later became adults, in accordance with the Rama. The house therefore remains in the orphans’ possession and the son receives none of the payment. If one heir was an adult and one a minor, the son is considered in possession vis-a-vis the adult but an extractor vis-a-vis the minor. The minor then receives one half of the payment, the adult one third, and the son one sixth. If one of the heirs is a firstborn, division is adjusted proportionately into four inheritance shares. See Baer Heitev through his discussion of a case where the scribe says that his intention included all of the upper stories, and the ruling is in favor of the lender. See Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, responsum 8, concerning an obligor who argued that a clause in a document stating, “When I collect the bills I will deliver half to so-and-so,” concerned property not yet in his possession and was therefore ineffective. It was further argued against him that the phrase “belongs to so-and-so” is language of admission. Counsel for the obligor claimed that “belongs” has two possible meanings: absolute ownership, or merely some legal relation, such as a lien. How, then, can he be charged on the basis of a doubt? He further claimed that the rule that documents follow ordinary speech applies only where speech itself completes a legal act, such as a sale, kiddushin, or vows; but where liability is imposed based upon an admission, the speaker may explain his own words even by a strained interpretation. Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg rejects this at length. The correct distinction, he writes, is that when a person comes to obligate himself, admit a fact, or transfer property, and certainly where he puts that obligation, admission, or transfer into a written instrument, he is presumed to have chosen his language carefully. There is no difference whether he wrote it himself or witnesses wrote it at his instruction. He therefore cannot later contradict the document or force upon it an unnatural interpretation. His language is presumed to carry its ordinary meaning as understood by listeners. By contrast, when he is merely presenting a claim before beit din and is not then undertaking an obligation, transfer, or admission, although he cannot wholly retract his claim, he may explain it and provide an explanation even if somewhat remote. Thus, one who pleaded before beit din, “I mortgaged to him a house up to the end of the upper story,” may afterward explain that he intended only one upper story, and this is not deemed a new and contradictory plea. The proof from the responsum of Raanach is likewise inapposite, because that case concerned a written compromise drafted by arbitrators, where one party argued that a certain matter was not included. Since the document was not written at that party’s instruction but reflected the arbitrators’ own assessment, he could claim that they did not intend to include the disputed matter, even by a somewhat strained interpretation, and the ordinary rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant applies. Raanach’s proof from a litigant who could explain the phrase “it belonged to my ancestors” as meaning that he relied upon the possessor as upon his ancestors likewise concerns a mere litigation claim, not a deliberate written obligation or admission. See there further regarding the distinction between a person explaining his own intention and a party attempting to explain the intention of arbitrators. See Shevut Yaakov, part III, responsum 156, concerning Reuven who borrowed money from Shimon and expressly made his seat in the synagogue an apotiki, stipulating that if he did not repay within two years the creditor could collect from the seat, and further stipulating that Reuven could not sell or mortgage the seat before first paying the debt. Reuven then wished to rent out the seat until the repayment date. Shevut Yaakov ruled that he may rent it throughout the loan term. Even if rental for a day has some legal character of sale, and even if the apotiki document contains strong clauses negating asmachta and standard-form limitations, documents are interpreted according to ordinary speech and the parties’ intention. The prohibition against “selling or mortgaging” means an actual sale or mortgage that would weaken the creditor’s apotiki by forcing him to pursue a purchaser or locate mortgaged property. Ordinary speech does not include rental in that language. The creditor suffers no disadvantage whether the debtor himself sits in the seat until the due date or another person sits there.
