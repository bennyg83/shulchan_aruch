The lender shall swear and collect. This is Rambam's ruling. Baal HaTerumot says the matter is not clear to him. Beit Yosef asks why this differs from an ordinary claim of repayment, where the creditor holding a document must take an oath before collecting. Shach answers that the borrower's claim resembles saying the document was a trust document. Such a claim does not ordinarily require the creditor to swear. This requires study. If the document's ordinary meaning indicates a particular currency and the borrower says he actually owes only a lower-value currency, that is like saying the document is partially a trust document: it states one hundred while only ninety were really advanced. If so, even the original document witnesses, when their signatures are not independently authenticated, should not be believed to say the currency was lower, just as witnesses are not believed to say the document was a trust document. Outside witnesses would likewise create a two-against-two situation. Yet Baal HaTerumot agrees that witnesses can establish that the parties lived in a place where the lower currency was used. Therefore this is not truly a trust-document claim. The original witnesses did not necessarily contemplate that the creditor would later sue in a different place whose currency has a different value. Their testimony was not false. Thus Beit Yosef's question remains. Ritva and Ran provide the key. They hold that the reason the document does not specify the place of the loan is that the parties implicitly agreed the debtor would pay according to the currency of whatever place the creditor later sues him. Baal HaTerumot can accept this basic reasoning yet add the principle that the holder of the document has the lower hand. The omission of the loan location shows there was an agreement concerning payment elsewhere, but the creditor was not necessarily given absolute credibility against witnesses concerning what the original currency was. Therefore, if witnesses prove the loan arose in a lower-currency place, Rambam's ruling can be accepted. But where the only challenge is the borrower's own claim, Baal HaTerumot is uncertain whether the creditor should need an oath, because the very omission may indicate that the parties trusted the document's holder regarding the place or currency. This resembles an ordinary trust clause: it can remove an oath but does not overcome witnesses. If the document expressly states the place of the loan and the borrower nevertheless says the actual currency was lower, that should be treated as a partial trust-document claim. The document explicitly says one thing and he says the substantive transaction was different. In such a case, if the original witnesses' signatures are independently authenticated, their later statement that the currency was lower should not simply overturn the document; outside witnesses would create ordinary conflicting testimony. Where the creditor sues in a place using a lighter currency and the borrower resides there, Rambam would still permit the borrower to demand a hesset oath if the creditor claims he originally lent heavier currency. According to Ran, however, the omitted location itself can be treated as a waiver or condition to pay according to the place of suit. In that model, no interest problem arises even though a lighter currency may later require repayment in heavier units elsewhere, because the agreement also carries the opposite risk. If the borrower moves to a place with even lighter currency, the lender can lose. The poskim generally follow the value of the currency at the time of the loan when the currency itself later changes in the same place. Rashba likewise writes that the operative reference point is the time of the loan, not the time of repayment. This creates a question concerning postdated documents. If the currency became heavier between the true loan date and the later date written in the document, an innocent reader could think the loan occurred under the heavier currency and require repayment accordingly, harming the borrower. Why then are postdated documents valid? Perhaps currency changes are uncommon enough that Chazal did not invalidate postdated documents for this remote concern. The answer is somewhat strained, but the law appears to require it.
