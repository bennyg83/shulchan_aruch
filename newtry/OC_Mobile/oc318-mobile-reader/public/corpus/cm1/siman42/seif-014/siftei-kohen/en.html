The creditor shall swear and collect. Baal HaTerumot writes that this ruling is not clear. The Beit Yosef asks why, since the borrower's claim resembles a plea of payment, against which the creditor swears and collects. Gidulei Terumah explains that the borrower did not demand an oath, but this is unsatisfactory. The Rambam presumably also requires the oath only where the borrower says, “Swear to me,” no more than in an ordinary plea of payment. Rather, Baal HaTerumot's reason is that the creditor is believed because the ordinary presumption is that the loan was originally made as the document indicates. The borrower's contrary claim therefore resembles a claim that the note was merely one of trust, not a claim of payment. A note is ordinarily intended to stand until payment, but there is no parallel presumption supporting the borrower's allegation here. The Rambam and later authorities make this distinction in siman 82. Accordingly, the creditor should collect on the strength of his note without first taking an oath, after which the borrower may impose a hesset oath upon him. This is why Baal HaTerumot wrote that the Rambam's requirement was unclear.
