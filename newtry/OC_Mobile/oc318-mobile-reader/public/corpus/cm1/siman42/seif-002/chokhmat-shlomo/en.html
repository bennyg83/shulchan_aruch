Seif 15. Rather, we follow language that ordinary people customarily write in that place, and even if it was not written we judge it as though it had been written. See Shach's lengthy discussion. See also my work Mizbeach Avanim on Even HaEzer 54, where I discussed Beit Shmuel and distinguished between cases in which we presume that the obligating party knew the custom and cases in which such knowledge is unnecessary. The distinction is whether the custom operates only through the obligor's presumed consent or whether the beneficiary's understanding under the prevailing custom is itself sufficient. Shach's words also require study. Ran, citing Rashba, writes that the matter depends on the judge's assessment, since one cannot assume that a woman knows the standard wording of legal documents. This implies that only with a woman do we fear that she did not know the customary language, whereas with a man whose affairs ordinarily require such knowledge we presume that he knew the custom. This differs from Shach, who appears to say that with anyone we may assume he did not know. I saw that Urim VeTumim resolves Shach's difficulty differently, distinguishing between a custom that the obligation itself exists even without being written and a custom merely to write a particular clause in every document. According to him, where the custom is only to write the clause, omission means it does not apply. In my view, however, Rashba's responsum cited by Rama below in siman 67 states: “If the custom is to write such a clause in documents and in one document it was not written, we do not say it is as though written, although regarding other matters we do say so.” His wording shows that this limitation is particular to that special case. Generally, even where the custom is to write a clause in documents, we do treat the omitted clause as though written. The Shulchan Aruch here likewise speaks of language that ordinary people customarily write. Thus Urim VeTumim's distinction is difficult. Shach's own view also requires qualification: unless it is actually known that a person was unaware of the custom, we should ordinarily presume knowledge, except where circumstances indicate otherwise, such as with a woman in the cited case. This appears correct to me.
