The lower clause is learned from the upper clause. Shach, citing Torat Chaim, asks: the name "Chanan" ends with a final nun, whereas "Chanani" contains an ordinary medial nun. How can one form be used to clarify the other? He overlooked Ritva to Megillah, cited in Ein Yaakov. Ritva explains that ordinary people did not always write in Ashurit script. They used the older Libona'ah script, and even some sacred texts were written in that form. That script did not have the special final-letter forms of mem, nun, tzadi, peh, and kaf. Because most everyday use was in that script, the final-letter forms were forgotten from the generation and later restored. Accordingly, the distinction between final and medial nun creates no difficulty in the case discussed. In the script then used for such documents, there was no separate final-nun form at all.
And it is known that the erased document is invalid. Sema writes that this applies specifically when the document is held by a third party. But if it is in the creditor's own possession, he may collect whether the ambiguity arises from omission or erasure. Shach distinguishes. If the document is in the possession of the person named "Chan" in the lower clause, that person can say the borrower erased letters because he retracted or wished to alter the identification. But if the document is held by "Chanani," the person named in the upper clause, Shach says he cannot collect because whichever way one understands the erasure, the document does not clearly belong to him. I do not understand even Sema's leniency in the first case. If "Chan" holds the document, how can he extract money out of doubt? Perhaps the erasure was made precisely to invalidate his claim. The burden of proof remains on the person seeking payment. Darkei Moshe's own language confirms this. It says only: if the creditor is Chanani and the document is in his possession, even though the lower clause says Chan, we do not remove the document from Chanani's hands. It does not say he may collect the money. The point is merely that we do not take the physical document away from him because he claims it is his and that the lower name was written mistakenly. But he still cannot extract payment from the borrower while the identity remains doubtful. This is simple and clear.
