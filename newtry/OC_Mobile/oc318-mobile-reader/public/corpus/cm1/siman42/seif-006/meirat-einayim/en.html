If one letter was erased from the lower text, etc. The general rule of these laws is as follows. A single letter is something a scribe will sometimes abbreviate or that may become erased by itself unintentionally, so we infer the lower text from the upper. Two letters, however, are different. Therefore, if two letters are missing because they were never written, we say the borrower specifically instructed the scribe to write it that way, because in the end he borrowed not from Chanani but from Chan, and the money should therefore be given to Chan. But if two letters were originally written and were subsequently erased, they were certainly erased intentionally, and we do not know whether they were erased because the lender retracted and ultimately lent from Chan, or were erased in order to invalidate the document. Therefore neither claimant may collect with it. This applies specifically where the document is found in the hand of a third-party custodian who does not know to whom to give it. The same applies if another person produces a promissory note whose upper line names the borrower as Chanani and lower line as Chanan: he collects from Chanani and not from Chanan. If the lower line reads Chan, he collects from Chan and not from Chanan, because one can say the writer retracted from the upper designation and the loan was ultimately to the person named below. But when the document is in Chanani's or Chan's own possession, whichever of them holds it may collect with it, whether the issue is a missing letter or an erasure. So write the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe. See also what I write about this in Perishah.
The lower text is learned from the upper. Maggid Mishneh at the end of Hilchot Malveh writes that in the Yerushalmi Rabbi Yitzchak asked: if above it says Chanan and below it says Chanani, what is the law? Do we infer the lower from the upper, or the upper from the lower? The question was not resolved. Beit Yosef, seif 9, writes that it appears one may collect from neither of them, for the burden of proof rests on the claimant. If it is a deed of gift, no one receives the gift, for the burden of proof remains upon the claimant. See Responsa Rosh, klal 61, siman 39, cited by Darkei Moshe, seif 9.
And it is known that the document was erased. The Tur writes, 'it is known that it was erased afterward,' meaning after the scribe had already written Chanani and only later were the letters erased. Therefore it is invalid. This differs from where from the outset he wrote only Chan, which is the case of missing letters that the Mechaber discusses afterward, where we treat the lower writing as a retraction and give it to Chan. See the alternative reason written in Eir Shushan concerning the invalidity of erasure. What I have written here and in Perishah appears primary.
