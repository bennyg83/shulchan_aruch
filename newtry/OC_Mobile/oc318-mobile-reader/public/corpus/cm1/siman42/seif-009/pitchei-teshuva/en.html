Whereby the document would be entirely void, we do not say so. See the responsa of Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 8, and the gloss there. He writes that this principle is stated specifically regarding a written document. With respect to something acquired orally, by speech alone, if there is a doubt regarding the meaning, we always say that the burden is upon the party seeking to extract money, even where that interpretation would render his words entirely ineffective. The reason is that a document involves an affirmative act of writing and signing. See there, and see what I write below in siman 245, seif 1, seif katan 1.
On the first Pesach to come. See Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 75. He writes that where a document states that the debtor obligated himself to pay “after several years,” one might seemingly compare it to an obligation to pay “on Pesach,” where he must pay on the first approaching Pesach and cannot say that he meant some later Pesach, for otherwise the document would be entirely meaningless, and in such a case we do not say that the holder of the document has the inferior position. Accordingly, here too he might be required to pay after three years, the smallest period encompassed by the expression “several years.” See what I write below in siman 60, seif 3, seif katan 7. Nevertheless, there is room to distinguish between this case and the case of Pesach. There, the reason is that a person is presumed to obligate himself for the first available time, whereas that rationale does not necessarily apply here. It therefore appears that the matter should be judged according to local custom and the ordinary period for which a loan of this kind would be extended, taking into account the amount of money and the status of the person involved. If the matter cannot be resolved by local custom, it appears that at the very least he must pay after nine years, because once one approaches a round number of ten, people often reckon toward that total, similar to the Gemara’s treatment in Bava Batra 176 concerning isterei and zuzei. These are his words. See there.
Where it is possible that he erred. See Baer Heitev through his statement that with the expression “I will give it,” concerning which no authority holds that an effective present transfer occurs, we should not say that the giver was mistaken. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat write that this requires study in light of the ruling above in siman 59, seif 12, where likewise, according to most authorities, the formula is ineffective, since most poskim do not follow Shmuel. See there. See Shevut Yaakov, part III, responsum 173, concerning a firstborn from his mother who possessed a deed of gift from his mother, drafted in every legally effective manner and with all customary strengthening clauses, stating that she gave him the gift “for his firstborn share.” The mother later argued that the gift was void because she had made it under the mistaken belief that a maternal firstborn receives a double inheritance like a paternal firstborn. The son argued that she had given him an absolute gift and that the scribe merely erred in mentioning the firstborn share; moreover, the deed stated that no omission or addition should detract from its force and that everything should be construed in favor of the holder; and, finally, because the deed contained a clause of responsibility it should be treated like a sale. Shevut Yaakov ruled for the mother. The deed itself contains its own refutation, because the error is apparent, and customary strengthening clauses do not cure an evident mistake. Had the deed merely said “because he is her firstborn,” it might have been interpreted as referring to the special sanctity of a maternal firstborn, who does not require pidyon under certain circumstances and has other halachic significance. In that case the strengthening clause might permit an interpretation favorable to the holder. But the deed says “for his firstborn share,” showing that she mistakenly believed a maternal firstborn receives a larger inheritance. This is a complete mistake. The son’s argument from the responsibility clause proves the opposite, since it further indicates that she believed he had a legally enforceable firstborn share and therefore wrote responsibility, although responsibility is not ordinarily written in a gift, as the Sema writes in siman 205, seif katan 10. Since the entire transaction rested upon a mistake, the gift is void from its inception. Nevertheless, the mother must accept upon herself, under the appropriate solemn undertaking, that she did not intend an absolute gift but only acted because she believed he had a firstborn share by law. See there. Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, responsum 100, further writes that even according to Maharik, who holds that where an error is plausible we say that the holder of the document has the inferior position even if this nullifies the document, everything depends upon the character of the scribe and the parties. The dispute of the Sema and Shach regarding “I will give” can therefore vary according to the person involved. Not everyone is learned in the law. If the obligor is an unlearned person, even with a kinyan formulated as “I will give,” we may say that the holder has the inferior position, because the obligor may genuinely have thought that this formula effected a kinyan. But where the obligor was a leading Torah scholar and the entire document and signature were in his own handwriting, we certainly do not say that he erred in such an elementary matter. Knesset Yechezkel, responsum 91, likewise accepts the reasoning that a Torah scholar who drafted the document did not err. Nor do we say that when the learned obligor himself drafted the document he deliberately chose an ineffective formula in order to deceive the recipient. We do not suspect a Torah scholar of scheming to deceive others, and even if he later makes such a claim he is not believed. Maharit, responsum 69, states this explicitly and proves it from the principle that a great scholar is not presumed to feed another a prohibition. Moreover, apart from his scholarly stature, he cannot say “I deceived him,” because a person is not believed to make himself wicked. Likewise witnesses are not believed to say that their testimony concerned a sham transaction, since they are not presumed to have signed upon wrongdoing. See there.
