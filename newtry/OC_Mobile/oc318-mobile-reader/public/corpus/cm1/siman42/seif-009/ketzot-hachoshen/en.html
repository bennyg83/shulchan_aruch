Mishneh LaMelech cites Maharanach, who argues that whenever an item is destined to return to its original owner, even movables presently held by another should be treated under the original owner's presumption for purposes of doubt. Maharanach therefore ruled concerning an ambiguous debt instrument whose language could mean repayment after three months or after three years that the creditor should prevail, because the money is ultimately destined to return to him. He compares it to rent where land remains under the owner's presumption. Mishneh LaMelech objects from Tosafot in Perek HaShoel. Tosafot distinguishes land from movables: land cannot be removed from its place, so even at the end of the month it remains under the owner's presumption; movables are governed by present possession. Yet Tosafot in Perek HaMokher, particularly Rabbeinu Yitzchak's position, supports Maharanach by saying that because a borrowed object must ultimately return, the lender can retain the stronger presumption. Even so, I do not accept Maharanach's application to a loan. The authorities debate documents that say repayment is "until Pesach" and ask whether the first Pesach is meant. Their arguments rest on not interpreting the instrument in a way that nullifies it, not on saying the creditor is the possessor because the money must eventually return. If Maharanach's principle were correct, then even in an oral loan where borrower and lender dispute the due date, the lender should always be believed because repayment must eventually be made. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch plainly rule otherwise: in an oral loan the borrower swears hesset regarding the time. One might say he has a migo of payment or denial, but even where the loan was witnessed and repayment restricted to specified witnesses, it appears the borrower should remain believed regarding the date. The distinction is that a borrowed object itself must return in specie. The original owner therefore retains a special property presumption. Loan money is given to be spent. The borrower owes only a personal obligation to repay from his assets. The creditor has no present possession or property right in those assets, because a creditor collects only prospectively. Therefore the borrower's assets remain under the borrower's possession. Rambam's rule that in a documented loan the lender is believed where one says the time arrived today and the other says five days remain is because the ordinary meaning of an undifferentiated written obligation supports immediate maturity. It is not because the lender is deemed the possessor. Where the language itself remains genuinely ambiguous and either interpretation leaves the document effective, the rule that the holder of the document bears the weaker hand applies. Thus in Maharanach's own case, where the document remains valid even under the three year reading, the borrower should prevail. The principle that an object destined to be returned remains under its original owner's presumption applies to a specific object or land, not to a consumable loan obligation.
