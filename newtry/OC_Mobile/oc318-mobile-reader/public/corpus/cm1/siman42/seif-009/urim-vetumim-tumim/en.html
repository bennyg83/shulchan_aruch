But where the document is void because of it, no. Kenesset HaGedolah cites Mahari ibn Lev, who was uncertain whether, where only part of a document is invalid rather than the whole document, we still say that the holder of the document has the lower hand. It appears that we can resolve this from Baal HaTerumot, at the end of Shaar 56. He proves from a document whose date falls on Shabbat that we validate the document rather than assume it is antedated. From this he derives that where the document would otherwise become invalid, we do not apply the rule that the holder of the document has the lower hand. One may ask: the case is where the document gives a date such as the seventh of Tishrei, and according to the calendar that date fell on Shabbat. Suppose the real writing was on the eighth of Tishrei and the witnesses mistakenly thought Elul had been full, so that in their calculation the seventh of Tishrei fell on Sunday. The document would then be antedated by one day through mistake. According to Baal HaTerumot, an antedated document is still valid for collection from free assets, though not from encumbered property. Thus the entire document is not void. Why not then apply the lower-hand rule and invalidate it at least as to encumbered property? The answer must be that even partial invalidity is enough. Once the proposed interpretation would entirely destroy the document's force in the particular legal respect now at issue, we do not apply the lower-hand rule in that respect. Thus, if one interpretation would preserve collection from encumbered property and the other would nullify that right entirely, we prefer the interpretation that preserves the document rather than using the lower-hand rule to destroy that part. This is a strong proof that Mahari ibn Lev's doubt should be resolved in favor of not applying the lower-hand rule where even part of the document would thereby be rendered wholly ineffective.
