Some say, and so forth. The first opinion also agrees with this distinction, as Maharik states explicitly. The rule that a document is not construed against its holder where that would nullify it entirely applies when the doubt concerns the actual intent of the giver or borrower. Thus, one who undertakes to pay one hundred after Pesach must pay after the first coming Pesach, for otherwise the document would have no practical effect and could be postponed indefinitely. Here, however, the giver may have believed that the phrase “I leave” was legally effective, following the Rosh's opinion, while the law is uncertain whether that view is accepted. In that situation the property remains in its prior possession, the holder of the document has the inferior hand, and the testamentary wording is ineffective even though the entire instrument is thereby nullified. The Rama introduced this as “some say” only because the first opinion did not state the distinction expressly, as is his practice in many places.
This applies specifically, and so forth. The Bach argues that this distinction is inconsistent with the Maggid Mishneh, Nimukei Yosef, Baal HaTerumot, and Hagahot Maimoniyot concerning a document dated on Shabbat. He was not precise. Those sources discuss a case where construing the document against its holder immediately establishes a definite defect, namely that the document was antedated. Maharik's case differs, for the giver may simply have erred and believed that the language used was effective. No authority disputes Maharik in that situation, and his ruling is clear.
For it is possible that he erred. The Sema explains this as referring to expressions of future transfer, such as “I will give him this field,” where the giver mistakenly believed that the language effected a gift. I do not understand why he invented another example instead of citing Maharik's actual case, in which a testament stated, “I leave to so-and-so.” Moreover, the Sema's rule regarding “I will give” requires study. Maharik spoke of “I leave,” because the Rosh holds that such wording is effective, and the giver may therefore have followed that opinion. But no authority holds that a mere future promise, “I will give,” presently transfers property. One cannot simply assume the giver made so basic an error. Therefore, if a document can be read either as an ineffective future promise or as language that does effect transfer, it is not construed against its holder in that manner. See also siman 49, seifim 2 and 6.
