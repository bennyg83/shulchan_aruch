He gives him two. An unspecified plural of selaim in a document means no fewer than two. This is not like a partial oral admission that would require a Torah oath. Just as one does not swear a Torah oath on denial of an obligation secured by land, meaning something written in a document, so too an admission arising from that land-secured document does not create a Torah oath. Below in siman 88 the Tur and Mechaber rule that even if the creditor says five and the debtor says three, the debtor does not swear a Torah oath on the remainder, because the amount he admits beyond the document's minimum is treated as though he returned a lost object. The present rule mentions the debtor saying two because it records only what follows directly from the language of the document and is agreed by all opinions.
If he seized, we remove it from him. This means where he seized before witnesses. Otherwise he would be believed by a migo. This differs from the earlier rules in this seif where the holder of the document has the weaker hand but, if he seizes, we do not remove the property even if the seizure was witnessed. Here the language of the document itself supports the borrower, because an unspecified plural of selaim means only two. According to this reasoning, in the preceding case where the document says "which are" and the amount was erased, possession should perhaps help. Yet Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe do not appear to accept that distinction, because they infer from the Tur's omission of seizure after all these laws that seizure does not help in the ordinary plural case. On that reasoning perhaps it does not help in the erased-amount case either. The matter requires study.
