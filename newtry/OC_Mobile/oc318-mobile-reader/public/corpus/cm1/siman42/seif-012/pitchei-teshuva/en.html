See Baer Heitev, through the case where the scribe says that his intent was to include all the aliyos, and the law follows the lender, etc. See Responsa Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, siman 8, concerning one who argued against a written obligation that the clause, 'when I collect the bills I will deliver half to so and so,' concerns something not yet in his possession. Those seeking to obligate him relied on the phrase 'belongs to so and so' appearing there, treating it as an admission. The sage who had drafted the document argued that the expression 'belongs' has two meanings: one, that the property is absolutely his, and the other, that he has a legal connection to it, meaning a lien upon it. How, then, can one obligate him on the basis of doubt? He further argued that the rule that documents are interpreted according to ordinary human language applies only to matters where a legal act is completed by speech, such as sale, kiddushin, and vows. Where we seek to obligate a person by virtue of his admission, however, he may explain his words even by a strained interpretation. He brought proof from Responsa Raanach, siman 101, which cites the case in Bava Batra where a person claimed that property belonged to his fathers and was permitted to explain that he meant that he relied upon the seller as he would upon his fathers, as codified in Choshen Mishpat siman 146. Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg rejects this argument at length and then writes: The distinction is not as the questioner imagined. The correct distinction is that where a person comes to obligate himself in some matter, admit some fact, or transfer ownership, and all the more so where he writes his admission, obligation, or transfer in a document, he is assumed to have chosen his words carefully. There is no distinction whether he wrote it in his own handwriting or witnesses wrote it, because they wrote it at his instruction. We therefore presume that he was precise. If he later seeks to contradict the document or give it a strained interpretation, he is not believed, for presumably his intent followed ordinary human language and the understanding of listeners. By contrast, where he does not intend to obligate himself, transfer property, or make an admission, but merely presents arguments before beit din, even though he cannot completely retract his earlier claim, he may explain his words and give a plausible rationale for them, even if the explanation is somewhat remote, and this is not considered a retraction and new claim. Indeed, even if a person claimed before beit din, 'I mortgaged to him a house up to the end of the upper story,' he could later explain that he meant only one upper room, and this would not be considered a new claim. The proof from Responsa Raanach is likewise no proof. There, the case involved a written compromise drafted by arbitrators, and a doubt arose whether a certain matter was included. The defendant argued that it was not included, and Raanach ruled that the document could be explained even through a somewhat strained interpretation. This does not prove anything regarding a person's own handwritten document or a document written by witnesses at his instruction. Only in those cases do we say that he was precise and certainly intended the meaning understood by listeners, because if he intended otherwise he should have explained himself. In the compromise, however, the text was not written at his instruction but according to the arbitrators' own understanding. Therefore, he may plausibly say that the arbitrators did not intend to include a particular matter, even if that interpretation is somewhat strained, and we return to the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to extract. It is to this point that Raanach brought the proof that a person may explain his words by saying that when he said the property was his fathers' he meant that he relied upon the seller as upon his fathers, because there too the principle of careful drafting does not apply. He was not writing an obligation or admission, but merely stating a litigation claim. Raanach further discusses whether there is a distinction between the speaker himself later explaining his intent and a litigant attempting to explain the intent of the arbitrators. See also Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part III, siman 156, concerning Reuven, who borrowed a sum from Shimon and gave him an explicit apotiki on his seat in the synagogue, stipulating that if the debt were not repaid within two years Shimon could collect from that seat. They expressly stipulated that Reuven could not sell or mortgage the seat before paying this debt. Reuven now wished to rent the seat until the due date. Shevut Yaakov rules that he may rent it for the entire term of the loan. Although a lease for a day can be regarded as a sale for that day, and even if the apotiki document includes the clauses 'not as an asmachta' and 'not as a mere form document,' so that according to many authorities the holder of the document has the upper hand, documents are nevertheless interpreted according to ordinary human language and the parties' intent. The prohibition on selling or mortgaging refers to an actual sale or mortgage, because those would weaken the creditor's apotiki right by forcing him to pursue a purchaser or redeem the pledged seat. Ordinary human language does not include a lease in those terms. What disadvantage does the creditor suffer if, until the due date, another person sits in the seat instead of the borrower?
