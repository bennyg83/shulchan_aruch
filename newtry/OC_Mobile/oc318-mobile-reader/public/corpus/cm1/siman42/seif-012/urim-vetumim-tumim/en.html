And if he seized, etc. Gidulei Terumah asks why this differs from a mortgage document that states an unspecified number of years. We rule that if the creditor remains in possession and claims three years, we do not remove the land from him because the matter is one likely to become clarified. Shach answers from Nimukei Yosef that the mortgage case is different because the borrower allowed the creditor to consume the fruits, weakening the borrower's possession. Gidulei Terumah certainly knew that Nimukei Yosef. His question is that Nimukei Yosef used that distinction only to defend Ramban's separate ruling that where a document says merely "sela'im," the debtor must still take a hesset oath if he denies owing more than two. If "sela'im" truly meant only two with certainty, why should seizure ever help in the mortgage case? But below, siman 88, we appear to accept Ramban's oath ruling. According to R. Shmuel Vidal, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, the mortgage rule applies even where the borrower was overseas and therefore did not voluntarily allow the third year's eating. Thus the difficulty remains. The true distinction is between possession taken with permission and possession taken without permission. In the mortgage case, the creditor entered the land lawfully under the mortgage. Even if he later continues for a disputed additional period, his original possession began with permission. That is treated as possession with permission, like a tenant who lawfully occupies a bathhouse for twelve months and then claims the lease also included an intercalated month. Here, by contrast, seizure under an ambiguous loan document cannot realistically be possession with permission for the disputed amount. If the debtor gave the seized property specifically in payment of the loan, there is no dispute. If he gave it as a deposit, loaned it, or for some unrelated obligation, that is not possession with permission as to this disputed loan claim. Thus the creditor's seizure is unauthorized with respect to the disputed amount and we remove it. This resolves Gidulei Terumah's question.
We remove it from him. Shach asks from the law below, siman 54, where a receipt says merely "dinars" and can invalidate even a document for a thousand dinars. Why does broad possession help there while seizure does not help here? Bach, following Maggid Mishneh, says unspecified "dinars" can never mean more than two even in a receipt. That answer cannot fit Mechaber's law. Shach distinguishes a receipt: the witnesses intentionally wrote "dinars" generally because they did not know the amount of the underlying debt and intended the receipt to cancel whatever debt existed. That is strained. If they did not know the amount, they could simply write, "the above debt is paid." And if the receipt was written directly on the debt document, or they did know the amount, Shach's explanation fails. The better answer emerges from Tosafot at the end of Perek HaShoel. One who borrows an axe "to dig a vineyard" may use it for all his vineyards. Tosafot ask from a sale clause, "land and date palms," which is interpreted minimally. They answer, among other things, that in a sale the seller remains the possessor, whereas in a loan of movables the borrower is the possessor. Ramban accepts this possession distinction. Why then, if the creditor here has seized property, do we not say he too is the possessor? Because, as explained above, there is a difference between possession that began with permission and seizure that did not. The borrower of the axe received the object lawfully for use and seeks only to continue using it within the claimed scope of the original permission. That is possession with permission. Likewise, a mortgagor who allowed the creditor into the property gave him lawful possession. But a creditor who seizes unrelated property to satisfy an ambiguous debt did not receive possession of that property for this debt. That is unauthorized seizure and does not shift the burden. This also answers the receipt case. The debtor already possesses his own money. The creditor is trying to take it from him by saying the original debt remains unpaid. There is no stronger example of possession with permission than a person holding his own funds from before the dispute. Therefore ambiguity in the receipt can protect him far more than a creditor's later seizure can help the creditor. Still, Tosafot's first distinction, between a sale and a loan, leaves some difficulty, as do other Rishonim who distinguish land from orchards. The broader principle is the Torah rule, "If you grasped the greater amount, you have grasped nothing; grasp the lesser amount." The reason, as Tosafot explain, is that an interpretation without limit has no endpoint. In the axe case, even if we interpret "my vineyards" broadly, the maximum is finite: only the vineyards the lender owned at the time of the loan. The borrower cannot continue into future years indefinitely. Likewise in the mortgage dispute, the disputed additional consumption is bounded by what was actually eaten. But if a loan document says merely "dinars" and the creditor seeks the largest possible amount, there is no natural ceiling. A thousand, ten thousand, or all the wealth in the world could fit. Where one interpretation has no natural endpoint, the Torah rule requires adopting the minimum. This also resolves Sema's question concerning a document whose number of sela'im was accidentally erased before witnesses and the witnesses do not know what number stood there. Even though the erasure was involuntary and not the creditor's fault, seizure does not help. If we allow the creditor to claim the larger amount, there is no endpoint at all. Therefore we award only the minimum. In truth there is an additional reason. Seizure before witnesses helps principally where the document itself contains ambiguous wording. Here the document has no surviving ambiguous wording at all. The numerical content is gone. The creditor is effectively making an oral claim that the erased figure was larger. Ordinary seizure before witnesses does not validate such a bare oral uncertainty. Thus even in a witnessed accidental erasure, the creditor cannot retain more than the minimum. There is another way to see why the rule of "grasp the lesser amount" applies here. A document that says simply "dinars" contains a linguistic minimum. Hebrew plural ordinarily establishes at least two. The court therefore has a concrete floor from the document itself. If the creditor wants more, he must prove how much more. The fact that the debtor might have borrowed a much larger amount does not help because there is no textual upper endpoint. By contrast, in the axe case the language "my vineyards" identifies a closed class of objects already existing at the time of the loan. The borrower can claim the whole class because the words naturally refer to it. Likewise, in a mortgage for an unspecified period, the creditor's actual possession and consumption identify a finite disputed period. This is not an abstract possibility of limitless extension. The same distinction applies to a receipt. The receipt does not create a new affirmative claim for an unlimited amount. It is defensive. The debtor says, "Whatever claim you now bring against me has already been discharged by this writing." Since the creditor is trying to remove money from the debtor's existing possession, and the receipt directly relates to that very debt, the ambiguity operates against the creditor more readily. A loan document is the reverse. The creditor is trying to use uncertain wording to extract an affirmative additional amount. That is why identical vague plural language can have different effects in a receipt and a loan document. Accordingly, there are really three reinforcing reasons for the present ruling: the creditor's seizure is not possession with permission as to this debt; the larger interpretation has no natural endpoint; and the document itself contains no affirmative language establishing more than the minimum. Where all three are present, seizure cannot transform an uncertain claim into a right to retain the excess.
