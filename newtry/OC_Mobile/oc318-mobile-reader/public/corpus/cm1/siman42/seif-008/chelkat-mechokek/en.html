She is no longer believed to say, "I intended only to jest." It requires explanation why, in monetary law, where one says "I was jesting with you," we sometimes advance that claim for him even if he initially denied everything, because people do not remember idle statements. Perhaps due to the severity of kiddushin we are stringent, and the phrase "the kiddushin are valid" means only that she cannot be released without a get. Yet the responsum of Maharam Padua dealt with a particular case: one witness saw the kiddushin and she saw him, while another witness saw the kiddushin but she did not see that witness. Since she denied the event, we say that she clearly did not intend merely to jest, and at minimum there remains a case of kiddushin before one witness. If, however, neither of two witnesses was visible to her, then even if she was later shown to have falsely denied receiving the money, there are no kiddushin according to the witnesses' own account. Rashba, cited by the Beit Yosef, writes that where she did not see the witnesses there are no kiddushin, because everyone knows that kiddushin without witnesses are ineffective. Even if she said aloud that she accepted for kiddushin, we presume that her words did not reflect genuine intent, since the witnesses themselves know she was unaware of them. Thus Maharam Padua's proof from the rule that one who says "I never borrowed" is treated as though he said "I did not repay" is not decisive. Here, even if we know she did not intend to jest, the kiddushin still fail because she did not know of the witnesses. Rivash, siman 266, explicitly supports this, for there too the woman denied the matter, yet he voided the kiddushin because the witnesses were not visible to her.
