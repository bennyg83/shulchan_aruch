And all the more so we say this regarding a receipt, etc. Sema asks from the law below in siman 71. Where a creditor writes a clause of trust in favor of the debtor, Rif and Tur rule that the debtor may still have to take an oath. We interpret the ambiguous trust clause narrowly: it means only that the creditor is not automatically believed merely because he holds the document, not that the debtor is exempt from every oral-claim oath. The holder of the document has the lower hand. But the debtor is the possessor of the money. Why not interpret the ambiguous clause in his favor? Sema therefore challenges Rama and Beit Yosef's general rule that ambiguity is always construed against the party relying on the document. Shach answers that in the trust-clause case, remove the document from consideration and the creditor can still sue orally and require the ordinary oath. Taz answers that the debtor there does not make a definite claim that the creditor explicitly exempted him from an oral-claim oath. He relies only on the ambiguous text. Both answers are difficult. What does it help to say, "remove the document and sue orally"? The debtor responds that the very document contains a clause that may exempt him from that oath. When money is already in his possession, doubt should prevent imposing the oath, just as in other cases the possessor can say kim li. Shach himself rules elsewhere that even regarding an oath, a defendant can rely upon authorities who exempt him. Why should an ambiguous trust clause be weaker? Even if the debtor's claim is uncertain, he possessed the money before the doubt arose, and such possession normally helps even in a doubtful matter. One possible answer is that, according to the view that migo does not exempt from an oath, uncertainty about an oath is itself resolved by requiring the oath. If the defendant is truthful, he loses nothing by swearing. But that cannot explain Tur, because Tur holds migo can exempt from an oath and treats an oath as sufficiently monetary for such rules. The better explanation is evidentiary. In the trust-clause case there is a concrete indication that the debtor was never granted exemption from an ordinary oral-claim oath. The trust clause is physically written in the creditor's document and remains in the creditor's possession. If the debtor had truly negotiated a broad exemption from oath liability, why would he leave the only proof of that benefit in the creditor's hands? The creditor can hide or destroy the document and sue orally, saying, "You owe me one hundred; swear." The debtor would then have no written proof of his exemption. The debtor's willingness to leave the document with the creditor therefore shows that the parties never intended the trust clause to exempt him from every oral-claim oath. Rather, the clause was intended only to limit the creditor's special evidentiary strength under the written document itself. This resembles the general principle of trusting a third party because the party placed the relevant evidence in the third party's control. Where one could have protected himself and instead entrusted the decisive instrument to another, that conduct reveals the scope of the trust. Thus the creditor is believed to say the trust clause did not include exemption from the ordinary oral oath. For this reason, everyone can agree that the holder of the document has the lower hand in that case. It is not merely an abstract linguistic ambiguity. The surrounding conduct itself points to the narrower reading.
Not like the dissenting view, etc. This refers to Maharik, responsum 7. The case concerned a person who reached an agreement with the townspeople to pay charitable assessments equally per household rather than according to wealth. Later a ransom for captives arose. The parties disputed whether ransom of captives was included in the earlier agreement concerning charity. Maharik ruled that the holder of the document has the lower hand and therefore the person must contribute according to wealth. Bedek HaBayit objects that this conflicts with the many authorities who rule that where a party already holds the money, uncertainty in a waiver or receipt is resolved in favor of the possessor. Nimukei Yosef likewise cites a dispute regarding a receipt that says merely "dinars." Raavad understands such a receipt broadly enough to cancel the relevant debt, while Ritva is more restrictive. Shach, seif katan 18, tries to eliminate the disagreement. He says even Ritva agrees that the holder of a receipt ordinarily has the upper hand. In the "dinars" case, Ritva's reason is simply that the ordinary meaning of unspecified "dinars" is two dinars, so there is no genuine ambiguity. I am surprised that Shach overlooked Ritva's explicit words in Ketubot 83b. The Gemara says: "Perhaps he removed himself from everything." Abaye answers that the holder of the document has the lower hand. R. Ashi gives another interpretation. Ritva cites those who say R. Ashi rejects Abaye's principle in waivers and receipts. Since the creditor is trying to extract money from the possessor, the burden should remain on the creditor. They cite the rule that a receipt reading "the larger document in your possession is paid" defeats even the larger document, and the rule that an undated receipt can impair all earlier documents. Ritva rejects this. He says R. Ashi does not disagree with Abaye's rule. Those other cases are different because the receipt itself contains no ambiguous wording. If it says, "the larger document in your possession is paid," a large document unquestionably is a "document." The debtor is not stretching ambiguous language. Likewise, an undated receipt has an objectively broad effect because nothing in its language limits it to one date. But where the receipt or waiver itself has two possible meanings, the party relying upon that ambiguous writing has the lower hand, and the underlying debt remains established. This is exactly the opposite of Shach's explanation. Ritva clearly distinguishes between uncertainty in the words of the receipt itself and a definite receipt whose legal range is broad. Thus in all the examples discussed here, where the document itself is linguistically ambiguous, Ritva does not automatically give the possessor the upper hand. Maharik appears to follow the same idea. He himself cites Abaye's statement concerning one who writes to his wife, "I have no claim or dealings in your property," where the holder of the waiver has the lower hand. That proof is the same one Ritva uses. However, it is difficult to say Maharik simply follows Ritva against the many authorities cited by Bedek HaBayit. Maharik rarely disregards Tosafot and other major Rishonim without discussion. Moreover, Tosafot in Ketubot appear to say R. Ashi does not accept Abaye's general lower-hand rule in this context. Maharsha tries to soften their language and say R. Ashi merely gives a different textual explanation in this case, but the simple reading of Tosafot is broader. According to Abaye, when the husband's waiver is ambiguous whether he removed himself only from an already completed sale or also granted her permission to sell initially, the holder of the waiver has the lower hand. We therefore interpret it narrowly. According to R. Ashi, who does not apply that rule here, the woman can rely on the broader waiver meaning and sell initially. That appears to support the opposing principle. There is also Tosafot in Ketubot 20a, concerning two against two regarding validity of a receipt. They apply the rule that the party seeking extraction bears the burden, which favors the holder of the receipt. Hagahot HaRosh likewise supports that approach. Why, then, did Maharik rely on Abaye's rule without addressing these sources? Shach answers that Maharik's case differs because the party relying on the agreement had only an uncertain claim. The underlying charitable obligation was certain, and he sought to exempt himself through an ambiguous settlement. An uncertain waiver cannot remove a definite obligation. This is an important distinction. Where the debt is definite and the release is doubtful, possession does not necessarily help. I have explained in the laws of possession, and again below in siman 65, that where a certain obligation is confronted only by a doubtful waiver, even both parties' uncertainty may not defeat the established debt. This can reconcile Maharik. Still, I am not entirely satisfied that the charitable obligation in Maharik's case was already definite. The agreement was made before this particular ransom obligation arose. At the time of the settlement, no present duty to pay this ransom yet existed. When captives were later taken, the question was whether the prior agreement governed the newly arising obligation. In what sense was there an established certain debt that a later doubtful waiver was trying to remove? Moreover, a person cannot ordinarily waive something not yet in existence. If the townspeople merely "forgave" the higher contribution that might someday become due, such a waiver would not work. The settlement must therefore have had a different legal structure. The correct understanding is that the townspeople undertook among themselves to indemnify this individual. They agreed that for future charitable assessments he would contribute only on a per-household basis, and if his legal share according to wealth exceeded that amount, the other townspeople would bear or reimburse the excess. Otherwise the agreement would be ineffective regarding future obligations not yet created. Once the matter is framed this way, the individual is indeed the claimant under the agreement. The underlying public charitable assessment may obligate him according to wealth. He then seeks to enforce the townspeople's contractual undertaking to protect him from the excess. He is therefore the holder of the agreement and seeks payment or indemnification under its terms. Since the document is ambiguous whether ransom of captives is included in "charity," the holder of the document has the lower hand. This resolves Maharik without requiring him to reject the authorities who favor a possessor under an ambiguous receipt. Maharik's citation of Abaye's rule is then understandable as an analogy: one who seeks to expand an ambiguous written waiver or undertaking beyond its clear scope bears the burden. The obligation of the townspeople to indemnify him is not established beyond the document's language. There is still a broader question regarding R. Ashi's disagreement with Abaye. Rivash, responsum 404, provides an important framework. He holds that, in the husband-wife property context, Abaye views the husband as the possessor of the property rights. Therefore ambiguity in the wife's waiver works against her. R. Ashi can view the wife as the possessor and therefore interpret differently. If so, R. Ashi's disagreement does not establish a universal rule that waivers are always construed in favor of the recipient. It depends on who is legally treated as the possessor in that property regime. This also explains the later halachah concerning fruits and fruits of fruits. The Sages can regard the wife as the possessor of the underlying property, so the husband's waiver is read broadly enough to exclude him from present fruits unless he reserves rights expressly. But under R. Yehudah, the husband's continuing rights are stronger, and unresolved questions regarding the extent of his waiver can be resolved in his favor as the effective possessor. Rif, Rosh, and Tur rule in an unresolved question that the husband retains fruits of fruits unless the waiver expressly reaches "forever." That practical ruling shows that the conclusion does not simply abandon the lower-hand principle. Rather, the result depends on the particular ownership posture. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 92 appears to understand R. Ashi as rejecting the lower-hand rule altogether in our present halachah and therefore reads an unrestricted waiver broadly. In my view that is difficult. The accepted law following R. Yehudah in the unresolved issue shows the husband's possession still matters. Maharik can therefore say similarly that a community stands in the position of a possessor vis-a-vis an individual concerning communal assessments. I wrote above regarding taxes that the community has special strength against an individual. The same can apply to ransom of captives, which is a communal obligation imposed by the King of the universe no less seriously than earthly taxation. Thus, where the individual relies on an ambiguous settlement to reduce his communal burden, the community can be treated as the possessor and the document construed against him. This gives Maharik an additional independent basis. Accordingly, the case does not establish that every ambiguous receipt or waiver is always construed against its holder. The correct rule depends on the structure: If a definite debt exists and the alleged waiver is doubtful, the established obligation prevails. If the party seeks affirmative indemnification or exemption under an ambiguous agreement, he bears the burden of proving the broader meaning. If the issue is a true receipt against a creditor seeking to extract money from a possessor, many authorities give greater force to the possessor, especially where the receipt's wording is objectively broad rather than internally ambiguous. And where the parties' legal possession status is special, as between husband and wife or community and individual, that status can control the interpretive presumption. Maharik's ruling is therefore sound without overturning the broader law of receipts.
