All the more so, this rule is applied to a receipt. The Sema explains the Rama to mean that if possession acquired without permission after the doubt arose is effective, then certainly a receipt, whose holder already remains in possession of his own property, should be construed in his favor. The Sema objects that an ambiguous receipt should perhaps not defeat a clear promissory note in the creditor's possession. He also argues that this would conflict with the Rif and Tur's ruling in siman 71 concerning a creditor who trusted the borrower, where the borrower must swear that he paid because the holder of the document has the inferior hand. In my view, the Rama and Beit Yosef are correct. A receipt is ordinarily written to defeat the promissory note. Therefore, against the receipt the creditor's document is construed against its holder, and correspondingly the receipt is construed in favor of its holder. Once the note has been defeated, however, the borrower becomes the claimant with respect to the remaining oral obligation and must take a hesset oath. Thus, siman 71 presents no contradiction. The receipt cancels the written security, but it does not exempt the borrower from the oath applicable to an oral claim. Likewise, where the creditor retained money, the borrower may require a hesset oath. The Beit Yosef's ruling concerning an ambiguous reading such as “sefel” or “kefel” is similarly correct and is supported by the Rosh's responsa and the later rulings in siman 43.
Not like those who disagree. The Darkei Moshe cites Maharik as ruling that even one in possession cannot use an ambiguous document to exempt himself from an obligation imposed by law. The Beit Yosef and Lechem Mishneh questioned how Maharik could disagree with the authorities who rule that property already seized is not removed. In my view there is no dispute. Maharik addressed a person who, without the document, was certainly obligated to contribute to charity according to his means. He claimed that the expense of redeeming captives should instead be divided equally because their agreement stated that charitable expenses would be paid equally. Maharik ruled that redemption of captives was not necessarily included in that wording. The person had no true possession to protect, for the law itself already obligated him according to his means, and he sought to displace that certain obligation through an uncertain reading of the document. A doubt cannot displace a certainty. By contrast, where the creditor asserts with certainty that the borrower owes him, he is, according to his own claim, entitled even without the document. Therefore, if he seized property, even before witnesses, it is not removed from him, because his claim is definite. The same applies to a receipt where the borrower says that even without the receipt he owes nothing. Since he is in possession and claims a definite waiver, the receipt is construed in his favor. The Rosh and Mechaber's ruling that a receipt waiving all claims through a stated time creates a doubt likewise assumes that the holder claims this particular note was expressly waived. The Ritva also agrees that a receipt is construed in favor of its holder. He disputes only the word “dinars,” which linguistically indicates two and therefore does not establish a larger amount. It may further be distinguished that the cases of the Rosh and other authorities involve possession taken before the doubt arose or taken with permission. I discussed this at length in my work Tekafo Kohen, siman 56.
