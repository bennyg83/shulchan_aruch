If he seized movables. See Shach, who says seizure does not help for land, even with land produce. His intent is not that in every documentary doubt, seizing detached produce is ineffective. Rather, he speaks where the underlying doubt itself concerns land, as in the law of an intercalated month in a rental dispute. Even then, seizure is ineffective only where the matter is not naturally destined to become clarified. Where the ambiguity in the document is one that can later be clarified by witnesses, such as an unstated number of years, possession can have greater force. The distinction is between seizure of the very object in dispute and seizure of another object merely on the basis of a lien. In a genuine uncertain monetary interest, one who already possesses the very object by right can sometimes retain it; one who merely has a claim of lien on the object cannot create stronger rights by later seizure. This explains the difference between an ambiguous receipt or mortgage held by right and a lender who seizes property merely because of an uncertain documentary lien. Ketzot HaChoshen correctly cites the distinction that an ordinary loan, unlike a borrowed object, remains in the borrower's legal domain until repayment; therefore in a genuine doubt about the date of repayment the borrower can remain the possessor.
All the more so what they say in a receipt. See Shach and Urim VeTumim. Since we rule that a litigant may say, 'I am certain like the authorities who exempt me even from the oath,' the same principle applies here. Urim VeTumim tried to explain the opposing view by saying that because the lender delivered the document containing the credibility clause into the borrower's hands, he effectively trusted him as a shalish and therefore empowered him to insist upon the oath. This is not satisfactory. If so, a separate writing granting credibility and placed in the borrower's hands should likewise defeat hesset, a rule never stated. Moreover, even a shalish's authority derives from the fact that the instrument is treated as though absent because he could destroy it. Here, even without any document at all, if witnesses established an ambiguous credibility clause and the person relying on it is the possessor, the ordinary rule remains that the possessor has the superior hand. Not like those who disagree. Shach reconciles Maharik by saying Maharik speaks where the party himself is uncertain. He then explains the rule in siman 43 either where the defendant claims with certainty that this debt too was expressly forgiven, or where he seized before the doubt arose, or seized with permission. His formulation that seizure with permission helps even with an uncertain claim is difficult. In Maharik's case the person was already holding his own money, which would be the strongest possible possession with permission. The real distinction is that a doubt whether a debt preceded or followed the receipt is not ordinarily called seizure before the doubt arose, and possession is not 'with permission' merely because the owner originally placed the money in the person's hands for another purpose. The principal distinction is one I explained in the rules of seizure: where a party seeks to acquire a right solely from ambiguous wording, an unresolved expression does not itself convey the right. By contrast, in siman 43 the uncertainty concerns an external event, whether the deed preceded the receipt or the receipt preceded the deed; there the existing possessor can rely on possession. Moreover, Maharik's case concerning redemption of captives was really a communal obligation, not forgiveness by captives. The community was the obligor under an undertaking to pay whatever was due, and the community itself was in possession. That too supports the result.
