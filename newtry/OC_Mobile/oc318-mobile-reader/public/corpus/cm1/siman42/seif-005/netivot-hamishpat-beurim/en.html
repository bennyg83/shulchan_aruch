We follow the lower wording. See Rashbam and Tosafot. At first glance, if the upper clause states that the loan was two hundred and the lower clause concerns responsibility for only one hundred, why should the lower amount control? The two can be reconciled: perhaps he borrowed two hundred but accepted responsibility only for one hundred. One can answer, as Shach implies, that this is an artificial reconciliation and we do not force a strained reconciliation when ordinary document interpretation treats the lower wording as the final statement. Alternatively, the lower clause may itself refer explicitly to 'the aforementioned one hundred,' making it a direct restatement of the sum. This supports Shach's general rule that documents are not saved through forced constructions.
The holder of the document has the inferior hand. Knesset HaGedolah cites a responsum applying this even to acceptance of an oath. One might object that doubt concerning an oath is a matter of prohibition and should be treated stringently. The answer is that where the party knows with certainty what he intended, there is no halachic doubt at all. The case is only where the language he used is objectively ambiguous and he himself is unsure what the language encompassed. In such a case, just as a vow until 'before Pesach' does not extend through the doubtful endpoint because a person does not ordinarily place himself into a doubt, so an ambiguous self-imposed oath may be construed narrowly. Rambam at times appears to treat such linguistic doubts more stringently, so the matter can require caution, as discussed elsewhere.
