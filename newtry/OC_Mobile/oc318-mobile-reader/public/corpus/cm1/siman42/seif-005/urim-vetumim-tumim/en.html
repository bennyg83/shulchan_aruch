And it is possible to reconcile both. See Urim regarding Shach's claim that where two clauses conflict we should not force a strained interpretation merely to preserve both. In my view the proof points the opposite way. Bava Batra discusses a phrase such as, "one hundred zuz, which are twenty sela'im." Rashbam explains that we can interpret the hundred zuz as extraordinarily poor-quality coins worth only twenty ordinary sela'im. If so, suppose the upper part of a document says twenty and the lower part says one hundred. Why automatically award one hundred? Why not say there is no retraction, but the hundred are such poor coins that they equal the value of twenty? Indeed, in Bava Batra 105 and Bava Metzia 102, where a phrase reads in effect, "one hundred istira, one hundred ma'ah," Rav follows the latter phrase. The Gemara says one might have thought the latter merely explains the former, and Rashi explains that perhaps the large coin mentioned first equals the later total. Why not actually adopt that reconciliation? The answer must be that where preserving both clauses requires an unusually strained interpretation, it is better to say the later clause retracts or corrects the earlier one. In the case "one hundred zuz, which are twenty sela'im," the word "which are" forces us to explain the expressions consistently and prevents treating the second figure as a retraction. But where ordinary language allows us to say the second clause changed the first, we do not distort the meaning simply to reconcile them. This proves against Sema and supports Shach's basic principle: a strained reconciliation is not preferred over a natural reading of retraction.
The holder of the document has the lower hand, etc. Maharam Mintz, responsum 16, ruled concerning an ambiguous phrase in a document that also involved an oath. It was unclear whether the oath applied to the disputed matter. He nevertheless applied the rule that the holder of the document has the lower hand. This requires study. Where there is uncertainty in prohibition, we ordinarily rule stringently. Why should the monetary rule of construing a document against its holder make the oath disappear? Kenesset HaGedolah cites Rashba, responsum 9, who rules explicitly that where parties accepted an obligation upon themselves under a ban and the wording is doubtful, the doubt in the ban is treated stringently. I have likewise written in the laws of possession that where one uncertainty has both monetary and prohibitory consequences, the monetary aspect can be treated leniently toward the possessor while the prohibitory aspect remains stringent. See also Shach below, siman 73, seif katan 24, who rules that even in a dispute among authorities one cannot say kim li to escape an oath merely because the monetary law is doubtful. All the more so where the uncertainty arises from the language of the document itself. Below, siman 73, seif 9, we rule that one who swore to pay on Rosh Chodesh Adar must pay on the first day because the uncertainty is one of prohibition. Purely as a matter of document interpretation, one could not extract payment until the second day because the holder of the document has the lower hand, and the principal day of Rosh Chodesh is the second day. Yet the oath requires stringency. The same should apply here. Perhaps Maharam Mintz's case involved a party who wished to obtain release from the oath, as his responsum suggests. An oath ordinarily should not be released without the opposing party present. Maharam Mintz writes that where the obligor paid a penalty, he may no longer need the opponent present for release. If so, the initial issue in his responsum may have concerned only whether release required the other party's presence. Even according to the stricter authorities, release granted without the opponent can work after the fact, and the requirement of presence is primarily procedural. Thus the uncertainty there may have concerned only a rabbinic or procedural prohibition, and one could apply the lower-hand rule more readily. But without release, or in a case where release is unavailable, a genuine uncertainty whether the oath itself covers the matter should be treated stringently. Maharam Mintz need not disagree. There is one important qualification. If the obligor himself says with certainty, "That was not what I intended," even though the wording is ambiguous, beit din should not compel him merely because of an oath. An oath follows the person's own intent in the sense recognized by the laws of vows and oaths. Where he makes a definite claim about what he intended and the document does not clearly refute him, we do not impose an oath obligation based solely on a doubtful interpretation. Kenesset HaGedolah likewise supports this distinction. This accords with the rule: doubts in vows are stringent, but interpretation follows the speaker's established intent where he makes a credible definite claim.
Has the lower hand. Sema and Shach cite Piskei Mahari concerning a document that says merely, "A kinyan was made from him," but does not say, "with an instrument fit for acquisition." They understand Mahari to say that perhaps the parties used some other type of kinyan rather than kinyan sudar, and because the holder of the document has the lower hand, the document is doubtful. Shach asks: where that interpretation would invalidate the entire document, we do not ordinarily apply the rule that the holder has the lower hand. R. Tzvi, in his glosses to Taz, correctly explains that they misunderstood Mahari. The case there involved something that cannot be acquired by kinyan sudar, such as coins. Since the document does not expressly say, "with an instrument fit for acquisition," which would clearly indicate kinyan sudar, perhaps the parties actually used a kinyan that does work for the subject matter, such as acquisition together with land. Mahari debates whether we should interpret the vague phrase "a kinyan was made" in that effective manner. He ultimately rules that the matter remains doubtful and the burden of proof rests on the claimant. We therefore assume it may have been the ordinary kinyan sudar and the document may be ineffective. This also answers Shach's question. Ordinarily, when an interpretation would completely void a document, we hesitate to adopt it. But here most scribes are not expert and often assume kinyan sudar works for every type of property. Moreover, the ordinary meaning of "a kinyan was made from him" is kinyan sudar. Had a different mechanism been used, one would expect the document to specify it. Therefore the possibility that the parties used an ineffective kinyan is substantial even though it can invalidate the document. In this setting the holder of the document has the lower hand.
