And not like the one who is stringent. Maharik was stringent even where the witnesses themselves stand before us and say, "We never signed this document," if the document had already been brought before beit din and read there. In my view this is contrary to reason. Rashba, responsum 1210, makes no such distinction and writes simply that where their handwriting is not authenticated from another source, the witnesses are believed to invalidate the document. This appears to have been so even though the document had been produced in beit din. Where the witnesses are absent, the document has not been authenticated, and the woman claims it is forged, it is possible to say that Torah law does not require authentication. Rashba's concern that the witnesses may die suggests that as long as they are not present to deny their signatures, her claim of forgery would not defeat the document by Torah law. Hagahot Mordechai, however, writes that where there is an actual contradiction before us, Torah law itself requires full authentication. It proves this from the requirement of dating gittin and other passages. See there. Rashba also writes that even if a signed document is externally authenticated, if the very witnesses whose names appear on it testify that someone told them to forge it, the document is treated as defective and is not used for collection. In this context, where the woman remains presumptively permitted to the world, we may even permit her to marry initially. Moreover, before the purported document appeared she would have been permitted, and once permitted she does not lose that status. Also, where the actual signatories do not appear, she may claim forgery by a migo that she could have said, "He divorced me." Although a migo does not prevail against witnesses, here the document itself may be forged. See also Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 4, siman 2.
