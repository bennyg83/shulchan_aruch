And that amount was erased. Sema writes that if the lender claims the erased sum was five and the borrower says it was two, the borrower is exempt from a Torah oath. If the borrower says it was three, however, he should be obligated in a Torah partial-admission oath. This is not like the case of 'selaim, dinarim,' where the document itself supports the admitted minimum and the defendant is treated like one returning a lost item. Here the erased amount supplies no documentary support. Nor is the borrower simply returning a lost item through a migo that would require brazenness. One cannot argue that perhaps the lender himself erased the amount to invalidate the deed. That concern applies where the erasure is in the lender's or borrower's name, making the identity of a party uncertain and thereby invalidating the instrument. An erasure in the sum does not invalidate everything; we still learn at least from the amount that remains un-erased.
