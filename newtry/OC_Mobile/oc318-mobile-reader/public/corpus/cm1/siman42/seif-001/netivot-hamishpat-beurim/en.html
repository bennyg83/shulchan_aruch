A deed of acquisition. See Shach, who writes that even if one signed his name on material capable of alteration, and the claimant later produces it in a form stating an obligation, denial can make him an established denier. Urim VeTumim correctly objects that the signer can say he signed a blank or trivial writing and that the holder later altered it. Tosafot's statement that an acquisition deed written on erasable material can nevertheless function was stated only according to Rabbi Elazar, who invalidates such material specifically for evidentiary documents by scriptural decree, whereas a constitutive deed is not principally made as evidence. It does not mean that every later writing on erasable material conclusively binds the signer. To defend the Shach one can say that according to Rabbi Elazar it is prohibited to sign even a blank erasable surface, because a dishonest person could later use it together with persons of the same name to create a fraudulent instrument. Since signing in this way itself was prohibited, perhaps the signer is not believed to claim that he did so. Even so, the principal practical law follows Urim VeTumim that such a signature alone does not make him an established denier. The Shach further writes that a constitutive deed on erasable material can work through signed witnesses and the obligor's admission even without delivery witnesses. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that a private handwritten writing without delivery witnesses does not transfer. The cases are not comparable. A writing with no witnesses at all is not a deed. Here there are signed witnesses; the defect is only that the material is erasable. If the signers themselves appear and establish that no alteration occurred, or the obligor admits it, the instrument has full documentary form. The same is true in monetary deeds, where a party's admission can stand in place of delivery-witness proof. The additional discussions in Shach concerning gittin and the measurement of parchment for Shabbat do not alter this conclusion.
