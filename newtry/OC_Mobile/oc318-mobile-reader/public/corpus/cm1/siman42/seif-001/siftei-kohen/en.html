It is invalid even for immediate collection. So rules the Tur: if the document was written upon material that can be altered, it is invalid even for immediate collection; Rabbeinu Chananel validates it when collection is sought at once, but the Rosh rejected that view. This requires study, for the Rosh does not clearly disagree. The Rosh writes that Rabbi Yochanan validates even monetary documents, whereas the Rif rejects his ruling because the Gemara asks generally that a document must be written on material that cannot be altered. Rabbeinu Chananel nevertheless rules like Rabbi Yochanan. The Rosh then offers another explanation: Rabbi Yochanan validates monetary documents only immediately, while the witnesses still remember their many conditions, but not after ten days. According to this conclusion, the Rosh may agree with Rabbeinu Chananel regarding immediate collection. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise records the Rif and Rambam as invalidating even immediately, Rabbeinu Chananel as validating, and another opinion as validating only immediately. It may be that the Tur understood that even the Rosh's second explanation does not reconcile Rabbi Yochanan with the Gemara's statement concerning parchment prepared for writing a bill of divorce. Yet this is not compelling, for an ordinary monetary document is made to endure and is not ordinarily written for immediate use. In practical law, the Mechaber may have followed the Rif and Rambam, as is his general method, but the Rosh, Rabbeinu Chananel, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the Rashba all indicate that immediate collection is valid. The matter therefore remains a doubt in law. Were I worthy to decide, I would say that Rabbeinu Chananel's view is primary. The law generally follows Rabbi Yochanan against Rabbi Elazar, his student. The Baal HaItur likewise rules like Rabbeinu Chananel. Moreover, the objections of the Rosh and Rashba do not compel limiting Rabbi Yochanan to immediate use. Rabbi Yochanan's statement and the Gemara's proof indicate that he validates even after time has passed, provided the witnesses to delivery appear and testify, for then the court relies on them and not on the alterable document. The Ramban states this explicitly. Without those delivery witnesses present, however, all agree that alteration is feared, as is clear from the Gemara, Rashi, Tosafot, the Ran, and the ruling in Even HaEzer. The earlier Gemara's challenge can therefore concern a document whose delivery witnesses are absent, or can be directed specifically against Rabbi Elazar's position. Likewise, the measure stated concerning parchment merely uses a case valid according to all opinions. Accordingly, Rabbeinu Chananel's ruling appears sound even after time has passed, though the issue remains one requiring practical determination.
But a document of conveyance. This means a document by which a field or house itself is acquired, such as one stating, “This field is sold to you” or “given to you.” It operates as the act of acquisition and is not primarily a document of proof, as the Ran explains. This refutes the Bach's objection to the Sema's distinction. The Bach claimed that without the seller's handwriting or witnesses to delivery, a conveyance document written on alterable material is invalid. Yet the Sema's distinction is that of the Ran and is supported by Tosafot, the Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, and Sefer HaTerumah. Tosafot's statement that the case follows Rabbi Elazar, who holds that witnesses to delivery effect the instrument, concerns the requirement that the instrument be self-proving. In divorce and betrothal two witnesses are independently required because a matter of personal status cannot be established by fewer than two. In a sale or gift, however, the seller's or donor's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. Thus, where he admits delivery, the property is acquired by this document even without delivery witnesses. It further appears that even if the seller denies delivery, the buyer or recipient is believed to say that it was delivered, when the document is in his possession and its authorship is established by the seller's handwriting or by signed witnesses. The seller may claim that the instrument contained a condition that was later altered, but he may not claim that he never delivered it at all. Tosafot describe conveyance documents as instruments not primarily made for proof, although they can later serve as evidence. A bill of divorce likewise may later serve as proof, yet its essential purpose is the immediate legal act. This also resolves the difficulties raised by Maharsha and Rabbi Eliezer ibn Chaim. The concern in Ketubot is that one might write on the shard a deed of gift or sale and later use it as proof against a denial of the transaction. That concern exists according to Rabbi Elazar, because such a conveyance can be effective even though the writing is not self-proving. According to Rabbi Meir, the document is entirely invalid because it is not self-proving. It therefore follows clearly that witnesses to delivery are not required for a sale or gift instrument in the manner asserted by the Bach.

He apparently understood that a document made in the form of a folded document is invalid only according to the Rosh's general rule that any document not prepared according to rabbinic form is invalid, and that according to the Ramban it would be valid. This is not so. The Nimukei Yosef's text should read that the Rashbam, not the Rosh, explained the Mishnah's invalidity as resulting from failure to follow the form instituted by the Sages. This rule is independent of the dispute discussed at the beginning of siman 44. Where the Sages established the essential form of a document, such as requiring the witnesses of a plain document to sign within it, changing that form leaves the instrument without the legal status of a document even when no practical risk of forgery exists. By contrast, the requirement to repeat the substance of the document in its final line was not imposed as an indispensable form. It was sound advice intended to permit reliance upon the last line. Failure to repeat it does not invalidate the document; it merely prevents reliance upon that line. The Ramban himself writes that when the witnesses of a plain document sign on its reverse, the document is invalid because they did not sign upon the instrument in the manner required. In a folded document their reverse signatures are effective only because that is the form established by the Sages. Thus, a plain bill of divorce or monetary document whose witnesses signed on the reverse as though it were folded, or signed at the side, is invalid even without any suspicion of forgery. No authority disputes this, and it is not comparable to the rule at the beginning of siman 44. The Rama's contrary implication is therefore incorrect.
