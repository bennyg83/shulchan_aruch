But a man who was compelled. The plain language of the Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot Ishut, chapter 4, implies that as a matter of basic law there is no difference between a man compelled to acquire and a woman compelled to be acquired. Just as a compelled sale can be valid, the same applies to a compelled purchase, and all the more so because a person is generally more willing to acquire than to sell. The authorities discuss a compelled gift only because the giver receives nothing and therefore does not ordinarily resolve to transfer ownership. Likewise, there should be no difference between being forced to sell an object for money and being forced to exchange it for another object, even though the latter party is also called a purchaser. The dispute here concerning a man compelled to effect kiddushin is therefore not over the underlying law of compelled acquisition. Rambam holds that the Sages did not apply the special penalty of uprooting his kiddushin, but left the matter under the ordinary rules of compelled acquisition. Those who disagree hold that the Sages did impose the penalty because he acted improperly. I am therefore surprised by the Rama in Choshen Mishpat 205, who rules, based on the Itur, that one compelled to buy acquires nothing. The discussion here strongly implies that no such distinction exists between buyer and seller.
