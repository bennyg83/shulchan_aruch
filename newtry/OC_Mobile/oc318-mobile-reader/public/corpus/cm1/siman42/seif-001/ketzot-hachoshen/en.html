An acquisition document is valid. Tosafot in Gittin distinguishes between documents intended as enduring evidence and documents used only momentarily to effect an acquisition. A surface that can be altered or erased can serve for an acquisition document because it need function only at the moment of transfer, though it is unsuitable as enduring evidence. Bach argues that even an acquisition document on alterable material requires either the seller's own handwriting or witnesses to delivery. The Sema, Shach, and Taz read the authorities more leniently. After analysis, Bach is correct. According to Rabbi Meir, who holds that signed witnesses effect the document, an alterable instrument is invalid even for acquisition, get, or kiddushin, because it is not self proving from the signatures. Even if everyone later knows that no forgery occurred, a document that is not legally proven from its own signatures lacks the very signed testimony Rabbi Meir requires. Therefore the Gemara establishes the permissive ruling according to Rabbi Eliezer, who relies upon witnesses to delivery. Under Rabbi Eliezer, an alterable acquisition document can be valid when witnessed at delivery, because enduring documentary proof is unnecessary. But where there were no witnesses to delivery and the only purported witnesses are signatures on alterable material, the document lacks a valid witnessing mechanism. The giver's present admission that he handed over that piece of paper does not cure this. If a person privately hands another a document written in someone else's hand, without signed witnesses and without witnesses to delivery, his admission that he handed over the paper does not make it an acquisition document. The same applies where the signatures are legally ineffective because the document is alterable. Tosafot's statement that in monetary matters the giver's admission can replace witnesses to delivery presupposes a valid document with valid signed witnesses. The admission supplies proof of delivery because a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. It does not create a valid document where the document has no valid signed testimony at all. Tosafot in Gittin 10 says this expressly concerning gift documents signed by gentiles: where there are neither valid signed witnesses nor valid delivery witnesses nor an operative admission tied to valid testimony, the paper is mere pottery and cannot acquire. Thus Bach's conclusion is correct: an alterable acquisition document without valid witnesses to delivery is ineffective unless the instrument is otherwise in a form, such as the giver's full handwriting, that itself supplies a valid basis. Ran has a different conceptual approach. He holds that Rabbi Eliezer agrees signed witnesses can also suffice because their signatures establish that delivery from the giver occurred. On that model, if the giver's signature itself proves the paper came from him, there may be room to validate even alterable material because delivery is known, and the recipient may be treated as trusted regarding its contents much like one who receives a signed blank. But even under that approach there must be a legal basis proving the document came from the giver. A random alterable writing with no valid signatures and no delivery witnesses cannot be transformed into a conveyance merely by later admission. Therefore the primary rule remains that Bach's objection is substantial, though Ran's model may validate some signed handwritten cases. This also resolves several questions from Tosafot in Ketubot and from the law of dephtera: documents of acquisition can be included within the category of writs valid on such material, but documentary evidence intended to stand for years cannot.
