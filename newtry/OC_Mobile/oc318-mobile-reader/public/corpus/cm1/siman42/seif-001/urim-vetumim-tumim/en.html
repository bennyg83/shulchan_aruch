Even immediately. Shach questions Tur's statement that Rosh does not follow Rabbenu Chananel, who validates the document when it was erased or altered only immediately after writing. Shach notes that in the second chapter Rosh cites Rabbenu Chananel and distinguishes between an immediate case and one after a long time, which could imply that Rosh agrees with him. That passage is not decisive. In the first chapter, however, Rosh discusses the Gemara's question, "But we require writing that cannot be forged," and says the question follows R. Elazar, who stated that even in other documents he validates only writing that cannot be forged. Since the Gemara asks the question without qualification, Rosh says this indicates the halachah follows R. Elazar. That statement plainly shows Rosh rules stringently and invalidates the problematic writing even immediately. His citation of Rabbenu Chananel in the second chapter was therefore only to explain Rabbenu Chananel's own view, not to adopt it. Practically, Rosh follows the other poskim that the document is invalid even immediately, and Tur's attribution is correct.
But documents of acquisition, etc. See Shach, who cites the dispute between Sema and Bach. According to Sema, if one writes on material that can be altered, "My field is acquired to you," and delivers it without witnesses to delivery, but the seller admits he delivered it, the acquisition is effective. The document is not functioning as evidence but as the instrument of acquisition itself, and the Torah decreed that acquisition by document is valid. Bach disagrees and holds that without delivery witnesses it does not work even if the seller admits delivery. Shach rules like Sema. According to R. Meir, who requires the proof to be evident from within the document itself, such a document is invalid. But according to R. Elazar, who does not require that, once the seller admits delivery it is equivalent to delivery before witnesses because a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. This is correct according to Tosafot in the first chapter of Gittin 4a, but I will explain below in siman 51 that it is not unanimous. There is a genuine dispute, and Tosafot themselves do not state the matter conclusively. Thus Sema and Bach's issue remains a matter of disagreement. Shach adds a novel ruling. If the purchaser produces a shard on which another person wrote, "My field is sold to you," and below it appears the seller's signature or the signatures of witnesses, then, if the seller says, "This never happened," Shach says he is established as a liar. This ruling has no root or branch. Both halachic reasoning and ordinary sense reject it. Why should the seller be established as a liar based upon a mere shard? Perhaps some meaningless or unrelated text originally appeared above his signature, and someone later erased it and wrote, "My field is sold to you." The seller may have signed innocently and know nothing of the fraud. If, regarding two people with identical names, we do not allow one to collect a debt document against the other because perhaps the real borrower repaid and recovered his document, all the more so here the seller can say, "I gave you some other paper or receipt, and you erased it and wrote a sale statement over my signature." Many similar explanations are possible. Accordingly, this proposed ruling should be rejected entirely. Shach's entire innovation was designed to answer a question of Maharsha and R. Elazar ben Chaim on Tosafot in Kiddushin and Ketubot. Tosafot cite Abaye's warning that a person should not sign his name at the bottom of a blank sheet, lest a dishonest person find it and write whatever he wishes above the signature. Rather, he should sign on a shard. Tosafot ask: is a shard not itself material that can be altered? Yet a baraita says one may write on a shard, "My field is sold to you." They answer that the baraita follows R. Elazar, who holds delivery witnesses create the document. Although R. Elazar agrees that ordinary proof documents must not be written on alterable material, documents that themselves create an acquisition resemble a get. Abaye's warning, Tosafot say, can follow R. Meir. Maharsha asks: even according to R. Elazar, if one writes a statement intended merely as evidence on that shard, it should be invalid. Why did Tosafot need to say Abaye follows R. Meir? Shach answers through his new theory: a dishonest person could write, "My field is sold to you," above the signature and thereby establish the signer as a liar. That answer is unnecessary and incorrect. Tosafot may mean, as Shach himself earlier suggested, that the halachah follows R. Yochanan, who says R. Elazar validates even other types of documents. Since R. Elazar and R. Yochanan are disputed and the general rule is that the halachah follows R. Yochanan, Tosafot's distinction between proof documents and acquisition documents was offered only to reconcile the baraita with one view. Practically, Tosafot can still hold that Abaye follows the view that alterable material is invalid in ordinary documents, and they call that position R. Meir for the limited issue. There is no need to say Abaye adopts R. Meir's entire theory that signing witnesses create the document. Indeed, Abaye elsewhere follows R. Elazar in the broader dispute. One can also say that Abaye follows the authority who rules like R. Elazar in gittin but not in ordinary monetary documents. Rashba says explicitly in Perek HaMegareish that a gift document resembles a get for these purposes. Then Abaye can follow R. Elazar in gift documents while accepting R. Meir's stringency in other proof documents. Thus Tosafot can be understood without Shach's invention. There is, however, another difficulty in Tosafot. Even if Abaye follows R. Elazar, R. Elazar validates an alterable document only with delivery witnesses. If a dishonest finder writes above the discarded signature, he would still need witnesses to a false delivery. Why fear that? Ritva asks this in Kiddushin and Ketubot. The answer appears to be this. Suppose the signer genuinely owes someone one hundred or two hundred in a valid shard document delivered before witnesses according to R. Elazar. If he carelessly signs a separate blank shard and throws it away, the creditor could later find it and write above the signature exactly the same text as the genuine debt instrument. Since alterable material can be modified, he can make the false shard correspond exactly to the genuine one. When the borrower later pays the true debt, the creditor returns the genuine shard. The borrower sees what appears to be the original signed text and suspects nothing. Afterward the creditor presents the second shard together with the same delivery witnesses, who truly witnessed the original loan and delivery. The borrower can then be forced to pay twice. This is why Abaye warns not to sign a shard under R. Elazar. Under R. Meir, however, such a shard document can never operate because the proof must be evident from within an unalterable document. This also answers Maharsha's question. The concern must involve a document whose principal function is the operative transaction itself. If the false text were merely, "My field is given to you," there is no comparable repayment sequence. Once a gift or sale is complete, returning a document does not ordinarily undo the property transfer. The special double-collection danger therefore belongs primarily to loan or payment documents. Accordingly, Tosafot's words do not support Shach's claim that a random signed shard can establish the signer as a liar. Shach also seeks proof from Tosafot's phrase that an acquisition document can later serve as evidence. He argues that this means even an alterable acquisition document can be used evidentially to contradict a party who denies the transaction. This is not Tosafot's meaning. Tosafot say only that an acquisition document written on proper, unalterable material can later also serve as evidence. One might therefore think it should be classified as a proof document and be subject to the same restrictions as documents whose primary purpose is evidentiary. Tosafot answer that the same could be said about a get. A get can later prove divorce, yet its principal function is to effect divorce, not merely to serve as evidence. Likewise an acquisition document is classified according to its primary operative purpose. That does not mean a document written on alterable material becomes reliable proof of every later denial. Therefore Shach's novel rule has no basis.
And some disagree. Rama rules like the lenient view. Shach, seif katan 3, argues that Rama misunderstood the source. Rama apparently thought the issue depended on the dispute cited below in siman 44: where a document was made contrary to rabbinic form, even though the original reason for invalidating that form no longer applies, is it nevertheless invalid? Rama thought Rosh invalidates and Ramban validates. Since we rule like Ramban there, Rama ruled leniently here as well. Shach says this is mistaken. The "Rosh" mentioned by Nimukei Yosef is not Rosh at all but Rashbam, commenting on the Mishnah in Perek Get Pashut that a plain document whose witnesses signed on the back is invalid. Rashbam says it is invalid because it was not made according to the rabbinic enactment. According to Shach, this differs from siman 44. There, the Gemara did not explicitly invalidate the form. Here the Mishnah itself explicitly says such a document is invalid. Shach further cites Ramban, who rules that a plain document whose witnesses signed only on the back is invalid because the witnesses did not sign upon the document at all. Since they were supposed to sign inside and instead signed outside, they did not testify to the contents. Shach therefore concludes that everyone agrees a plain document signed on the back is invalid, even today. With due respect, Shach's analysis is inaccurate in several respects. Rama's case, taken from Nimukei Yosef, is not a plain document in which the witnesses signed only on the back. Nimukei Yosef follows Ramban's explanation of the Mishnah. Ramban says the Mishnah need not be read as a document with no signatures inside at all. Such a document would obviously be invalid because the outside signers did not sign on the operative writing. Rather, Ramban explains a case in which the document was properly signed inside, and additional witnesses were also signed on the outside in the form of a folded document. Likewise, a folded document was properly signed outside and additional signatures appeared inside. Ramban gives one explanation based on concern for antedating or postdating, and another based on the party's insistence upon the requested form. If he ordered a plain document and they made something that is not truly plain, or ordered a folded document and they made something that is not truly folded, his instructions were not followed. Nimukei Yosef adopts this explanation. He says: in a place where both plain and folded documents are used, if the party requested a plain document and it was properly signed inside but also had folded-document signatures outside, the document is invalid because it is not the form he requested. He then says that nowadays, when folded documents are no longer customary and people do not know them, perhaps such a document should not be invalid. The party could not reasonably have intended to insist on a distinction that no one uses. Nevertheless, Nimukei Yosef considers invalidating it according to Rashbam's explanation that the form was not made according to the rabbinic enactment. Rama copies precisely this modern case. Thus Shach's argument from Ramban's statement about a document signed only on the back misses the point. That case is indeed invalid, in every era, because no signatures were placed where a plain document requires them. Rama never rules otherwise. The issue here is a document properly signed inside and also signed outside. Under Ramban's explanation, the only defect is the party's insistence on the requested form. That concern has no obvious application today, when folded documents are unknown. Rambam in his Commentary to the Mishnah likewise explains the invalidity through the party's insistence on form. Accordingly, Rama's leniency has a real basis. Shach further says that since the Mishnah expressly invalidates the form, Ramban must agree it remains invalid even if the original reason has disappeared. But according to Ramban, the Mishnah's invalidity is precisely because the party is assumed to have insisted upon the form. Where that factual assumption no longer exists, the Mishnah itself does not establish invalidity. Ramban never says a document remains invalid merely because the Sages once regulated a different form. Indeed, his broader method in siman 44 is that where the original defect does not apply, one should not create a new invalidity merely because the writing differs from a usual rabbinic form. Nimukei Yosef's attribution to Rashbam is itself difficult. Rashbam explains the Mishnah more simply: a plain document whose witnesses signed only on the back is invalid because it was not made according to rabbinic form. Tosafot give a similar explanation in one answer. Rashbam does not discuss Ramban's different case where the document was already properly signed inside and then additionally signed outside. Ramban himself notes that Rashbam and Rashi use different interpretations. Therefore one cannot simply transfer Rashbam's invalidating rationale to Ramban's separate factual case. Accordingly, even Nimukei Yosef's reason for stringency is not compelling. There is also a strong logical proof that the party-insistence reason cannot operate today. Suppose one says that even where a plain document is properly signed inside, adding outside signatures makes it invalid because he requested a plain document and they produced something folded. Then certainly, if they placed no signatures inside and signed only outside, the same party-insistence issue should exist. Yet where valid delivery witnesses are present, Even HaEzer siman 130 recognizes that a get can be valid despite unconventional signature placement, because under R. Elazar the delivery witnesses establish the get. Why should the properly signed plain document be worse than a writing supported by delivery witnesses? The answer must be that today no meaningful insistence exists concerning plain versus folded form. Once folded documents are no longer part of ordinary practice, the entire concern disappears. This is nearly self-evident. Even a document signed only outside in an attempted folded form could, in theory, have room for leniency today if the parties truly intended to create a folded document and knew enough of its structure. Rambam largely omits the practical laws of folded documents, as does the law of a bald get in Perek HaZorek, because the institution fell out of use. Tosafot give one reason for invalidating a plain document signed on the back: otherwise it could later be converted into a folded document and become antedated. If someone today deliberately prepared a folded document but lacked expertise in all its technical rules, that particular antedating concern may be absent if the document correctly doubles the year as folded documents require. One could then ask what substantive defect remains. There is room for leniency from this perspective as well. Still, Tosafot themselves contain a complication. Before Rabbi knew that a folded document dates one year as two, he nevertheless knew the Mishnah says a plain document signed on the back is invalid and vice versa. At that stage, he could not have explained the invalidity through antedating based on the doubled-year system, since he did not yet know it. It seems he must have understood the invalidity through failure to follow rabbinic form, as Tosafot's second answer says. Maharsha says this explicitly. Why then do Tosafot later introduce a different antedating rationale? If Rabbi initially understood the Mishnah through failure to follow the enactment, why assume he abandoned that reason after learning the dating rule? At first I thought Tosafot could say that even at the earlier stage Rabbi did not understand the issue as simple failure to follow rabbinic form. He may have understood it through the party's insistence, like Ramban. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who validates a folded document signed inside when the party requested plain form, would then say there is no insistence because the document can still be treated as plain. That works only before we know folded documents use a different year-counting rule. After learning that difference, Tosafot in Gittin ask why a folded get signed in the plain manner is not simply a postdated get whose divorce takes effect only later. Then the party's insistence becomes obvious: he wanted to divorce immediately, yet the resulting document would delay the divorce and continue support obligations. That is a far greater deviation from his intent. Therefore the discussion can no longer rest solely on insistence, and Tosafot introduce the antedating defect for the particular authority. This may explain the shift. Still, another problem remains. Tosafot say that the ability to convert a folded document into a plain one also applies to a woman's get and use this to prove that a postdated get can be valid. If so, under their first answer based on antedating, what is wrong with a folded get signed inside? A postdated get is valid. The concern of a receipt creating loss applies naturally to monetary documents, not to divorce in the same way. Moreover, why do Tosafot explain that a plain document signed outside is invalid because it could be turned into a folded document and therefore be antedated, forcing themselves into the extra step of decreeing two folds because of three? They could instead say more simply: if outside signatures on a plain document were valid, every folded document could be converted into a plain document. The resulting date would be later, and if the debtor already paid and received a receipt he could face a later-dated instrument. Why not use this straightforward delayed-document concern? The answer is that the first Tosafot explanation is specifically for Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. For the Sages, the simple Rashbam explanation suffices: the document did not follow the rabbinic form. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel cannot use that explanation universally because he validates a folded document signed inside when it can still be treated as plain. Therefore Tosafot supply a more targeted antedating concern for his view. He also does not accept the usual receipt concern in the same way in that discussion, so the alternative delayed-document argument is unavailable. This explains why Tosafot's first reason is not the general reason for the Mishnah. Their second answer says even Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel accepts the general rabbinic-form principle, but only where the signatures are positioned so they cannot be read together with the text. A side signature, for example, is treated as not signed upon the get. By contrast, a folded document signed inside can still be unfolded and read together with the signatures as a plain document, so it is not outside the rabbinic form in the same sense. This reconciles the Tosafot approaches. Rashbam and Rashi's comments in Gittin, saying outside signatures on a plain document are invalid because the form is contrary to rabbinic enactment, need not conflict with Rambam's stronger phrase that such a get "is not a get." The conceptual explanation is as follows. By Torah law, witnesses can testify whether their signatures appear inside or outside the writing. But once Chazal established that plain documents are signed inside, witnesses who deliberately sign only on the outside of a plain document signal that they did not intend those signatures to attest the operative text. If they meant to attest it, they would have signed in the prescribed place. Thus the defect is not that Torah intrinsically rejects an outside signature. The rabbinic form determines what the witnesses' act signifies. In a folded document, Chazal themselves established outside signatures as the proper form. There, the same physical act necessarily indicates testimony to the document. This is exactly Ramban's language: because they were supposed to sign inside and instead signed only outside, they did not testify to what was written. Accordingly, Rambam can say "it is not a get" without implying that the Torah has an independent location rule. This also solves a deeper problem. If signing outside were intrinsically void by Torah law, how could Chazal ever validate folded documents whose signatures appear outside? One would have to invoke retroactive annulment of the marriage every time a folded get was used. Then Tosafot's famous question about the date requirement in a get would return with full force. If Chazal already annul the marriage through the very form of the folded get, why would the date matter to prevent a husband from covering up his wife's adultery? The marriage would already be retroactively undone. The better answer is that the Torah accepts the act of witnessing in any location. Chazal's rules determine when the signature objectively counts as testimony to this document. A plain document signed only outside, contrary to established form, indicates no attestation of the text. A folded document signed outside, precisely according to established form, does attest it. There is therefore no need to invoke annulment of marriage at all. Rashi and Ran's statement that the document is invalid because it was not made according to rabbinic enactment can be understood in this very manner. They need not disagree with Rambam. Now return to our modern case. Where witnesses sign both inside and outside because they are intentionally imitating a folded document, the inside signatures unquestionably attest the text. The outside signatures do not negate that direct testimony unless there is a reason of party insistence or another concrete legal concern. Today, when folded documents are not customary and no one reasonably insists on that distinction, the reason disappears. At minimum, the document should remain effective against the obligor personally. Rashbam's language suggests that even in the classic case the main consequence of failing to follow the rabbinic form may be loss of collection from encumbered property rather than total invalidity for free property. Therefore, in a modern document intentionally styled as folded but whose operative text is properly signed inside, there is strong basis to validate at least collection from free assets. Rama's leniency is thus far more defensible than Shach allowed.
