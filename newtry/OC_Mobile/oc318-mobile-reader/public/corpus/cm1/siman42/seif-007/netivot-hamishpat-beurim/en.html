Even though the holder may have a migo that he could erase one letter of the lower word and force reference to the upper wording, that is a migo against the document itself, because the lower wording is presumptively a deliberate correction. Urim VeTumim asks why we attribute the missing letter to a fly rather than suspect that the holder himself removed it. The answer is that a fly can remove wet ink before the document dries. Once the ink is dry, removing a letter leaves an evident erasure. Witnesses would not hand the instrument over while the ink remained alterable in a way that allowed easy fraud. A further difficulty arises: if erasure of one lower letter allows reference to the upper text, then a deed reading a larger amount above and a smaller amount below would be vulnerable to fraud by erasing the lower initial letter. This supports Tosafot's view that the rule of learning from the upper text when one letter is missing applies especially to names, where abbreviations are common, not to the monetary amount itself. In the amount, a missing letter does not allow the holder to reconstruct the larger sum from above.
