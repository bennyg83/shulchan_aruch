Because "kefel" is less than "sefel." Sema asks: this implies that if "kefel" were the larger amount, we would not simply award it. Why not, if the alteration cannot be attributed to a fly? Sema answers that even there we could blame a fly, saying it extended part of a samekh and turned it into a kuf. Taz objects at length that flies erase ink but do not draw or extend letters. He overlooked the words of R. Yehudah HaChasid concerning the forms of the letters, cited by Beit Yosef in Orach Chaim siman 36. Regarding the letter kuf, R. Yehudah HaChasid says its right leg should incline somewhat leftward and the left leg should not be overly long, because otherwise we can say a fly turned a kuf into a samekh. This shows that a fly can spread or connect ink lines, both by lengthening a stroke and by closing spaces. However, the very same source undermines Sema's particular answer. To turn a kuf into a samekh, the fly can add ink and connect previously separate lines. To turn a samekh into a kuf, however, one must remove ink: the left stroke of the samekh is connected to the top and the enclosure is closed, while in a kuf the left leg is separate. A fly can add or spread ink, but the proof of R. Yehudah HaChasid assumes it cannot reliably erase part of an existing stroke. Otherwise there would be no reason to regulate the shape of the kuf. Thus Sema's explanation that a fly can turn a samekh into a kuf is difficult. The better explanation is based on who holds the document. The Gemara asks, "Perhaps a fly erased it," rather than, "Perhaps the creditor erased it." This indicates a setting in which the document is still held by the witnesses or a third party, so deliberate creditor forgery is not suspected. Rambam states the rule broadly. If the upper clause says sefel and the lower says kefel, the creditor receives only kefel in every setting because kefel is the lesser amount. Even where the creditor holds the document, we do not need to rely upon a fly. The lesser amount follows the ordinary rule that the holder of the document has the lower hand. Likewise, if the upper says kefel and the lower says sefel, he receives only kefel. Even if the document is with a third party and creditor forgery is impossible, we can still fear a fly altered a kuf into a samekh by adding ink. But if kefel were the larger amount and the document were held by a neutral third party, so creditor forgery is excluded, there would be no reason to say a fly turned a samekh into a kuf, because that requires removing ink rather than adding it. In that case the larger reading could control. Thus Rambam's broad language is exact, and Sema's proposed fly mechanism is unnecessary.
