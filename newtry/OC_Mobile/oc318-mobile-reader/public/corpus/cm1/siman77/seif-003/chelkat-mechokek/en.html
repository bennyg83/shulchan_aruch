For if he wishes to divorce her immediately, etc. Under the law of the Gemara this is simple, because the husband could divorce her against her will. Why should he violate the prohibition against withholding conjugal rights? Indeed, one who has relations with his wife while intending to divorce her violates the ethical prohibition, "Do not devise evil against your fellow," as explained below, siman 119. After Rabbeinu Gershom enacted that a woman may not be divorced without her consent, however, one might say that if he withholds conjugal relations because he wants to divorce her, we should continue adding to her ketubah since he cannot simply divorce her against her will. The Bach writes that even today, if he wants a divorce, no additional amount is added, and moreover he is exempt from supporting her. He cites Responsa Mizrachi, siman 30, which likewise exempts support, based on the opening of Ketubot regarding a bride whose time for marriage arrived but nisuin was delayed. According to that approach, Rabbeinu Gershom's enactment only prohibited forcing a divorce so that he could marry someone else, but permitted him to withhold food, clothing, and conjugal relations until she agrees to accept a get. This requires serious qualification. Mizrachi's proof is not decisive, because one cannot compare obligations before marriage to obligations after marriage. Illness itself proves the distinction: before nisuin, if she becomes ill at the appointed time he need not support her, whereas after marriage he must support her during illness. Perhaps Mizrachi intended only support, on the theory that where he is ready to divorce her he may say, "I will not support you and will not take your earnings." But he may not reduce conjugal rights. Responsa Rosh, rule 42, quotes Rabbeinu Gershom's enactment as equalizing the woman's power with the man's: just as the man divorces only voluntarily, so too the woman; wherever we compel the man to divorce, we may compel the woman to accept a get, and if she refuses we may withhold food, clothing, and conjugal relations. This clearly implies that where the man could not himself be compelled to divorce, the woman likewise may not be coerced and he must maintain her food, clothing, and conjugal rights, and the ketubah may be increased as in the law of rebellion. Maharik, siman 107, likewise indicates this. There he discusses a wife with a defect and says that although the husband once waived the defect, we may not compel him to remain with her and produce children from a woman he now hates, because perhaps Rabbeinu Gershom never enacted his ban in such a claim-based case. But for ordinary women, where the enactment certainly applies, there is reason to compel him because otherwise he would nullify procreation. Even in the defect case Maharik appears to require food and clothing, because once he waived the defect and became obligated in the ketubah, why should he not remain obligated in the ketubah conditions? Only conjugal relations, which involve bodily distress, may not be deemed permanently waived. Therefore, in practice, one should not rely on the Bach. See below, siman 79, seif katan 3, in the name of Maharshal. Where a husband has no clearly established reason why she is repulsive to him, he has the law of a rebel: the ketubah is increased, and he must provide food and clothing to the extent possible. The Rama's own inference from Rambam is also puzzling. Of course Rambam implies that a man who wishes to divorce is not a rebel, because under Rambam's law a woman could be divorced without her consent. Maharik already notes that Rambam does not mention Rabbeinu Gershom's enactment because it did not spread in his country. There is therefore no novelty in saying that under Rambam a husband who divorces his wife is not called a rebel.
