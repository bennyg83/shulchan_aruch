I heard from my father-in-law, and saw practiced many times, in accordance with the Shach in Choshen Mishpat 77:19, that one partner can waive a claim belonging to the partnership, although a partner who did not consent may then sue the partner who waived it. I recall this being practiced without distinction. I later considered the objection of Urim VeTumim, who questions the Shach. Where two persons are not partners but merely lent one borrower a fixed sum under one document, Urim VeTumim holds simply that one lender cannot waive the other's share. Perhaps his reason is that money is regarded as already divided among them. But where they are partners in the very transaction being waived, even if they are not partners in other matters, perhaps he would concede that the case is like ordinary partnership property and one partner's waiver may be effective. The Shach's proof from payment can also be defended, because a borrower who says the debt was paid assumes that the other partner will not be able to sue him merely because he states that the payment entered the partnership and it is not his task to complete the partnership accounting. By contrast, if one partner expressly waives the debt, the other will certainly sue the waiving partner, who will not be believed to say that his fellow sent him to waive it, since there is a presumption that a person does not waive damage. This resembles the rule that one who says, 'tear my garment,' without adding 'and be exempt,' is not presumed to have waived liability, and one who sees another cutting down his trees is not presumed to have waived his loss. In any event, commercial custom follows the Shach in practice, and in such matters we follow accepted business custom. Certainly, if the acting partner says all the partners authorized him, this appears effective according to everyone, all the more so where their trusted agent knows of it. In leases and similar partnership businesses, people commonly conclude transactions with one partner alone. In one incident, all the partners claimed that they had expressly told one partner not to sell, yet he sold and accepted part of the money. This itself provides some indication that they authorized him, especially once he accepted payment. The seller must swear, and his own share is certainly sold if the buyer wants that share alone. Where he refuses to swear and another partner possesses the sold property, the matter requires study. If one partner sold merchandise while away and afterward the partner at home sold the same merchandise to another person, and the first buyer incurred expenses in bringing wagons, perhaps the partners are liable for those expenses because they should have anticipated that a partner might sell while traveling. On the other hand, the partner at home might say that he assumed his fellow would refrain from selling out of concern that the goods might already have been sold at home. The matter may depend on whether there was a practical way to notify him. Regarding a partner's waiver, the Shach gives two explanations. It may be that where the waiver causes little loss and perhaps some benefit, it is effective and the waiving partner is exempt toward his fellow. If there is no benefit and he expressly waived without his partner's knowledge, perhaps everyone would agree the waiver is ineffective. If he did not say whether the others consented, perhaps the borrower is like one who says, 'I do not know whether I repaid.' If the borrower caused himself loss in reliance on the waiver, market-protection principles may have relevance, but perhaps only where the partner affirmatively said the others agreed. With an ambiguous statement he should have investigated. If one partner says that the partner now suing had told him there was no waiver, although that testimony is affected by interest and is therefore only an uncertain claim, partners may be regarded as suing with authorization from one another, which could support imposition of a hesset oath.
