Even if both are written in one document, and so forth. Shach questions this. If the creditor named in the document is himself a partner, why can he not forgive the debt? Partners generally transact concerning partnership property. His objection is not compelling. Rashba's responsum does not describe ordinary business partners with general authority over one another's affairs. It says that because two lenders are written together in one note, they resemble partners for purposes of collecting the common debt and can be treated as mutually authorized to demand payment. That collection authority does not imply authority to destroy the other's right through forgiveness. Even true business partners do not necessarily authorize one another to waive partnership assets gratuitously without consent. There is proof below in siman 93. If one partner says a partnership debt was repaid, he can sometimes discharge the debtor but must reimburse his partner. Yet where the note explicitly states the money belongs to the partnership, the other partner can still collect his share from the debtor. If a partner always had authority to forgive the other's share, this distinction would collapse. Shach himself elsewhere says an assertion that a note is paid can operate like forgiveness. That makes the proof even stronger: the partner cannot forgive the other's substantive right merely because his own name appears on the note. The same is indicated below in the laws of heirs and transferred notes. Accordingly, the fact that both lenders are named in one document creates mutual collection authority, not unilateral authority for either one to forgive the other's share.
