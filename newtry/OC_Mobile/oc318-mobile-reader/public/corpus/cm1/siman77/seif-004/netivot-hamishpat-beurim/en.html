The Sema's distinction, based on the fact that he could have denied it, is very strained. The Shach likewise strongly questions the Levush. In my view their positions can be reconciled, and the dispute among the authorities can be understood as merely a difference in how each understood the other's intent, with no practical dispute at all. The Taz writes that the principal case concerns a situation where the second partner claims that the partner who borrowed the money owes him an offsetting amount. The second partner is entitled to make that claim. This must be the case, for otherwise why would the second partner not have to pay when he admits that the money was borrowed for the partnership? It must therefore concern a case where the second partner seized funds and says that his fellow owes him the equivalent amount. According to this, everything is clear. Rama's statement that if the matter was established by witnesses the second partner is liable means that witnesses were present at the time one partner borrowed the money, the lender explicitly said that he was lending the money to both partners, and the witnesses also knew that the loan was for partnership needs, such as building a jointly owned house or another partnership expense. In that case everyone agrees that both partners are liable. Even if the lender advanced the funds for the partnership without either partner's knowledge, both would still be liable to the extent of the benefit, no worse than one who improves another's property without authorization and may recover the value of the improvement. The case necessarily involves an actual benefit, for without benefit the other partner would be exempt even if his fellow had spent his own money, as explained in siman 178. Since there was benefit, both are liable even if the money was advanced without their knowledge, all the more so where one partner was present when the partnership loan was made. Furthermore, none of the Rosh's reasons that one partner cannot encumber his fellow applies here. Even if the second partner had been present and refused to borrow, had the matter come before beit din, beit din could have compelled the necessary borrowing against his will, as explained in siman 178, seif 2, in Rama. Rambam also correctly writes that each partner is the other's agent in matters necessary for the partnership, because had the second partner been present he would certainly have agreed. This is no worse than a wife who regularly conducts business and borrows money, where the husband must repay, as explained in siman 96. Therefore the second partner cannot seize the borrowed funds as payment of a debt owed him by his fellow, since the loan obligation rests upon him as well. But where the second partner merely admits facts, the case cannot mean that he admits he actually saw the lender lend for partnership needs, because then he was present at the loan and, if he consented, he certainly became liable. Nor can it mean that after the lender advanced the money to both partners for partnership needs, the second partner was present when his fellow brought the money into the partnership, consented to use it, and actually dealt with the lender's money. In such a case he surely became liable, no worse than where the lender sends money through an agent to both partners and one takes it in the other's presence, while the other remains silent and then uses the money. Rather, Rama must mean that the second partner admits only that at the same time his fellow says he borrowed the money, that fellow contributed an equivalent sum to the partnership. In that case the Rosh correctly rules that the second partner did not become obligated to the lender. He can say, as the Taz explains, that his fellow owes him an offsetting amount. He can say, "I do not know whether you borrowed for the partnership. Perhaps you borrowed solely for yourself and then contributed your own borrowed money to the partnership. In that event only you are the lender's borrower, not I." When the Rosh says that one partner is not encumbered merely by his fellow's statement, he means that the borrowing partner is not believed to say that he borrowed for partnership purposes so as to encumber the second partner. Since the first partner alone became the lender's borrower and acquired the money through the loan, the second partner can seize it for a debt owed him by his fellow, as explained in siman 99. The case assumes that the partnership funds have been exhausted. Rama's words are therefore clear. It appears that the Shach too would agree, because this is manifestly correct. When the Shach requires the second partner's consent and instruction, that is specifically regarding guaranty, as will be explained. We can now answer the Shach's question against the Levush. The Shach asks: if the second partner admits that with his consent and instruction his fellow borrowed for partnership needs, what difference does it make whether the funds actually entered the partnership? The lender gave them to the first partner as the second partner's agent, which is as though he gave them to the second partner himself. This objection is not understandable if the second partner was absent from the actual loan. He can say, "Even though I instructed you to borrow for the partnership, since the loan money never entered the partnership, you must either have borrowed it for yourself or not borrowed it at all, and you and the lender are conspiring against me." If one person tells another, "Borrow money for me," and the lender or agent later says that he delivered it, while the intended borrower says he did not receive it from the agent, the intended borrower is exempt if he did not authorize transmission through that agent. Even if he did authorize the agent to send it, there is a dispute regarding an agent appointed without witnesses. The Shach must therefore have understood the Levush to be discussing a case where the second partner was actually present and instructed the borrowing. When the Shach says that the second partner told the lender not to give the money unless it was delivered in his presence, his intent is that even if he made such a condition, if the funds actually entered the partnership he certainly becomes liable despite the lender's deviation from the instruction. The Levush, however, is speaking where the second partner was not present at the loan. To explain fully the Levush's distinction between establishment by witnesses and admission by the partner himself, it appears that he is discussing guaranty, namely whether, if the borrowing partner cannot pay, the second partner who was absent from the loan must pay the entire amount. If witnesses establish the matter, meaning they saw the lender lend for partnership needs and also knew that the second partner had appointed the borrower as his agent, then the borrower was an agent appointed before witnesses. Although the second partner says the money never entered the partnership, we apply the chazakah that an agent carries out his agency and presume that it did enter the partnership. The reason the Levush needs this chazakah rather than simply saying that the second partner became liable immediately when the money reached the agent is that he is speaking about mutual guaranty. The Levush repeatedly states that the issue is whether the second partner becomes guarantor for his fellow. For mutual guaranty between partners who borrow, there are two rationales. First, the lender advanced the money on the understanding that each would guarantee the other, because he agreed to lend only to both jointly. Second, Rama writes in a responsum that because each partner benefits from the entire loan, that benefit is sufficient to create guaranty. Here the witnesses did not tell the lender at the time of the loan that the borrower was acting as the second partner's agent. Thus, if the money never entered the partnership, even though the second partner truly became liable for his own share immediately because the borrower was in fact his agent, he did not become guarantor. Even a guarantor at the time money is given becomes bound only because of the benefit that the lender trusted him. That rationale is absent here because the lender did not know that the borrower was the second partner's agent. Once the money entered the partnership, however, the second partner becomes guarantor for the reason stated by Rama, because each partner benefited from all the money. This is why the Levush needed the chazakah that an agent performs his mission. The Shach's second question against the Levush is likewise not difficult, for we often recognize a migo even against a chazakah. It is entirely possible that the Shach himself would agree with these practical rulings but understood the Levush differently. According to this explanation, there is no practical dispute among the authorities. The resulting rule is this: regarding whether the second partner can say that his fellow owes him an offsetting debt, if witnesses establish that the lender lent for partnership needs, he cannot make that claim. If there are no witnesses and the second partner was not present at the loan, he can say, "The money was yours, borrowed for yourself, and you owe me an offsetting amount." Regarding whether he becomes guarantor for the whole debt, witnesses are required to establish that the borrowing was done at his instruction. We then presume that the funds entered the partnership and he becomes guarantor. If there are no witnesses and he merely admits that his fellow borrowed at his instruction but says the money never entered the partnership, he is believed and does not become guarantor at all.
