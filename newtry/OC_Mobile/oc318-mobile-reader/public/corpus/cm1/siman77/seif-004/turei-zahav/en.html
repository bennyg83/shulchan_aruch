(Ibid.: Through the migo that she could say, ‘I paid you.’) Maharshal says this migo is essential. If payment was impossible, for example because she was known to have no money, she is not believed. Where the migo exists, the creditor cannot say, “Why is your document still in my hand?” because the document was written in both names. Maharshal therefore rules that when two people borrowed in one document and one paid, took the document, and sought contribution from the other, the second is believed to say he already paid his share. Once the creditor was paid, the document’s lien was discharged; the creditor’s supporting testimony is only one witness. Even a claim of payment after the first borrower redeemed the document is believed with an oath. If the creditor wrote the payer a receipt for the full debt, however, the document remains evidence against the other borrower, like a document retained by a guarantor after payment. With contemporary bearer notes, where discharge of the original lien does not destroy the paper’s evidentiary function, the note ordinarily supports contribution. Yet because the joint signer originally undertook liability only to whoever lawfully held the note, not necessarily to his co-signer, a receipt or endorsement from the former holder is still needed to show that the note was transferred through payment. Whether oral joint borrowers are treated like written joint borrowers is disputed. The Rosh’s responsum suggests yes; the Rashba expressly limits the rule to a joint instrument because the lenders chose to write one document. The Tur and Tosafot also incline to that limitation, and the matter requires study.
