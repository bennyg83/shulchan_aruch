(I also found the following.) The Shach leaves unresolved whether one partner may waive his fellow partner's share. Nevertheless, in sales the accepted ruling is that when one partner sells his fellow's share, the sale is effective against the purchaser. This also follows from what the Beit Shmuel writes at the end of siman 85 in the name of the Beit Hillel. Where there was price fraud, however, the matter is not explicit. The Beit Hillel discusses only a case with no fraud at all, where the partner objects because the property was sold before the time they had fixed between themselves or before the proper market time for that merchandise. If there was fraud of one sixth, and the partner who made the sale waited longer than the time needed to show the item to a merchant, then even though the sale remains effective as to his own share, the market regulation protecting purchasers does not necessarily apply to his fellow's share. The Shach's reason is that otherwise no one would transact with a partner. That concern does not apply to price fraud, which is not the normal manner of commerce. Only the selling partner, by waiting beyond the period for showing the item to a merchant, can be said to have waived his claim. His fellow did not consent, whether he had already protested, had not yet learned of the sale, or had not yet had the opportunity to show the item to a merchant from the time he learned of the sale. If the merchandise was not in his possession but remained with the selling partner, perhaps the time limit for showing it to a merchant does not apply to him. Although one can distinguish between merchandise in the purchaser's possession, where the purchaser is not expected to return it to the seller merely so the seller can have it appraised, and merchandise still held by the selling partner, where the other partner could ask that it be handed to him for appraisal, nevertheless perhaps the absence of the merchandise from his possession causes him to put valuation out of mind and rely on the selling partner to have assessed it properly. The Sages stated generally that where the merchandise is not in his possession, the period for showing it to a merchant does not apply. This may hold even with standardized commodities whose approximate value merchants know without seeing the particular goods, because when the goods are not in his possession he naturally ceases to focus on their value. This requires study. There was also room to argue that just as partners act as agents for one another even where the act is disadvantageous, perhaps the same applies to sale and even to waiver, so that the selling partner's failure to object within the appraisal period would bind the other partner as well. Yet the Urim VeTumim brings proofs that one partner cannot waive the other's right. The Shach himself rests only on the concern that otherwise no one would buy from a partner, and that reason does not fit ordinary fraud of one sixth. In addition, a sale in which the partner openly sells his fellow's share may be effective because he explicitly acts regarding that share. Price fraud is different, for the transaction is defective in a way that is not normal commercial conduct. Silence after the appraisal period therefore proves much less regarding the fellow's share. The selling partner can say that he regarded the sale as void as to his fellow's share and therefore did not involve himself further. Even with fraud of less than one sixth, there is room to say that the sale is not binding on the fellow partner according to the Urim VeTumim's position that one partner cannot waive the other's rights. No decisive proof was cited that such waiver is effective. The responsum of Maharam cited by the Shach, and the material in siman 176, concern a non-Jew, where hearing some basis for waiver may cause him not to pay. In ordinary agency the law is explicit that any fraud can void the transaction, and partnership is no better where no distinction from agency has been stated. It may even be weaker, because where a principal expressly sends an agent to sell, some authorities say the agency ordinarily extends even to an unfavorable transaction, whereas a partner did not necessarily authorize his fellow to sell without his knowledge. Still, with fraud of less than one sixth, the market regulation may apply, because most ordinary sales do not scrutinize such a small deviation. If so, in matters for which the formal laws of onaah do not apply, such as sales by estimation or bulk and perhaps land or similar property, if the deviation is nevertheless so large that merchants ordinarily would not tolerate it, the market regulation should not apply and the sale should not bind the fellow's share. Perhaps, if the other partner remains in possession of the merchandise, even where there was no fraud at all he is believed to say, "You were not my agent," and the market regulation does not apply. This requires careful study.
