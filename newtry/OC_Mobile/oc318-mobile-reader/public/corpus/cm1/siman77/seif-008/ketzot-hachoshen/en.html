The creditor released one of them. Mabit rules concerning Reuven, Shimon, and Levi, who jointly bought merchandise and signed a note to Yehudah for the entire debt, each as a kablan guarantor for the others. Reuven and Shimon had funds, Levi did not. Yehudah released Reuven from his guaranty for Levi on condition that Reuven pay his own share, and afterward collected Shimon's share plus Levi's share from Shimon. Mabit held that Shimon cannot recover half of Levi's share from Reuven, because Yehudah could originally have collected the whole guaranty from either one. Once he chose Shimon, Shimon paid his own independent guaranty obligation and did not pay on Reuven's behalf. The Shach objects that Tur in siman 132, following Rambam, says that where one of two guarantors pays the whole debt he may recover the other's share. Rosh likewise rules so. In my view Mabit is correct under our practical understanding of two ordinary guarantors. The Tur and Rosh speak within their model where each guarantor is treated like a kablan guarantor for the other guarantor as well, much like two co borrowers according to their approach. In that model, when one pays the whole debt he has indeed paid a portion that the other was personally bound to him to bear. Our ruling concerning two co borrowers, however, is that they are only ordinary guarantors for one another, not kablan guarantors. Yet with two people who independently guaranteed one debtor, we still allow the creditor to collect from either one. That cannot be because each became kablan for the fellow guarantor, since we do not adopt that model. Rather, as Rashba and Maharik explain, each independently gave the creditor security for the whole debt. A security lien is not naturally divided. Each therefore stands separately as full security for the principal debtor. There is no partnership or mutual guaranty between the two guarantors themselves. Accordingly, if one guarantor pays, he has merely discharged his own full guaranty to the creditor. He has not paid the other guarantor's debt, because the other guarantor never guaranteed him. This resembles two purchasers whose separate fields are each subject to a creditor's lien. If the creditor chooses one field, the purchaser whose field was taken has no claim against the other purchaser merely because the creditor could have chosen the other field. Each field bore its own lien independently. The rule concerning brothers who divide inherited property is different. There the heirs stand in their father's place and share the obligation to pay his debts. When one portion is taken, the division can be undone or adjusted because the division itself rested on both heirs jointly bearing the father's obligations. Ordinary guarantors have no comparable division or mutual undertaking. Thus Mabit's ruling is correct. All the more so where the creditor expressly released one guarantor before collecting from the other. The paying guarantor cannot claim that he benefited the released guarantor by payment. The latter had already been discharged by the creditor's waiver. There is therefore no basis to recover from him.
