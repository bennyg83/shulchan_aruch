We do not listen to him, and so forth. Shach, following Gidulei Terumah, asks from simanim 122 and 176, where one partner can litigate for his own share even when the other partner is absent. Sema distinguishes between litigating and actually receiving money. Shach instead says the Yerushalmi rule applies only where the absent partner's exact share is unknown. If the shares are known, the present partner can receive his portion. That reading does not fit Ramban and Rosh well. If the amount of the share were unknown, there would be no need for the verse excluding denial against one partner. The obvious reason for withholding payment would simply be that no one knows how much belongs to him. Nor would the Yerushalmi's reverse inference work: from the rule that one partner cannot receive alone it infers that two co-borrowers are mutually responsible. If the first rule depended only on uncertainty of shares, it would prove nothing about co-borrower liability where each one's share is known. Ramban and Rosh therefore understand a deeper principle: where two parties created the claim jointly, the debtor may insist on dealing with the joint claim as such and need not split payment merely because one partner appears alone. Nevertheless, where the absent partner has gone overseas for a long period, beit din should be able to separate and pay the present partner's established share, just as one can sometimes collect or adjudicate in a party's absence to prevent serious hardship. If the other partner is nearby, readily available, or expected shortly, there is no reason to force division. That is the best practical application under Ramban and Rosh.
However, I do not understand why Mechaber presents their view without mentioning the substantial opposing authorities. Rashba follows Raavad and says the opposite: one partner may demand the entire joint deposit because the partners are treated as having authorized one another. Baal HaTerumot reads the Yerushalmi differently. The verse concerns denial where two partners together sue and the defendant admits dealing with only one. The exemption from the special deposit-oath liability does not prove that an individual partner lacks a monetary claim to his share. Rambam's formulation in Hilchot Shevuot likewise supports Baal HaTerumot's reading. If one partner sues and the defendant denies, the special oath consequence can apply; if both sue and the defendant says he dealt with only one, the special liability can differ. This implies one partner can indeed possess an actionable monetary claim. Thus Rambam, Raavad, Rashba, and Baal HaTerumot form a substantial opposing camp. The law should therefore be regarded as disputed rather than settled absolutely.
