We do not listen to him, etc. The Ir Shushan and Sema write that he cannot compel payment of his share, but the defendant must nevertheless litigate with him regarding that share even when the other partner is not in the city, as the Tur and Mechaber rule at the end of siman 122. In my opinion this is incorrect, as I explain below in seif katan 25.
And he heard but did not come. This implies that if we know he did not hear, even though he is in the city, the custodian need not pay the partner who appeared until the absent partner is notified. The Sema states this, but the point is obvious. More precisely, even if we do not know that he heard, payment is withheld until it is established that he did hear.

All this applies to two, etc. The distinction between two people who deposited jointly and one depositor who died leaving two sons requires explanation, and the source for the Rama’s distinction is unclear. Rashba’s responsum that one of two sons may receive his share proves nothing, because according to Rashba that son may demand both his own share and his brother’s. Rashba follows his own view that one of two joint lenders or depositors may demand the entire amount, construing the Yerushalmi like Raavad and unlike the Rosh and Ran. But according to our ruling that one partner cannot demand even his own share until the other appears, the same might apply to brothers. The Rama’s distinction may be that where two people originally lent or deposited together, the obligor became liable from the outset to both as a unit. He cannot divide the obligation without both present. By contrast, upon a father’s death each son newly acquires a known half. A stronger distinction is that in an ordinary joint deposit the respective ownership shares may be unknown, and the entire fund may belong to the absent partner. The Rosh and Ran carefully write that the obligor may not give one of them “half of the money,” not “his share,” implying that the share is not known. The Tur therefore says, “Two who lent or deposited,” rather than “partners,” because the case need not involve equal ownership. With heirs, however, the father’s estate is known to pass equally to both, and one son may receive his half. Gedolei Terumah also gives this distinction. Accordingly, even partners may receive their respective shares when equal ownership is established, and this is the principal ruling. This also reconciles the Tur and Mechaber at the end of siman 122, who rule that even when the other partner is absent, the present partner may sue for his own share. It is strained to say, as the Ir Shushan and Sema do, that he may litigate but cannot compel payment. If the defendant need not pay him, and need not even deposit the money with beit din, why must he litigate? The Rosh and Tur there plainly indicate that he must pay the known share. Baal HaTerumot likewise says that if the debtor admits the debt and insists upon paying only the present partner’s share while depositing the absent partner’s share with beit din, he may do so. This appears to conflict with the ruling here only if the shares are known. Rather, here the ownership shares are unknown, while there they are known. The Mechaber’s wording here may imply that in every case the absent partner’s share may be deposited with beit din, but in my opinion the distinction stated above is correct.
