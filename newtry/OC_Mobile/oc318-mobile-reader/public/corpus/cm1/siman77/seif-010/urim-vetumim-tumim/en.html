And the same applies if both are written in one document, and so forth. This ruling requires study. Its source is Rashba, who reasons that two creditors written in one note are like partners. But even full partners who jointly deposited or lent money are not, according to Ramban and Rosh, automatically entitled to have the debtor pay only one of them without the other. What does writing them in one document add? If a written document changes the rule, the Yerushalmi's analogy should run both ways. Two borrowers written in one note would then perhaps cease to be mutually responsible, which is implausible. If anything, a joint written document strengthens their shared obligation. The correct explanation is that Rashba follows his own broader position, like Raavad, that one partner can sue for the entire joint claim because the partners are presumed to authorize one another. Then the fact that both lenders are written together in a single note shows a formal joint-credit relationship and supports treating each as authorized to collect. But we do not generally rule like Rashba on this point; Mechaber follows Ramban and Rosh above. Accordingly, Rama's extension from a single joint document is difficult to reconcile with the governing rule and requires further study.

The language can also mean that collection requires both parties acting together, and ambiguous document language should ordinarily be construed against the holder. Rivash, responsum 481, had an additional fact: the document expressly created ketubah security for the wife against the obligation. From that Rivash proves the parties cannot have intended that collection always require both spouses' consent. If the husband died or divorced her and refused consent, the ketubah lien could become impossible to realize. Therefore the better reading there was that neither spouse could collect the other's half, but each retained an independent half-interest. Rama in Even HaEzer likewise reflects that special context. Here, however, Rama omits the ketubah-security fact. Without that contextual proof, one cannot state with certainty that the document grants separate half-collection rights. The holder of an ambiguous instrument bears the burden. Rivash also gives a second rationale. Even if joint consent was originally required, where the wife's ketubah can be collected only from this very asset and she comes through the husband's own obligation, his refusal to consent cannot be used to destroy her lien. Her enforcement can then be treated as equivalent to his consent. That rationale can produce a different result where she has other ketubah assets available. Therefore Rama should ideally have stated the contextual basis, because the two explanations have important practical differences.
