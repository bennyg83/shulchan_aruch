We do not listen to him. See Shach, seif katan 25, who writes that if his share is known, we do listen to him. Tumim objects from the Yerushalmi, which compares this to the law of two lenders, where we do not give each lender half because it is as though each lent the entire amount. So too with two borrowers, each is guarantor for the other and it is as though each borrowed the entire sum. If the Shach is correct, then even with two borrowers, whenever the amount borrowed by each is known they should not be guarantors for one another. This is not precise. With two lenders, the matter depends only upon the lenders. Once each lender's share is known, it makes no difference to the debtor which one is owed which amount. With two borrowers, however, even when each borrower's internal share is known, it is still considered as though the division is not known from the lender's standpoint, because everything depends upon the lender's intent. He did not lend a separate amount to each borrower, but lent to both jointly. Tumim further says that he does not understand the proof the Shach brings from Baal HaTerumot. In my view the Shach's intent is straightforward. He first disagrees regarding a case where the other lender is not in town, ruling that when the absent lender's share is unknown, nothing is paid to the claimant, since perhaps the entire amount belongs to his fellow. The Shach then cites Baal HaTerumot, who writes that if the other lender is in town one may give that lender's share to beit din. Earlier, however, Baal HaTerumot writes that when the other lender is in town, the entire amount is given to the claimant, and the Tur likewise states this without qualification and does not say that the absent lender's share is deposited with beit din. It must therefore be that a further distinction is necessary. When the other lender's share is known, that known share is placed with beit din, because it is known to belong to him. But when his share is unknown, the entire amount is given to the claimant who appears while the other is in town. Since the absent lender's share is unknown and he heard of the claim but did not appear, we infer that the whole amount belongs to the present claimant, and it must all be paid to him. This is why the Shach concludes that the Mechaber's ruling requires study. Here the Mechaber discusses a case where the share is unknown, yet writes that the debtor has the option to place the money with beit din. But where the share is unknown, he should have to give the entire amount to the claimant, as explained above.
