By virtue of a migo. Meaning, in siman 130, seif 3, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write that a guarantor cannot collect from the borrower, even if the promissory note is in his possession, until he brings witnesses that he paid his fellow's debt, because possession of the note is not proof that the borrower's obligation to him has become a debt evidenced by a note. If the lender wrote to the guarantor, 'I have received from you the amount of the note,' this enables him to collect from the borrower. Here too, if the husband already paid and now comes to collect half from the wife, she cannot say, 'You took all the money,' unless the lender did not write to the husband, 'I have received from you,' and the like. In that case she is believed to say, 'I repaid you after you paid the lender.' The Tur and Shulchan Aruch add, as a greater novelty, that if the husband is still alive, even against him she cannot make such a claim, and all the more so against his heirs. Thus concludes the Sema. See the Taz, who cites the responsum of Maharshal concerning two people who borrowed on one note. One of them paid and took the note into his possession, while the other claimed that he had paid his share. Maharshal ruled that he is believed, because once the note was paid its lien was extinguished. This has practical relevance to our mamramot, whether the rule that the lien has been extinguished applies to them. See there.
