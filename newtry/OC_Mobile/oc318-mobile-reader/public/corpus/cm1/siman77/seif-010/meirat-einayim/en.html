To pay half from her ketubah. The Tur and Mechaber are teaching only that half of the debt ultimately falls upon her and that husband and wife are treated like two joint borrowers. They do not spell out all the creditor's collection rules.
It appears that if the wife has no separate property and the husband has enough to pay the whole debt, the creditor need not wait until the ketubah becomes collectible. He collects the entire debt from the husband, and the husband's heirs later deduct half of that debt from the wife's ketubah. We do not say that she should now sell the ketubah for its present market value and pay half, because a wife commonly waives claims against her husband and there may be no purchaser, unless the husband himself buys it where there is an excess beyond the basic ketubah amount.They later recover half from her.
The Levush writes that this should apply specifically where women customarily conduct independent financial business. Where women do not ordinarily transact independently, although the lender who relied upon her may collect half from her, it is reasonable that the husband or his heirs should not be able to recover that half from her. She is believed to say that the husband actually received all the money and that she entered as a co-borrower only to facilitate his livelihood.Except through a migo that 'I repaid you.' Below, siman 130, the Tur writes that a guarantor cannot recover from the borrower until he brings witnesses that he paid on his behalf.
Even if the promissory note is in the guarantor's possession, the borrower may say, 'I repaid you,' because the note in the guarantor's hands does not make the guarantor a documented lender. But if the lender wrote to the guarantor, 'I received from you the amount of the note,' that enables him to collect from the borrower. So too here: if the husband already paid and seeks half from the wife, she can say, 'you took all the money' only if he lacks a receipt from the lender.
If he has such a receipt, she cannot use a repayment claim against him. The Tur and Mechaber state the law even where the husband is still alive to teach that she cannot make such a claim against him, and all the more so against his heirs after his death.Borrowers who borrowed together.
