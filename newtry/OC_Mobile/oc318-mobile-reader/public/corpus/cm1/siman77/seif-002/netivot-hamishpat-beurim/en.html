The guarantor is not pursued first. Ketzot HaChoshen seeks to answer the authorities' objection to those who hold that joint borrowers are treated as primary obligors, based on the case in the chapter HaShoel where partners borrowed an item and the owner was in the service of one of them. Ketzot answers that even if each is regarded as having borrowed the whole item, it is still as though one borrower lent part of it onward to his fellow. Siman 291 explains that if a guardian improperly transfers an item to another guardian, then even if the first guardian enjoyed the exemption of being with the owner, he is liable for the period in which the item was in the second guardian's possession. The same, Ketzot argues, applies here. This is not precise. That case concerns a guardian who violated the rules by transferring the item where transfer was forbidden, thereby reducing the level of safeguarding. But if he transferred it to someone to whom the owner regularly entrusts property, so that the transfer was permitted, the first guardian is exempt even if the second later commits an act that ordinarily creates liability, such as negligence. This is certainly true according to those authorities who exempt the first guardian when the second is negligent. Even according to those who hold the first guardian liable, their reason is that otherwise every guardian could hand the property to his wife or children and thereby exempt himself. That rationale applies only where, had the loss occurred while the item remained in the first guardian's domain, he would have been liable. His transfer cannot create an escape from an existing duty. Here, however, the first guardian was with the owner and would have been exempt had the event occurred in his own domain. Why should he become liable merely because he transferred the item to a person whom the owner regularly trusts, without reducing the safeguarding? The concern that every guardian will transfer the item simply to escape liability does not apply, because he was already exempt even if he retained it. All the more so here, where he transferred it with the owner's consent, since the partners borrowed it jointly. Indeed, the item was not transferred at all, for it died while within the shared domain of both partners. His possible liability therefore cannot be based on being a borrower during the other's possession, but only on being treated as a primary obligor for his fellow, and this is straightforward.
