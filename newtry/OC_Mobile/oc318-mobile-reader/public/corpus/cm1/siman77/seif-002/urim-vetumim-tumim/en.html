If one partner himself sues the other and says, "I borrowed money and placed it into the partnership," and the second partner admits that fact, the second plainly owes his share. There is no difference between one partner contributing his own money and borrowing it from another before placing it into the common business. Partnership accounting must recognize the contribution. Even if the first partner wrongfully obtained money but the second knowingly approved its use for the partnership, partnership liability can arise. All the more so with a lawful loan. Once the first partner has a direct claim against the second, the original lender may sometimes reach that claim through R. Natan.
Therefore, the deeper dispute between Rambam and Rosh may concern how much of the debt the second partner owes when the first cannot pay. According to Rambam, two who borrowed together are guarantors for one another. If one lacks assets, the other can ultimately be responsible for the full amount. According to Rosh, if the second partner was never personally party to the loan and merely became liable internally for his partnership share, he may owe only that share and not become guarantor for the balance. Rama's distinction between proof by witnesses and mere admission can perhaps be defended this way. If witnesses show that, at the very time of the loan, the first partner borrowed expressly on behalf of the partnership and with the other's authorization, then the lender may have relied on both. The second can become a true guarantor because the transaction itself was visibly partnership borrowing. If the second partner only later admits that the borrowed money happened to enter the partnership, the lender may never have known or relied on him. The classic guarantor rationale of "in consideration of the lender's reliance upon me" is missing. Still, most authorities understand Rosh more simply: the second partner is liable only if witnesses or his own admission establish that the first acted as his agent in borrowing. The distinction is evidentiary, not between witnesses and admission as independent legal mechanisms. The apparent contradictions in Rosh's responsa can be resolved by cases where the first partner has assets, where R. Natan does not operate against the second, or where internal offsetting claims exist. Thus Shach's general reading remains the more persuasive one, though Rama's narrower distinction has a defensible basis.
