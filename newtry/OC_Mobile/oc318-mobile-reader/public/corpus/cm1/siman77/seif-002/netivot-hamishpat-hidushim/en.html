If there are witnesses that the lender expressly lent the money for the partnership to both partners, and the loan benefited the partnership by generating gain, then the second partner is himself a borrower and direct litigant against the lender and must pay him. The second partner cannot say to the first, "You owe me an offsetting amount," because he too became directly obligated to the lender even though he was not present at the original loan. Likewise, if he admits that when the first partner brought the loan proceeds into the partnership he told him that the lender had lent them to both partners for the partnership, and the second partner agreed to use the money for partnership business, he too became directly liable for his share. But if he admits only that an amount equal to the loan entered the partnership, without admitting that the lender lent it to both partners, he did not become a direct borrower toward the lender, only toward his partner, and may assert offsets against that partner. As to whether the absent partner became a guarantor: if there are witnesses that he instructed the first partner to borrow for the partnership, then if the lender was informed that the loan was for both partners he certainly becomes a guarantor. Even if the lender was not informed, if the second partner claims that the funds never entered the partnership, he nevertheless becomes a guarantor because an agent is presumed to perform his agency and the second partner benefited from the loan. This applies only where there are witnesses that he instructed the borrowing. If there are no such witnesses, he becomes a guarantor only if he admits the funds entered the partnership. If he denies that they entered the partnership, he is believed through a migo that he could have denied authorizing the borrowing.
