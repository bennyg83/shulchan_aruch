And some say that if it was not established, etc. Migdal David and the Sema extensively challenge the Ir Shushan. Although the Ir Shushan’s explanation of the Rama is not correct, his legal conclusion appears sound. Rabbi Yosef bei Rav’s distinction between a partner’s admission and proof by witnesses is unpersuasive. The Rosh’s reason is that one partner is not subjected to liability by the statement of the other, and that reasoning makes no such distinction. The Rosh’s case itself appears to have been established by witnesses. The Beit Yosef likewise does not accept the distinction. Rabbi Shlomo Cohen, Mahari ibn Lev, Bach, Mabit, and Mahara Sasson all question or reject it. The Rambam can be understood as speaking where the borrowing was undertaken for the partnership by the second partner’s express instruction. In that case all agree that he is liable because the borrower acted as his appointed agent. The Rambam was not discussing the ordinary rule of mutual guaranty alone. Therefore, whether the partner admits or witnesses establish that the other borrowed for partnership purposes, the second partner is not liable to the lender merely because of that fact, unless it is established or admitted that the borrowing occurred with his consent and instruction. Since the Rosh is later than Ramban, and the partner is in possession, money cannot be extracted in a case of doubt. The Ir Shushan further distinguishes where the partner says the money never entered the partnership. He suggests that if witnesses establish that the borrowing occurred with the partner’s consent and direction, the partner is not believed to say that it never entered the partnership, because an agent is presumed to have carried out his mission. But where the partner himself admits giving consent, he is believed through a migo to say that the money never entered the partnership. This does not appear correct. Once he admits that the borrowing was made with his consent and instruction for the partnership, the lender delivered the money to his agent, which is equivalent to delivery to him. Moreover, the presumption that an agent performs his mission applies whether the authorization is proved by witnesses or admitted. The distinction can stand only where the second partner told the lender not to deliver the money unless it was brought before him for partnership use. On that understanding, Rabbi Yosef bei Rav may also agree with the practical conclusion. His phrase “a matter known to be for the partnership” would mean that the loan was actually taken for the partnership under the second partner’s direction. In that circumstance the Rosh also agrees that he is liable. If the money in fact entered the partnership, liability is clear. This is the principal ruling.
The partner who admits need not pay, etc. This is not contradicted by siman 93, end of seif 13, where Shimon claims that Reuven knows a debt is partnership money and Reuven must swear that he does not know. The Sema explains that there Shimon says that he and Reuven jointly borrowed one hundred from Yaakov and that this very debt remains unpaid. My revered teacher correctly observed that the cases are not comparable. Here the partner is exempt from the lender’s direct claim. Between the partners themselves, however, one may claim that the money entered the partnership and that each bears an equal share. Siman 93 concerns that internal accounting. Accordingly, the Beit Yosef’s statement that the Rambam clarified the present rule at the end of Hilchot Shutafin applies only to the proposition that one partner’s admission does not itself prejudice the other. Baal HaTerumot, gate 44, part 1, should be read similarly. For two guarantors, or one who became guarantor for two debtors, see siman 132, seifim 4 and 5 and my comments there.
