The entire debt is forgiven, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh appears to base the rule on the presumption that the creditor forgave after being appeased. If it is known that he forgave voluntarily, without appeasement, the forgiveness applies only to the person named and not to his fellow. He further infers that if the creditor forgave only part of the debt, even after appeasement, the forgiveness benefits only the person forgiven. The Darkei Moshe cites this. In my opinion, the Rosh implies the opposite. The Rosh writes that what was forgiven to one partner is forgiven to all, based on the Tosefta: “Partners who were forgiven, the forgiveness goes to the common account; if they said, ‘We forgave for the sake of so-and-so,’ what they forgave is forgiven to him.” Rabbeinu Simchah explained that the first clause concerns a partner who sought forgiveness. Since he acted as the others’ agent, the forgiveness is for all. The latter clause concerns a voluntary forgiveness, without any request, and therefore it benefits only the named person. Since people do not ordinarily forgive without appeasement, an unspecified forgiveness is presumed to have followed appeasement and therefore benefits both. The Rosh then adds that the Tosefta concerns a partial forgiveness, whereas in the case before him the language shows that the entire debt was forgiven. One cannot explain that the creditor merely released Reuven from collection while retaining the entire claim against Shimon, for if so Reuven needed no release at all, since the creditor could simply collect from Shimon. Thus, even if it is known that the creditor forgave without appeasement, the Rosh’s two concluding reasons still show that the fellow debtor is also released. The Sema likewise rules plainly that even voluntary forgiveness releases the fellow debtor, and explains that this is why the Tur and Rama omit the distinction between forgiveness after appeasement and voluntary forgiveness. The Rama’s own view requires study, however, because in Darkei Moshe he cites the Beit Yosef without objection and later explains another responsum of the Rosh as concerning a known voluntary forgiveness, which releases only the named debtor. Perhaps he retracted when he wrote the gloss here without qualification. The inference of the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe that a partial forgiveness after appeasement does not benefit the fellow also seems incorrect, for the Rosh expressly explains the Tosefta as concerning partial forgiveness, and nevertheless the first clause says that it goes to the common account. Perhaps the Beit Yosef had in mind another responsum of the Rosh concerning five co-guarantors, where one produced a release in favor of two of them. The Rosh ruled that only those two are released and the creditor may collect the remaining three shares, comparing this to partial payment. But there the question states that the creditor gave those two a complete release from every claim he had against them, so he had forgiven them the entire debt as against themselves. The phrase “if he forgave part” therefore means part of the debtors, not part of the debt. The distinction must be that by expressly naming two out of five he showed that he intended to release only them. Mahari ibn Chaim similarly explains that once the creditor descended to a specific number, he revealed that he did not intend to release all. Another responsum of the Rosh states that if the creditor gave one borrower a general release from every claim against him, the other borrowers must still pay their shares. The Beit Yosef distinguishes that the release did not specify all claims arising from that particular instrument. Chazeh HaTenufah, the Bach, the Sema, Mahari ibn Chaim, and Gedolei Terumah agree. The reason is that a general release from every kind of claim rests upon that particular person and cannot be treated as though he acted as agent for the others, especially since it includes claims in which they have no share. Another good reason is that the Rosh’s argument, “Why did Reuven need the release if the creditor could simply collect from Shimon?” does not apply where the release also covered unrelated claims, for Reuven needed it on account of those other claims. Gedolei Terumah asks why the same reasoning does not release the second of two guarantors below in seif 8. The answer is clear. Two joint borrowers are each principal debtors for their own share and guarantors for the other’s share, so forgiveness to one borrower ordinarily extends to the guaranty attached to his fellow. Ordinary guarantors, however, guarantee only the borrower and not one another. Even after one guarantor is released, the principal debt remains against the borrower, and there is no reason to infer that the second guarantor was also released. The Sema further writes that the rule applies only when the creditor says, “I forgive every claim I have against you under this instrument.” If he merely says, “I forgive what I lent you together with your fellow,” the fellow is not released. I do not know the source for this distinction. Since both are borrowers and reciprocal guarantors, forgiving what was lent to one together with his fellow should release the fellow as well. The Sema also writes that the rule applies only to gratuitous forgiveness. If the creditor released one debtor because that debtor paid his own share, even a broad release of every claim under the instrument does not release the fellow. The payment shows that the release was not made in the debtor’s capacity as agent for the other. This accords with the Beit Yosef and Mahari ibn Chaim and appears correct. The Rosh’s reasoning also supports it, because the debtor needed the release to establish that his own payment discharged him and prevent a renewed claim against him.
For then he must sue each one, etc. The Sema writes that the Rama follows his own view in Darkei Moshe, accepting the ruling of the Beit Yosef and the Mechaber above in seif 1. He adds that the Rosh holds that even without writing “from whichever of them I choose,” it is treated as though written. This observation is unnecessary. We plainly rule unlike the Rosh on this point, because all the early and later authorities disagree with him, as I explained in my work.
