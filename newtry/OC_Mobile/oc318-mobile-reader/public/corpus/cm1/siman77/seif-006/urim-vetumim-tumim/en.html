Only half is forgiven. Mechaber's rule is based on Beit Yosef's understanding that Rosh's statement, "If one of the partners was forgiven, the whole debt is forgiven," follows Rosh's view that co-borrowers are like full joint debtors. If they are only guarantors, forgiveness of one borrower's share should release only that half, leaving the other borrower liable for his own half. I have difficulty with this. Rosh derives his rule from the Tosefta about partners: if a creditor forgives through one partner after that partner's efforts, the benefit of the forgiveness can belong to the partnership as a whole. Rosh says that where the entire debt is forgiven, there are strong grounds to infer the creditor intended the forgiveness for all. If so, even where the partners are only guarantors, why should the other necessarily remain liable for his whole half? Where forgiveness was obtained through partnership efforts, the benefit can be shared. Rosh himself says ordinary forgiveness is generally presumed to have followed some persuasion. There is a similar difficulty in Rosh's responsa involving five co-obligors where two received releases. He makes the other three pay three shares, suggesting the released portions belong only to the released parties. The best explanation is that when the creditor names particular obligors and releases them individually, the very act of counting and identifying them shows an intention to free those persons rather than proportionally reduce everyone's obligation. Had he meant a general shared reduction, there would be no reason to grant separate releases to precisely two of the five. This can reconcile Rosh's cases. There is also a conceptual distinction between true joint debtors and mere guarantors. If both are full joint debtors, each acts to some extent as agent of the other in the common obligation. A negotiated release can therefore be understood as partnership activity for both. If each owes only his own half and merely guarantees the other's half, there is less basis to treat one debtor's private negotiation as agency for the other. Then even a negotiated forgiveness can naturally attach only to the one who obtained it, unless the creditor indicates otherwise. This may be Beit Yosef's intended distinction. Still, there is another major dispute. Maharam holds that even among true partners, an express forgiveness to a specifically named individual can belong to that person alone. Rosh follows Rabbenu Simchah more broadly toward partnership sharing. Rama below cites both views in tax cases. Therefore where the creditor expressly names one obligor, there is at least a safek among major authorities whether the forgiveness benefits all co-obligors. If the creditor is in possession or the debt structure is otherwise doubtful, he may be able to say kim li like Maharam. At minimum, where only part of the debt is forgiven rather than the whole, the scope of the forgiveness remains a substantial dispute and cannot be stated absolutely.
