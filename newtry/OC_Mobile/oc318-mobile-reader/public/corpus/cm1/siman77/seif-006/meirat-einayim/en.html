He waived only his share, etc.; and some disagree, etc. Those who disagree are the Rosh, consistent with his own position. The Tur writes that the Rosh holds that where two jointly borrowed from one lender under an ordinary document, even if the document does not expressly state that they became primary guarantors for one another, they nevertheless have the law of primary guarantors, and the lender may collect the entire debt from whichever one he chooses even if the other has assets. For this reason the Rosh likewise holds that if the lender waived to one borrower all claims he had against him under that document, since he could have demanded the entire amount from that borrower, the entire debt is waived. The lender cannot say to the other borrower, "I waived only collection from that person, but I will collect from you," because they were partners in the debt, and partners are agents for one another. Thus, when one partner secures a waiver regarding a shared obligation, such as a tax or similar burden, the benefit of the waiver belongs to all, as the Tur and Mechaber state below at the beginning of siman 178. Although there it is explained that this applies specifically where the tax collector waived to one partner after that partner persuaded him, for then we say he acted as agent of all the partners, whereas if the tax collector voluntarily waived to one without persuasion the benefit belongs only to that individual, the present case is different. There the language of the waiver did not itself indicate that the entire shared obligation was waived. Here, however, the lender said, "I waive to you every claim I have against you under this document." Since the entire amount of the document was claimable against him, we presume that the lender waived the entire amount. It is as though the borrower paid the entire debt, and nothing may be collected from the other borrower even if the waiver was without persuasion. This is why the Tur and Rama state the rule here without distinguishing whether the waiver followed persuasion. Although Rama did not cite in seif 1 the Rosh's view that where two borrowed jointly the lender may collect the entire debt from either one because we do not rule that way, he cites the Rosh's rule concerning waiver in order to teach that where the parties actually are primary guarantors for one another, the same result applies even according to our ruling. Since the Rosh originated the principle that waiver to one is waiver to all, Rama attached it to his words. From what I have written we learn that this law applies specifically where the lender says, "I waive to you every claim I have against you under this document." If he merely says, "I waive to you what I lent you together with your fellow under this document," the fellow is not released. We also learn that the rule applies specifically where the lender waived without receiving money. Since he knew that they were partners in the debt and therefore agents for one another, we say that he waived to both. But where he waived only because one borrower paid his share, even if he then waived every claim against that borrower under the document, we see that the borrower was not acting as agent for all. He had to pay his own money, and presumably the lender waived only because he received that borrower's share, not the share of the fellow who had not paid. The Beit Yosef writes this in the name of the Rosh. The Rosh himself notes that others challenged his principal ruling that the waiver applies to everyone. It is therefore a novelty and should not be extended beyond its precise case. His responsum further explains that we say the whole debt was waived only where he says, "I waive every claim I have against you under this document." Since the waiver addresses something in which they were partners, it extends to all. But if he does not mention the document and simply waives every claim he has against that person, we say that out of personal affection he waived all matters between them, without intending to benefit the other partners. He may then collect the other borrower's share. All of this is derived and carefully inferred from responsa of the Rosh, klal 73, simanim 10, 11, and 12, as I explained in the Derishah and in my glosses to Darkei Moshe. I have written at length because these distinctions have practical significance even according to our ruling where the document expressly makes them primary guarantors for one another. I have seen judges err in this law because they failed to consider these distinctions. Rama was too brief here, where fuller explanation was appropriate. Know these distinctions well and examine them carefully.
