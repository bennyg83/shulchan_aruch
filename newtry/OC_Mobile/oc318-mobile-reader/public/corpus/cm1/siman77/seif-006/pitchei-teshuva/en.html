By virtue of a migo that he could have said, “I repaid you.” See Baer Heitev and its reference to the Taz. The text there contains an error. Regarding our promissory notes, the correct reading is that the rule of a discharged lien does not apply to them. Baer Heitev also does not reproduce the Taz’s conclusion accurately, because its wording implies that with our promissory notes, where the lien is not discharged, the co-obligor cannot claim, “I already paid my share.” The Taz’s actual conclusion is otherwise. After citing Maharshal regarding two persons who borrowed on one document, the Taz writes that if the creditor gave the one who paid a receipt stating, “I have received from you the amount of the debt,” the original document could afterward serve as proof against the second debtor, similar to the law of a guarantor and principal debtor. Maharshal, he says, did not address such a case. Consequently, with our bearer promissory notes, where discharge of the lien does not apply, the note certainly remains evidence against the second obligor, and he must pay his share. Nevertheless, since the form of the promissory note itself shows that when he signed, he did not obligate himself specifically toward his co-signer, but only toward whoever would later hold the note, the co-signer who paid must also possess an appropriate receipt identifying the person who produced the note in beit din, analogous to the rule concerning a receipt made out to an unspecified bearer. See also Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 7, and Mahadura Tinyana, simanim 12 and 13, where another rationale is offered. A co-obligor may be believed to say, “I paid you,” because the usual inference, “If you paid, why is your document still in his hand?” does not apply strongly here. He may say that he was not concerned to leave the note with his co-obligor, assuming that the latter would surely destroy it, since he too was signed upon it and would fear that if it were lost and found by a third party, that third party might collect from him as well. Wherever there is a reasonable explanation why the debtor was not afraid to leave the document in the creditor’s possession, the inference from possession of the document is weakened. See also Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 70.
