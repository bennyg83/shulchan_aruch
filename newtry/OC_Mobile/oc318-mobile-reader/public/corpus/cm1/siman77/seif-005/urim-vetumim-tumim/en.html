His testimony is not valid, and so forth. Bach asks: If the admitting partner has enough assets to pay the entire debt, why does the lender need testimony from him at all? The lender can simply collect from him. If he lacks assets, why is he considered interested testimony? Since he cannot pay now, perhaps he has no present financial benefit in shifting liability to the other partner. Bach therefore questions the ruling and suggests future possible enrichment should not count as present interest. Shach answers that the first partner may have assets overseas or only inferior property, while the second has accessible intermediate-quality assets. There is a simpler answer. The first partner admits he personally borrowed the money. If the second partner is not held jointly responsible, the first remains liable for the unpaid portion and can be branded a wicked borrower who does not repay. He therefore has a clear personal interest in establishing the second partner's liability even if he presently lacks collectible assets. Bava Batra expressly recognizes avoidance of the status "borrower and do not pay" as sufficient personal interest in testimony. Accordingly, the partner is interested and cannot testify to shift part of his debt to the other.
They may testify for one another. Maharshal discusses three lenders to one borrower who feared he would deny the loan. They arranged that one would sue while the other two testified, obtained payment, and later admitted all three had been the lenders. Maharshal says the money must be returned. Even if they now have a migo, it is a migo against witnesses, and their conduct also damages their credibility because they deliberately created a misleading evidentiary structure. Shach rejects this, correctly. There is no "migo against witnesses" in the ordinary sense because no valid witnesses contradict their present claim. The problem with their original testimony is that they were financially interested, not that outside evidence proves the facts false. If their present possession is supported by a valid migo, the prior interest does not automatically destroy that migo. Nor is this the problematic "migo of two people" where each needs coordination with the other's claim. Each could independently have remained silent about his own interest. Even deliberate procedural wrongdoing does not necessarily erase a monetary migo. We find that people who seized collateral improperly, including prohibited collateral, can still retain ordinary migo rights under the established rules. Accordingly, Maharshal's categorical requirement to return the money is not justified merely by the earlier interested testimony.
