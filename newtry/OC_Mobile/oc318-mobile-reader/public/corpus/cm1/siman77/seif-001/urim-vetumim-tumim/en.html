Or they received a deposit together. In Responsa Rama, siman 27, the Rama argues that the law cannot mean an ordinary joint deposit. He proves this from the Gemara's case of an ox entrusted to five people, where if one withdraws but the ox would still be guarded without him, the Gemara says that he caused nothing and is not liable for the whole loss. He also cites the rule that one who deposits does so with the understanding that the custodian may entrust the object to his wife and children. Many authorities hold that if the wife or children are negligent and cannot pay, the husband is exempt. If joint custodians were automatically responsible for one another, why should this be so? The husband was the original custodian, and entrusting the object to his wife would seemingly be no better than the two of them having accepted custody together. From this the Rama concludes that the present rule must involve an express stipulation, comparable to an unpaid custodian stipulating to assume the liability of a borrower. The Shach objects that Ramban and Ran do not sound this way. He also answers the proof from the ox: even if one of the five must ultimately pay the entire amount as guarantor or surety, the substantive primary liability for the damage may still rest only on the others. If he pays, he can recover from them. This is a fair response, but the Rama's question remains: why did the Gemara not use that very case to explain the baraita that one who prepared only part of the damage becomes liable for the whole damage, namely where one of five custodians withdrew, the others could not pay, and he therefore paid the full amount through joint responsibility? The Gemara instead seeks another case. One can answer that the Gemara wanted a case of direct liability rather than secondary surety liability, but the Rama's inference is not the simple mistake the Shach suggests. The Rama's reference to an unpaid custodian who stipulates to be like a borrower is also best understood narrowly. He does not mean that the two custodians expressly stipulated that each would be responsible for the other. If so, the entire extensive dispute whether joint borrowers are guarantors or sureties could simply be resolved by examining their wording. Rather, his point is that an ordinary deposit gives the custodians no benefit and therefore no reason automatically to enter reciprocal surety. A borrower, who receives all the benefit, resembles a borrower of money and can be treated as jointly responsible. The Rama therefore understands the case as a deposit in which they assumed the higher borrower type liability. This reconciles his responsum somewhat with the authorities discussing joint borrowers. Still, the plain language of the authorities and even the Rama's own unqualified ruling here does not sound like his responsum. The stronger answer to the proof from a husband who gives the deposit to his wife is the principle that a custodian who gives to another competent custodian can say, "I placed an equivalent responsible person in my place." The second custodian replaces the first. With two people who accepted custody simultaneously, however, both were custodians from the outset and assumed reciprocal responsibility. One cannot later remove himself and say the other alone should remain. This explains why each joint custodian at least remains liable for his own share, whereas an original custodian who permissibly transfers to a second custodian may be entirely exempt. Thus the Rama's proof does not establish his limitation. Asifat Zekenim also cites authorities indicating that joint depositaries can have stronger reciprocal liability than the Rama assumed.
Whether orally. The Shach and Gidulei Terumah object to the Mechaber and initially require a written instrument. The Shach ultimately agrees that if the circumstances make clear that the loan was made jointly to both, an oral loan is sufficient. This is correct. The Yerushalmi derives the rule from the converse case: where two people lend to one borrower and he denies one of them, the denial may be treated as an empty denial because he cannot properly repay one partner without the other. If this reciprocal responsibility is derived from that case, it necessarily applies orally as well, for a documented loan would not carry the same oath issue discussed there. Responsa Rosh likewise indicates expressly that an oral loan can suffice. Gidulei Terumah's proposed distinction between a father and son and other borrowers has no source. Everything depends upon whether the money was received as one joint loan, rather than each person borrowing a separate half. Where it was jointly received, there is no distinction between oral and written form.
But if he has assets, etc. Maharik, in responsa 120 and 183, holds that even Rambam, who ordinarily treats two joint borrowers as guarantors for one another, speaks where they did not receive the entire fund jointly for common use. Even if they borrowed at one time, each took his own fifty and did not share the other's profit. But where they conduct business jointly with the whole fund, each receives benefit from all the money and is therefore a borrower with respect to the whole. The creditor can then collect the full amount from either. Beit Yosef disputes this, while Mabit and several other Spanish authorities agree with Maharik. Ramban's proof from joint borrowers of an object seems difficult. There, too, both receive benefit from the entire borrowed object, yet Ramban says each is responsible only for half as primary liability. Maharit already raises this. The answer emerges from the Rosh concerning partners who borrowed an object and one paid. Rashi says he paid the half imposed upon him. The Rosh explains that this does not mean he is ultimately exempt if the other refuses to pay or swear; the joint borrowers remain responsible for one another. But because he immediately paid the portion directly imposed upon him, and the other may yet pay or swear, the first acquires the relevant rights at once. Why does the Rosh call only half the amount "imposed upon him" if the lender may in some circumstances collect the whole from either? The distinction is between direct liability and secondary surety. When two people accepted custody together, both became primary custodians and also sureties for one another. But if the owner seeks to treat one alone as the exclusive litigant for the entire object and says, "I have nothing to do with the other person, you return everything or swear for everything," that custodian can respond that even if he alone had initially received the object, he then delivered part of its custody to another competent custodian known to the owner. The owner cannot reject the other's oath merely by choosing one party. Reciprocal surety is different from making each one the sole primary custodian of the whole. Maharik can therefore distinguish a money loan from borrowing an object. In an ordinary monetary loan for a joint business, the rule of a custodian giving to another custodian has no relevance. If both use all the money jointly and each profits from the whole, each can be regarded as a borrower of the whole. With a borrowed object, the use and custody can remain divisible between them, and the law of one custodian transferring to another remains relevant. This also reconciles apparently conflicting statements attributed to Ramban. In one place he says the creditor can collect everything from either, while in the law of partners who borrowed he appears to assign only half as primary liability. The former can be understood with respect to a loan of money or the ultimate surety obligation, while the latter concerns primary custodial liability for a borrowed object. Nimukei Yosef and Asifat Zekenim cite Rashba distinguishing joint guarantors from joint borrowers because guarantors support one undivided debt, while ordinary joint borrowers may each have taken and used his own portion. This closely supports Maharik: where both actually use the entire fund in partnership, the reason for treating them as separate borrowers disappears. At minimum, Maharik has substantial early support.
He then recovers his share from his fellow. The Shach asks that if we rule that joint borrowers are only like guarantors, the paying borrower should face the dispute below concerning one who became a guarantor without being told expressly, "Pay." Some authorities treat such payment as voluntarily paying another's debt without authorization, which creates no reimbursement right. Raavad does not prove otherwise because he holds that an ordinary guarantor can recover, and Rosh is even less of a proof if he regards the parties as sureties. Maharam in Mordechai nevertheless indicates that even where each is only a guarantor, the one who pays more than his share can recover from the other. The reason is commercial custom. Partners ordinarily pay obligations for one another and afterward settle between themselves. When they accept a joint loan, they do so against that background. It is therefore as though each said to the other, "Become my guarantor and pay if necessary," which creates reimbursement according to all opinions. Maharam supports this from the Gemara's rule concerning one who stands at the threshing floor for his fellow. There is also a double uncertainty. Perhaps the halachah follows those who treat the joint borrowers as sureties, and even if they are only guarantors, perhaps the halachah follows those who require the principal debtor to reimburse an ordinary guarantor. Added to the broader dispute concerning one who pays another's debt, this strongly supports reimbursement. Thus the Mechaber's rule can stand.
