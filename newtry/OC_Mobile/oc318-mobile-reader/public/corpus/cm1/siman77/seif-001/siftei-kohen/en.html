Or they received a deposit, etc. The Rama writes in responsum 27 that two custodians who jointly accepted custody are not automatically guarantors for one another. He suggests that Ramban’s statement, cited by the Maggid Mishneh, that a deposit with two people is like a loan to two people, refers only to an express condition. He seeks proof from the case of one who entrusted an ox to five people: if one withdraws from guarding and the remaining custodians could still guard it, the one who withdrew is not held liable as a guarantor. This does not appear correct. Ramban, the Rosh, and Ran in Shevuat HaPikadon indicate that even an ordinary joint deposit has the same law as a loan to two people. The Maggid Mishneh and Ramban in Baal HaTerumot, gate 44, part 1, likewise clearly imply this. The proof from the ox is no proof. That discussion asks whether the negligent custodian is liable as a tortfeasor. If the animal remained adequately guarded without him, what damaging act did he perform? The one who was negligent may indeed bear primary liability, while the other may have secondary liability as a guarantor if the negligent party cannot pay. Even under the view that one may collect the entire debt from either of two joint borrowers or custodians, this does not make the non-negligent party a tortfeasor. The practical difference is that the negligent custodian must reimburse the entire amount to the non-negligent one who paid. Had both been liable as tortfeasors, he would reimburse only his share. This resolves the matter.
Whether orally. The Beit Yosef writes that the rule applies orally as well as in writing, and Darkei Moshe cites him. In my opinion, the proof is not compelling. The Yerushalmi’s phrase “two who borrowed” does not prove an oral loan, for it adds, “even though they did not write that they are responsible,” implying a written instrument. The Rambam’s reference to a note is difficult to treat as imprecise, and the Tur and other authorities also speak specifically of two who borrowed in one note. The Beit Yosef argues that the authorities also mention two who purchased one acquisition, although an ordinary purchase is not written. But the phrase “in one note” may refer to both the loan and the purchase. Alternatively, each case follows its ordinary form: purchasers of one item are naturally partners because the item requires appraisal and division, whereas borrowers of money can divide it immediately, and money is regarded as already divided. Their partnership therefore arises from the single note. Rashba, responsum 1082, likewise indicates that a single note is essential. He writes that two lenders under one note are partners, for otherwise why did they lend under one note; and similarly two borrowers under one note are treated as guarantors for one another. Siman 67, seif 26, also distinguishes five borrowers under one note for purposes of a prosbul, because one note makes them guarantors for one another. It appears that the Beit Yosef intended only that where the circumstances clearly show a joint borrowing, the rule applies whether written or oral. A single note itself ordinarily demonstrates such partnership. The Rama’s gloss should be understood in that limited sense.
Both are guarantors for one another, etc. The Beit Yosef understood Maharik, responsum 120, as saying that this applies only where the borrowers divide the money, but not where they borrowed jointly for a partnership. He disagreed, and the Rama and Rabbi Shlomo Cohen followed him. In my opinion, Maharik also agrees that the partners are mutually responsible. He merely says that Rambam uses the term “responsible” rather than “guarantors” because, where both hands are equally involved in the partnership property, ordinary guaranty terminology is less precise. As a matter of law, however, each remains liable for the other. Perhaps the Beit Yosef’s objection concerns an implication that where partners borrowed for the partnership, each is a principal borrower and the creditor may collect the whole from either even when each has enough assets. The authorities generally state only that they become guarantors. Mabit, part 2, responsum 177, similarly writes that partners borrowing for partnership purposes may be collected from at the creditor’s choice, and cites Maharik as support. Darkei Moshe and Maharshal, however, understood Maharik more simply. The matter should be read carefully.

If one of them paid, etc., he may recover, etc. This ruling requires study. Although Baal HaTerumot and the Rosh state it, they follow Raavad’s view that when one says to another, “Become my guarantor,” it is as though he authorized him to pay. The same basis appears in Baal HaTerumot, gate 44, part 1: because joint borrowers become guarantors for one another, each is treated as having authorized the other to enter the guaranty and may recover his share after payment. The Mechaber, however, presents Rambam and Raavad in siman 130, seif 2, without deciding between them. According to Rambam, even authorization to become a guarantor does not obligate the debtor to reimburse payment unless he also authorized payment. Since this is a legal doubt, the possessor retains the money. The same doubt should apply here. Furthermore, the Rosh and Tur may follow their own view that each joint borrower is liable for the whole debt as though each were a guarantor for payment. In such a case, even Rambam agrees that one compelled to pay may recover. The Rosh expressly reasons that because the creditor could legally collect the entire debt from him, he was compelled to pay and is not merely one who voluntarily paid another’s debt. According to our ruling that they are ordinary guarantors rather than guarantors for payment, the creditor could not collect the whole from one while the other had assets. If one nevertheless paid the whole voluntarily, he resembles one who pays another’s debt without authorization and may lose his money. Rashba’s responsum cited in siman 65 also implies this, and the Sema appears to understand it as a case of mutual guarantors. See also Maharshadam, responsum 177.
He may recover, etc. The Beit Yosef cites Rashba in siman 65: if the borrowers died and the heirs of one found the note among their father’s papers and claimed from the heirs of the other that their father had paid it, they cannot collect on that assertion. The Sema’s gloss appears to distinguish the borrower himself from his heirs, suggesting that the borrower may recover while heirs may not. But Rashba’s responsum rules the same even regarding the borrower himself. The only distinction concerning heirs is that their claim is treated as an oral loan and cannot be collected after the due date unless the claim arose within the original term.
Therefore, two people, etc. The Sema explains that not only where one expressly stated at payment that he was paying for his fellow may he recover his fellow’s share; even where he paid without specification, we presume he intended to recover. My revered teacher wrote that this explanation is strained, for nothing in the Rama’s words indicates an unspecified payment more than in the Mechaber’s words. He suggested that if one debtor paid half the debt, the creditor may demand from the other only one quarter of the original debt, because the payment is attributed proportionally to both debtors rather than solely to the payer’s own share. The insight is elegant, but it is not found in Maharik, which instead clearly discusses payment of the entire debt. The proposed law also requires study, for why may the creditor not say that the first debtor paid his own share? The Rama’s actual intent is that when one debtor paid the entire debt, he is presumed to have paid for his fellow as well. The creditor cannot claim that half was applied to this debt and the other half to a different debt owed by the payer. Maharik states this explicitly. See also siman 58, seif 3, in the Rama and my comments there.
He certainly paid, etc. Although I wrote in seif katan 5 that the Mechaber’s preceding ruling requires study, this present rule may nevertheless be correct. The Rama introduces it with “therefore,” but in truth it does not depend upon the preceding ruling, and Maharik does not make it dependent upon it. Maharik concludes that a responsum of Maharam cited in the Mordechai, chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui, proves that even according to Baal HaIttur and those who regard joint borrowers only as ordinary guarantors, payment of the debt may still be presumed to have been made on behalf of the fellow debtor as well.
