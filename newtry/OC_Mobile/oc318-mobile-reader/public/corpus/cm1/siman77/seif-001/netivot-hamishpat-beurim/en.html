Or they received a deposit together. See Shach, seif katan 1, and note the textual correction there: it should read that a deposit given to two has the same law as a loan made to two. The Shach continues that each must pay his share. In the responsum of Rama cited there, Rama brings an additional proof from the rule that one who deposits does so with the understanding that his wife and children may handle the property. If they are negligent, the original guardian is exempt from payment. This should be no worse than if the item were entrusted to both jointly, since the original guardian is the primary guardian. The Shach does not cite this proof at all. It appears that its refutation was obvious to him. When one deposits on the understanding that the guardian's wife and children may handle the item, the reason for exemption is the same as in the law of one guardian who transfers property to another where the concern of "I trusted you with an oath, not him" does not apply, such as where the owner regularly entrusts property to the second guardian. The first guardian has appointed another reliable person in his place and thereby performed proper safeguarding, just as though he had placed the item in a locked chest. He is therefore discharged from his own duty of care. The fact that the wife or children may become liable for their own negligence does not make the first guardian a guarantor for their payment. They undertook only an obligation to guard properly, not an obligation that he would guarantee their eventual payment. Indeed, according to Abaye, the first guardian is exempt whenever he transfers the item to any competent guardian. Where two people jointly accepted a deposit, by contrast, each also becomes a guarantor for the other's monetary liability. If the second is negligent and becomes liable to pay, the first is also liable by virtue of guaranty. It appears, however, that Rama brought this proof only against the opinion that two people who jointly accept a deposit are each primary obligors for the whole amount. In Rama's case, the second person had funds to pay, but the claimant wished to collect from the first as though he were a primary obligor. A primary obligor is not liable merely because he guaranteed the other's payment, but because he is treated as having accepted the entire obligation himself. Thus, with a loan it is as though he borrowed the whole sum, and with guardianship it is as though he personally accepted the entire safeguarding duty. If so, it should be no worse than if he accepted the item alone and then permissibly delivered it to the second guardian, in which case he is discharged from the safeguarding obligation. Why should a primary obligor be in a worse position than someone who originally received the item himself? According to this, the Shach did not need to refute this proof once we rule that the joint recipient is merely a guarantor for the other's payment, rather than a primary obligor. The same approach explains the Shach's challenge to Rama's first proof. Rama's intent is that if joint recipients were each primary obligors, meaning that each is viewed as having personally accepted the entire safeguarding obligation, then where one transfers the ox to the other he should remain liable for all damage as though he himself had been negligent, because his liability would not rest on guaranty but on his own status as guardian. He would therefore be liable as one who caused damage through negligent safeguarding. The Shach's objection applies only according to our ruling that each is merely a guarantor for the other's liability. Rama's own position is consistent. Nevertheless, Rama's proofs can still be challenged from the case of one who gives his ox to a guardian and the ox causes damage. The guardians became primary obligors only toward the owner who entrusted the ox to them jointly, not toward the injured third party. We rule that if an owner gives his ox to a guardian and it causes damage, the guardian is liable and the owner is exempt even if the guardian has no means to pay, as explained in siman 396, seif 8. Here the discussion concerns a forewarned ox, since the rule is stated where the owner enabled the entire damage. Once the owner is discharged by entrusting the ox to the guardians, the injured party has a claim only against the guardian. The guardians never jointly undertook safeguarding toward the injured party, so they did not become primary obligors toward him. Their liability to him is imposed by the Torah upon whoever has the ox under his control. If one guardian then delivers it to the remaining guardian, the first should be discharged just as the owner was discharged when he originally gave it to a guardian. Nor is there liability because one guardian transferred it to another, since he transferred it to a person whom the owner himself trusted.
