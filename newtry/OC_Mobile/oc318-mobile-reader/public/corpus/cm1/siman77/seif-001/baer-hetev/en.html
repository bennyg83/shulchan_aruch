Deposit. The Rama writes in a responsum that where two custodians jointly accepted responsibility for an item, we do not automatically say that each became guarantor for the other. Although Maggid Mishneh cites Ramban that when one deposits with two people the law resembles one who lends to two borrowers, the Rama suggests that Ramban may have meant only where they expressly stipulated joint responsibility, just as an unpaid custodian may stipulate to be liable like a borrower. The Shach rejects this. He writes that Ramban, Rosh, and Ran indicate that even an ordinary deposit entrusted to two people is treated like a loan made to two people, and Maggid Mishneh likewise appears to say so. Ramban in Baal HaTerumot is also explicit. See the Shach's full discussion.
Orally. Beit Yosef writes this, but the Shach objects that the Yerushalmi from which it was derived appears to deal with a written document. Gidulei Terumah likewise challenges Beit Yosef. The Shach suggests that Beit Yosef meant only where the circumstances show that they borrowed jointly. With a single written document, joint borrowing generally establishes mutual guaranty; with an oral loan, the joint character must be evident from the circumstances. The Rama's gloss should be understood this way, though it is abbreviated.
First. This differs from two guarantors for one borrower, where the creditor may collect from whichever guarantor he wishes. Guarantors are not themselves primary debtors; the borrower owes the whole debt and the guaranty functions as security resembling a pledge, so each guarantor enters the entire obligation. But where two people themselves borrowed, each may be viewed as primary borrower for half and guarantor for the other half. Therefore the creditor should first pursue each as borrower for his own half rather than bypass him and collect that half from the other merely as guarantor. Maggid Mishneh in the name of Rashba and Maharik; Sema and Shach.
Guarantor. See siman 129: beit din advances for a guarantor the possible claim that the borrower already paid; the lender must swear before collecting from the guarantor; and the guarantor is not pursued until it is known that the borrower lacks assets from which to pay. See there for further laws of guaranty. Sema.
And collects. The Shach writes that this ruling requires study. Baal HaTerumot and Responsa Rosh state it, and the Tur appears to follow it, but they may be following the Raavad, who holds that when one tells another, "Become my guarantor," this is equivalent to authorizing him to pay. The Mechaber in siman 130, seif 2 cites both Rambam and Raavad without deciding, creating a doubt of law; the burden of proof therefore lies on the one seeking recovery. According to the Rambam, permission merely to become a guarantor does not obligate reimbursement unless there was also authorization to pay. The same should apply here, so one co-borrower who paid more than his share may not automatically recover from the other. The Shach further says that Rosh and Tur follow their own view that two borrowers are like kablan guarantors, so the creditor could collect the whole from either. In the case of a kablan, even Rambam and Mechaber agree that the paying party has stronger recourse. But according to our ruling that two borrowers are only ordinary guarantors for one another, the paying borrower was not compelled to pay the other's share, so his payment resembles one who voluntarily pays another person's debt without authorization and may lose his money, as in siman 128. Responsa Rashba cited in siman 65 likewise suggests this. See Responsa Maharshdam 176.
His share. Beit Yosef cites Rashba in siman 65 that if both borrowers died and the heirs of one found the debt document in their possession and sued the heirs of the other, claiming that their father had paid the document, they collect nothing merely on that claim. The Sema's wording may imply a distinction between the borrower himself and his heirs, but the Shach notes that the responsum rules the same even regarding the borrower himself. The only distinction for heirs is that their claim for reimbursement has the status of an oral loan and is collectible only if still within its term.
His fellow. The Shach writes that although he questioned the preceding ruling, this rule may still be correct independently. Maharik concludes from Responsa Maharam that even according to those who treat co-borrowers only as guarantors for one another, a payment may be understood as made also on behalf of the other. The Shach's teacher suggested that if one borrower paid half the total debt, the lender could then collect from the second only one quarter, because the first half-payment is deemed to have covered both borrowers proportionately and the lender cannot say, "Your colleague paid only his own half, so you still owe your whole half." The Shach says Maharik does not actually support that reading and the legal result itself requires study. He explains the Rama instead as referring to one borrower who paid the entire debt. In that case it is clear that part of the payment was made for his fellow, and the lender cannot say that half was applied to the payer's share while the other half was applied to some separate debt owed by that same payer. The Taz writes similarly. See siman 58, seif 3.
