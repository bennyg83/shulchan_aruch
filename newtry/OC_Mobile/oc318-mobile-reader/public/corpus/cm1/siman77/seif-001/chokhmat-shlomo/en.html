Seif 1. Note: See Shach, seif katan 2, who writes that with partners it is as though they stipulated that each partner’s acts would remain effective permanently. Beit Shmuel writes similarly in Even HaEzer siman 86 in the name of Baal HaItur. See my fifth-edition work on Yoreh Deah, siman 195, where, with Hashem’s help, I brought a proof from the Yerushalmi, fifth chapter of Gittin, halacha 4. There, in the gloss: or they accepted a deposit jointly. See Shach, who cites the Rama’s view that two people who jointly accepted responsibility to guard an item do not become responsible for one another. At first glance there appears to be a clear proof that they do become guarantors for one another from Bava Kamma 28a. Ravina asks concerning a fifth-payment and double-payment imposed on two different men: for example, one delivered his ox to two people, both claimed it had been stolen, one swore and later admitted, while the other swore and witnesses came, and the question is whether a fifth and double payment attach to one monetary object. If the two guardians are not mutually responsible, each would become a guardian only for his own half. Upon their denials and claims of theft, each would be liable only for his own share, so one would pay double on his half and the other a fifth on his half. It would then not be one monetary liability at all. It is difficult to say that the Gemara speaks only where they expressly accepted mutual responsibility, because it should have said so rather than simply stating that one delivered his ox to two men. It would therefore seem that even without an express stipulation they become mutually responsible, each being liable for the whole, and the Gemara can properly treat it as one monetary object. See also the Rosh, chapter HaMafkid, siman 3, who does not answer by saying that they expressly stipulated not to be mutually responsible. This too appears to be for the same reason, that had the case required an express stipulation it should have been stated. However, on further reflection I see a proof for the Shach from the Mishna in Parah 8:1 regarding three people who guarded water of purification and one became impure. If the obligation of guarding applied to each only with respect to his fractional share, when one became impure a corresponding portion would remain without a qualified guardian. It therefore follows that when two people jointly accept guarding, the entire duty of guarding rests on each of them. Consequently, each can be liable for the whole. In my opinion this is a clear proof to one who understands it. Further there, “both are guarantors for one another,” etc. I saw in Shaarei Mishpat on this siman that he disputes the Rama’s reasoning regarding joint guardians and also the Shach’s ruling. He argues that according to opinions holding that a guarantor is obligated only where the borrower receives benefit, a guarantor who says “throw a maneh into the sea” is not obligated because no benefit was received, and therefore in the case of unpaid guardians no obligation of guarantee should arise. With respect, this is mistaken. He overlooked an explicit Gemara in Bava Metzia concerning an unpaid guardian who stipulates to have the liability of a borrower. The Gemara asks how such an undertaking is effective, and Rabbi Yochanan answers that even without a formal acquisition, because of the benefit that a reputation spreads that he is a trustworthy person, he resolves to obligate himself. Thus an unpaid guardian does receive benefit, and the case is not comparable to “throw a maneh to the dogs,” where there is no benefit at all. This is clear. Moreover, even if his argument were correct with an ordinary guarantor, it would not necessarily apply to two people who jointly accepted guardianship. Each becomes a guarantor for the other, and one can say that since each wishes to acquire the rights of the arrangement, he fully resolves to obligate himself in return. In that setting all would agree that the undertaking can take effect even without a separate benefit. It is preferable to minimize disputes, especially since some authorities hold that even in the case of “throw it to the dogs” the law of guarantee can apply. Compare the law of asmachta where, if one seeks to acquire, he fully resolves to convey. I have been brief. And what the Shach wrote here in seif katan 12, “where he seized it before witnesses.” This is difficult, because the Shach explained that “he did not demand it from him” means that the claimant himself is uncertain. If so, even without witnesses there is no migo. I was already asked this question from the community of Kamenitz. See my work on Orach Chaim, entry 119, page 162b, where I answered it incidentally.
