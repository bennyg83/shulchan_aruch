One should not first sue the guarantor. Rosh and Tur hold that the creditor may collect from whichever co-borrower he chooses, even if the other has assets, because each is regarded as a borrower for the entire amount. Ramban, cited by the Ran in chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, holds that each is a borrower only for his own half and merely a guarantor for the other's half. Ramban proves this from Bava Metzia 96b, where the Gemara asks concerning partners who borrowed an animal and the owner was in the employ of one of them: do we require the owner to be in the employ of the entire borrower, which is lacking, or is that partner at least exempt regarding the half he personally borrowed? If each partner were personally liable as a borrower for the whole animal, why would he not be exempt for the whole when the owner was in his employ? One cannot answer that because there is another borrower with him he is not considered the sole borrower, for the same would then be true even as to his own half. Thus, says Ramban, liability for the other's half must be only as guarantor, so the exemption of borrowing while the owner is employed applies only to his own half. According to Rosh and Tur the Gemara can be explained from Rambam's rule in the first chapter of Hilchot Sechirut. If a bailee transfers the object to another bailee, then even if the first had borrowed or rented it while the owner was in his employ, the first becomes liable if it dies in the second bailee's domain. The Maggid Mishneh explains that the exemption of she'eilah beba'alim applies when the animal dies or is lost through the first borrower's negligence while in his own domain, but not when he handed it to another. The second bailee did not borrow while the owner was in his employ, and the first remains liable for what happens in the second's domain. Shulchan Aruch rules likewise in siman 291. According to Rosh's approach, each partner is a borrower for the whole and, correspondingly, lends the other partner the half that the other uses. Each is thus a borrower of the entire item and a lender of half to his fellow. If the animal dies in one partner's hand, the exemption of borrowing while the owner is employed applies only to the half that remained in his own domain. Regarding the other half, he had in effect transferred it to the other partner, whose use is not protected by the owner's employment. The Gemara's question is therefore precise: perhaps the exemption requires that the owner be employed with respect to the whole borrowed item, and that is lacking because half was transferred to the other partner; or perhaps he is at least exempt for the half he personally borrowed and did not transfer. The language 'that half which he borrowed at least' is exact. He is certainly liable for the other half, because as borrower of the entire animal he remains responsible for what occurs in the second borrower's domain. The doubt concerns only his own half. This reconciles the Gemara with Rosh and Tur.
