A deliberately abandoned loss is ownerless. Tur treats an item knowingly left exposed by its owner as ownerless. Beit Yosef rejects this. Merely failing to guard one's property does not prove an intention to abandon ownership, and he therefore rules like Rambam. Shach defends Tur from the Talmudic use of the expression "a deliberately abandoned loss." In the case of refuse that is not normally cleared away, the Gemara says an item intentionally placed there can be treated as deliberately abandoned, and there the finder acquires it. Likewise, at the beginning of Perek Elu Metziot, scattered fruits in the threshing area are explained by Mar Ukva as a deliberately abandoned loss and therefore belong to the finder. Shach argues that it is forced to give the phrase one meaning in one passage and a different meaning in another. The dispute is therefore whether the Talmudic category itself implies full ownerless status or only a loss of the ordinary mitzvah of return. Tur and Shach read it as ownerless property; Rambam and Beit Yosef do not infer abandonment merely from negligent exposure.
Shach's generalization that every deliberately abandoned loss is ownerless merely because one Talmudic passage uses that phrase for ownerless property is not compelling. The expression cannot have one fixed meaning everywhere, because in Perek HaSefinah the small child sent to a shopkeeper is described as involving a deliberately abandoned loss, yet the father's coin and flask do not become ownerless. The shopkeeper remains liable for the coin he retained, and if he takes the flask for his own use he can become a wrongful taker. Thus there the phrase certainly means only that the owner knowingly exposed his property, not that he relinquished title. If the language is to be understood consistently, Rambam's view is actually stronger: "deliberately abandoned loss" never itself means ownerless. Shach's proof from refuse not ordinarily cleared away is likewise answered by Sema, who says even there Rambam does not treat the property as ownerless merely because it was left unguarded. The passage concerning scattered produce in the threshing floor can also be reconciled with Rambam. Abaye's problem is that unknowing despair does not work because the object came to the finder before the owner despaired. The Gemara answers that the case is one of a deliberately abandoned loss. Such a finder never became obligated in return, for Scripture excludes a loss deliberately exposed by its owner. Tosafot explains that when an object came into the finder's hand while the obligation of return already attached, later despair cannot transfer it because he is already bound to restore it. Here no such obligation arose. Ramban gives an even clearer formulation. A lost object picked up without intent to return it is like an object still lying on the ground and can later be acquired through despair. Only one who lifted it for return becomes the owner's custodian, whose hand is legally like the owner's hand. A deliberately abandoned loss carries no obligation of return and no custodial status. It therefore remains like an object on the ground and can be acquired once the owner later despairs. This is the meaning of the threshing floor passage, without saying the property was ownerless from the outset.
Bach was asked about a person who gave a coin to his young child to play with. The coin reached the hands of a household servant, who used it for kiddushin. Bach ruled that because the owner had not guarded the coin and had given it to a child for play, it was ownerless according to Tur's view that a deliberately abandoned loss is ownerless. He acknowledged that Rambam disagrees and holds that one who deliberately loses property is merely excluded from the mitzvah of return, while another person may not take it for himself. Bach nevertheless sought proof for Tur from the opening of Perek Elu Metziot, where scattered produce at the threshing floor is called a deliberately abandoned loss and belongs to the finder.
In Bach's actual case, however, everyone should agree that the coin was not ownerless. Perek HaSefinah expressly discusses a father who sends a small child to a shopkeeper with money and a flask. Both are treated as deliberately exposed to loss, yet the shopkeeper remains liable for the coin and, if he takes the flask for his own measuring, can be liable for it as well. Tur rules this explicitly. Thus even Tur cannot mean that every item entrusted to a child becomes ownerless. His rule must refer to property intentionally left in a place where it is not guarded, in circumstances showing abandonment. Giving an item to a child, though negligent and a deliberately exposed loss, is not in itself abandonment of ownership. Accordingly, where the father gave the coin to the child merely to play with, the coin remained his property according to all views. The servant therefore had no title with which to effect kiddushin, and there is no reason to be concerned for the kiddushin. There is no meaningful distinction whether the father said expressly that the child should play with it or merely handed it to him.
Hagahot Ashri similarly writes concerning one who saw that his father had hidden money in a field and said it was second tithe money: this is specifically where the father hid it in a guarded place. If he hid it in an unguarded place, it is a deliberately abandoned loss. This language supports Tur's view that leaving property in an unguarded location can constitute abandonment.
