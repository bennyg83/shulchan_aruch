The Bach likewise rules that the Rambam's position is primary. In my view, however, they did not grasp the Tur's reasoning. The Tur does not argue merely from logic that one who fails to supervise his property presumably abandons it. Rather, he argues that throughout the Gemara the term 'intentional loss' means that the property is ownerless. This is proven regarding a refuse heap, where the Gemara says that it is an intentional loss, necessarily meaning that it is ownerless and belongs to the finder, as I wrote in siman 261 seif 11. The same is proven at the beginning of Elu Metziot, where Rabbi Shimon says that scattered fruit belongs to the finder, and the Gemara explains that the case concerns a threshing floor and is an intentional loss. Rashi explains that it did not fall from the owner but was knowingly left there, and therefore it is ownerless. Thus, wherever the Gemara uses 'intentional loss,' it means ownerless property. According to the Rambam one must say that the phrase here does not have the same meaning as it has there, which is exceedingly forced. The Bach's proof from siman 273 seif 3, concerning the formal requirements of abandonment, is also not decisive. Certainly, where a person's intention to abandon the property is evident, the property becomes ownerless, as I wrote in siman 264 seif 5 regarding honey and a fire, and as the Gemara says concerning ordinary dung that people normally abandon it. There are many similar cases. Therefore the Tur's view is primary, and the Hagahot Ashri cited at the beginning of siman 260 likewise implies this. See there, seif katan 2. I later found this explicit in Tosafot, Shabbat 18b and Elu Metziot 30b, who write that regarding a tub, lamp, and pot, although no formal declaration before three people was made, we are witnesses that the owner abandons them in his heart. Shach.
