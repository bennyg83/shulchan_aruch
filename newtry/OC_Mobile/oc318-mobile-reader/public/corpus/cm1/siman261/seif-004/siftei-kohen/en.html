Some say that deliberate loss is ownerless, etc. The Beit Yosef and Bach reject the Tur, arguing that negligence toward one's property does not prove abandonment. They did not grasp the Tur's intent. He does not infer abandonment merely from negligence. Rather, throughout the Gemara the expression 'deliberate loss' means that the object is ownerless. Thus, regarding a refuse heap not intended for removal, the Gemara asks that it is deliberate loss, necessarily meaning that it belongs to the finder. Likewise at the beginning of Elu Metziot, scattered produce is explained as produce deliberately left on the threshing floor, which Rashi calls ownerless. The Rambam must therefore distinguish the expression here from its ordinary usage, which is strained. The Bach's proof from the formal declaration of ownerlessness in siman 273 is no proof, because an act that clearly demonstrates intent to abandon also creates ownerlessness. This appears from the cases of honey and a fire, manure, and many other passages. The Tur's ruling is therefore primary. Hagahot Ashri cited above supports it, and Tosafot in Shabbat 18b and Bava Metzia 30b explicitly write that where circumstances attest that one abandons the item in his heart, no verbal declaration is required.
