Some say that intentional loss is ownerless property. See Shach, seif katan 3, who proves this from many Gemara passages where intentional loss is treated as ownerless. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this from Bava Batra 87 concerning a minor, who is called an intentional loss, yet one who takes an item from the minor without permission can be liable as a borrower without permission, showing the property was not ownerless. In my view "intentional loss" is indeed classified as lost property, but complete acquisition by others still depends on despair. Where one places property in a refuse heap destined to be cleared, there is complete despair because whoever finds it need not announce an intentionally abandoned item. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 24 write that whenever the finder has no duty to announce, the owner generally despairs. Thus the property can become fully ownerless. The same can apply to scattered kernels where the owner knowingly abandoned them like dung. But if one throws a purse with an identifying sign into a public domain, Rambam can hold there is no complete despair. Even if the person who saw the owner throw it need not announce because he knows it was deliberate, another finder who did not see that conduct will see the identifying sign and announce it. And someone who knows the owner can simply return it, since there is no permission to use the property. It is therefore possible that Rambam too agrees that after someone takes such an item, he need not return it because despair then becomes effective. Rambam says "he is not required to return it," not merely that he need not attend to it, which can imply the finder may retain it. This resembles doubtful deliberate placement without an identifying sign, where one should not touch the item, but if he takes it he acquires because once it leaves the former location the owner's despair can operate. A minor is different. There is no ordinary despair from a minor's property. Even rabbinically, taking a minor's property is treated as theft because of social order. Also, property in the hands of an intelligent minor is somewhat guarded, as Tosafot in Bava Kamma write. Since most people entrust objects to an intelligent child, there is an evident presumption that the owner did not despair. The child is called an intentional loss only in the limited sense that one who takes the item need not guard it more carefully than the owner himself did. Likewise, when parents throw utensils upon a bier, the items do not become ownerless because others are commanded to save them under the prohibition against needless destruction. The owner therefore has reason not to despair. So too where one throws a vessel from a roof and another breaks it, there can be liability if people below could save it; the presence of persons obligated to prevent destruction means there is no complete despair.
