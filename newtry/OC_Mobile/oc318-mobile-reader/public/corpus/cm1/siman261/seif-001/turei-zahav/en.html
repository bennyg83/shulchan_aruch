(Seif 4, because it is treated as ownerless.) This is the Tur’s view. At first glance one might bring proof from the beginning of Elu Metziot, where the Gemara establishes the case as scattered grain at the threshing floor and calls it a knowingly abandoned article, yet it belongs to the finder. It appears, however, that the case is different because we are witnesses, as it were, that the owner will not trouble himself to collect one kav spread over four cubits. In my humble opinion Rambam’s ruling is primary. Proof may be brought from Bava Batra 87 and from siman 188 above. One who sends his young son to a shopkeeper with a flask, and gives him an issar with which to purchase oil, and the shopkeeper breaks the flask and loses the issar, is liable according to the Sages for the flask, the issar, and the oil. The Gemara asks that the flask was knowingly exposed to loss. Rashbam explains that although the pundion given to the child was also knowingly exposed to loss, the Sages nevertheless obligate the shopkeeper to return it, because the father sent the child only to notify the shopkeeper to send the goods through an adult. If knowingly exposed property were fully ownerless, the shopkeeper would acquire the coin he received from the child. Why, then, must he return it to the child’s father? We must conclude that it is not fully ownerless. The owner merely did not care about the possible loss, and if it breaks, it breaks. The same applies to a found article. This appears correct to me, although I lack authority to decide conclusively.
