The broker can return it to the first seller. All commentators question Rambam because Bava Metzia 42 appears to say that the broker must return the money to the orphans, while the cattle keeper pays him only the reduced value of the meat. In my view Rambam can be reconciled simply. Shitah Mekubetzet and other poskim ask why the broker had to return anything at all. He could say that he sold the animal for slaughter, like the case of a goring ox. They answer that orphans ordinarily buy cattle for plowing. Rambam does not speak specifically of orphans. Therefore his case must be one in which the animal was expressly sold for plowing. The Gemara's case is different. The orphans bought without specification, and although their usual purpose was plowing, the seller to the broker could still claim that he had sold for slaughter. The broker therefore had no claim against the first seller and could not return the animal to him. Rambam speaks where the first seller would indeed have been obligated to take it back. In that situation the subsequent buyer can likewise be liable for preventing the return. This is why the Gemara says that had the orphans suffered an actual loss, the cattle keeper would have paid the entire value. Only in the actual case, where the orphans ultimately suffered no loss, does the Gemara award merely the reduced meat value as a compromise according to Rashi, or as strict law according to Tosafot. Where the orphans would have suffered loss because the broker could no longer be found, the cattle keeper would pay the whole value on the theory that had he informed them in time they would have returned it immediately to the broker. The subsequent buyer can be liable on the same reasoning. This follows Raavad's approach cited by Shitah Mekubetzet, that a buyer who is holding an item standing to be returned has the law of a paid custodian. The dissent cited by Rama follows Ritva, who treats him only as an unpaid custodian. According to that view, if there was actual negligence he would nevertheless be liable because timely notice would have allowed return to the first seller.
He caused the loss to himself. This implies that even if the animal's intrinsic value is lower, the holder is liable for the larger loss because he could have returned it immediately to the seller, and now the seller is gone. At first glance this is difficult. If the animal itself is worth only a small amount and the greater value lies solely in the right to return it, why should the custodian owe the larger sum? A debt instrument has no intrinsic body-value and a negligent custodian can be exempt from its face value. If Reuven took a cheap sickle-handle as collateral for one thousand zuz under an arrangement where its loss cancels the debt, and then entrusted the handle to a custodian who lost it, would that custodian really owe the entire thousand? The object itself is not worth that amount. Likewise, judges who erred in a matter where the judgment can be reversed are exempt even if later recovery from the litigant becomes impossible, because their act itself did not directly cause the loss. It was only indirect causation. Why then should the cattle keeper or buyer be liable merely because they failed to notify in time and the original seller later disappeared? We must therefore say that liability here rests on reliance. A broker sells immediately and ordinarily cannot inspect everything himself. By accepting the role of checking the animal, the keeper is effectively in the position of one told, "Go examine whether it has sound teeth, because I rely on you." Rashba in this sugya uses precisely this reasoning to exempt the guardian: the principal had effectively said to the cattle keeper, "Go inspect it; I rely on you." This is analogous to one who shows a coin to a moneychanger and relies on his professional opinion. Tur in siman 227 says that if one showed merchandise to a merchant and the merchant said it was sound, even where the money had already been paid, the appraiser can be liable because the owner relied on him. Here too, failure to notify within the proper time is equivalent to an affirmative representation that the animal was sound. If that representation was negligent, liability follows from professional reliance. Even if the buyer himself is only an unpaid custodian and is not liable for theft or loss before discovering the defect, he can still be liable for negligent failure to disclose after inspection because that negligence resembles the moneychanger case. An unpaid professional appraiser is sometimes exempt for an unavoidable mistake, while a paid one is liable. But here the failure to notice or report is negligence, not an unavoidable professional error, and therefore even an unpaid person can be liable. This also resolves Rambam against the Gemara. Where the orphans suffered actual loss, the cattle keeper is indeed liable for this reliance reason. Where they suffered no loss, the animal owner never relied on the keeper at all; he did not even know him. The keeper is therefore liable only for the reduced meat value based on his custody relation to the orphans, not to the original owner. The guardian likewise can be exempt where he reasonably entrusted inspection to the cattle keeper and therefore did not personally undertake the professional reliance.

This rule applies specifically where the hidden property was of a kind that ordinarily could never have been discovered, so that it was effectively lost from everyone and therefore ownerless. But if one found money wrapped in cloth without knowing what was inside, or another hidden thing of a kind that is ordinarily discovered, the finder acquires once the proper acquisition occurs. Mordechai derives the present rule from the case of an object found in an old wall. Rambam, chapter 14 of Hilchot Gezeilah, explains that the reason there is that the property was lost from everyone. Maharik, siman 118, likewise limits the rule to something that is not ordinarily found.
