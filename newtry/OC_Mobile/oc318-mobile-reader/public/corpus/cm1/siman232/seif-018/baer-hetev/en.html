And he did not know. These points depend on one another. Since a dealer does not ordinarily keep the animal for long, we presume he did not know of the defect, and the burden of proof rests on the claimant. But if the seller is the animal's owner, we presume he kept it for some time and knew of its defect, feeding it soft foods that did not require molars and by which it remained alive. Otherwise we would have to say that even while in the owner's possession it could certainly have survived only one day, since it had not died before the sale. Sema.

And is exempt. This is somewhat difficult, because one does not administer an oath on a doubtful claim. Why should we assume that the seller knew the object contained tin? He too may have purchased it believing it was gold. Perhaps the case is where the buyer claims: You did not buy it from another person, but instructed a goldsmith to make it for you with tin inside. Therefore the seller must swear. Thus far the Sema. The Shach writes that in his opinion the case is straightforward and there is no difficulty. Since the buyer says with certainty, 'I know it contained tin,' it is as though he says, 'I am certain you owe me,' and the seller answers, 'I do not know whether I owe you.' He therefore swears that he does not know, as stated in siman 75, seif 10.
