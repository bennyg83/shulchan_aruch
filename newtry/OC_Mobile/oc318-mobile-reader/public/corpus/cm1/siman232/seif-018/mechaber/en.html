If an object has a hidden defect and the object is lost because of that defect, the seller must return the purchase price. For example, one sells an ox lacking molars. The purchaser places it with his cattle and sets food before them all, not realizing that this ox cannot eat, until it dies of hunger. The purchaser returns the carcass and receives his money. The same applies in similar cases. If the seller was a broker who buys an animal and resells it the same day without keeping it, and he did not know of the defect, he takes a heset oath that he lacked knowledge and is exempt. The purchaser should have examined the ox separately and returned it before it died, enabling the broker to pursue the prior seller; by failing to do so, the purchaser caused his own loss. Rama: Some disagree and require even the broker to pay, because although he himself was deceived, he may not deceive others. This is the primary ruling. Certainly, where the purchaser was not negligent, such as when a ring sold as gold was broken and found to contain tin, the seller must refund the money even if he too was deceived. If he disputes the finding, he swears that he did not know and is exempt from any additional claim. If a broker bought an article believing it to be tin and sold it, and it was later discovered to contain silver or gold, the purchaser acquires the precious material, because the broker never acquired what he did not know existed.
