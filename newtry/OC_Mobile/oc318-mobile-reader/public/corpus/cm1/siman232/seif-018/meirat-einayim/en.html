If the seller was a broker who purchases, etc. In the Gemara such a person is called a safsara, as in chapter HaMafkid concerning this incident. An ordinary broker does not purchase the merchandise at all; he merely brings seller and buyer together and arranges the price. The Tur writes this explicitly in Yoreh Deah siman 151. Rambam, who uses the term "broker," immediately explains it as "a broker who purchases from this person," thereby teaching that the case is not an ordinary broker. The Tur and Mechaber followed Rambam's wording. See the Perishah.
And he does not retain it, etc., and he did not know, etc. These points depend on one another. Since the broker does not ordinarily retain the animal, we presume he did not know of the defect, and the burden of proof rests on the claimant. If the seller is an ordinary owner, by contrast, we presume he kept the animal and knew of the defect, and sustained it by feeding it soft foods that did not require molars. Otherwise, if we did not say this, we would have to assume that even in the owner's possession the animal could survive only one day, since it remained alive until the sale and did not die while still with him.
And some disagree, etc. Their dispute appears to depend upon differing interpretations of the Gemara, which I set out in the Derishah. I also gave a sound rationale for Rambam's view, which is the Mechaber's ruling. See there.
All the more so with something where he was not negligent, etc. Meaning, if with an ox the purchaser is somewhat negligent because he should have inspected its eating and health and promptly returned it to the broker, allowing the broker in turn to return it to the person from whom he bought it, and nevertheless we say the broker bears the loss, then all the more so where one buys a ring represented as gold. A purchaser does not ordinarily open and inspect the hidden interior of a ring. The loss therefore falls upon the seller even if he is a broker.
He swears that he did not know of it and is exempt. Mordechai writes this. It is somewhat difficult, because one does not impose an oath on the basis of an uncertain claim. Why assume the seller knew there was tin inside? He too purchased it believing it was gold and may not have known. Perhaps the case is one where the purchaser who found tin accuses him specifically: "You did not buy this from someone else; rather, you instructed a goldsmith to make it for you with tin inside." He must therefore swear.
