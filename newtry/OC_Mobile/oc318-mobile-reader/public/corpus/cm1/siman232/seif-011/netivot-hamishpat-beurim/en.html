One who sells an animal to his fellow for slaughter, and after slaughter it is found to be a tereifah. The Tur does not specify that it was sold for slaughter, implying that even an unspecified sale is a mistaken transaction if the animal was already a tereifah. A tereifah is not expected to live and is not considered sound, so even if it was bought for plowing the defect is material. It appears that the Mechaber specifies "for slaughter" because of seif 12, where he rules that even a tereifah based on a sircha can cancel the sale. A sircha creates only doubtful tereifah status, as Sema, seif katan 28, explains. See Shach, siman 306, seif katan 9, who explains why the seller cannot say, "bring proof that it was actually a tereifah": since people avoid an animal because of the doubt, there is no greater commercial defect. That reasoning applies specifically where the animal was bought for food. Even if it is only doubtfully tereifah, it is prohibited to eat and therefore defective for the intended purpose. If it was bought for plowing, however, the reason an actual tereifah is defective is that it will not live and is not certainly healthy. With a doubtful tereifah, the seller can say, "bring proof that it truly is a tereifah and will not live." The Mechaber therefore had to specify in the opening clause that it was bought for slaughter, because his later law of sircha applies only in that setting. The Tur, who rules that sircha does not cancel the sale, did not need that qualification. His opening law applies equally whether the animal was bought for slaughter or plowing, while in his later sircha case the sale is not cancelled even for slaughter because the buyer should have stipulated. He therefore states the rule generally.
Within three days it is a mistaken transaction. Ketzot HaChoshen cites the Rivash concerning one who sold the innards of an animal before its lung was inspected, stipulating that the sale would stand whether the animal proved kosher or tereifah. It was later discovered that the animal had actually become a neveilah through improper slaughter. Rivash writes that the result depends on ordinary language: if people call a neveilah "tereifah," the condition covers it; if some do not, the possessor prevails because monetary law does not follow the majority. Ketzot asks that there should be no practical difference between neveilah and tereifah, since both are forbidden to eat, and therefore no mistaken transaction. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. If the seller had sold the meat positively "as tereifah," and it turned out to be neveilah, perhaps there would be no mistaken transaction because both are equally unusable for food. Here, however, the seller did not sell it as tereifah. He merely made a condition that the buyer would bear the risk "if it turns out to be tereifah." This is like stipulating that the buyer will not return the item for a specified defect. Any defect not included in the condition remains grounds for return even if it is economically similar to the specified defect. If a seller stipulated that the buyer could not return an animal for a tereifah in the lung, and it was found to have a tereifah of the spinal cord, the buyer could return it. He accepted one particular uncertainty, not every distinct uncertainty. Therefore, if ordinary language does not include neveilah within the word tereifah, the case is exactly like stipulating away liability for a lung tereifah and then discovering a spinal tereifah.
