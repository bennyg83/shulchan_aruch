He slaughtered it and it was found to be a tereifah. Rivash discusses a butcher who slaughtered an animal, failed to inspect the signs properly, and later the owner sold the intestines for seven coins on the condition that the buyer would pay whether the animal was kosher or tereifah. It was then discovered that the slaughter itself had been invalid, so technically the animal was a neveilah rather than a tereifah. Rivash ruled for the seller. In commercial language people do not carefully distinguish between an animal invalidated by defective slaughter and one properly slaughtered but later found halachically defective. Both are commonly called "tereifah." Since the buyer expressly agreed to take the intestines whether kosher or tereifah, the condition includes this result. Rivash adds that even if the majority of people would distinguish the terms and only a minority would call this a tereifah, the seller who already holds the money can retain it because we do not follow a majority in monetary law to extract from a possessor. The buyer cannot say that because he saw a slaughtered animal he assumed the slaughter had been proper. Once the seller expressly said, "whether kosher or tereifah," the buyer should have specified that an invalid slaughter was excluded. His unexpressed assumption is only a matter in the heart. This implies that if the purchaser still holds the money, and a minority of people distinguish and call the animal specifically a neveilah, he need not pay. Since the condition mentioned only kosher or tereifah, the seller would then be the one seeking to extract. One may ask why there should be any practical difference between tereifah and neveilah, since both are forbidden. The answer is that Rashba rules that certain intestines from a properly slaughtered tereifah can be sold to a gentile, while intestines from an animal rendered neveilah through invalid slaughter may remain subject to the prohibition of a limb from a living animal for the gentile. Thus the buyer of intestines has a genuine commercial reason to distinguish between the two categories. Accordingly, where intestines are being sold and the language is ambiguous, the distinction matters. But if ordinary meat were sold on condition "whether kosher or tereifah," and the animal was later found to have become neveilah through slaughter, then even if most people use a different word there may be no commercial defect at all because both categories are equally unusable for the Jewish buyer. In that setting the buyer would still owe the price. Tosafot in Meilah further notes that if intestines are not ordinarily eaten by people in the locale, their poor eating quality does not constitute a defect at all. The practical law therefore depends on local custom. Today, where people prepare and eat them and distinguish between kosher and forbidden intestines, the distinction can constitute a defect unless the buyer expressly accepted both kosher and tereifah.
