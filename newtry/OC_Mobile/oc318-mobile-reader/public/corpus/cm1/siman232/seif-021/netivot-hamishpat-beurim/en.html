Even if it was lost or stolen. See Sema, seif katan 51, who writes that the buyer is liable only if he had time to notify the seller and failed to do so; if there was no time to notify him, the buyer is exempt. This is very difficult. If the buyer has the law of a paid custodian, then even without time to notify he should be liable for theft and loss. If he is not a paid custodian, why should failure to notify make him liable where he otherwise guarded properly and the object was stolen? Where the item deteriorated because of a defect and the seller could have eaten or sold it had he been informed, failure to notify is understandable as negligence. It resembles an unpaid custodian who could have saved the object by hiring shepherds or using sticks and failed to do so. But if the object was properly guarded and nevertheless stolen, why should the buyer be liable merely because earlier notice might have led to return? A finder who is an unpaid custodian according to one opinion does not become liable for theft merely because the owner could have taken the object back had he been notified earlier. Siman 72, seif 43, likewise rules concerning a pledge: after the loan is repaid, the borrower asks for his pledge, the lender says, "Come tomorrow," and the pledge is stolen. According to the opinion that the lender is then only an unpaid custodian, he is exempt from theft. Yet one could equally say, "Had you returned it immediately, it would not have been stolen." Bach, cited by Shach, seif katan 17, holds that the buyer is liable even without time to notify because he is a paid custodian. Bach gives two reasons. First, he compares the buyer to a purchaser who paid money for an object and then retracted, where the seller is a paid custodian over the money. I do not understand the comparison. There, the seller was expressly given permission to use the money. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 43 say he acquired the money entirely. Until he tells the buyer, "Take your money," there is no completed repayment. Here there is no comparable use-permission. Second, Bach compares the buyer to one who takes an item from a craftsman's house for inspection and is a paid custodian on the return. That too is difficult. There he was initially a borrower and liable even for ones during the outward period; afterward he becomes a paid custodian because, having benefited, he must provide benefit. Here the buyer was never under such liability. If the object was stolen or suffered ones before the defect was discovered, and later it becomes known that the defect had existed, the buyer is exempt. Why should he suddenly become a paid custodian afterward? Ritva nevertheless writes in HaMafkid that where the buyer already paid the money, he is a paid custodian because he holds the object against the money he paid. Even that requires explanation. Tosafot write that one who takes collateral at the time of the loan is not a paid custodian merely because he holds it against the money; the lender receives no new benefit from having advanced the funds. In my view the stronger reason is different. Once the defect becomes known, if the buyer wishes he can nevertheless affirm the sale. Even without informing the seller, once he resolves to keep the object the seller cannot later retract. Therefore, if the buyer wants to use the object to betroth a woman, acquire slaves or land, or if he finds an opportunity to resell it at a profit, he can elect to keep the sale and use the object. That continuing option is a benefit to him. Because of that benefit he has the law of a paid custodian from the time the defect becomes known, whether or not he already paid the price. This is, in my view, the primary rule. Shach also cites the opinion that the buyer is a paid custodian and appears to treat it as significant.
And if he did not notify him. It appears that if it is known that this type of merchandise is ordinarily transported elsewhere, or the buyer is known to live elsewhere and would plainly take it home, transporting it there is itself like notice. It is no worse than seif 13, where if the buyer caused another defect through an act ordinary for that item, he is exempt even though the additional damage may be substantial, and the ruling implies this even without prior notice. Therefore the present rule concerns merchandise not ordinarily transported elsewhere, where the seller had no reason to anticipate that the buyer would move it. The buyer is then liable for the expense of returning it for the reason I wrote there: since he could have inspected and discovered the defect before transporting it, his act resembles an accident comparable to loss, for which a damager can be liable. From this it appears that if Reuven seizes Shimon's object and carries it to another place, Reuven must return it at his own expense. Here the buyer acted inadvertently and nevertheless must bear the return expense; all the more so where one acted intentionally. This is also no worse than the rule, "Go, and I will come after you," where the person can be liable for the other's expenses, as explained above in siman 14, seif 5, in the gloss. Only one who intended to steal the object for himself and carried it elsewhere can be exempt from having to bring it after the owner, absent an oath, because of the special enactment encouraging repentance, as Sema explains in siman 367. That enactment was made for a robber, not an ordinary damager. Similarly, if a borrower originally lived in the same place as the lender, borrowed money there, and then moved to another city, he must repay the loan in the place where it was made, bearing the expense caused by his move. If the lender moved, the lender caused his own inconvenience and the borrower need not follow him. Maharik, siman 17, writes explicitly that where the lender sent outside his established residence, the borrower can be compelled to go to the lender's home to repay, under the rule that a borrower is servant to the lender. This supports the foregoing.
