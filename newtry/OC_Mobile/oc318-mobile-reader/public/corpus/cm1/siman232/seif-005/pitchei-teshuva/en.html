See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 211, concerning one who hired a maidservant and afterward learned that before entering his house she had engaged in promiscuous relations. Her brother argued that since she was not pregnant, what concern could there be, and in a comparable sale of a slave the buyer cannot rescind because there is no ordinary defect claim regarding slaves, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 232, seif 10. Chavot Yair rejects this. Once she has acquired familiarity with prohibited behavior, she requires supervision lest she go out at night and lest dissolute men be drawn to the house. There are multiple future concerns. Even where one purchases a maidservant and hidden physical defects that do not interfere with work do not permit rescission, that concerns defects that exist against her will and through circumstances beyond her control, from which no future damage is expected. This case is different because her disposition and inclination are themselves problematic. Chavot Yair suggests that if she had been forced, the employer would have to retain her; perhaps even if she had consented only once by happenstance and it were known that before and afterward she was consistently modest, the employer would be obligated to keep her, provided he exercises appropriate supervision and she does not act frivolously or leave the house without permission. Chazal said something similar regarding a slaughterer who once caused a mishap. See Yoreh Deah, siman 2, seif 2 in the gloss and Shach there. Responsa Shav Yaakov, part 1, siman 174, however, writes that Chavot Yair was not precise in implying that promiscuity is a defect even in a Canaanite maidservant, since the Gemara and poskim state that ordinary defect claims do not apply to slaves at all, and several Mishnayot presume a maidservant may be promiscuous. Chavot Yair's further implication that this rule extends even to a Jewish servant is likewise mistaken. It applies specifically to Canaanite servants, whereas a Jewish male or female servant retains the general presumption of Jewish moral fitness. For this reason Shav Yaakov rules in a case where one hired a Jewish maidservant and later learned that she was established as a thief, that the employer may certainly retract, even if she could find other employment. For a Jew, clear proof sufficient to remove the ordinary presumption of fitness establishes a genuine defect. See there.
