A seller told the buyer, "I am selling you dirt and ashes," and afterward part of the merchandise was found substantially inferior to the portion that had been shown to the buyer, producing an overcharge of one sixth. I have heard that merchants transporting the product called "totin" used to employ such language, though I do not know how their tribunals ruled upon it. A question of this kind has now been presented to me. The questioner suggested that it may resemble siman 227 seif 22, but perhaps the opposite is true. The distinction suggested is that saying of something worth one hundred that it is worth one zuz is obviously false and therefore meaningless, whereas "I sell you dirt and ashes" may be ordinary mercantile exaggeration intended to alert the buyer that the goods contain inferiority. Yet if that were sufficient, the same should be true where one says that an item worth one hundred is worth one zuz, since merchants also exaggerate. Rather, when the plain meaning is known to be false, the statement has no legal force. It may even be understood as praise: just as you have no doubt that the item is not literally worth one zuz, so you need not suspect ordinary overcharge. Since merchants customarily use emphatic praise, such language cannot itself establish that the buyer accepted a defect in the goods. A separate issue arises because the buyer transported the merchandise to his own city. Appraisal should be made according to the value at the place and day of purchase, but the value there is now uncertain, and it may also be difficult to prove that the merchandise presently appraised is the same merchandise. Returning it there entails expense. I wrote elsewhere, based on siman 232 seif 21, that if the seller knew the buyer was transporting the item elsewhere and the sale is later voided for a defect, the seller bears even the original transportation expense. Where appraisal establishes only a recoverable overcharge and not rescission, however, it requires study whether those expenses combine with the overcharge to reach the threshold of voiding the sale. Perhaps if the sale remains valid the seller bears no transportation costs at all, since the express discussion of expenses concerns defects, and even there the authorities dispute the rule, though we follow the Rambam. One might distinguish an overcharge of one sixth, where error is common, from a defect which the seller may be presumed to have known. Still, where the seller is not merely a broker, it is difficult to find a basis to distinguish a defect that voids the sale from an overcharge that voids it. Where the sale stands and only the overcharge is returned, it is less clear that expenses fall upon the seller, although it leans somewhat toward counting them when determining whether the total loss reaches the amount that voids the sale. This requires further study. Even where a broker did not know of the defect and ordinarily is not liable because the buyer knew he was only a broker and should have independently examined the item, that reasoning need not exempt him from transportation costs if he knew the buyer was immediately taking the goods elsewhere and had no opportunity to examine them there. If the buyer is not blameworthy, the broker's innocent error does not shift the resulting loss to the buyer, for if the defect prevented title from effectively passing, the transportation loss caused by that defect remains attributable to the sale. There is room to compare the seller's responsibility for such expenses to the liability of a guarantor. A guarantor is liable because the lender acted in reliance upon him, even where both parties knew the borrower equally well. Likewise, the buyer incurred the transportation expense because of the seller's representation. Yet one can distinguish a formal guarantor, who expressly accepts liability, from a broker or seller who did not undertake transportation expenses. Some authorities nevertheless hold that one who says "so and so is reliable" can become liable as a guarantor if the loan was made on that representation, even if he spoke mistakenly. But if the lender had reason to know that the statement might be mistaken, perhaps all would agree that the speaker is exempt. This requires further study.
