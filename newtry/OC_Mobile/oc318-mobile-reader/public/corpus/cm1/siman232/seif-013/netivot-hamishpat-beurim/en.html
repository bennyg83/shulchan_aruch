And he pays for the defect he caused. Beit Yosef derives this from the case of a butcher. At first glance the comparison is difficult. There, the animal was bought for slaughter, neither party knew whether it was treifah, and the defect could not be determined without slaughter. When the buyer damaged it in a way that was not part of the normal permitted use and the act would constitute damage even to a treifah animal, liability is understandable. But where the seller knew of the defect and misled the buyer, why should the buyer be liable for damaging what he reasonably believed was his? This resembles heirs who inherited a borrowed cow from their father, slaughtered and ate it thinking it belonged to them, and pay only the reduced value of the meat based on benefit. If they had merely damaged the cow without benefit, they might be exempt because their act was accidental and they reasonably believed it was theirs. Perhaps the answer is that tort law distinguishes levels of accident. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 27 distinguish between an accident comparable to theft, which can exempt, and an accident comparable to loss, which can still impose liability. Here the buyer could have inspected for the defect before using the item. His failure to inspect makes the accident comparable to loss, for which a damager is liable. This also explains seif 15 concerning a courtyard. If the buyer occupies it, he must pay rent. That appears so even where he is not ordinarily a renter, so long as the courtyard is ordinarily rented. Although the seller could have prevented the loss by disclosing the defect and renting the courtyard elsewhere, the buyer's occupation without inspecting is treated as sufficiently negligent to make him liable for the benefit or damage caused. The matter nevertheless requires further study.
