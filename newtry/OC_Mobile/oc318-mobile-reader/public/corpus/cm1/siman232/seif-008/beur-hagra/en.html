One who sells, etc. This is the first clause there. 'If it had one evident defect and he showed it to him,' etc., is the latter clause there. 'But if it has defects that are evident,' etc., is what is stated there when Rav Acha said to him, etc.: 'This is what we say,' etc. Kesef Mishneh explains it this way, and so writes the Derishah.
(Likkut) One who sells, etc., and seif 9, 'If it had,' etc. All this is the Tur's wording. Rashi explains that in the first clause, where the seller says, 'This cow is,' etc., because he piled defects together but not all of them were actually present, it is a mistaken sale if it has one undisclosed defect. In the latter clause, 'this defect,' etc., even if there was no other defect, once he mentioned the defect by itself the buyer should have examined it. Rav Acha asked, etc. The case must be where the cow had, etc., but the buyer cared only about one of the defects; otherwise what was the question? The buyer could simply say, 'Just as this one is absent, the others are absent too,' while the Baraisa explicitly says, 'and it had one defect.' Tosafos, however, s.v. 'if it had,' explain that the latter clause, 'this defect,' etc., concerns a case where it actually has the other defect as well. The seller mentions only two defects: this defect, which matters to the buyer, and another defect that it has, about which he does not care. The question is therefore whether this rule applies specifically to two defects, while if it had all the defects it would still be a mistaken sale, or whether even if it had all the defects the sale is valid and the expression 'this defect and another defect' is merely an example. Tosafos write, 'the fact that it teaches this defect and another defect is the same,' etc. Rashi deletes the word 'even' from the Gemara, and Rambam's text likewise omits it, because according to his explanation the clause continues the first case and 'even' would be inappropriate. According to Tosafos, however, 'even' fits well. Tosafos then write that the Tosefta implies that even with only two defects, if the other one is absent the sale is nevertheless valid. This also explains why it says here 'and another defect.' Under both explanations of Tosafos, 'this defect and another defect' does not mean, as Rashi says, that the seller mentioned one defect by itself and then others, but that he mentioned only two defects. Their statement that there is no mistaken sale 'because he mentions only another' means because he mentions only two. The Rif and Rosh explain that in the first clause none of the listed defects was present, but the animal had some other hidden defect, according to the version 'and it had another defect.' There is no practical difference if the text reads 'one defect.' They also hold that the listed defects are not visible. In the latter clause the seller mentioned one visible defect and one hidden defect, both of which were actually present, and also listed many additional hidden defects that were not present. Had he not mentioned the visible defect, it would have been a mistaken sale, as in the first clause; but because he showed a visible defect, the buyer should have investigated the second defect as well. Rambam explains differently and reads in the first clause 'and he said that it had another defect,' meaning that one of the hidden defects was actually present, but he also mentioned other visible defects that were not present. If all were visible or all were hidden, there would be no mistaken sale. Rambam writes in chapter 15 of Hilchos Mechirah: One who sells a cow to another and lists visible defects and hidden defects, and none of the visible defects he listed are present, but one of the hidden defects is found, this is a mistaken sale and may be reversed, even though he expressly mentioned the defect found. The buyer may say: when I saw that the visible defects you listed were absent, I assumed that the hidden ones were likewise absent, and you listed them merely to improve my opinion of the animal. For example, he said, 'This cow is blind, lame, biting, and prone to crouch,' and it was found only to crouch or only to bite; this is a mistaken sale. If it had all the defects he listed, it is not a mistaken sale. This is the question above. 'All the defects' is not literal; it suffices that one visible and one hidden defect be present. Once some visible defect is present, there is no mistaken sale concerning the hidden one. Rambam's phrase 'all the defects' means all the classes of defects mentioned in the Baraisa. He continues: 'Likewise, if it was lame and was found to bite, even though it was not blind, this is not a mistaken sale.' Then he writes: 'If he showed him the defect it had and said, This defect is present, and this other defect and that defect,' even though the additional defects he listed were visible and all or some were present, it is not a mistaken sale. This is the latter clause of the Baraisa. According to Rambam, where all the defects are visible or all are hidden, there is already no mistaken sale. The Baraisa therefore teaches a separate novelty: even when all are visible and he showed the buyer only one, the buyer cannot say, 'Since he showed me only this one, the others must be absent.' The Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 8 follow Rambam, but seif 9, which they present as an explanation of the latter clause, is impossible for me to reconcile with his approach. Their expression 'even if it had,' etc., follows the reading of Tosafos and the Rosh, which includes 'even' and explains it as referring to the latter clause. I cannot reconcile all this. Bach and the Derishah and Prishah offer exceedingly strained explanations of the Rif, Rambam, and Tur. I have grown weary of recording all the forced explanations and the objections to them.
