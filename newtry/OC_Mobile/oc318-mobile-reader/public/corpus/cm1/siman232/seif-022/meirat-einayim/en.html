Meaning, where notifying the seller would have provided some practical benefit, such as enabling him to consume the food quickly or take other measures to prevent the loss. Only then do we say that the item remains at the purchaser's risk because he failed to notify the seller that he was rescinding. In the case of theft or loss discussed earlier, so long as he has not notified the seller it ordinarily remains at the purchaser's risk, because notice generally would allow the seller to protect the item. This is why the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber did not combine theft and deterioration into one clause. It also resolves the questions of the Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh. The Ir Shushan's formulation is surprising. He implies that if the buyer had time to notify the seller and failed to do so, deterioration is always at the buyer's risk, and similarly that theft and loss are always at the buyer's risk even before he had time to notify. That is not correct. In theft or loss, once he notified the seller, risk passes to the seller. In spontaneous deterioration, even if time existed to notify, the loss ordinarily remains the seller's unless it is evident that notice would have enabled the seller to prevent it.
