If he used it. See Sema, seif katan 10, who says that if the defect was one the buyer could have discovered, his use afterward can show waiver. The reason appears to be analogous to the rule that one must show ona'ah to an expert within the ordinary time. Mishneh LaMelech asks that even if one actually saw a defect and remained silent, that silence alone does not constitute waiver until he uses the item. All the more so, mere ability to discover the defect should not be enough. A further difficulty arises from Tosafot in Bava Batra 96, who say that with wine one cannot assume waiver merely because the buyer could have tasted it immediately. Tur in siman 230 and Sema there rule similarly. Thus even where immediate inspection was possible, failure to inspect does not by itself show waiver. In my view Sema means that, just as actual knowledge of the defect together with continued use shows waiver, so too where the defect was readily discoverable and the buyer continued to use the item in a manner consistent with acceptance, circumstances can prove waiver. Mere silence is not enough. Mishneh LaMelech distinguishes whether the purchase money had already been paid, suggesting that if it had not, the buyer's silence may reflect reliance on withholding payment. This is difficult. In ona'ah too, if the injured party has not yet paid, why should silence after the inspection period necessarily waive the claim? Shach in siman 227, seif katan 4, implies that without a migo we do not make such a distinction. It therefore appears that if the buyer could not practically return the item because the seller was absent, and the item is one that normally works and produces benefit, the buyer may continue using it in the ordinary way until return becomes possible, even for many days. Once the sale is void, he resembles a finder or custodian holding an item that works and consumes, who may use it to preserve value. In ona'ah of exactly one sixth, where the sale itself remains valid and only restitution is due, continued use is certainly no proof of waiver, because the object is already his. Only where he could readily return it and chose not to do so can continued use evidence waiver. With ona'ah greater than one sixth, where the sale is voidable, the rule more closely resembles a defect. Therefore even if return was temporarily impossible, substantial use after the normal inspection period can indicate waiver unless the use was reasonably necessary to preserve the item.
