But if he used it. The Sema, citing Maggid Mishneh, writes that the same rule applies where the defect was of a kind the purchaser could have detected immediately. Be'er HaGolah also cites this. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Mechirah chapter 15, writes that Rambam clearly disagrees with the rule brought by Maggid Mishneh. Rambam says that the purchaser may return the item provided that he did not use it after seeing the defect. This implies that merely seeing the defect and remaining silent does not constitute waiver, for otherwise Rambam would not need to add the case of use. If actual awareness followed by silence does not waive the claim, certainly the mere ability to inspect, without inspection, does not. Maharit Tzahalon, responsum 225, and Raanach likewise maintain that according to Rif, Rosh, and Rambam, since a defect may always be asserted and the purchaser is not barred merely because enough time passed to show it to a merchant, the purchaser does not waive merely because he could have discovered it. Shevut Yaakov, III, siman 169, argues that there need be no dispute. If before completion of the sale the purchaser could immediately inspect the item and failed to do so, that indicates acceptance of the risk of that defect. But if the sale was already completed and only afterward the defect became apparent, silence for some time does not bar rescission. The purchaser may simply have had no present need for the item and knew that he could rescind when the issue became relevant; so long as he did not use it after discovery, there is no waiver. Terumat HaKeri independently writes the same. Netivot HaMishpat explains the Sema differently, limiting the rule to where the purchaser both could have discovered the defect and thereafter used the item. Just as one who sees a defect and then uses the item waives it, one who could have discovered it and uses it is also deemed to have waived. This distinction is certainly reasonable, although it is difficult to read it into Maggid Mishneh, the Sema's source. The explanation of Shevut Yaakov is therefore preferable. Mishneh LaMelech further writes that even according to Maggid Mishneh, the rule applies only where the purchaser already paid the price. If he bought on credit and still holds the purchase money, his failure to inspect immediately may have resulted from his confidence that he still held the money, and he may say that he never waived. Netivot questions this from the Shach, siman 227, but several authorities agree with Mishneh LaMelech that even without a migo he may say that he did not waive. Gilya Masechta adds that if the purchaser already knew of the defect and nevertheless used the item in a manner that constituted actual misappropriation, retaining the purchase money does not help him. Netivot further writes that if the purchaser was unable to return the item because the seller was absent, and the item is one that normally produces benefit while being maintained, mere continued possession does not show waiver. Even after rescission the purchaser resembles a guardian of a lost object, who may use a productive item in the manner necessary for its upkeep. Teshuvot Maimoniyot likewise indicates that with a horse, diminution caused by riding after discovery of the defect must be paid for, but the use itself need not prove waiver where immediate return was impossible. Regarding onaah, if the overcharge is one-sixth, the sale remains valid and the excess is refunded, so use does not prove waiver unless the buyer could have demanded the refund and failed to do so. If the overcharge exceeds one-sixth, the law resembles a defect, and use after the period allowed for showing the item ordinarily indicates waiver, unless circumstances prevented return. Ritva explicitly states that if the buyer notified the seller of the onaah and then used the item, the use does not waive his claim; if he later rescinds, he must pay for the use or depreciation. Machaneh Ephraim applies the same principle to defects: once the buyer notified the seller of the defect, subsequent necessary use does not necessarily constitute waiver. Gilya Masechta disputes this extension from onaah to defects. In onaah, the buyer ordinarily wants the item itself and objects only to the excess price, so notice to the seller explains why continued use is not waiver. With a defect, however, the buyer's claim is that he does not want the very object purchased, and once he continues to use it, the use normally demonstrates acceptance of the defect despite prior notice. Machaneh Ephraim, however, equates the two. In the case before Gilya Masechta, the purchaser had not yet paid the price, so in any event the stronger position is that of the purchaser who seeks rescission; where the seller already holds the money, the issue requires further consideration. Orchot Mishpat raises two additional questions. First, where the purchaser is an unlearned person who does not know that using the item after discovering a defect legally constitutes waiver, and he protests continuously that he wants to rescind, does his ignorant use nevertheless bind him? It appears that the law does not ordinarily vary with each person's knowledge, much as other rabbinic measures are applied uniformly. Second, if the buyer discovers the defect while traveling, for example in a wagon or similar property that he must use in order to return home, such use is compelled by circumstance and should not be deemed waiver. The example of a horse is generally less apt because a horse is ordinarily a productive item that may be used for its maintenance. In principle, however, use caused by genuine compulsion does not demonstrate waiver, just as unavoidable delay does not bar a claim of onaah.
