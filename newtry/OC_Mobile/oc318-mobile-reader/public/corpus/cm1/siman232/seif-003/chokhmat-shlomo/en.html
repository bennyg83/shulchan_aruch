Seif 23. Note: We have a general rule that in monetary matters we do not follow the majority. Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Kamma, chapter HaManiach, ask why with judges we do follow the majority. See what I wrote in my commentary on tractate Avodah Zarah, page 40, in the addenda, where I responded to their words and explained their intent, including what is considered a majority present before us and what is considered a majority not present before us. I also explained the words of Tosafot in Yevamot 67b and 119a, s.v. "such as," and wrote the entire analysis there. See there. As to whether this rule is d'oraisa or d'rabbanan, the Rosh Yosef on Chullin writes that it is only d'rabbanan, but in my opinion this is not so. It is evident from Tosafot that it is d'oraisa, from their question at the beginning of HaManiach and likewise in Sanhedrin, why we follow the majority of judges when in monetary matters we do not follow the majority. Against whom is their question directed? If they are asking about Torah law, then according to that premise monetary matters should also follow the majority. If they are asking only about rabbinic law, what is their question? One could answer that in order not to close the door before borrowers, the Rabbis left this matter on Torah law. Indeed, according to the view that Torah law requires three expert judges, the Rabbis nevertheless permitted three laymen because of the concern that the door not be closed before borrowers. If they were lenient against the Torah's formal requirement for that reason, all the more so would it be improper to create a stringency against Torah law in the opposite direction. Thus Tosafot's question, how the Torah itself could say "incline after the majority," proves that the monetary rule is d'oraisa. There is another proof from Tosafot in Bava Metzia, beginning "what, did prohibition precede money?" They ask that on the contrary, monetary law is more stringent because in monetary matters we do not follow the majority. What is their question? According to their second answer there, the concern for two people with identical names is only d'rabbanan, which implies that in the initial approach they assumed this concern was d'oraisa. If so, the Gemara's question was sound: what proof can be brought from monetary law to a Torah-level concern, if monetary law is more lenient on the Torah level and follows the majority? Necessarily, Tosafot hold that not following the majority in monetary matters is itself d'oraisa. We thus see that monetary law is treated more stringently. This is a clear proof that the rule is d'oraisa. Examine this carefully. I have heard people ask: If so, why did the Torah obligate the rapist and seducer to pay the fine to the girl's father? The Gemara's question remains, perhaps he is not really her father. If the answer is that we follow the majority, in monetary law we do not follow the majority. I answered that in my humble opinion, with a fine we do follow the majority. The reason we generally do not follow the majority in monetary cases is because the chazakah of possession supports the current holder, and possession is powerful. This makes sense with ordinary debts. If the claimant is telling the truth, the debtor's property was already subject to his lien. That claim therefore contradicts the presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him, and for that reason we do not follow the majority against possession. But with a fine, liability does not begin until the parties stand in judgment. Even if the act occurred earlier, the claimant has not yet acquired a lien over the offender's property. The doubt therefore does not contradict an existing chazakah of possession. It concerns only the future, whether the property will now become subject to him. With regard to the future, chazakah does not apply in the same way, just as we are concerned that perhaps a person will die. Therefore, with a fine we follow the majority. I now see a proof for this from Bava Kamma 110b. The Gemara asks: Here it is impossible to obtain an olive's bulk of meat without slaughtering, yet the Mishnah teaches that he pays double payment. Double payment, yes, but fourfold or fivefold payment, no. It answers that he ate carrion, etc. I find it difficult: Why did it need to establish the case with wicked people eating carrion? It could have said that they slaughtered it properly, but by a person whom we did not know to be proficient in the laws of slaughter or who had no established chazakah that he would not faint. The permissibility of the meat would then rest on the majority, because most people found near slaughter are experts and competent. Since in monetary matters we do not follow the majority, he should be exempt from fourfold or fivefold payment because perhaps the slaughter was invalid. Why does the Gemara not answer this way? Necessarily, with a fine we do follow the majority. Examine this carefully. Indeed, perhaps the Gemara's answer, "it became carrion," can be understood simply as a case of doubtful carrion, since such language is sometimes used elsewhere, and this is sufficient. There, seif 23 in the gloss, wherever there is a mistaken sale, etc., it is treated like an ordinary creditor. Note: See my commentary on Even HaEzer siman 28, sixth edition, siman 28, Hilkhot Kiddushin, where I expressed difficulty with the Rama here for not explaining the distinction between money and an object. If one gave an object as payment for the sale, it should be treated only as a deposit and not as an ordinary creditor, but rather under the laws of a bailee.
