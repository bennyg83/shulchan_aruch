Baer Heitev cites the Sema's discussion of why Tur here appears to disagree with Rambam concerning defects, while in siman 227 concerning onaah Tur did not state such a distinction. Netivot HaMishpat suggests that onaah differs because the object itself is worth less than the price, whereas here the object was sold for its actual value in its defective condition. This is difficult, since Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch expressly discuss a defect that reduces the price. Netivot elsewhere, siman 60, offers another reconciliation and notes that the Sema overlooked Ramban; Shaar HaMelech similarly discusses the point, and Gilya Masechta treats it at length. VeShav HaKohen, siman 45, explains that Tur did not mean to dispute Rambam's basic rule that a general waiver of defects is ineffective unless the defect is specified. Tur agreed that, as with onaah, a purchaser who merely says that he waives defects without knowing them does not necessarily waive them. Tur objected only to the broader language concerning other indeterminate obligations. VeShav HaKohen further raises a question: the reason a general waiver of onaah or defects is ineffective is ordinarily that we presume the party did not truly intend to waive an unknown right. What if he admits that at the time he made the condition he actually intended to waive every possible defect, but he now seeks to retract? Since monetary conditions are generally valid, perhaps his admitted intent should bind him. On the other hand, because a general formulation ordinarily has no legal effect, the unexpressed intention may remain mere matters of the heart and be ineffective. He leaves the matter unresolved. Shevut Yaakov, II, siman 166, discusses a man who sold a counterfeit ring. The buyer thought it genuine. The seller said, in the language of uncertainty, that he would not guarantee that the ring was genuine because perhaps it was counterfeit. The buyer answered that he did not want the seller's guarantee and purchased it. When it was later established that the ring was entirely counterfeit, the buyer sought rescission. The seller argued that the buyer had waived any claim. Shevut Yaakov rules that the sale is void. The buyer's statement that he did not need the seller's guarantee meant only that he did not require an additional guarantee, because if the ring turned out not to be what it was sold as, ordinary law already entitled him to recover his money. If the seller knew it was counterfeit and only pretended uncertainty, this was outright deception and even an express waiver would not help him. Even if the seller was genuinely uncertain, the buyer's supposed waiver was based on mistake and is ineffective, especially because the buyer did not know the ring was counterfeit when he spoke.
