The custom not to void sales of living animals because of treifot applies when the animal was purchased alive and the purchaser knowingly accepted that risk. Where no contrary custom exists, the ordinary halachah remains in force. One sold meat on the third day after slaughter, when prompt soaking or salting was necessary, but did not inform the buyer. Because several hours passed without soaking, the meat became prohibited for cooking. The buyer may return it and recover his money. Although we do not generally follow a majority to extract money, virtually no one pays the ordinary price for meat fit only for dry spit roasting. If three days had already passed when it was sold without disclosure, it is plainly a fixed defect. Even where it was sold earlier that day, since soaking is commonly done toward evening before cooking, failure to disclose may likewise justify rescission. If the seller did not disclose until after the three days, it resembles selling nonkosher meat as kosher. Even if the buyer learned the fact from someone else in time to avoid an actual prohibition, the seller nevertheless sold him an item containing a halachic pitfall. The seller might argue that he sold intending to notify the buyer but later forgot or was prevented, and because a minority of buyers soak immediately upon bringing meat home perhaps majority evidence alone cannot extract money from him. If the buyer is already in possession of the money, however, it leans toward allowing rescission, for forgetfulness is not a basis to extract from him and the buyer can say that he does not know that he ever became obligated. If the seller did notify him in time and the buyer himself had no opportunity to soak it, the buyer's later accident does not shift the loss back to the seller, since title had already passed. One who sells a cow for slaughter before pregnancy can be recognized, and pregnancy is then discovered before slaughter, may face a mistaken sale because a pregnant cow is worth considerably more for milk and breeding. Yet the rule that valuation follows its place and time creates a difficulty: at the time of sale no one could know whether it was pregnant, so there was then no measurable error in number or market value. If, however, the seller was to keep the cow for several days under the agreement and pregnancy became apparent before delivery, perhaps the later discovery establishes retroactively that it was pregnant at the time of sale, especially since under the agreement the matter was expected to become ascertainable before the purchaser took possession. This resembles a sale in a village or field where no expert is present and an expert later reveals an existing overcharge or defect. One may distinguish that case because the absence of an expert is incidental to the place, whereas pregnancy was objectively unknowable to everyone at the sale. This requires further study. The fact that buyers do not rescind after slaughter merely because a fetus is found does not prove otherwise. After slaughter, discovering pregnancy has no practical benefit and buyers knowingly accept that uncertainty. Before slaughter, where pregnancy becomes discoverable and materially affects value, or where experts could have recognized it at the time of sale, the sale may indeed be mistaken. A person bought the hide and intestines of a calf that had recently been weaned and fed normally. He paid the entire price in cash, but made neither meshichah nor a situmta and they did not specify whether the purchase was to stand if the calf proved a treifah. The calf was rendered a treifah because of an adhesion to the heart. I mediated a compromise weighted in favor of the buyer, placing only one third of the loss upon him. A money payment alone is subject to a dispute among the authorities as to whether it effects acquisition. Even if the money was fully paid, there remains doubt. There is also a dispute whether ordinary adhesions void the sale. The settled commercial custom for purchases of whole animals is that the sale stands regardless of treifah, but no such custom was established for a transaction of this sort. Moreover, adhesions are not common in a young calf, though after weaning there may be some uncertainty. I did not ask the slaughterer whether the adhesion was thick enough to show that it predated the purchase, though the buyer claimed that it was large and the seller did not deny this. Because one may not knowingly buy definite treifah merchandise for resale, I inclined to leave title with the seller. There was a double doubt in that direction: perhaps money did not acquire, and perhaps the buyer never bought on the assumption of treifah. To declare it the buyer's would create a double doubt toward prohibition. Even according to those later authorities who consider money effective, there remains at least one uncertainty, and property that came to him by uncertainty may be treated as having come to him incidentally. The economics of the transaction also suggested that the parties contemplated a kosher animal, for otherwise it would resemble a speculative purchase dependent on an unusually favorable outcome. I therefore compromised that one third of the loss be borne by the buyer, though the legal inclination favored him.
