But if he was found to be a thief. Chavot Yair, siman 211, discusses one who hired a maidservant and later learned that before entering his household she had engaged in promiscuous conduct. Her brother argued that since she was not pregnant, no practical defect existed, and compared this to the rule that one who buys a Canaanite maidservant cannot ordinarily rescind for hidden defects. Chavot Yair rejects the argument. A person known to have acted promiscuously may require special supervision lest she go out at night or draw dissolute men to the house, and there are many continuing risks. Hidden physical defects in a Canaanite slave that do not interfere with work are different because those defects exist against the slave's will and create no continuing danger. Here the problem concerns disposition and conduct. Nevertheless, if she had once been coerced, or even had once sinned casually but was otherwise known throughout her life as modest before and after, the employer would have to retain her, while supervising her appropriately. He compares this to a shochet who once caused a mishap. Shevut Yaakov, I, siman 174, objects to Chavot Yair's implication that promiscuity is necessarily a defect even in a Canaanite maidservant. The Gemara and poskim say broadly that there is no ordinary claim of hidden defect in slaves, and several Mishnayot presume a maidservant may be promiscuous. He also rejects any implication that the same rule applies to a Jewish servant. A Jewish man or woman is presumed kosher. Accordingly, where one hired a Jewish maidservant and later established by clear testimony that she was habitually a thief, the employer may rescind, even if she could find employment elsewhere, because such conduct is a genuine defect in a Jew who otherwise has a presumption of fitness.
