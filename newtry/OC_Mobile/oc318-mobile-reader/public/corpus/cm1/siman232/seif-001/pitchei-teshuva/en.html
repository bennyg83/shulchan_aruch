But if he used it. See Sema, who writes in the name of Maggid Mishneh that the same applies where the item was something the purchaser could have inspected immediately, and Be'er HaGolah also cites this. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Mechirah, chapter 15, writes that there is no doubt Rambam disagrees with the rule cited by Maggid Mishneh. Rambam says, "provided he did not use it after he saw the defect," implying that even if he saw the defect and remained silent he may still retract. Otherwise, why mention use? Mere sight and silence would already constitute waiver. All the more so where he could have examined but did not, he certainly retains the right to retract. See there. Responsa appended to Beit Meir, siman 6, likewise records that in the author's margin to Sema he wrote: See Responsa Maharit Tzahalon, siman 225, who disagrees; Mishneh LaMelech there; and Sema, siman 230, seif katan 14. Shitah Mekubetzet also cites Maharit Tzahalon as disagreeing. Responsa Shav Yaakov, part 3, siman 169, mentions Maharit Tzahalon and also Responsa Raanach, part 1, siman 40, who write that according to Rif, Rosh, and Rambam, who rule that a defect can always be grounds for rescission and that delay beyond the time required to show the item to a merchant does not constitute waiver, even if the defect could have been detected they do not infer waiver merely from failure to inspect. Shav Yaakov writes that there is actually no necessary dispute, as Sema and other later authorities imply. He distinguishes between a defect that could have been detected before completion of the sale, which is what Maggid Mishneh means by "immediately"; if the buyer could have inspected before finalizing and did not, that indicates acceptance even if that defect exists. But once the transaction was completed and the defect was afterward discovered, mere silence for some time does not waive the claim. The buyer may simply have had no current use for the item and known that he retains the right to rescind whenever the need arises. So long as he does not use the item, there is no waiver. Terumat HaKri writes virtually the same distinction without seeing that Shav Yaakov had preceded him. Netivot HaMishpat interprets Sema differently, namely that Sema means only where the purchaser used the item after he could have inspected it. Just as one who sees the defect and then uses the object waives, so too one who could have detected it and nevertheless used the object waives. This reasoning is certainly sound, and Sema's wording can perhaps be forced into it, but Maggid Mishneh, the source of Sema's statement, cannot be interpreted that way. The correct explanation is therefore that of Shav Yaakov. See Responsa Rif, siman 153, and Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, at the end of siman 60. Mishneh LaMelech further writes that even according to the opinion cited by Maggid Mishneh, the rule applies specifically where the purchaser has already paid the seller. If he bought on credit and still holds the purchase money, he can say that because the money remained in his hand he did not hurry to test the item and never waived. Netivot HaMishpat questions this on the basis of Shach, siman 227, seif katan 4, implying that without a migo one cannot establish nonwaiver. I already wrote in siman 227, seif 7, seif katan 2, that several leading authorities agree with Mishneh LaMelech and hold that even without a migo one is believed in such circumstances to say, "I did not waive." See there. See Mishnat Chachamim, page 92, and Responsa Pri Tevuah, at the end of siman 20. Responsa Galia Masechta, at the end of siman 10, comments on Mishneh LaMelech that if the purchaser used the object where he had no right to use it, so that he is certainly treated as a robber, it does not help that he still holds the purchase money. This is obvious. Netivot HaMishpat further writes concerning Shulchan Aruch's phrase, "but if he used it," that if the purchaser could not return it because the seller was away and the item is of a type that produces and consumes, the rule is different. The restriction against use applies only where he has no need to preserve the asset. If he does not accept the defect and refrains from ordinary productive use merely because he intends to rescind, he can still return it even after many days. Although the sale is void, his position resembles that of a finder of lost property, siman 267, seif 22, who must allow an object that produces and consumes to produce and consume. A responsum of Maimonides printed with Sefer Kinyan, siman 7, supports this. Concerning a horse sold by Reuven to Shimon in which defects were later found, it rules that Shimon must compensate Reuven for deterioration caused by labor performed after Shimon learned of the defect. Shiltei HaGibborim to the chapter HaZahav asks that once the buyer used the item after seeing the defect we rule that he waived and cannot return it. Netivot's distinction resolves the question, and Responsa Galia Masechta likewise notes this. Netivot further writes that in ona'ah, where the purchaser used the item but did not return it because the seller was absent, if the ona'ah is exactly one sixth the sale remains valid and only the ona'ah is refunded. Use of one's own item does not show waiver. Only if the purchaser could have returned the item and did not do so is waiver inferred. If the ona'ah exceeds one sixth, however, the case resembles a defect. Therefore, even if he could not return the item because the seller was absent, if he used it after the ordinary inspection period we say he certainly waived. Netivot's analysis here exactly matches Ritva, cited by Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 50b, s.v. Yeter Al Shetut. Ritva adds a third rule: even where the ona'ah exceeds one sixth and the buyer uses the item after discovering the ona'ah, if he first notified the seller and then used it, his use does not establish waiver. If he wishes to annul the sale, however, he must compensate the seller for the use or for any loss caused by it. Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchot Ona'ah, siman 5, cites this and adds that the same should apply to a defect. If the buyer discovers a defect, notifies the seller, and afterward uses the item, he can still rescind and the use is not treated as waiver. Divrei Mishpat to this seif cites Machaneh Ephraim. Responsa Galia Masechta, siman 10, discusses a case where the purchaser notified the seller of a defect and afterward continued using the item. A great rabbi ruled that the purchaser may return the item but must pay for the use, relying on Ritva's statement regarding ona'ah. Galia Masechta argues at length that ona'ah and defects should be distinguished. In excessive ona'ah, the object itself is satisfactory and the complaint is essentially monetary. Notice to the seller can therefore preserve the monetary claim while use continues, because the seller's failure to immediately annul the deal can be understood as willingness to refund the excess and both parties may still desire the transaction. With a defect, however, there is no mere monetary dispute. The purchaser says he does not accept the underlying object at all. Giving notice should not give him license to continue using what he claims not to have purchased; continued use necessarily indicates acceptance of the defect. Galia Masechta apparently did not see Machaneh Ephraim, who equates the cases. In the particular case addressed by Galia Masechta, the purchaser had not yet paid the purchase price. Therefore, the ruling of the great rabbi appears correct because one should not extract money from the purchaser. If the purchaser had already paid and now sought recovery from the seller, the matter would require study. Galia Masechta's distinction has substantial logic. See Nachalat Tzvi for what I wrote. See also Orchot Mishpat, netiv 92, siman 17, who raises a further doubt about the basic rule that use implies waiver. What if the purchaser is an ordinary unlearned person who does not know that use after discovery of a defect constitutes waiver and does not know that he has no right to use the item while intending to return it? He may mistakenly permit himself to use it until the seller returns the money, while loudly insisting that he never waives and certainly intends to rescind. All the more so where the defect is discovered while traveling, such as one who buys a horse, finds a defect midway, and continues riding it back to his city intending to pursue a din Torah. He has unquestionably used it after seeing the defect, but it is clear that necessity, not waiver, caused the use. Must he abandon the horse, walk home, and drag the wagon with its load by hand? Orchot Mishpat leaves these questions requiring very great study. In my humble opinion, regarding the first case, use caused by ignorance of the law, the purchaser cannot retract. Chazal did not make their rules vary according to individual legal knowledge. Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 51a, s.v. Aval BeLokeach MiBaal HaBayit, similarly writes regarding certain cases of ona'ah that although the actual purchaser may not have waived, the Sages made a uniform rule, otherwise the law would differ between an ignoramus and a Torah scholar. It is possible to distinguish that case because it is itself a rabbinic classification, but the analogy is significant. Regarding the second case, use compelled by circumstances, the example of a horse is not precise, because a horse is generally an item that produces and consumes, so the ordinary waiver rule may not apply for the reason already discussed. He should have given an example of a wagon or similar item. But as to the underlying principle, use caused by unavoidable necessity should plainly not constitute waiver, just as delayed assertion of ona'ah caused by an unavoidable impediment does not, as stated in siman 227, seif 7.
