The Sema writes, following the Tur in seif 3 in the name of the Raavad, that sometimes the entire sale is void, as where the seller says, "I sell you this sack which contains a se'ah," whereas sometimes he merely completes the deficiency, as where he says, "this sack is a se'ah," and both parties know that it is not actually a se'ah. This raises a question where neither party knew, but both knew that they did not know. The same question arises where full vessels are sold subject to later measuring or weighing, and afterward adulteration or refuse is found in them, reducing the measure or weight. Can the buyer void the sale by saying that he estimated that a vessel of this kind ordinarily contains at least a certain amount and bought on that assumption? It appears that if the seller supplies the deficiency, the buyer has no further claim, since where the contents were not visible he did not insist that the entire contents be of identical quality. Thus, with honey ordinarily brought from the hive, the seller can supplement it with other honey. If he does not supplement, however, the buyer's claim may rest on the accepted estimate that such a vessel contains not less than a specified quantity. If the defect caused a definite shortfall, it resembles an express statement that this vessel contains a se'ah. The precise wording of the Raavad does not resolve the matter, since the textual inferences point in opposite directions. Where it is known that both parties were unaware, it is clear that the sale is not void according to all opinions if the deficiency is supplied. Regarding earthy sediment mixed into honey, which can be corrected by dissolving the honey in water so that the sediment falls and the mixture can then be used to make the beverage called mead, the buyer may argue that he did not buy on that basis, but in order to sell the honey in its refined and clean state. It appears that this is a valid claim whenever local custom treats such sediment as significant. Some people do not wish to make that beverage because they do not want the labor involved or for similar reasons, and the ordinary manner of selling honey is to sell the clean honey itself. In such a place the buyer's claim is valid. Compare the laws of wine bought for a cooked dish and an ox bought for plowing. If the buyer merely asks that the seller make good the deficiency according to value, that is certainly the preferable resolution.
