Seif 1. How so? If he sold him one hundred nuts for a dinar and there were found to be one hundred and one or ninety-nine, the sale stands and the error is corrected even after many years. Note: It appears that this applies specifically where he did not sell on a basis of trust, but made an ordinary sale. In that case the buyer cannot rescind; rather, the seller must make up the deficiency. But if he sold on trust, stating that he himself paid such-and-such for this sack, then even if he told the buyer that it contained one hundred and it was later found to contain fewer, the seller may say: Granted that there were fewer, nevertheless I paid such-and-such for it, and you must pay me according to what I paid together with the profit you promised me. It is true that the seller cannot compel the buyer to accept the sale, for the buyer may say, "I assumed there were one hundred; with fewer, they are too expensive for me." However, this means only that the seller cannot force the buyer to take the goods as they are. The buyer likewise cannot compel the seller to make up the deficiency. The seller may say: I paid such-and-such for it, and I sold it to you on trust. Since the sale was on trust, either take it as it is or return my merchandise. If the buyer wants the seller to make up the deficiency, he must instead return the merchandise. If there is a profit in the goods, that profit also belongs to the seller when the sale is rescinded. This appears correct in law and clear in reasoning.
