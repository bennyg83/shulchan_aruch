The buyer must bring proof. If tefillin are examined and found invalid, and the purchaser claims against the scribe or seller, then if it is evident that the invalidity existed from the time of writing the money must plainly be returned. If the defect, such as smearing or erasure, could have developed with age, or certainly did develop through age, the scribe may retain and even collect the purchase price under the principle "here it was found, here it is presumed to have arisen," provided the buyer took them as a purchase. Even if it is later discovered that, had the defect existed initially, the sale would have been void, that principle still applies, as is explicit regarding wine and animals in the Shulchan Aruch. This supplies some support for the Taz in siman 75 concerning a counterfeit coin, that there is a presumption of payment even though later events may reveal retroactively that valid payment did not occur. We judge the presumption according to the state in which the matter was previously held, even though it may later turn out that the assumption was mistaken. The Shach in siman 232 cites Maharshal or Maharashdam in a manner that points somewhat the other way. One cannot distinguish our case merely because the purchaser had the item in his domain and could dispose of it, since with fungible goods one may sometimes use another's equivalent item and later replace it. Thus the force of "here it was found" rests on the established status of the sale. At minimum, one cannot extract money from the scribe in the tefillin case. Even a double doubt does not overcome possession. A disease affecting animals can constitute a mistaken purchase even where no physical change occurred in the particular animal after the sale. Perhaps this resembles damage that is not externally recognizable, which is treated more leniently in tort law. At the time and place of sale the animals may have had the very same market value, and the later rumor or concern is not physically visible. On the other hand, to persons who know the concern it may be considered recognizable, similar to Tosafot's distinction that a legal defect created by Torah law can be considered recognizable while one existing only by rabbinic enactment may not. A person registered for royal service is treated by Chazal as having a defect. Perhaps that is because the king has a proprietary right under royal law, though what lies outside the laws of kingship stated by Shmuel may not amount to ownership. It remains possible that a sale defect may exist even when not physically visible. This requires further study. If a cow, seven weeks after purchase, ceases eating and experts say the cause is an internal rupture, the buyer may argue that it already existed at the sale but only manifested later. Perhaps ordinary chazakah does not resolve an internal condition that was never verified at the relevant time. If the internal condition were a treifah, the majority of animals being kosher could create a presumption, as is used regarding cheeses. If the internal rupture is not itself a treifah, the issue is different. Still, it is unlikely that a serious internal defect could remain concealed for so long without any external indication. Experts should be consulted whether that occurs. One should not impose even a general acceptance of a ban upon the seller merely on speculation, especially where chazakah supports the proposition that the defect arose later. If, however, there is a concrete basis for suspicion, such as that he sold substantially below the usual price, an oath or acceptance may properly be imposed. If one sells a slaughtered bird as kosher and afterward a broken bone is found that renders it a treifah, the rules of ona'ah discussed elsewhere indicate that the seller must return the money. The current custom not to make claims against the seller of an animal for certain treifot other than lung defects may rest on the fact that the animal is purchased subject to the known uncertainty of lung inspection. That reasoning does not apply to a slaughtered bird, where treifah is not a common concern and the buyer did not contemplate such a risk. Whether the same claim can be made for other treifot in animals requires further study.
