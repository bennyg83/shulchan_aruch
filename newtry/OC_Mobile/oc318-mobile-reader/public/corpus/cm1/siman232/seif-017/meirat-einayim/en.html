The seller shall swear, etc. He swears in order to exempt himself. Shimon does not swear in order to extract money from Reuven. So writes the Rosh in responsum, as cited by the Tur. At first glance this implies that if the purchase money is still in the buyer's hand and the seller is the claimant, then the buyer should swear and be exempt. This is not conclusive. One could say that once Shimon made meshichah on the oil and brought it into his domain, he acquired it and the money became a debt in his hands. This somewhat resembles exchange of a cow for a donkey. Yet it is not identical, because there the uncertainty arose in the possessor's domain and we apply the rule that the defect is presumed to have arisen where it was found. Here the oil is presently cloudy, and ordinarily we presume that it was cloudy from the outset.

If the purchaser wishes to take it. The Mechaber deliberately writes "if he wishes," although neither the Tur nor the responsum of the Rosh uses that phrase. He holds that if the purchaser does not want the oil, he can force the seller to take it back because this is a mistaken sale. I found the same in Shiltei HaGibborim. Proof comes from the Mishnah concerning one who sells fine wheat and it is found to be inferior, where the purchaser may rescind. Even if the purchaser prefers to keep the article and demand the price difference, he cannot compel the seller to accept that arrangement, because in a mistaken sale either side may annul the transaction.
