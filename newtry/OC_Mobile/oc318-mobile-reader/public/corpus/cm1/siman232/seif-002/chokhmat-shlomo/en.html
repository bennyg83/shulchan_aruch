Seif 3. But if he used it, etc. Note: See the Sema, who cites the Maggid Mishneh concerning an item that can be tested immediately and tasted, etc. In my humble opinion, that rule applies specifically to an item in which fraud or defect is common, such as wine, where it is common for it to become vinegar, or similar cases where defects are frequent. In such cases the buyer should have considered the possibility and tasted or tested it. But where fraud and error are not common, he had no reason at all to suspect falsification and test for it. In every such case, once the error becomes known, the sale is a mistaken transaction. This is clear and reasonable. While discussing this, I should mention several points requiring examination in this siman regarding the Shach. First, what he writes in seif katan 203, concerning one who swore that he does not know and was exempt, that here it is treated like a definite claim. This contradicts what he writes in siman 255, seif katan 13, where he leaves this very reasoning unresolved, whereas here he states it simply. Also, what he writes in seif katan 15 in the name of Responsa Maharshadam, that the case is one of "I do not know whether I repaid you," should be compared with the Taz in siman 75, who writes the opposite. I find this difficult, because the Rama here in seif 18, where he rules that one swears that he does not know whether there was tin mixed into the goods, is explicit like the Taz. Here the seller certainly received a fixed sum of money, and he is uncertain whether he delivered goods containing tin or not. That too is a case of "I do not know whether I repaid you." This is obvious if the seller first received the money and afterward delivered the goods, for according to Maharshadam that is certainly "I do not know whether I repaid you." Even if the buyer first received the goods and afterward paid, it would still be a doubtful payment made before the loan, and according to the Shach in siman 75 he would be liable. It therefore requires study how the Shach could rule here simply in the opposite direction. As for the Taz, it is difficult that he did not bring proof from here. Yet this difficulty is not decisive, because one could say that here the case is where the goods were delivered first and the money received afterward. In my view the law is unlike the Shach: where there is doubt concerning a payment made before the loan, one is exempt, and there is no proof from this case. But according to the Shach, who rules above that even doubtful prior payment creates liability, it remains difficult how he could rule oppositely here. Perhaps he distinguishes between money that came into one's hand as an obligation he undertook and money that came into one's hand not as an obligation but only as payment. Even if there is doubt whether he thereby discharged his duty toward the other party, so long as the money was not received as an obligation it is not considered "I do not know whether I repaid you." This is strained and requires study. For practical halachah, the main view follows the implication of the Taz and the Rama here, and the Urim VeTumim and Netivot HaMishpat likewise agree. Examine this carefully. I have also found proof for the Sema's rule in the name of the Maggid Mishneh from the Gemara, Bekhorot 13b, where it says: "It is not a mistaken sale, since he paid money; if he wanted it, he should have examined it and then performed meshichah," etc. This supports the rule that where one could have tested the merchandise, the sale is not considered mistaken, as the Maggid Mishneh holds. That passage also seems to prove that this applies specifically where money was paid. In the first clause, where money had not been paid, although meshichah had been performed, the transaction is considered mistaken even though he could have examined it. Thus the Maggid Mishneh's rule would apply only where money had been paid, not where it had not. However, if so, the Gemara seems to prove the opposite as a matter of halachah, because Rava says that both the first and second clauses are mistaken transactions. He therefore does not accept the reasoning that the buyer should have examined it. Since the halachah follows Rava against Abaye, this appears contrary to the Maggid Mishneh. Perhaps there the buyer had no reason to inspect, because he would not have imagined that the gentile would sell his idolatrous object. But generally, where there was reason to suspect a defect, perhaps Rava would agree with Abaye. It is preferable to minimize the dispute. In any event, Abaye's position proves at least that even though he holds one should have inspected, where no money was paid, although meshichah occurred, we do not say that he should have inspected. The same should apply in the Maggid Mishneh's case. This is, in my humble opinion, a novel legal distinction. Regarding the Maggid Mishneh's rule cited by the Sema, that when an item can be inspected and it was sold without qualification the buyer cannot rescind, I saw that the Netivot HaMishpat of the gaon of Lissa writes that this applies only if the buyer used the item after he could have inspected it. See there. Even according to his words, it appears that the buyer need not have used the very defective object itself. Rather, if he bought one purchase containing merchandise or similar goods, sold some of it, and afterward claims that the remainder was defective, where he could have inspected the goods immediately he is deemed to have waived the defect. Since he bought the goods together, he should immediately have checked whether some were defective. Since he did not inspect all of them and proceeded to use this purchase, he waived the defect. His principal proofs are from the Mishneh LaMelech, Hilkhot Mekhirah chapter 15, and from Tosafot. Thus we should limit the rule to a case where he did not use this purchase at all. If he did use the purchase, one may say that since he could have inspected the entire lot and did not do so, he waived the defect. We should not extend the innovation further than necessary. In truth, I do not find the Netivot's principal interpretation convincing either. The wording of the Sema, who comments on the Mechaber's phrase "if he used it" by writing "the same applies," indicates that actual use is not required in the Maggid Mishneh's case. The Mishneh LaMelech's question from Rambam, Hilkhot Mekhirah chapter 15, that even where the defect was visible actual use is required, is not difficult. That case may concern one who saw the defect and used the item even though he could not notify the seller because the seller was absent and return was impossible. If he used it, he waived the defect. If he did not use it, he did not waive it, and his failure to notify the seller is irrelevant because the seller was absent. The Maggid Mishneh and Sema, however, discuss a case where the seller was present and could have been notified. There, even if the buyer did not actually see the defect, so long as he could have detected it, he waived his claim. Alternatively, Rambam may discuss a case where the buyer had not yet paid. Then his silence proves nothing, because he relied upon the fact that payment had not yet been made, so actual use is also required. The Maggid Mishneh and Sema may discuss a case where payment had been made. There, the mere fact that the buyer could have inspected the item suffices to show waiver. As for the proof from Tosafot in Bava Batra concerning wine, with due respect, it is not exact. The Sema writes only where the seller sold without qualification. But if he expressly stated that the item was good, the Sema agrees that there is no waiver even though the buyer could have tested it. Tosafot concern one who sold wine, and ordinary wine is presumed to be good, not vinegar. The unqualified term "wine" means wine that does not taste or smell like vinegar. Therefore the buyer's ability to test it is irrelevant. Accordingly, with due respect to the gaon who authored the Netivot HaMishpat, I believe his innovation should be withdrawn, and we should retain the plain meaning of the Maggid Mishneh and the Sema. This appears correct to me, with Hashem's help.
