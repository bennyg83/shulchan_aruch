If one says, "May so-and-so not be blessed," he is not lashed. This follows the rule that we do not infer a positive statement from a negative formulation in this context. One must understand this according to Rashi's version in Shevuot. Rashi writes that R. Meir does not accept inference from negative to positive in monetary matters, but he does in matters of prohibition. Shevuat HaEdut is treated there as an area of prohibition that also contains monetary consequence. If so, ordinary cursing of another person contains no monetary element at all. All the more so cursing a judge is purely prohibitory. One might therefore have expected that saying "may he not be blessed" should indeed count as a curse. We must say Rambam does not follow Rashi's distinction but rather Tosafot's explanation: inference from negative language applies only in a more severe prohibition, while an ordinary lashes-level prohibition is not sufficient. Accordingly, with cursing father or mother, which carries capital liability, there is room to say that even the negative formulation may count. Rambam should perhaps have stated this in Hilchot Mamrim. Yet another point remains. Even though cursing one's parents is a more severe offense, Rambam derives its warning from the prohibition against cursing the deaf person. We generally do not expand the form of the warning beyond its source. Since the deaf-person prohibition itself is in the lighter category where "may he not be blessed" does not create formal lashes, perhaps the parental prohibition should follow the same form despite its greater punishment. The matter therefore requires study.
Likewise, one who curses the dead is exempt. Sema follows Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh, who infer this from the fact that Scripture requires a special inclusion for one who curses his father or mother after death. This implies that ordinarily a deceased person is outside the regular prohibition. Semag also cites Torat Kohanim: just as the deaf person is alive, so too the ordinary person included in the prohibition must be alive. Many have wondered that an explicit Gemara in Sanhedrin 85 seems even stronger. If a person has been sentenced to death and his son curses him, the son can still be liable. Another person who curses him is exempt. The Gemara explains that although the condemned man repented and is again "one of your people," the verse still refers to one who is destined to remain among your people, not one whose execution has already been decreed. Rashi explains that someone whose sentence is final is no longer included. If even a living person awaiting execution is outside the ordinary prohibition, then certainly a dead person is. Why then do the commentators need indirect derivations? A possible answer is as follows. The reason Torah prohibits cursing a deaf person cannot be that the curse necessarily has some mystical effect upon the victim. A causeless curse does not inevitably take effect. Rather, the prohibition addresses the curser's corrupt heart and hatred toward another Jew. Torah commands "you shall love your fellow as yourself," a foundational principle of Torah. Accordingly, one who knowingly curses a person who is already dead, or one who is certain to die immediately, lacks the same interpersonal context. The victim no longer stands in ordinary human relation to him, and the Torah's special lashes-level prohibition may therefore not apply. But suppose a person curses someone believing him to be alive, while unbeknownst to him the person had already died. The curser's evil intent and hatred are fully present. One might therefore have thought the offense should depend on the curser's intention and still be punishable. Torat Kohanim teaches otherwise by deriving from the deaf person that the victim himself must actually be alive. Likewise the special verse concerning father and mother after death teaches that only there does Torah extend liability beyond the ordinary living-victim framework. This may justify the commentators' derivations despite the Sanhedrin passage. The answer is somewhat strained and requires further study.
Nevertheless, there is at least a prohibition. Bedek HaBayit writes that R. Meir and the Sages appear to disagree only about lashes. Even where lashes are absent, the formulation is still forbidden. This requires clarification. In Hilchot Nedarim chapter 1 Rambam rules that if a person says, "Not a korban shall I eat from you," the neder is entirely permitted. The Gemara explains that even if the formulation could imply, "it shall not be a korban if I do not eat, but if I do eat it shall be a korban," it remains permitted because we do not infer the positive from the negative. If even where the formal inference fails there is still some prohibition, why does Rambam call the neder completely permitted? We must distinguish categories. In a lighter prohibition that also has a monetary character, such as nedarim, there may be no prohibition at all when the formulation is only inferential. Ran explains that nedarim are considered an issur that contains a monetary element because the person forbids an object upon himself. By contrast, cursing another Jew is a pure prohibition and the Mishnah's language says the Sages "exempt," not that they "permit." This implies that although lashes are absent, the act remains prohibited. One might ask: Tosafot's distinction between more severe and lighter prohibitions was forced precisely by the problem from nedarim. If according to Ran nedarim are already a monetary-type prohibition, why is another distinction necessary? Rambam may have been forced to the distinction because he understands the dispute of R. Meir and the Sages concerning "may God not strike you" as referring back to the Mishnah's ordinary case of cursing another person, as he writes in his Commentary to the Mishnah. That case contains no monetary element. Yet the Sages say only "exempt," not "permitted." Therefore Rambam must distinguish between lack of lashes and complete permissibility. See also my novellae concerning negative inference, where I explained that Rambam may derive the law of verbal expression in nedarim from shevuot, from the verse concerning what a person utters, and that unexpressed implication is insufficient there. That further resolves Tosafot's difficulty.
