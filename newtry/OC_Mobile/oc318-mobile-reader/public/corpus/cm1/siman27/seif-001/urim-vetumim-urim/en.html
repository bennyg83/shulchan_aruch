One of Israel, etc. Rambam and Sema explain that the prohibition is learned from the case of a deaf person, who cannot hear the curse and therefore does not suffer distress from hearing it. All the more is it forbidden to curse any other Jew who can hear and suffer. See Tumim for what I wrote about this.
Or by one of the names by which gentiles refer to Him. Because such a name is treated as a Divine appellation. Sema. Through our many sins, most ordinary people are not careful about this and say in German phrases meaning, "God should punish him," or "God should strike him." They thereby violate a Torah prohibition. More generally, through our many sins people are careless with Divine appellations and imagine that when a Divine reference is written or spoken in a gentile language it is merely a descriptive title. They are mistaken. They write at the beginning of correspondence the French expression "adieu," whose meaning invokes God, and afterward the paper is cast into refuse. The Sages already established a festival when the Divine Name ceased to be mentioned in ordinary notes, because on the next day after a debt was repaid the document bearing the Name would be thrown into refuse. Now, through our many sins, people have learned from gentile practice and the corruption has returned even more strongly. This is among the causes of our great abasement, where references to Heaven are common, especially in degrading and needless contexts. The sages of the generation must use wisdom, persistence, and rebuke to uproot this practice from the populace.
If there were witnesses, etc., he receives lashes, etc. The Mechaber mentions lashes, and that one who curses a judge violates two prohibitions, although through our many sins formal ordination has ceased and we have no court empowered to administer Torah lashes. The practical consequence is disqualification from testimony. As stated below in siman 34, one who violates a prohibition for which there are no lashes is not disqualified from testimony on a Torah level. This also explains why the Mechaber notes two prohibitions. Even if a person did not know that cursing an ordinary fellow Jew is forbidden, and therefore would not become disqualified by that violation because, as explained below in siman 34, a transgression widely regarded by ordinary people as permissible does not disqualify him, this prohibition too unfortunately being widespread as I wrote, he may nevertheless know that cursing a judge is forbidden. In that event he becomes disqualified because of the prohibition, "You shall not curse a judge." See Tumim, where I wrote that today, after formal ordination has ceased, it appears that this particular prohibition of cursing a judge does not apply in that form.
And "cursed" is an expression of cursing. Meaning, if he says of his fellow, "Cursed is he by God," or uses another Divine appellation, he is liable. The novelty is that one might have thought "cursed" is only a formula of oath and not of curse. But he certainly must mention the Divine Name or an appellation. Sema. Kenesset HaGedolah writes that Responsa Maharam Mintz appears to impose liability even without the Name. I do not currently have that sefer before me to examine it. In Shevuos 36, however, Rashi, s.v. "arur bo," writes that one who curses his fellow by saying, "Cursed is he by God," has uttered a curse and is liable. Thus the phrase must include the Divine Name, as the Sema writes.
