By the Divine Name or by a substitute name. There in the Mishnah.
Or by one of the names. Sanhedrin 60a: if Eglon king of Moav, who was a gentile and knew only a substitute name, etc. There it also says, excluding a substitute name, which is not, and this disagrees, etc. Rather, it is derived from a gentile. If it meant the unique Divine Name, did they know it? Rather, it must refer to a substitute name. We learn that the names gentiles use are called substitute names. Likewise regarding vows, at the beginning of Nedarim it asks, "what are substitute expressions?" Rabbi Yochanan says they are gentile forms of speech.
If he was. In chapter 3 of Ketubot and in many places in the Talmud.
(Collected note) He receives lashes. Temurah 3a, 3b, 4a, and likewise in the Yerushalmi cited by the Kesef Mishnah: one who curses himself or his fellow with any of them violates a prohibition. Does he receive lashes? The colleagues said he does not. Rabbi Yose said to them: why? Because it is a prohibition without an action? Yet one who substitutes a consecrated animal and one who swears falsely are also prohibitions without an action. (End of collected note.)
Because of "you shall not." Torat Kohanim, Parashat Kedoshim, chapter 2: "you shall not curse a deaf person." I know only a deaf person; from where do we include every person? Scripture says, "you shall not curse a leader among your people." If so, why state "a deaf person"? Just as a deaf person is alive, excluding the dead who are not alive. So too in Sanhedrin 66a.
(Collected note) Because of "you shall not curse a deaf person." Gemara, Shevuot 36a. (End of collected note.)
(Collected note) Because of "you shall not, etc." Shevuot there, Temurah there, and Torat Kohanim. See Beer HaGolah, seif katan 5, where "it was not stated, etc." is the Rambam's language. So too in the Pesikta cited by the Kesef Mishnah: "you shall not curse a deaf person" includes even a Jew who does not hear, and all the more so one whom you curse in his presence. (End of collected note.)
And if he was a judge. There, "if he was his father, etc."
(Collected note) And if he was, etc. Mekhilta: "you shall not curse elohim." I know only a judge. From where do we know a leader? Scripture says, "and a leader among your people you shall not curse." Could I therefore read only "a leader among your people you shall not curse," which would include both judge and leader? What then does "you shall not curse elohim" teach? To make one liable separately for this and separately for that. From here they said: one can utter one statement and become liable for four violations. A son of a leader who curses his father is liable because of father, because of judge, because of leader, and because of "among your people you shall not curse." Meaning, there the prohibition regarding any person is derived from "among your people," as stated there. (End of collected note.)
