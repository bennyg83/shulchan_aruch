Because of "you shall not curse a deaf person," etc. Sanhedrin 66a derives the warning against cursing one's father from the laws of a judge, a nasi, and a deaf person. The Gemara asks: perhaps a judge and nasi are different because their elevated status causes the special prohibition; the deaf person proves otherwise. Then perhaps the deaf person is different because his lowly condition causes the Torah to protect him, as Rashi explains, so the judge and nasi prove otherwise. The Gemara ultimately derives the prohibition from "you shall not curse judges," using the verse beyond its immediate context. Tosafot write that the prohibition against cursing an ordinary Jew can be derived through a binyan av from father and deaf person, because the objection that both are exceptional no longer applies once the father is included. Rambam, however, writes that the Torah singled out the deaf person to teach that even one who does not hear and is not pained by the curse is nevertheless included. Tur writes similarly. Kesef Mishneh and Bach ask: the Gemara itself says the deaf person's vulnerability makes his case more understandable, not less. Bach answers that the prohibition against cursing an ordinary Jew is first learned through the combined derivation from father and deaf person. Once every Jew is included, the Torah's separate mention of the deaf person teaches the additional point that even where the victim does not hear and suffers no embarrassment, liability remains. That explanation works well for Tur if he follows Tosafot. It is harder for Rambam because in Hilchot Mamrim he writes that the prohibition against cursing one's father and mother is itself learned from the verse concerning the deaf person rather than from "you shall not curse judges." If so, the combined binyan av of father and deaf person cannot serve as the basis for ordinary cursing. There is also an apparent contradiction between Sanhedrin and Shevuot 36, where the Gemara seems simply to derive cursing another person from cursing a deaf person alone. I suggest the following. Tosafot ask why Sanhedrin does not derive cursing one's father from cursing the deaf person together with cursing oneself, since cursing oneself is prohibited from "guard yourself and guard your soul." They answer with difficulty. Rambam may resolve this through another Tosafot question in Shevuot. The Gemara says everyone agrees that cursing oneself is prohibited from "guard your soul," yet Bava Kamma records a Tannaitic dispute whether one may injure oneself, even though bodily injury is also included in "guard your soul." Rambam can understand "everyone agrees" there as referring only to R. Meir and the Sages in the local Mishnah, not literally every Tanna. The Tanna in Bava Kamma who permits self-injury would likewise permit self-cursing. Accordingly, Sanhedrin seeks a derivation valid according to all Tannaim. It therefore cannot rely on self-cursing, because the permissive Tanna would not provide that proof. It instead derives the warning from "you shall not curse judges." But the Shevuot passage discusses the Mishnah according to the Tanna who does prohibit self-cursing. There, one may combine cursing the deaf person with cursing oneself, exactly as Tosafot suggested, and derive the broader prohibition. Rambam rules that self-injury and self-cursing are prohibited. He can therefore follow the Shevuot derivation and write that cursing one's father is learned from the deaf person. Once the ordinary prohibition is derived through the deaf person, the Torah's special mention of a deaf person indeed requires explanation. The answer is Bach's: it teaches that even where the cursed person does not hear and experiences no distress, the prohibition applies. This resolves Rambam as well.
If he was a judge, etc. Rambam, followed by Mechaber, imposes lashes for cursing a judge under the verse "you shall not curse judges." The same verse is also used regarding blasphemy, as Rambam writes in Hilchot Avodah Zarah, yet Rambam does not treat it as a prohibition given merely as a warning for a capital offense. Since the verse also independently prohibits cursing a judge, it remains a lashes-bearing prohibition in that respect. This is comparable to the principle discussed by Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot Shabbat regarding driving an animal. Maharsha in Sanhedrin 66 appears to assume otherwise, but the Maggid Mishneh's rule resolves the matter. Tur does not write that one who curses a judge receives two sets of liability, and Sema infers that Tur may disagree with Rambam. Perhaps Tur holds that because the verse also warns against a capital offense, it cannot generate lashes for cursing a judge. That explanation is difficult, because even where actual lashes are not administered due to the technical rule of a prohibition linked to capital liability, the act still has the halachic severity of a lashes-level prohibition and can affect matters such as disqualification from testimony. The greater severity should not become a leniency. A more compelling explanation is this. The special prohibition against cursing a judge is learned from the word "elohim." In the judicial context, "elohim" means ordained judges, as the Gemara explicitly says. If so, the extra prohibition applies only to a truly ordained and qualified judge. In our time, because of our sins, we have no semukhim. Our judges act as agents of the earlier ordained courts. Therefore, cursing a contemporary judge remains prohibited like cursing any Jew, and certainly carries an added moral gravity because of his office, but the special Torah prohibition of cursing an "elohim" judge does not create a second formal violation. Tur therefore begins broadly by saying it is forbidden to curse a judge, but he does not say that nowadays there are two separate prohibitions. This distinction has practical consequences, including questions of testimony disqualification, as I noted in Urim. I am surprised this point is not stated explicitly by the commentators, for it appears straightforward. There is also a dispute concerning whether lashes require use of the explicit Divine Name. Mechaber follows Rambam that cursing with a Divine appellation can suffice. Raavad, in Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 26, says one is lashed only for cursing with the unique Name, citing the Yerushalmi. Kesef Mishneh assumes Raavad refers to a Yerushalmi passage where the Sages say there are no lashes for cursing and R. Yosi challenges that reasoning from other verbal prohibitions. But there is no indication in that passage of any distinction between the unique Name and appellations. Kesef Mishneh's reading of the Yerushalmi is correct, but it cannot be the Yerushalmi intended by Raavad. Raavad must refer to a different Yerushalmi. At the end of Shevuot HaEdut, concerning the formulation "may God not strike you" and similar expressions, the Bavli discusses whether one infers the positive from the negative. Older textual versions say R. Meir applies that inference in matters of prohibition, while the Sages exempt. Tosafot preserve that reading and explain that the Sages do not necessarily reject inference from negative language. Rather, they hold there are no lashes when the curse uses only a Divine appellation; with the unique Name they would agree. The halachah then follows the Sages against R. Meir. Rashi emends the Bavli differently, so the proof does not emerge there. But the Yerushalmi explicitly states, in the name of R. Yochanan, that this is R. Meir's position based upon inferring a positive from a negative. That supports the older Bavli text. Accordingly, the Yerushalmi shows that the Sages' exemption can indeed rest on the distinction between the unique Name and appellations. That is the Yerushalmi Raavad intended. His position is therefore well grounded.
