Nor for one whom he hates. Responsa Chavot Yair, in the additions at the end of the volume, records an incident in which someone borrowed money from him, claimed to have repaid part of it, and swore falsely. A year later that person came before him for judgment, and he refused to involve himself in the case. He explains that this was not merely because it is permitted to hate a wicked person, for if so the same concern would arise in every case involving a wicked litigant and a righteous judge, which occurs constantly. Rambam, Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 20, cited by the Tur in siman 17 and by Shulchan Aruch there, seif 10, applies to such a person the verse, “You shall not pervert the judgment of your destitute person.” The Torah commands that although one is permitted to hate him, at the time of judgment both litigants must be regarded equally. Where the hatred is not natural but results solely from the command of Hashem, there is no concern that the judge's mind will mislead him and prevent him from seeing the litigant's merit, as can happen with an ordinary enemy. But where the person committed a wicked act against the judge himself or did something that left resentment in the judge's heart, the judge should certainly distance himself from judging him, for there is concern that because of this resentment he will fail to perceive his merit.
