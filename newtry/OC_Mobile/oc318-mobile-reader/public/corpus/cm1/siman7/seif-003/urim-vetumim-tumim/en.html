Only from eighteen years of age. This creates a difficulty. The Mishnah teaches that there are people fit to testify who are not fit to judge, and the Gemara strains to establish the Mishnah according to R. Meir, who disqualifies a person blind in one eye from judging. Why not give a simple example: a male from age thirteen who has produced two hairs is valid to testify, but according to this opinion is unfit to judge until age eighteen? The authorities who hold this opinion, as Tur records, can answer according to Tosafot in Niddah 50b. Tosafot write that the Gemara could have given other examples, but because the opening clause "whoever is fit to judge is fit to testify" already requires attribution to R. Meir, since according to the Sages one blind in both eyes can be fit to judge but unfit to testify, the Gemara also explains the later clause through R. Meir's position concerning one-eyed blindness. But Mechaber accepts as primary law that even according to the Sages one blind in both eyes is disqualified from judging. The foregoing answer therefore no longer resolves the Mishnah according to him. Why not explain it by the person between thirteen and eighteen? It is therefore clear that Tur and Mechaber's statement that one under eighteen "is not fit to judge" means only that initially he should not be appointed to judge. If he did judge, his judgment is valid. We find this with Yoshiyahu himself. He returned money that he had adjudicated from age eight until age eighteen. How could the sages of Israel have allowed him to judge all those years without protest if his judgments were absolutely void? Presumably he was responsive to their instruction. Rather, because the defect does not invalidate after the fact, the sages did not choose to confront a king over it. Likewise, Midrash states that Shlomo's entire life was fifty-two years and that he reigned for forty years. Thus when he began to reign and judge Israel he was twelve. Necessarily age eighteen is not an absolute condition. Shlomo was exceptional because the wisdom of God was within him. One should not ask how Yoshiyahu could judge from age eight or Shlomo from age twelve when, before halachic majority, everyone agrees a minor is disqualified. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 69b write that in the early generations, when people reached reproductive maturity much earlier, halachic majority likewise began earlier, even from age eight. We indeed find in the generations of David and Shlomo unusually early reproduction. The Gemara infers this from Bat Sheva, mother of Shlomo. Achaz likewise fathered Chizkiyahu at age eleven: Achaz died at thirty-six, while Chizkiyahu was twenty-five when he began to reign. Thus in those generations physical and halachic maturity came earlier. There is therefore no difficulty.
Even if he has not produced two hairs. Sema asks: if so, how can the Mishnah say, "Whoever is fit to judge is fit to testify"? Here is a person who has reached the relevant age and is fit to judge even without two hairs, yet below in siman 34 such a person is unfit to testify. Sema answers that both cases still involve the general category of minority: for judgment the disqualification ends at thirteen, while for testimony it continues until two hairs appear. The strain in this answer is evident. Tosafot in Niddah, discussing the opinion that a woman is fit to judge, ask the same type of question: if a woman may judge but may not testify, how can the Mishnah say everyone fit to judge is fit to testify? Tosafot answer that the Mishnah means: every man who belongs to the category of testimony and is fit to judge is fit to testify. Women are outside the category of testimony. The same answer can be given for minors. The Mishnah is speaking about adults who belong to the category of testimony. Minors are outside that category. Were I not afraid to disagree, I would suggest an additional limitation. Tur writes that a minor who has not produced two hairs but is an individual expert may judge alone. I find it difficult to believe that the Yerushalmi ever meant that such a person may judge alone. How can a minor, who is not fully within the category of legal understanding, judge alone? In Gittin we say that although all may write a get, even a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, these are not people of legal cognition. If a minor judge errs in a case where an ordinary judge would have to pay from his own property, how could the minor pay, when even direct damage caused by a minor is exempt? Shlomo, wisest of kings, asked Hashem specifically for an understanding heart in order to judge the people. How can we assume an ordinary minor can do so alone? It therefore appears that the Yerushalmi's statement that a minor can judge means that two adults sit with him. D'oraisa one adult judge is sufficient according to Rambam, and the requirement of three is rabbinic. Chazal could therefore allow a sharp, knowledgeable minor who has reached the relevant age to join two adults. We find a similar conception in the Yerushalmi concerning zimun, where according to one view a minor can join two adults to form a group of three. Even more plausibly, the Yerushalmi may mean that the person has reached the proper age but has not been examined to determine whether he has produced two hairs. In a Torah matter we would not rely upon the presumption of maturity, but where two adult judges are already present and the third position is only rabbinic, we may rely upon the ordinary presumption. Thus the phrase "even if he has not produced two hairs" can mean either that we know he has not, but he merely joins two adults, or that he was not examined. To say that he may sit alone as the sole judge is very difficult. This would also answer Sema's question from "whoever is fit to judge is fit to testify." If the minor is fit to participate only when two adult judges are with him, we find a parallel in testimony: below, siman 35, a minor can sometimes be believed regarding something he saw in childhood when an adult joins with him. Moreover, all of this depends on the view that one judge is d'oraisa sufficient and the third position is only rabbinic. But the Mishnah in Niddah is established according to R. Meir, and R. Meir holds from the hekesh of disputes to negaim that d'oraisa three judges are required, as he says, "If disputes require three, perhaps negaim too should require three." Under his system a minor cannot fill one of the required three positions at all. The Mishnah therefore remains consistent. My view is of course nullified before Tur's explicit words, but another difficulty remains even for Tur. A minor cannot generally be subjected to judicial process, as explained below in siman 96. We have the principle "adorn yourself and then adorn others": one who cannot himself be judged should not judge others. As I wrote at the end of siman 3, where there is no legal possibility of judging a person, he cannot act as judge. If the minor sits only together with two adult judges, the difficulty is reduced, because Tosafot indicate that this principle does not apply in the same way where he is not judging alone. According to Tur, however, who allows him to judge alone, the difficulty remains. We must therefore limit Tur's case to movable property. Once a minor has reached the age of pe'utot, he can be subjected to litigation concerning movables, as explained in siman 96, seif 2. He may therefore judge such matters. With land, however, where his transactions are not legally effective and he cannot be subjected to ordinary adjudication, as explained in siman 235, he cannot judge alone. The principle of "adorn yourself and then adorn others" applies. This distinction also gives another answer to Sema's question. The Mishnah's phrase "fit to judge" can refer to one fully fit as a sole expert judge in all civil matters, both land and movables. A minor lacking two hairs does not possess that full qualification, because he cannot judge land matters alone. In any event, the principal practical point seems clear: regarding land, such a minor certainly may not judge alone.
