One who has drunk wine, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah asks from Midrash Rabbah, Parashat Naso, which says: one who drank a revi'it of wine should not issue a halachic ruling, and one who drank a revi'it should not judge. He suggests that our Gemara disagrees. This requires study, because Tosafot's proof is not sufficiently clear to reject the Midrash. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 42 derive their position from the rule that in capital cases the judges did not drink wine the entire day, based on the verse, "It is not for rulers to drink intoxicating drink", those occupied with the secrets of the world should not become intoxicated. This sounds specific to capital cases rather than ordinary civil cases. But that is not decisive. In a civil case, if the judges postpone final judgment until the next day, they can drink wine afterward because by the time they return to issue judgment the wine will have left their system and they can reconsider the matter with settled judgment. In a capital case, however, even where final judgment is delayed until the next day, the judges may not drink throughout that day. The reason is that they must not divert their minds even temporarily from searching for arguments in the defendant's favor. Wine could cloud their thought and cause them to miss a basis for acquittal. That concern is unique to capital cases. It therefore does not follow that a civil judge may sit and render judgment while wine is still affecting him. Why should civil law be lighter than ordinary halachic ruling? There is no field of Torah more intricate than monetary law. Small distinctions can completely reverse the result, and extraordinary clarity of mind is required. Kenesset HaGedolah implies that the primary proof is from the expression "one who drank should not issue a ruling," suggesting that judging remains permitted. But anyone examining Tosafot sees that this is not itself their proof. Tosafot say only that the statement about issuing rulings does not automatically include judging. That removes a question; it does not affirmatively prove permission. It therefore seems necessary to distinguish between an individual halachic decisor and a panel of judges. A posek may issue a ruling alone, with no one required to review or correct him. It is therefore prohibited for him to rule after drinking. Civil judgment ordinarily requires three judges. It is uncommon that all three will drink enough to become impaired. If one judge has drunk, the other two can remind and correct him. If they see that his mind is not clear because of the amount he drank, they can insist that the court wait before issuing judgment. This also explains the distinction between capital and civil cases. In capital law, even one member of a court of twenty-three may not drink, because every individual judge must continuously search for grounds of acquittal. In civil law, an individual member can be protected by the presence of his colleagues. But an individual expert who judges alone should certainly be prohibited from judging after drinking, exactly like a sage issuing a halachic ruling. This also explains the Midrash Rabbah, which forbids both ruling and judging. I later found that Bach likewise rules stringently on the basis of this Midrash, and R. Yonah writes similarly in Iggeret HaTeshuvah, though he does not distinguish between a single judge and a panel. In practice, one who has drunk enough to become intoxicated should certainly refrain from judgment until the wine has left him.
If they saw it at night. In Urim I cited Bach, who writes that the same rule applies if the judges saw the event on Shabbat or Yom Tov. Since they cannot immediately judge because of the rabbinic prohibition against judging on those days, a witness cannot become a judge. Taz questions this. Since if they nevertheless judged on Shabbat their judgment would be valid after the fact, the principle "hearing should not be greater than seeing" should still apply. That objection can be answered. According to Rashbam and those who follow him, even commencement of judgment at night is valid after the fact if they mistakenly judged, as Rama rules above in siman 5. Yet the Gemara plainly says that if the judges saw the event at night, we do not invoke "hearing should not be greater than seeing." We must therefore say that once they are prohibited from judging initially at the time of seeing, direct seeing cannot become judicial seeing. The same would seem to apply to Shabbat and Yom Tov. Taz has another, stronger objection. Nighttime is disqualified by Torah law, whereas the prohibition against judging on Shabbat and Yom Tov is only rabbinic. In rabbinic testimony, such as certification of documents and similar matters, we allow a witness to become a judge because Chazal did not make their own enactments fully equivalent to Torah law. Perhaps the same is true here: even if the testimony itself concerns a Torah matter, where the only obstacle to judging immediately is rabbinic, Chazal did not impose the full rule that a witness cannot become a judge. D'oraisa the judges were fit to judge at the moment of seeing. I have heard an interpretation of Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Makkot which, if correct, would provide proof for Bach. The Mishnah there says R. Akiva and R. Tarfon declared that had they sat on a Sanhedrin, no person would ever have been executed. The Gemara explains that in a murder case they would ask the witnesses: did you see that the victim was intact before the killing, or perhaps he was already a tereifah? Perhaps there was already a hole where the sword entered. The Gemara then asks what they would do in a case of forbidden relations and answers that they would require extraordinarily direct visual confirmation. Tosafot ask: what would they do with idolatry or desecration of Shabbat? They give two answers. First, such cases were not as common as murder and forbidden relations. Second, they could ask whether the Shabbat desecrator was a tereifah, because if he was a tereifah the testimony would be testimony incapable of being refuted by hazamah. Maharsha asks: if that is the answer, why not say the same regarding murder and forbidden relations? He also notes that Tosafot's second answer says specifically "the Shabbat desecrator" and omits the idolater, even though the question had included both. It was suggested that the reason is this. We rule that a tereifah who kills in the presence of beit din is executed because "you shall remove the evil from your midst." Sanhedrin 78 says so. Tosafot explain there that the judges must have seen the killing by day, for if they saw it at night a witness cannot become a judge. Accordingly, in idolatry, murder, and forbidden relations, Tosafot cannot simply ask whether the offender was a tereifah. If the offense occurred in front of beit din during daytime, then even a tereifah could be executed without the ordinary problem of testimony that cannot be refuted. Therefore R. Akiva and R. Tarfon could not guarantee that nobody would ever be executed merely by introducing the tereifah doubt, and Tosafot needed another answer, that such a case is exceedingly uncommon. But Shabbat desecration is different. If the judges personally saw the desecration, they necessarily saw it on Shabbat. If seeing on Shabbat has the same effect as seeing at night, as Bach says, they themselves cannot judge. They must testify before another court. Their testimony then has the ordinary requirement of being capable of hazamah, and the tereifah doubt prevents execution. This would explain why Tosafot's second answer specifies only "the Shabbat desecrator." It would also answer Maharsha's first question. The tereifah answer cannot eliminate murder or forbidden relations because those offenses could theoretically occur before beit din at a time when the judges can immediately act as judges. Shabbat desecration necessarily occurs at a time when the court is rabbinically barred from judging. If this explanation were correct, Bach's position would be almost compelled. However, this reading is quite strained. First, even if an offense seen directly by a Sanhedrin can sometimes result in execution, such an occurrence is itself extraordinarily rare. What person will deliberately commit a capital offense, after warning, in the presence of a full court of twenty-three? Tosafot could still say "no one would ever be executed" in the ordinary practical sense because that circumstance might not occur once in a jubilee. Indeed one could argue the reverse against Maharsha's question. Tosafot's concern that following a minority possibility cannot uproot explicit Torah laws can be resolved by saying the Torah's capital laws remain applicable where the offense is seen directly by beit din. But that still leaves the practical claim that executions would virtually disappear because such cases are extraordinarily rare. Second, and more importantly, the entire Mishnah's statement is R. Akiva's. R. Akiva himself holds that where beit din sees one person kill another, the judges cannot judge the case even if they saw it by day, because capital judgment requires the possibility of searching for merit, from "the congregation shall judge and the congregation shall save." According to him, direct seeing by the judges does not solve the problem. It therefore seems better to explain Tosafot in an entirely different manner. In Chullin 11b the Gemara proves that we follow the majority. Otherwise, perhaps the person against whom witnesses testified was a tereifah, making the testimony incapable of hazamah. Tosafot ask: why not imprison him for twelve months and see whether he survives? They answer that perhaps the sugya follows the opinion that a tereifah can live longer than twelve months. Even according to the opinion that a tereifah can live, however, the Gemara in Chullin 57 says that a tereifah does not reproduce. Tosafot in Avodah Zarah 6 write likewise. Accordingly, if a female Shabbat desecrator later gives birth, it becomes clear that she was not a tereifah at the time of the offense. The testimony would then be capable of hazamah. This matters because Tosafot had earlier established that although R. Akiva is concerned for minority possibilities, where taking the minority seriously would make a Torah law impossible to fulfill, even he must follow the majority. Tosafot illustrate that "possible" remains possible even through a remote scenario, such as where the membrane of the brain was exposed but intact and the murderer then pierced it. Now their second answer concerning Shabbat is clear. They could ask whether the Shabbat desecrator was a tereifah, creating testimony incapable of hazamah. One cannot object that this would uproot the Torah's explicit command that a Shabbat desecrator be put to death, because the command remains possible in the case of a woman who desecrates Shabbat and subsequently becomes pregnant and gives birth. Birth proves she was not a tereifah. True, this is extremely rare in practice. If a woman knows that pregnancy and childbirth will establish the fact necessary for her execution, why would she voluntarily place herself in that situation? But practical rarity is irrelevant to the textual question whether the Torah's law has any possible application. Since a possible case exists, R. Akiva can continue to consider the minority concern in all other cases. This explanation works specifically for Shabbat desecration because the Torah's command is stated generically and can be fulfilled through a woman. It does not work for idolatry in the same way where the relevant verse speaks explicitly of "a man or woman" and establishes separate application, nor for murder where Scripture explicitly says "a man who murders," and certainly not for male forbidden relations where Torah speaks expressly of the male offender. Regarding a male, childbirth cannot provide the necessary proof. If we treated the possibility of tereifah as sufficient there, we would effectively uproot an explicit Torah law. Therefore in those cases we must follow the majority and cannot use the tereifah doubt as Tosafot's universal device. This also explains Tosafot's exact wording. In the second answer they say specifically "the Shabbat desecrator" and omit the idolater because only Shabbat permits the foregoing resolution. Accordingly, Tosafot in Makkot provide no proof at all for Bach's extension of nighttime seeing to Shabbat and Yom Tov. Since the rule is rabbinic and I have not found an explicit earlier authority applying "a witness cannot become a judge" merely because judging was rabbinically prohibited at the time of seeing, Taz's objection remains persuasive. We should not create a new disqualification from our own reasoning where the earlier poskim did not state it. Bach also writes that witnesses to kiddushin and divorce involve matters with potential capital consequences. Certainly where a person killed another in the judge's presence, in a case that can be adjudicated nowadays under the extraordinary jurisdiction discussed in siman 2, Bach holds that the observer cannot serve as judge even if he does not testify, and cannot even receive other witnesses and judge on their testimony. This appears to follow R. Akiva's position that in capital matters one who personally saw the event cannot become a judge at all. I am surprised that Bach rules this way against Rambam where no explicit early authority is found rejecting Rambam. Tosafot and Rosh cite R. Akiva's view in order to explain the sugya and clarify the positions. They do not state that the halachah follows him. Rambam, in Hilchot Edut chapter 5, rules explicitly like R. Tarfon that one who saw the event can become a judge even in a capital case. Kesef Mishneh labors to explain why Rambam rules like R. Tarfon rather than R. Akiva, but the reason is actually straightforward. Rava states without qualification in Sanhedrin 78 that a tereifah who kills in the presence of beit din is liable. Tosafot explain that the court saw the killing by day, so that direct seeing is no less than hearing. This ruling accords with R. Tarfon. According to R. Akiva, even if they saw it by day they could not judge and would have to testify before a different beit din. Rambam therefore correctly follows Rava, the later authority, and rules like R. Tarfon. Bach's conclusion on this point should therefore not be maintained.
