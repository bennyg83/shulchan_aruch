Some say that one who has drunk wine, etc. The prohibition applies to ritual rulings of permitted and forbidden matters, but monetary judgment is permitted. With our weaker wines there is room for leniency even in ritual ruling unless he drank enough to create real intoxication. Sema. In Tumim I argue for stringency even in monetary judgment where significant drinking occurred, especially if one judges alone.
A witness cannot become a judge. Sema explains that testimony must be capable of being disproved through hazamah, and judges cannot accept discrediting testimony against themselves. For this reason, when they personally saw the event by day they may judge, because they immediately assumed the role of judges and no formal testimony rests upon them; the requirement of discreditable testimony therefore does not arise. That explanation cannot account for the opinion that if they were formally designated as witnesses, they cannot later judge even based upon testimony of others. In that case they could accept discrediting testimony against those other witnesses. One cannot answer that because they themselves saw the event they already know the testimony is true and therefore would not accept its disproof, for that would apply even without formal designation as witnesses, as Tosafot itself notes. It is therefore clear that according to this opinion the reason is not the technical rule of discreditable testimony. Rather, the verse "a witness shall not answer concerning a life" teaches that one who bears the status of witness may not act as judge. In monetary law even a ruling in favor of one party is necessarily adverse to the other. Ramban writes similarly in his commentary to Bava Batra. Therefore, so long as the person retains the formal status of witness, he cannot become judge. Formal designation as witness preserves that status.

But if they saw it at night, etc. Since at the moment of seeing they could neither judge nor formally receive testimony, the principle that seeing should not be weaker than hearing does not apply. They cannot judge later merely on the basis of their own nighttime observation. Sema. Bach infers from Tur's phrase "at a time fit for judgment," rather than simply "by day," that the same applies if they saw the event on Shabbat or Yom Tov, when judgment may not be held. In Tumim I explain that this inference is uncertain and Bach's extension requires further study.
But in a rabbinic matter a witness may become a judge. See below, siman 46:12, where the many details of this rule are discussed. Bach writes that although capital law is not ordinarily judged today, a witness to murder cannot become a judge in an extraordinary proceeding to punish the murderer under siman 2. Likewise, witnesses to kiddushin or divorce, whose testimony has gravity similar to capital matters, cannot become judges even merely to hear other testimony. In Tumim I argue that this distinction is not correct and that the operative rules do not differ in this way.
