(Anthology) If he was appointed, and some say, etc. The first view is that of the Rashbam in Bava Batra. He derives it from the first chapter of Makkot, where the Gemara asks what the judges say to the witnesses. We rule like the Ran, who rules like Rabbi. The Rashbam holds that even if a witness was not formally designated, once he intended his observation as testimony, he may no longer serve as a judge. He also proves this from the statement in Bava Batra that three who entered to visit a sick person may act as judges, implying that this is so only when they entered to visit. Tosafot and the other authorities disagree. According to the Rashbam, Rabbi Yehudah's position in Makkot would make it impossible for a witness ever to become a judge, whereas in Rosh Hashanah the Gemara asks simply, 'Should hearing not be as effective as seeing?' Moreover, Rav Yehudah rules in Makkot like Rabbi Yose, yet in Bava Batra he says that three who entered to visit may act as judges. Tosafot further write that even in Makkot the matter does not depend on their observation alone, but also on their having come to beit din to testify. See Tosafot in Makkot, s.v. Shmuel. Furthermore, the reason a witness cannot become a judge is either that testimony must be capable of refutation through conspiring witnesses, or because of the verse, 'The two men shall stand,' as the Rashbam writes there. Neither reason applies here merely because they were appointed. See Tosafot in Bava Batra and Sanhedrin 9a. The Ran rules like Tosafot where they merely intended their observation as testimony, and like the Rashbam where they were formally appointed. See the Beit Yosef. This is the first view, while the final view follows Tosafot.
