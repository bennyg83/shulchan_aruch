A witness may not become a judge. Tosafot gives two reasons for this rule. One is that the verse requires, 'the two men shall stand,' so one cannot serve simultaneously as witness and judge. The second is that testimony must be capable of refutation through hazamah, and judges will not accept a hazamah that would condemn themselves. Tosafot asks in chapter HaChovel that, if so, witnesses should also be disqualified when related to the judges, because the judges would not accept hazamah against their relatives. Yet we allow such witnesses because they can be refuted before another beit din. For the same reason, why should a witness not be able to sit as judge, with his hazamah handled by another beit din? The first reason is also difficult. If he already testified before another beit din and thereby fulfilled the requirement of 'the two men shall stand,' why may he not later serve as judge? The Gemara and poskim imply that a witness who testified may not become judge, and even a witness signed on a document, whose testimony is regarded as already investigated, may not act as judge. According to the hazamah rationale there is likewise a difficulty: once he testified before beit din specifying the day and hour, why may he not afterward judge, since the original beit din before which he testified can receive the witnesses who refute him? It therefore appears that the hazamah rationale is fundamental, and Tosafot's question from witnesses related to judges can be answered. Even another beit din cannot effectively carry out hazamah when the witnesses themselves later serve as the judges who conclude the case. We rule that false witnesses are not executed, do not pay, and are not lashed unless they are refuted after the judgment has been concluded on the basis of their testimony. If they are refuted before final judgment, they are not punished. We also rule that an eid zomem is disqualified retroactively. Therefore, if these very witnesses served as judges and were later refuted, they would be disqualified retroactively from the time of their testimony. The judgment would then have been concluded by invalid judges, which is equivalent to there having been no valid final judgment, and the punishment of hazamah could never take effect. This is why even witnesses signed on a document, although their testimony is treated as already investigated, cannot become judges. Hazamah could not be implemented, since their retroactive disqualification would invalidate the final judgment. Even according to the opinion that an eid zomem is disqualified only from the time of the hazamah, with respect to that particular testimony he is invalid according to all, as stated in Bava Batra. I later found this reasoning stated briefly by the Sar of Coucy in the Mordechai, and I thank God that I arrived at his view.
The three judges. Specifically three, but not two, as stated in the Gemara and in Shulchan Aruch siman 253. See Rashba's novellae to chapter HaChovel: although an expert judge may adjudicate alone, he cannot judge on the basis of what he himself saw, because as a witness he has no greater credibility than an ordinary person and the litigant can deny his observation. Therefore he can judge only on the basis of two witnesses, for a matter is established by two witnesses. If there is one other person with him, it appears the expert may judge on the basis of what the two of them saw. One might object that reception of testimony always requires three judges, even if they are experts, as the Shach writes in siman 46, seif katan 8. If so, the principle that hearing is not greater than seeing should not help when only two saw the event, since even hearing testimony before two is ineffective. This is no objection. The reason reception of testimony requires three, even experts, is that anything less than three is analogous to testimony from one witness through another. When the judges themselves saw the event, however, the problem of secondhand testimony does not exist. An expert can therefore judge with one other person on the basis of what the two themselves saw.
If they saw it by day. The Bach writes that if they saw the event on Shabbos or Yom Tov they cannot afterward become judges, even though the prohibition on judging then is only rabbinic. The Taz disagrees and brings proof from the principle that in rabbinic matters a witness may become a judge. The Shach leaves this requiring study. In my opinion the Bach's position has support from Bava Batra, chapter Yesh Nochalin: three who entered to visit a sick person by day may, if they wish, act as judges, but two may not. Rambam likewise writes in chapter 3 of Hilchot Sanhedrin that two who entered to visit a sick person and heard his instructions may write them but cannot act as judges, whereas three may act as judges. The reason that two cannot is that at the moment of observation they were not fit to constitute a beit din. According to Rambam, by Torah law one judge is sufficient for monetary cases. Thus the requirement of three is only rabbinic. Why, then, can two not become judges based on what they saw, since in rabbinic matters a witness may become a judge? We must say that the rule permitting a witness to become a judge in rabbinic matters applies only where the testimony itself is required solely by rabbinic law, such as authentication of documents. But where the testimony is required by Torah law and they observed it at a time when, even rabbinically, they could not act as judges, they acquired the status of witnesses, and a witness does not become a judge. Upon closer analysis, however, this depends on major authorities. Rashbam in chapter Yesh Nochalin holds that if testimony was accepted at night, it is valid after the fact. Accordingly, he holds that those who saw at night may afterward judge on the basis of what they saw, because hearing the testimony would have been valid after the fact, and seeing should be no worse. Tosafot and the Rosh infer from this that Rashbam must hold testimony at night valid after the fact, while they themselves hold it invalid even after the fact. Thus according to Rashbam, the rule that seeing is equivalent to hearing works only if they were fit to act as judges at least after the fact. If they were disqualified initially, they take on the status of witnesses rather than judges. According to Tosafot and the Rosh, however, if hearing at night were valid after the fact then seeing at night would also permit them to judge later, all the more so on Shabbos and Yom Tov, where the prohibition is rabbinic and a judgment rendered then is valid after the fact. Since we follow Tosafot and the Rosh that receiving testimony at night is invalid even after the fact, as stated in siman 28, and since the Shabbos and Yom Tov restriction is only rabbinic, there is room for leniency. They may judge afterward based on what they saw on Shabbos or Yom Tov, and this is the principal ruling.
