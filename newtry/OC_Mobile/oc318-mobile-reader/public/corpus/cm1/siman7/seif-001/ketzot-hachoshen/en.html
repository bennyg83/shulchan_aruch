And a convert may judge another convert. The Sema cites Darkei Moshe, who writes in the name of the Mordechai, chapter Mitzvat Chalitzah, that this applies specifically without coercion. The Sema says he did not find this implication in the Mordechai there. Yet Hagahot Mordechai to chapter Mitzvat Chalitzah states explicitly that a convert may judge another convert specifically without coercion, and this is what Darkei Moshe intended. As to the underlying law, Tosafot and the Rosh appear to hold the opposite distinction: the limitation applies specifically where there is coercion, but without coercion even a convert may judge an Israelite, for the verse requiring 'you shall surely appoint' applies only to compulsory authority. There is support for the Mordechai's approach from Yevamot 102a, where Rava says that a convert may judge another convert by Torah law, as it says, 'You shall surely set a king over yourself, from among your brethren.' Only over yourself do we require 'from among your brethren,' but a convert may judge another convert. If his mother is Jewish he may judge even an Israelite, while for chalitzah both his father and mother must be Jewish. This implies that one whose mother is Jewish may judge an Israelite by Torah law, for Rava introduced the matter by saying he can judge by Torah law and derives it from verses. How, however, can there be such a Torah-law judgment? Rava holds in the first chapter of Sanhedrin that by Torah law semichah is required even for admissions and loans. For capital cases one certainly requires that both parents be Jewish, and Rambam explains in chapter 4 of Hilchot Sanhedrin that one cannot receive semichah unless he is fit for all matters. A convert therefore cannot be a musmach. Even when judging another convert, although the verse 'from among your brethren' does not disqualify him, the requirement of semichah makes no distinction between a native-born Jew and a convert. We must therefore say that Rava speaks where there is no coercion. In that setting semichah is not required by Torah law. Nevertheless, he may not judge an Israelite because the verse says, 'from among your brethren you shall set over yourself a king,' and Rashi explains that this includes a judge. Tosafot and the Rosh, who permit without coercion, would need to explain why the verse still prohibits this. Perhaps the prohibition resembles the rule that one who is neither learned nor capable of reasoning may not judge even when accepted by the parties, whether or not he errs, as Baal HaMaor writes in Bava Metzia regarding discretionary judgment, calling such a judge an asherah. Several disqualifications remain even after acceptance, and here too the Torah prohibited an Israelite from being judged by a convert who is not included in 'your brethren.'
