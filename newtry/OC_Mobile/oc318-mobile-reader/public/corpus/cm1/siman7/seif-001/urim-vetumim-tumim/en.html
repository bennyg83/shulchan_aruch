Kenesset HaGedolah discusses at length how Shemaya and Avtalyon could have served as Nasi and Av Beit Din if, according to Rambam's introduction to Mishneh Torah, they were full converts. Eduyot likewise implies that one was Nasi and the other Av Beit Din. He concludes that the community accepted them upon itself because of their extraordinary stature, and that where the litigants accept such a judge, he may judge. According to this, one must say that acceptance by the recognized leaders of the generation can count as acceptance by the public even if the numerical majority of all Jews did not explicitly agree. Surely it is impossible to suppose that a majority of Jews in every place in Eretz Yisrael and the Diaspora formally accepted Shemaya and Avtalyon. Rather, acceptance by the Sanhedrin, leading sages, and recognized authorities of the generation was treated as acceptance by all Israel. I am nevertheless hesitant. Acceptance unquestionably works in monetary cases, because a person can waive his money or give it to whomever he wishes. He can say, "I accept whatever ruling you issue, whether to pay, forgive, or otherwise." Capital cases are different. A person has no authority to say, "If this disqualified judge sentences me to death, let them kill me." One cannot validate a relative or otherwise disqualified judge for capital punishment by private consent. The Great Beit Din in Jerusalem had authority over capital cases, the rebellious elder, and similar matters. It did not function merely as a civil tribunal. How then could people descended from converts stand at the head of that court? Two answers are possible. First, a king is different. A king has power to impose death and punishment for the preservation of public order, as Rambam explains in Hilchot Rotzeach. If the king sees a person of exceptional Torah wisdom and judgment, he can appoint him as chief judge even in matters involving severe penalties, as part of the king's governing authority. We similarly find that the Exilarch could confer authority even upon someone who was not fully learned or expert, by virtue of governmental power, as Rivash explains and as discussed above in seif 3. Accordingly, Shemaya and Avtalyon may have been appointed through royal authority. In their period there were righteous Hasmonean kings, and they could adjudicate coercively with the king's backing. Alternatively, perhaps they were never appointed to adjudicate capital cases at all. They served as Nasi and Av Beit Din in the sense of being the supreme transmitters of received Torah and the principal authorities for determining halachah in the Oral Torah, because of their extraordinary wisdom, understanding, and fear of Heaven. Their titles would then refer to leadership in Torah and received tradition, not to serving as actual judges in capital cases. The matter requires further study.
