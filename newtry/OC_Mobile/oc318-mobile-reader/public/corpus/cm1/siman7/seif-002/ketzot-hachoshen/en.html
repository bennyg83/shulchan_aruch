But one who is blind. Nimukei Yosef writes in Sanhedrin that one who is blind in one eye can judge, while one blind in both eyes is disqualified because he cannot testify, and anyone unfit to testify is unfit to judge. He brings proof from the laws of chalitzah, where the verse "before the eyes of the elders" is used to exclude blind judges and thereby proves that ordinary laymen can serve. His reasoning requires analysis. In the Gemara, one opinion uses "before the eyes" to require the judges actually to see the spittle leave the woman's mouth. Tosafot concludes that one blind in one eye is fit for chalitzah because one functioning eye suffices to see the spittle. This implies that according to the opinion deriving a disqualification of blind judges from the verse, even blindness in one eye may disqualify, just as in the laws of leprosy "all the sight of the priest's eyes" requires both eyes. If so, Nimukei Yosef's proof does not work. Perhaps chalitzah does not require ordained judges, but still requires experts, just as monetary law might. Moreover, why did the Gemara need a separate derivation if blindness already disqualifies from testimony? It appears that according to the opinion that monetary law by Torah law requires ordained experts, those experts must indeed be physically fit for all areas of adjudication. Rambam writes that ordination is granted only to one fit for every relevant matter, and one blind in one eye cannot be ordained because he is not fit for all matters. Thus either monetary law requires ordination, in which case physical completeness is independently required, or admissions and loans do not require ordination, in which case they also do not require formal experts, only learned judges. The proof from chalitzah is then directed specifically to the absence of ordination. This also explains why chalitzah judges are called laymen even though they must know the laws of chalitzah. On this basis one could even argue that a person blind in both eyes may judge ordinary monetary cases today, since no one today is ordained and our courts operate by rabbinic agency. This approach also helps resolve Tosafot's question in Sanhedrin concerning the comparison between monetary disputes and leprosy. Rabbi Meir compares disputes to leprosy to require daytime proceedings and sighted judges. Yet if monetary adjudication already required ordained judges, blindness would be disqualifying independently. The principal comparison can be understood as directed to the law of daytime proceedings, and it incidentally carries the blindness rule into later rabbinic courts that no longer possess ordination. The Mishnah that permits completion at night therefore rejects the comparison in its entirety. This is why Rabbi Yochanan could allow a blind neighbor to judge and rely upon the anonymous Mishnah permitting completion at night. If the comparison were accepted even partially, blindness could remain disqualifying. The remaining objections can be resolved similarly. Rambam's requirement that an appointee be fit for all matters concerns bodily disqualifications such as blindness, not temporary legal disqualifications arising from sin, which can be cured by repentance, nor lack of expertise in a specialty, which can be learned. It may also refer principally to the time of ordination. Nevertheless, blindness arising after ordination remains a bodily blemish that can disqualify because members of the Sanhedrin must remain free of bodily blemish.
