But one blind in both eyes is disqualified. See Sema, whose reasoning implies that the judgment is valid after the fact and that a judge who was sighted at the beginning but became blind before final judgment may complete it. Nimukei Yosef, however, appears to imply that blindness is disqualifying even after the fact, otherwise the verse 'before the eyes' in chalitzah would still be needed to invalidate it after the fact. Yet his words are difficult, because the Gemara derives the disqualification of blind judges in monetary law from 'you are entirely beautiful,' and because 'before the eyes' is also needed for one blind in one eye, who remains valid in monetary law. Ketzot HaChoshen already asks this. In my view Nimukei Yosef means as follows. If chalitzah required expert judges because it is analogous to monetary adjudication, then the verse excluding blindness could not merely be excluding one blind in one eye, for nighttime chalitzah is valid after the fact and such partial blindness is no worse than nighttime. The verse must therefore exclude one blind in both eyes. If monetary judges themselves could be blind in both eyes, then even this verse would be unnecessary, because chalitzah would not inherit such a disqualification from monetary law. The Gemara's inference is thus that full blindness is already invalid in monetary law, and the special chalitzah verse shows that chalitzah does not simply follow all the monetary-law qualifications. This explains Nimukei Yosef without making his words so difficult.
