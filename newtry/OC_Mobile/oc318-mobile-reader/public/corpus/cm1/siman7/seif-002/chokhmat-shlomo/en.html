Seif 9. First one must clarify several expressions in the Beit Yosef. He writes that the wording “he should not judge him” indicates the Rambam’s view. This requires study, for the Tur likewise writes that if he is not such an extreme friend or enemy he should not judge him initially. What proof, then, is there from the phrase “he should not judge” to the Rambam? The Beit Yosef also writes that the Rambam disputes the Tur in two respects, or at least in one. At first glance this is difficult to understand. If the Rambam’s entire discussion concerns only the initial propriety of judging, then he disagrees with the Tur only in the case of an actual friend or enemy with whom he has not spoken for three days out of hatred: according to the Tur the judgment is invalid after the fact, while according to the Rambam it is always valid. But with one who is not literally a close friend or enemy, both agree that the judgment is valid after the fact. If, instead, the Rambam is not speaking at all about validity after the fact, but only distinguishes for the initial appointment between a true friend or enemy and a lesser relationship, then perhaps there is no dispute at all. How, then, can the Beit Yosef say that they disagree in two matters, or at least in one? The explanation appears from the Beit Yosef below, siman 9, where, concerning bribery, he writes that from the Rambam it appears that the judge is legally disqualified, whereas the Tur in siman 7 also writes that one should not judge a person whom he loves. The Beit Yosef must therefore understand the Tur’s language there as describing only a pious practice by which the judge should refrain voluntarily, not an actual legal disqualification that enables a litigant to remove him. This is supported by the Tur’s earlier statement that if a litigant seeks to disqualify the judge, he must bring witnesses that the judge and the litigant have not spoken for three days because of enmity. If every lesser hatred also caused an initial legal disqualification, why would such proof be necessary? It follows that the Tur’s later statement, that the judge should initially refrain from judging one whom he dislikes, speaks only of a pious practice. If the judge nevertheless wishes to adjudicate, the litigant cannot disqualify him. To disqualify him at the litigant’s request requires proof of the more serious hatred. The Beit Yosef therefore correctly says that the Rambam differs in two matters: he holds that even where the judge had not spoken with the litigant for three days because of enmity, the judgment is valid after the fact, and he also holds that even a lesser friend or enemy is legally disqualified initially and the litigant may object. Even if one interprets the Rambam as not addressing the after-the-fact case, there remains at least this second disagreement. This, in my view, is the correct meaning of the Beit Yosef. See the Shach, siman 7, seif 5, who writes that even if a judge witnessed the matter on Shabbat or Yom Tov he does not thereby become an ordinary witness and may still judge. The Shach leaves the matter requiring study. At first glance this issue might depend on the dispute of Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah in the second chapter of Sukkah concerning a sukkah erected atop a camel or a tree. Rabbi Meir follows Torah law: since by Torah law the sukkah is valid, it is considered fit for all seven days. Rabbi Yehudah follows the rabbinic restriction. Most authorities rule like Rabbi Meir, that we follow Torah law for this purpose. Elsewhere I explained that although with respect to an oath of testimony we follow the rabbinic law, for example regarding a gambler, this is because liability for an oath of testimony depends on whether the witnesses can actually be used to extract money, and rabbinically their testimony cannot. But the sukkah issue depends merely on whether it is inherently fit for seven days, so the Torah status governs. The same reasoning applies here. Whether a person has the status of a witness or judge should follow the Torah law. Since by Torah law the time was fit for adjudication, the person who saw the matter then did not acquire the status of a witness but of a judge. We therefore follow Torah law. This supports the Shach. Even according to authorities who follow rabbinic law in the sukkah case, the disqualification would at most be rabbinic, and it is certainly appropriate here to rely upon the many authorities who rule otherwise, especially since this is the accepted ruling in Orach Chaim regarding sukkah. I cannot presently elaborate further. There, Seif 9. “All who are disqualified from testimony because of relationship or sin.” It appears to me that this is not necessarily exhaustive. A mute, who according to all opinions is disqualified from testimony when his testimony is only in writing, as discussed below in siman 28, should likewise be disqualified from serving as a judge, even if he can convey his opinion in writing. Witnesses and judges are compared, so a defect that disqualifies testimony also disqualifies judgment. Moreover, Scripture says, “By their mouth shall every dispute and every affliction be decided.” Just as with witnesses we expound “by their mouth and not from their writing,” so too for judges the judgment must be capable of being completed orally, not merely in writing. Therefore a mute is disqualified according to all views. Even judges who are capable of speech but issue their final ruling only in writing would, according to those opinions that reject written testimony even from witnesses capable of speaking, not have completed the judgment through that writing where formal completion of judgment is required. The authority of beit din to write certain documents without the borrower’s instruction rests on a different principle, namely the power of beit din over property, and does not prove that written delivery itself constitutes oral adjudication. Thus, wherever actual completion of judgment is required, a written ruling alone is insufficient according to those opinions, and a mute is disqualified according to all. This appears correct to me.
