The Sema further writes that the Levush derives the disqualification of a person blind in both eyes from the analogy between civil judgments and the examination of tzaraat, and criticizes him for “making the taught depend upon the untaught.” Responsa Panim Meirot, part III, siman 39, writes that the Sema's objection is unwarranted, for the Levush's explanation is well founded according to the Rif. On the Rif's approach to one blind in one eye, it is clear that even the Sages accept the analogy, and that a person blind in both eyes is disqualified because legal disputes are compared to tzaraat examinations; the difficulty concerns only one blind in a single eye. Thus the Levush made the taught depend upon that which is itself taught. Urim VeTumim similarly strengthens this from Ramban in Milchamot, concluding that no authority supports the Sema except the Baal HaMaor, who holds that the analogy is not made and nevertheless agrees that one blind in both eyes is disqualified. The Sema must therefore understand the disqualification as analogous to nighttime, but apart from this no support for his view is found among the commentators, and the Levush's words are correct. Urim VeTumim further writes that there is a practical difference between the Sema's reason and that of the Levush where the judge could see at the beginning of the case and became blind before the final judgment. According to the Sema, he may complete the case, just as final judgment may be rendered at night. According to the Levush, he is disqualified even for the final judgment by Scriptural decree. Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, siman 80, discusses an actual case in which two litigants appeared before three Torah scholars, presented their claims, and testimony was taken. Before final judgment, one of the judges became blind in both eyes when a firearm discharged and injured his eyes, and one litigant then objected to continuing before him. He discusses the matter at length and concludes that there are three views: according to Rambam he is disqualified even for final judgment; according to Tosafot, Mordechai, and Semag he is fit even for the beginning of the case; according to the Rif he is fit for final judgment but not for beginning a case. The Tur and Beit Yosef do not clearly determine which view they follow. Practically, he rules like the Rif as the intermediate view, and therefore the judge in that case should not be disqualified from completing the case. Moreover, the Bach writes that after the fact a person blind in both eyes is valid, relying upon Tosafot, Mordechai, and Semag. The present case is almost an after-the-fact situation, since the litigants already presented their claims and all testimony was received. Further, since there were three judges, one may also combine the view of Behag and Tosafot, who rule like Shmuel. He therefore concludes that this Torah scholar, whose blindness was brought about by Providence, should not be humiliated, and if the second litigant refuses, he may be compelled to proceed. However, the principal foundation of that responsum is its assumption that according to the Rif the judge remains valid for final judgment. Panim Meirot and Urim VeTumim cited above indicate otherwise. Netivot HaMishpat also writes briefly that even if he became blind only at the time of final judgment he is disqualified, and that the disqualification applies even after the fact.
