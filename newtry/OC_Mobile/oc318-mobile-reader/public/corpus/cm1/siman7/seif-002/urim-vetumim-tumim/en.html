One who is blind in both eyes is disqualified. Sema explains that this is because such a person is comparable to nighttime. Ir Shushan explains that it is because disputes are compared to negaim. He is criticized for inaccuracy, on the ground that this hekesh is the individual view of R. Meir. I am surprised, however, because Sema himself cites Rif's explanation that one who is blind in one eye is valid because, since final judgment may be rendered at night, a blind person by day is certainly valid. Baal HaMaor asks why such reasoning is needed at all if we do not rule like R. Meir's comparison of disputes to negaim. Ramban answers in Milchamot that even the Sages accept the comparison; nighttime final judgment is nevertheless valid because "they shall judge the people at all times" expressly excludes it from the comparison, just as according to R. Meir that verse excludes judgment on a cloudy day. But blindness was not excluded from the hekesh. Rif therefore needed the argument from final judgment at night to show that one-eyed blindness too falls within the broadening of "they shall judge the people at all times." It follows that regarding blindness in both eyes we indeed return to the hekesh, exactly as Ir Shushan wrote. Ramban infers this also from Rashi, and Hagahot Maimoniyot takes the same position, as I wrote above in siman 5. There he explains Rambam's ruling that one blind in both eyes is disqualified as following R. Meir, meaning that on this point the halachah follows the hekesh because the Sages did not disagree with R. Meir about it. Even according to Rambam, who holds that the hekesh of disputes to negaim applies only to commencement of judgment with regard to day and night, that limitation exists because Scripture separately broadens judgment through "they shall judge." With respect to blindness, however, the comparison remains complete unless Scripture broadens it. This is more convincing than Sema's analogy of blindness to nighttime. The nighttime rule is a specific Scriptural decree, and commencement at night remains invalid even when powerful lamps illuminate the room. What conceptual relationship does that have to blindness? Tosafot therefore write that even though commencement at night is invalid, blindness need not be invalid unless the hekesh teaches it. I have found no substantial support for Sema's analogy except Baal HaMaor, who rejects the hekesh and therefore must explain the disqualification of total blindness in another manner. Accordingly, Ir Shushan's explanation is correct. See Urim for the practical difference between the two rationales where a judge became blind after commencement of the case but before final judgment.
