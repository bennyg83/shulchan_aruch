Nevertheless, if they judged, their judgment is valid. The author of Chavot Yair, responsa 3 and 4, asks: according to Sema and Shach, if the litigants voluntarily come before a court, this is treated as though they accepted the judges upon themselves. If so, what novelty is there in saying that if these disqualified judges nevertheless judged, their judgment is valid? The case must apparently concern judgment without coercion, and voluntary appearance itself is acceptance. Even judges who are fully disqualified can be validated by acceptance. There is no substance to this objection. First, as the questioner in that responsum himself appears to recognize, the case can involve judges who proceeded through coercion. Second, they may have judged initially without anyone knowing that one judge was the litigant's close friend or enemy, and only afterward was the relationship discovered. The earlier appearance before them was then acceptance in error. One might have thought the judgment must be void. The ruling teaches that after the fact it nevertheless stands. All the more so according to the authorities who hold that acceptance does not help where there are two independent defects, the case can concern litigants who accepted a panel containing a double disqualification. The law that the judgment remains valid after the fact is therefore meaningful and not obvious.
Their judgments are invalid. Beit Yosef writes that Rambam's words appear to agree with the gloss to Rosh that if they judged, their judgments are valid. Perishah is likewise uncertain on this point, and Avodat HaGershuni, responsum 33, follows Beit Yosef and validates the judgment after the fact according to Rambam. It is true that in Mishneh Torah Rambam's wording is not decisive. But in his commentary to the Mishnah in Niddah, on the statement "whoever is fit to judge," Rambam asks why the Mishnah does not list an enemy, who is unfit to judge but valid to testify. He answers that this is not a permanent disqualification, because once the love or hatred passes he returns to fitness. See also Maharik, shoresh 21. If Rambam held that the judgment is valid after the fact, his question would have no force. The Mishnah's word "disqualified" implies an actual invalidity. How could one list a friend or enemy if, after the fact, their judgment stands and the status of invalidity does not attach to the judgment? It is therefore clear that Rambam agrees with Rosh that if such a person judged, the judgment is invalid. Accordingly, Tur was correct not to cite Rambam as disagreeing with Rosh, despite Beit Yosef's question. In Urim I cited Chavot Yair that if one litigant wishes to validate a judge who is the close friend of the opposing party, the opposing litigant cannot object. There is a proof from Tosafot in Niddah 50b. Tosafot ask why the Mishnah does not include a friend and an enemy as persons who are valid to testify but invalid to judge. They answer that this is not a fixed rule, "for they are valid for the whole world." This requires explanation. Relatives too are invalid only with respect to those related to them and valid for everyone else, yet all the authorities include relatives in the category of those invalid to judge. It therefore appears that "valid for the whole world" means that there is no person with respect to whom the invalidity is absolute. A relative is absolutely disqualified for his relative. Even if that relative wants him to judge, the opposing litigant can object and say, "I do not agree that your relative should judge me." The disqualification therefore has an objective form. A friend or enemy is different. If the opposing litigant wishes to accept him, the fact that the judge is the other party's friend does not allow that other party to prevent the acceptance. Thus even with respect to the friend himself there is no absolute disqualification once the opposing party agrees. That is Tosafot's meaning: they remain "valid for the whole world," including even the person toward whom the friendship exists, because the other litigant can accept them. The wording of Tosafot is precise according to this explanation. There is also a conceptual question regarding the rule that an enemy is disqualified from judging. The Gemara elsewhere says that one who sees another commit a transgression is permitted to hate him. If so, how can a judge who personally witnessed a serious transgression later judge that person, especially in capital law? One who hates should be disqualified even more strongly there. The answer is that the hatred that creates judicial bias is hatred arising from ordinary personal matters. When someone has harmed the judge in worldly affairs, natural resentment can prevent the judge from seeing the person's merit. By contrast, hatred arising solely from a transgression is a hatred for the sake of Heaven. Where the judge has no personal grievance and dislikes only the sin, he can still search for grounds of merit and imitate Hashem's ways, for Hashem desires the justification of the wicked and seeks arguments in their favor. Thus such a judge may judge both civil and capital cases. But where the person first wronged him personally and the judge's hatred therefore has a worldly source, the fact that the person later committed a transgression merely removes the prohibition against continuing to hate him. It does not transform the existing personal hatred into purely heavenly hatred. In such a case the judge can remain unable to see his merit and is disqualified.
Because of love or hatred, etc. Sema, seif katan 22, writes that this rule applies specifically where the judge is disqualified because of love or hatred. If he is disqualified because of family relationship, he may not appoint replacement judges. Terumat HaDeshen, Pesakim siman 258, however, writes that the custom is that even a relative may appoint a beit din. He adds that although there is room to investigate the custom, great sages have lived in the land and followed it, and one should not alter their practice. Thus even Mahari did not treat the matter as obvious, but he did not wish to overturn the custom. I say that the custom of great Jewish sages is itself Torah and has a basis. Maharik proves that one disqualified because of friendship may appoint another judge from the case of Rav's host, who had a dispute. Rav said to him, "I am disqualified to judge you," and then told Rav Kahana, "Go judge his case." This shows that a disqualified judge may appoint another. The same inference can be made for relatives from Ketubot, in the chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot. A man brought a gift to Rav Anan, and Rav Anan sent him to Rav Nachman to judge his case. Rav Nachman mistakenly thought the man was Rav Anan's relative and therefore gave his case precedence over the orphans' case. How could Rav Nachman have thought he was Rav Anan's relative? If a relative has no authority to arrange another judge, why would Rav Anan have sent his relative to Rav Nachman for judgment? Maharik's own question would arise. Necessarily a relative too may appoint another beit din. The established custom is therefore well founded. Ir Shushan rules so explicitly. Maharam Mintz, responsum 18, writes that even one disqualified because of hatred cannot arrange the court. He therefore disagrees with Maharik altogether. But according to Maharik there appears to be no basis to distinguish between love or hatred and family relationship in this matter.
One who placed him under nidui, etc. Rama derives this law from the incident in the chapter Asarah Yuchasin where Rav Yehudah placed a man under nidui and afterward publicized that he was a slave. Sema questions the proof. Perhaps Rav Yehudah had already released him from the nidui before judging the later matter. The Gemara, however, implies that the nidui had not yet been released, because Rav Nachman later summoned Rav Yehudah and asked him, "Why did the master place this man under nidui?" Even so, I remain uncertain about the substantive proof. Rav Nachman asked Rav Yehudah why he had placed the man under nidui. Rav Yehudah answered that the man had caused distress to an agent of the sages. Maharsha in Chiddushei Aggadot asks why Rav Yehudah did not simply answer that the man had insulted him personally, since a sage may impose nidui for affront to his honor. Maharsha answers that, out of humility, Rav Yehudah attributed the nidui to mistreatment of the rabbinic agent rather than to his own honor. But in truth this itself may explain why Rav Yehudah could later adjudicate the man's slave-status. Had Rav Yehudah placed him under nidui because of a personal insult, he might then have become personally involved and unable to judge him afterward. Because Rav Yehudah waived his personal honor and based the nidui solely on the man's mistreatment of the rabbinic agent, the matter was not personal to Rav Yehudah at all. He could therefore adjudicate the later issue as he saw fit. If so, the episode supplies no proof that a judge who personally imposed nidui upon someone because of a matter involving himself may afterward judge that person. Darkei Moshe itself leaves this law requiring study.
