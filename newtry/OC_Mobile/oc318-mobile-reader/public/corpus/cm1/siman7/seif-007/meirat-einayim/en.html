His close friend. During the seven days of a man's wedding, those attending closely to him are called his shushbinim, and only then is the judge disqualified on that account. So explains Rashi.
And some say that with an actual enemy, etc. The first view says that it is prohibited to judge a friend even if he is not an especially close friend or actual enemy. The wording implies that even with an actual close friend or enemy the defect is merely a prohibition. The second view says that with an actual friend or enemy the judgment itself is invalid. A further opinion disputes the first rule and says that with an ordinary friend who does not rise to the full level of friendship there is not even a prohibition, only a matter of piety. See the Derishah.
Because each one chooses his friend. The system of 'this one chooses one judge and that one chooses one judge' means that each litigant selects someone who will understand his claims thoroughly and present any legal basis in his favor to the other judges. The other selected judge does the same for the other litigant, while the third listens and decides between them. The Rosh, cited by the Tur in siman 13, explains this. Therefore, even though the selected judge is friendly to the litigant, this is no defect, because both selected judges appropriately identify any genuine legal merit for the one who chose them, and the matter ultimately depends on the third. Maharik writes this explicitly. This differs from Ir Shushan's suggestion that people merely do this as a custom even though it is not proper by law. A relative or one disqualified by Torah law is invalid even in this system, as Maharik says. Thus Rama's 'therefore' means that because the ordinary friend is excluded only as a matter of piety, he may serve as a selected judge, whereas a Torah-disqualified person may not.
To serve as judge for his student. All the more so for the student's father, even if he paid tuition, as the cited source states.
Because of love or hatred he may appoint, etc. Maharik carefully writes that even one who personally refrains from judging because of piety may still participate in appointing the judges, and he proves this from the Gemara. Mahari in Piskei writes that the practice is not to disqualify even a beit din from arranging the panel, but then remarks that although the custom might deserve reconsideration, great scholars established it and one should not alter it. Rama therefore writes here specifically about one disqualified because of friendship or enmity and does not broadly validate every disqualified judge for selecting the panel.
And he must bring proof for his words. The Tur says he must prove that the judge did not speak with him in enmity within the previous three days.
He may judge him afterward. Darkei Moshe says Rama derived this law independently from an incident in the Gemara and left it somewhat unresolved. In Hagahot I wrote that perhaps it applies specifically after the excommunication was already lifted, when they again stand like ordinary people toward one another, not while the person remains under ban.
