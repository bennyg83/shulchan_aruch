A person may not judge someone whom he loves, even if he is not his wedding attendant or a friend as dear to him as himself, nor someone whom he hates, even if he is not his enemy and does not seek his harm. Rather, both litigants must be equal in the eyes and hearts of the judges. If the judge knows neither of them nor their conduct, there is no judge more righteous than he. Rama: Nevertheless, if such a judge rendered judgment, the judgment stands. Some say that where there is actual hatred, meaning that he has not spoken with the person for three days because of animosity, or actual love, meaning that he is his wedding attendant or intimate friend, the judgment does not stand. Some say that whenever the relationship does not rise to actual love or hatred it is permitted to judge him, and refraining is only an extra measure of piety. Therefore one may serve as a judge where each litigant selects one judge, since each selects someone favorable to him. Certainly a rabbi may judge his student. Even a beit din disqualified because of love or hatred may appoint other qualified judges to hear the case. One who says that a judge hates him or loves his opposing litigant is not believed without proof. One who placed another under nidui because the other slighted his honor may afterwards judge him, provided he does not hate him. See below, siman 33, seif 6.
