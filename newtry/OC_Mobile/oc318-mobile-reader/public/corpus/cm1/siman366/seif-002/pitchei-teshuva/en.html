Therefore they should use it for public needs. Rosh, Bava Kamma, chapter 9, siman 2, writes that this refers to a case where the stolen property itself still exists. The abridged rulings of Rosh and Yam Shel Shlomo there, siman 3, say the same. Bach questions why Tur and Mechaber state the rule without this limitation and answers that they relied on what they had already written concerning a notorious robber. Responsa Shivat Tzion, siman 112, calls Bach's answer strained and suggests instead that Tur and Mechaber understood the enactment that one should not accept restitution from certain repentant robbers to apply only where the robber knows his victim and seeks to return the money directly. Where he does not know whom he robbed and the law requires expenditure for public needs, the enactment does not apply. The reason follows Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 161, seif katan 13, citing Rivash, that the robber remains obligated to return the stolen property, while the Sages instructed the victim not to accept it and to forgive him. If the victim is unknown and absent, no one can forgive the debt, so the robber's restitution obligation remains. See there. The point requires study.
