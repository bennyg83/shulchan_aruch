One who descends into another's field. See Sema, seif katan 1. His intent is that seif 1 concerns a purchaser who took the produce into his own house and is therefore in possession of it. He is believed by virtue of possession, as with other movable property that is not normally lent or rented. Thus, even if he did not first announce his intention, so that the reasoning that a person would not be so brazen does not apply, he is nevertheless believed because he is in possession. The case must therefore be one where it is unknown how the produce came into his hands, whether the owner himself delivered it to him or he took it on his own. If witnesses saw him take it by himself in the owner's absence, mere possession would not make him believed, for the produce would resemble roaming animals, as the Rif and Rosh write in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in, and as explained above in siman 90, seif 14. In that case he must specifically have said beforehand, "I will go and gather the produce," because then he is believed on the reasoning that a person would not act with such brazenness. Once that reasoning applies, he is believed after gathering even if he is not presently in possession, as in seif 2. Seif 2 therefore concerns one who announced before gathering, "I will go and gather," so that the reasoning of brazenness applies. Accordingly, even if he gathered the produce and left it in the owner's field, where he is not its possessor, he is believed whether the owner was present or absent. The Mechaber's wording in seif 2, "if the owner of the field is not here," appears only because he adds that beit din need not prevent him. If the owner is present, there is no point in saying that beit din need not prevent him, since the owner can prevent him himself. At first glance there is a difficulty according to the Rif and Rosh in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in, who explain the incident of one who went and cut down a palm tree as occurring when the owner was absent. If so, their interpretation does not furnish the proof drawn by Tosafot from the case of one who says, "I descended for the produce," namely that when he previously says, "I will go and gather," he is believed even though he is not in possession. Tosafot's proof rests on their question that without the reasoning of brazenness he should in any event be believed because he is in possession. According to the Rif and Rosh, there is no question, since the case may be where the owner was absent and mere possession does not help. It may be that the expression, "I descended for the produce," implies that it is unknown how the produce came into his possession or whether the owner was present. Yet even there the Gemara requires the reasoning of brazenness. We would then still need Tosafot's answer that this reasoning is necessary where the produce remains in the owner's domain. Alternatively, one can distinguish as follows. The Rosh wrote that possession does not help when one takes property in the owner's absence even where the challenger says, "You stole it." In the case of roaming animals, the reason he is not believed against such a charge is, as Urim writes in siman 90, seif katan 40, that even if the animals were in fact purchased, entering another's premises in his absence makes him a thief with respect to the taking. But where the possessor says, "I descended for the produce," meaning that he purchased the produce of the entire field, and certainly where the field is unguarded, his entry does not itself mark him as a thief. The owner is then not believed merely to say, "You stole it," and the possessor can say that it was purchased. Nevertheless, Tosafot's answer remains necessary for the case where the produce is still in the owner's domain.
Even if there are witnesses. See Tosafot, Bava Batra 33b, s.v. i ta'in, who ask that if the case specifically concerns one who seized the produce, why is the reasoning that a person would not be brazen necessary? He should be believed simply because he is in possession, like one who possesses movable property not normally lent or rented. On that basis Tosafot explain the case as one where he is not in possession. The Bach objects that in a mortgage dispute where one party says three years and the other says two, absent the special consideration that the matter is likely to become known, possession would not help because the produce derives from land, and we say that land remains in the chazakah of its owner. The Shach answers, among other things, that there the wording of the document supports only two years. Tumim objects that we rule like Rav Nachman concerning an intercalated month, where even at the end of the month the entire month belongs to the lessor because land remains in the owner's chazakah, although no document supports that result. In my view, the distinction is that in the case of the intercalated month, ordinary possession cannot prove the tenant's claim because he himself is uncertain. The only possible force of possession there, and likewise in the mortgage case, arises from the fact that there is an inherent monetary uncertainty that existed independently of the litigants' claims. Had the matter come before beit din at the time the document was written or the rental was created, there would already have been doubt about the duration of the right in the land itself, before there was any issue concerning produce, which did not yet exist. Since the doubt arose in the land itself, the rule that land remains in the chazakah of its owner applies, as Rashi explains in Bava Metzia 102. Here, however, when one descends into another's field, there was no uncertainty while the produce was still attached. Only after the produce came into the possessor's hands did a dispute arise through the parties' competing claims. The ordinary presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him is then affirmative evidence that his claim is true. Tosafot therefore properly asked that if he is in possession, the brazenness rationale should be unnecessary. Tumim also challenges the Shach's second answer, that the mortgage case is different because the claimant is brazen, arguing that Tosafot's very question was why the Gemara needed to mention brazenness at all. This is no difficulty. In his second answer the Shach is responding to the separate question why the mortgage holder should not in any event be believed by the affirmative reasoning that he would not be so brazen. He therefore answers that this reasoning does not apply there. Tumim further distinguishes between a sale of produce alone and a lease or mortgage of the land itself for its produce. He argues that when the produce alone was sold in a legally effective manner, the possessor can rely on possession or lack of brazenness; but when the land itself was leased or mortgaged for its produce, the landowner remains supported by the rule that land stands in its owner's chazakah, and the reasoning that people do not write documents merely for produce does not apply because people do write documents for leases. In my view this distinction cannot be accepted. If so, a lessor could always sue a tenant after the tenant had already lived in the house and demand rent again by saying, "I never rented it to you," forcing the tenant to pay twice. We have never heard of such a rule, and no posek has stated it. There is therefore no such distinction.
