Tosafot asks why one who says, "I will go and gather fruit," needs the special rule that a person is not brazen enough to harvest another's field. Since he is already holding the fruit, why is he not believed to say he purchased it like any other movable property? Ritzba answers that in ordinary movables, if we know a person entered another's house and took the object without the owner's knowledge, he cannot later say, "I purchased it earlier." The familiar rule that one seen hiding utensils under his garment can sometimes say they were purchased applies where we did not see him take them without permission and it is possible the owner handed them over. But if he took the object secretly, forcibly, or while the owner was absent, he is a robber and cannot convert the taking into a purchase merely by his own claim. Otherwise no one's property would be secure. This is also clear from Rabbi Abba's piece of metal. If two witnesses had seen the forcible seizure, the seizer would have to return the object and could not say he bought it first. The Rif's language in Shevuot likewise supports this. This explains the structure of the Shulchan Aruch. In seif 1 it does not say that the person announced, "I will go and gather," because that case concerns the owner being present. The possessor can then be believed to say the fruits were sold or given to him, just like ordinary movables, because we can assume the owner delivered them. In seif 2, by contrast, the owner of the tree or field is absent. The possessor then cannot rely merely on physical possession. His credibility rests specifically on the rule that a person is not brazen enough to go publicly and gather another's fruits under a claim of right. This is also the proper reading of the Tur.
