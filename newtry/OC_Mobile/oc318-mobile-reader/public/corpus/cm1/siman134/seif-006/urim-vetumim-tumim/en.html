And if he says, In my presence he admitted, etc.; and some say that this means, etc. The Tur writes that if a craftsman's son claims that he himself bought the object from its owner, he is like any other person, but if he claims that he inherited it from his father, he has the same status as his father. This appears contrary to Tosafot and the Rosh, who state explicitly that even if the son claims he bought it from the owner, he is not believed unless the object remained in his possession for some time. The Bach explains that when the Tur says the son comes with his own purchase claim, it means he made that claim while his father was still alive, when he had no legal connection to his father's possession, but not after the father's death. The Shach agrees. This interpretation does not fit the Tur. The Tur distinguishes between types of claims, a claim of personal purchase versus inheritance, not between times, during the father's life versus after his death. How could the Tur write generally that one who says he bought it from the owner is believed if he meant only during the father's lifetime? It would have been more natural to distinguish explicitly between the father's life and after death. It therefore appears that the Tur rejected the strained point discussed above: why should a craftsman not be believed when the object did not remain long in his hands, since he has a migo that after leaving his trade he purchased it? Likewise, why should the craftsman's heir not be believed to say, I myself bought it from you, even without prolonged possession? Tosafot themselves say that after a craftsman leaves his trade he can say he bought it. Why should his son be worse? The Tur therefore holds that if there are no witnesses to delivery of the object to the craftsman, the possessor is plainly believed even without prolonged possession, through a migo that he purchased it after the craftsman ceased his trade. The special rule requiring prolonged possession concerns a case where there are witnesses that the object was delivered to the craftsman while he was still a craftsman and the object is now seen in his hands, so that no migo of return remains. Since if the object had not been seen even a craftsman still in his trade could be believed, the case must involve seeing it. Tosafot's point is then that where an object has remained with the craftsman unusually long, the original witnessed delivery for repair becomes weakened. People do not ordinarily leave completed objects with a craftsman so long, and we therefore assume he returned it and later purchased it after the repair was complete. This explains Tosafot's phrase, even those utensils that were given to him while he was still a craftsman, meaning that it is known with certainty through witnesses that they were delivered to him while he was a craftsman. There is no question how Tosafot could infer that prolonged possession defeats even witnesses and current sight. Their problem was how the baraita about one who left his trade can be understood according to Rabbah and Rava, who require both witnesses of delivery and sight for the special craftsman rule. If there were no witnesses of delivery, leaving the trade would add nothing, because he would have been believed even while still a craftsman. If there were witnesses of delivery while he was a craftsman, then merely leaving the trade later should not matter. It is impossible that the witnesses delivered it for repair only after he ceased being a craftsman. Tosafot therefore had to explain that the object was indeed delivered while he was a craftsman with witnesses, and is now seen, but the unusually long retention weakens the original inference because people do not leave items with craftsmen for so long. Once this reasoning is accepted for Rabbah and Rava, it remains practical law. This also explains Tosafot's later statement that the Mishnah's rule that a craftsman's son can establish possession by claiming, In my presence the owner admitted, concerns a case where the object remained in the son's possession after his father's death. Otherwise, if witnesses identified the object as having been delivered to the father and it was then seen with the son, why should the son be believed any more than any ordinary heir who holds an object shown by witnesses to have been entrusted to his father? The heir has no migo of return. But where the object remained unusually long in the son's possession, the original witnessed delivery is weakened, and he can say that he bought it. This too is compelled by the Gemara according to Rava. It appears, however, that Tosafot's different comments were written from somewhat different stages of analysis. In their discussion of one who left his trade they innovate that prolonged possession weakens the original witnessed delivery. In the earlier discussion beginning, Sometimes even the grandson of a robber, they had not yet applied that reasoning. There they ask why we do not plead for the son of a robber that had his father been alive he could have claimed purchase. Rabbenu Yitzchak answers that we do not plead an uncommon event for heirs. Tosafot add that the earlier statement that the son of a craftsman or sharecropper can establish possession applies only where he claims that the owner admitted in his presence, and indeed this itself shows that without such an admission he would not have possession because the object originally came to the father as craftsman, sharecropper, or robber. The proof is initially difficult, especially from the grandson of a robber, because the Gemara's discussion there does not say that the grandson's case involves an admission. The Gemara asks only why the son of a craftsman differs from the son of a robber, and answers by establishing the craftsman's son's case as one where the owner admitted. One could say that Tosafot assume the same qualification applies across the parallel clauses, but that is strained. More plausibly, in that passage Tosafot assumed there were no witnesses of delivery, for if there were witnesses and current sight, even a claim that the owner admitted would have no migo to support it. Since they had not yet introduced the rule that long retention weakens the witnessed delivery, they necessarily treated the case as lacking delivery witnesses. Then the craftsman's son could ordinarily say, I bought it from you. Why, according to Rabbah and Rava, does the Mishnah specifically require him to say, In my presence he admitted? The answer is Rabbenu Yitzchak's principle that although the father himself could have been believed if he expressly asserted the uncommon purchase, we do not invent such an uncommon claim for his heirs merely because the item first entered his hands as a craftsman. Abaye indeed treats such purchase as so uncommon that even sight alone can defeat the craftsman's claim. This explains Tosafot. Alternatively, one may explain Tosafot's proof by saying that if a robber's grandson were generally entitled to a possessory presumption through a claim that his father purchased, then similarly a craftsman's grandson should be able to rely on the same inherited purchase theory without needing an express admission. Since the Gemara instead distinguishes only the robber's line while requiring the craftsman's son to rely on admission, it follows that absent admission we do not plead the uncommon purchase for heirs where possession originally arose through craftsmanship or sharecropping. Whichever precise reading is adopted, the practical point remains that where the craftsman's son needs prolonged possession after the father's death, this is a case involving witnesses of delivery to the father and current sight. The Tur's language is therefore coherent. Regarding a craftsman who left his trade, the Tur writes that he is believed even concerning utensils that came into his hands while he was still a craftsman, provided they remained with him for a period. This refers to a case where witnesses establish that they entered his hands as a craftsman, so absent prolonged retention he has no migo. With prolonged retention, however, the original delivery is weakened even though there are witnesses and sight. The Tur then writes that a craftsman's son who claims, I bought it from the owner, is like any other person. That means if there are no delivery witnesses and sight, he is believed just like an ordinary possessor. If there are such witnesses and sight, even an ordinary heir would not be believed to say that he bought property known to have been entrusted to his father, and certainly the craftsman's son is not believed. If the object remained with him long enough, however, he is believed all the more readily, since even the craftsman himself would be believed under those circumstances. But if the son says, I inherited it from my father, he stands in his father's shoes. The later passage of time does not help him, because he is not claiming that he acquired it after his father's death; he claims his father already owned it. During the father's lifetime there had been no prolonged retention after leaving the trade, so there is no basis to infer a purchase by the father. Thus the Tur says simply that with an inheritance claim the son is like the father and cannot assert that the father purchased it. The Tur omits the obvious case of an express admission by the owner because once he has already said that a personal purchase claim can be believed, certainly a claim that the owner admitted in his presence is believed through the same migo. The Tur often omits such obvious repetitions, and the law is also set out later in siman 152 concerning land. This explanation of the Tur is clear. Accordingly, the Mechaber and Rama's unqualified implication that prolonged possession is always required does not reflect the Tur. According to the Tur, it is required only where there are witnesses of delivery and current sight. Otherwise the son is believed immediately in his personal purchase claim, as the Sema argues. We can further learn from the alternative explanation of Tosafot above that without a claim, In my presence the owner admitted, even a grandson of a craftsman does not receive the plea that his father purchased, because we do not invent an uncommon event for heirs. See what I wrote above in siman 152. The Yerushalmi in Chezkas HaBatim states that even a sharecropper and the son of a sharecropper have no chazakah because we say the owner derived satisfaction from the father and therefore allowed the son to remain. This seems to contradict our Gemara, where Rabbi Yochanan holds that the son of a sharecropper can have a chazakah. According to what I have written, there is no contradiction. The Bavli concerns a son who says, In my presence he admitted, or, I myself purchased it. The Yerushalmi concerns one who comes only by inheritance. Even if the item remained with him, he is not believed, because the owner may have left it with the son out of regard for the father and failed to protest. This is precisely the Tur's distinction that prolonged possession does not help an inheritance claim. Examine this carefully.
