If he says, 'He admitted before me, etc.' He is believed because if he wished to lie he could have claimed that the owner himself sold it to him, a claim he would be believed to make, as Tur states below in siman 152. Sema. This is correct. See Tumim and below in that siman.
Some say this applies only if it remained, etc. This is Tosafot and Rosh. Tur writes that if the craftsman's son claims in his own right that the owner sold it to him, he is like any other person, but if he claims inheritance from his father he has his father's status. Tur omitted the distinction based on whether the object remained with the son for a time, and Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe wondered why. Sema says Mechaber and Rama therefore disagree with Tur. Bach attempts to reconcile them by limiting Tur's case. The Shach sides with Bach. See Tumim. I showed that Bach's reading is difficult. I then offered an explanation of Tosafot and of Tur: when a craftsman ceased his trade, he can be believed concerning items that originally came into his hands while he was a craftsman even without an ordinary purchase migo, once sufficient time has passed. If insufficient time passed and there are no sight witnesses, he may still be believed through migo. Concerning the craftsman's son, Tur says he is like any other person. Therefore, if there are sight witnesses, he is not believed merely because the item is in his hands, unless the circumstances otherwise support him. Tur did not need to repeat the 'remained for a time' rule because if the craftsman himself becomes believed after that period, all the more his son, who is treated as an outsider. I concluded that Mechaber and Rama require the lapse of time more broadly and therefore differ from Tur. I also showed from the Jerusalem Talmud that regarding an inheritance claim, mere passage of time does not help. See Tumim at length.
