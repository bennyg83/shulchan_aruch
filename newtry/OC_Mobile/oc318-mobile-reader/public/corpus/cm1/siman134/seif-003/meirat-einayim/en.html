Although he delivered it to him before witnesses, etc. This is difficult. If he delivered it before witnesses, how can the Mechaber conclude that the craftsman is believed when he says, "You never gave it to me"? Rama likewise writes that he is afraid to say, "This never occurred," yet even without any such fear he cannot make that claim because witnesses saw the delivery. Nor can we explain that he means, "You never gave me this particular object, but another one, which I returned," because the Mechaber says that the witnesses who now saw the object do not recognize it, without saying that the witnesses to the original delivery fail to recognize it. This implies that the delivery witnesses do recognize it. If so, why should the craftsman be believed? The owner can bring the delivery witnesses, combine them with the viewing witnesses, and thereby eliminate both the migo of denying receipt and the migo of claiming return. The text should then have said that he is believed if he claims, "I returned it to you," because where delivery was witnessed that is the defense upon which his credibility would depend. It may be that the Mechaber mechanically copied the Tur's language. In the Perishah I explained that the Tur's words, "although he delivered it before witnesses," refer back to the preceding law, not to this clause. That reading is impossible in the Mechaber, however, because the preceding seif already ended by saying, "although he delivered it without witnesses." This therefore requires study.
But if he claims, "You gave it to me to repair, and afterward, etc." The same applies, and all the more so according to the reasoning I wrote in seif katan 3, if he claims that he purchased it from the outset.
Because he is afraid to say, etc. For this reason, even if the owner delivered the object to him without witnesses, the craftsman is still not believed. See the Perishah.
