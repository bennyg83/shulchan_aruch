(Seif 6: and if he says, in my presence he admitted, etc.) Our master omitted this rule even though it is an explicit Gemara. The "some say" that this applies only, etc., is the view of Tosafos and the Rosh, and our master omitted that as well. The Beit Yosef therefore wonders why he omitted it, and the Shulchan Aruch records it here as a "some say." In my humble opinion, however, there is no dispute. Our master did not need to cite it, because it is obvious that an admission without subsequent possession is ineffective. He already taught this by writing that if the son says he inherited it from his father, the son is treated like the father and has no chazakah. This includes anyone who derives his claim from his father on any basis, whether through an admission or some other right. When our master says, "he is like his father," we also learn that just as the father's having ceased his craft can help him, so too with the son. If the father were alive, had left his profession, and the object remained with him, he would be believed to say it had been purchased. The same is true if it remained with the son after the father's death. It is enough for the son to say, "My father told me that he sold it to him." Why should he be worse than his father, who would have been believed? Tosafos in Perek Chezkas HaBatim 47 write that a craftsman's son who says, "He admitted in my presence," has chazakah only after the object remained with him. They state the stronger novelty: even where he says, "He admitted in my presence," continued possession is required. In truth, continued possession is enough even if he merely says, "My father told me that he sold it to him." The Gemara needed to state the rule of admission only with respect to a robber, teaching that there admission is never effective because it was made under fear, as explained in siman 152. This appears to me clear. The Sema entered into forced distinctions here, and it appears that even he did not ultimately maintain them, so I did not reproduce them in my glosses.
