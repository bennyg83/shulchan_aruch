You sold it to me. See Shach, seif katan 4. There is a scribal error there, and it should read, "And if the purchaser knows," while the word "then" is erroneous. If the purchaser knows that the object belonged to the claimant, the purchaser does not stand in the place of witnesses who saw the object, for the reason Shach gives in siman 72, seif katan 90, namely that the purchaser is interested testimony. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that he is not interested, because the claimant knows that he never sold it to him, comparing this to siman 121 where, if the sender accompanied the agent, the agent is not interested. I do not understand this comparison. Here the purchaser is certainly interested. The craftsman could claim that he returned the item to the claimant, and although it is now seen in the possessor's hands, one does not know whether the claimant sold it to him or whether it was stolen from the claimant. The possessor, who admittedly did not receive it back from the claimant but took it from the craftsman, is interested in saying that he purchased it from the craftsman. Otherwise the claimant will say, "Why is my object in your possession, since it did not come to you through a valid purchase from anyone?" and will remove it from him without paying. He therefore says that he bought it from the craftsman so that he may recover his money either from the craftsman or from the owner under the enactment for the marketplace. Nor can we say that the purchaser should be believed to testify through a migo that he could have said, "I returned it," as an agent can sometimes become a witness in kiddushin, for the Gemara concludes there that after the Sages enacted a heset oath, such a person becomes a witness who requires an oath and is therefore not a valid witness. As to Shach's statement that if the purchaser were not interested, the craftsman would be liable under the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, Ketzot HaChoshen writes that a purchaser is comparable to an heir, and just as an heir is not liable under that rule because Scripture says "between the two of them," excluding heirs, so too a purchaser who comes by another's right is excluded. This is incorrect. An heir inherits the property and it becomes his. Although his father was liable, after the father's death we cannot impose the father's personal obligation upon the heirs out of the inherited assets where Scripture exempts heirs from the consequence of inability to swear. But where a claimant seeks his own movable property from a purchaser or heir who has no independent right or chazakah in it, such as property held by a craftsman or property ordinarily lent or rented out, their uncertain claim of purchase has no force on its own. We merely advance for them whatever claim their transferor could have made. If the seller or predecessor himself would not have been believed to claim purchase through a migo of return because he would be subject to the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, the purchaser or heir who comes through him is certainly no stronger. Since we are not collecting their own property, the exclusion concerning heirs does not apply. They cannot receive more than their predecessor possessed, for one cannot transfer more rights than one has.
