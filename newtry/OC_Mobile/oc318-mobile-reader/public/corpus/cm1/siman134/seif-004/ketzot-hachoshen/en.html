Shach cites Sema as establishing the case where there are witnesses who presently saw the object. The purchaser knows it originally belonged to the claimant, and even if the claimant lacks two full witnesses to every stage, the purchaser can function like one witness. The artisan is then in the position of one obligated to swear but unable to swear. Shach previously proved from Tosafot in Perek Chazakat HaBatim that the purchaser can serve this evidentiary role, while Rashba in a responsum goes even further and treats the purchaser himself as equivalent to witnesses of observation. Shach ultimately rejects Rashba and says the purchaser is an interested witness, because he could claim that the artisan returned the item to the owner and later sold it to him. If the purchaser made that claim, he would have to take an oath against the owner's certain denial, and a witness who requires an oath is not a valid witness.
In my view the purchaser is not disqualified as interested in this way. The Tur, in siman 121 in the name of Baal HaTerumot, says that where the sender himself accompanied messengers and saw from a distance that they performed their mission, they can testify. Here too the challenger knows with certainty that he never sold or delivered the item to the purchaser. Since the challenger's own knowledge removes the hypothetical alternative, the purchaser can serve as a single witness regarding the artisan's nonreturn. There is a second argument from Rashba in Kiddushin. If the lender admits that the debtor gave him money but says it was applied to another debt, the underlying cause that would make the witness interested has disappeared, and the remaining witness can create the rule of inability to swear. So too here, once the artisan himself admits he did not return the object, the source of the purchaser's supposed interest is removed. The purchaser should therefore count as one witness. Rashba's ruling is well grounded.
It appears that Tosafot's treatment of the purchaser as a witness depends on their two answers concerning Rabbi Abba's piece of metal. Tosafot asks why the one who seized a metal bar is not believed through the migo that he could have denied the seizure and sworn that he seized his own property, just as one holding a written deposit can say he returned it through a migo of unavoidable accident. Their first answer is that this is not a true migo because a person is reluctant to contradict a witness and swear against him. Their second answer, in the name of Rivam, is that it is indeed a valid migo, but Scripture specially decreed that when one witness obligates him he must either swear or pay. According to Rivam's answer, the migo exists in substance. The special scriptural rule applies only directly between the original litigants. It does not automatically extend to heirs or successors. Thus, if the witness appears only against the purchaser or heirs, we can advance for them the seller's migo, because the Torah's special decree of "swear or pay" was not addressed to them in the same way. This resembles the rule that a suspected original litigant would pay, while his heirs do not inherit that personal oath disability. According to Tosafot's first answer, however, there is no migo at all. If the seller himself lacked a migo, beit din cannot advance that nonexistent defense to his heirs or purchaser. Thus Tosafot in Perek Chazakat HaBatim and Rashba's responsum can be read as following the first answer, while under Rivam's answer the purchaser or heirs can still receive the seller's migo. The Shach in siman 75 writes that the primary law follows Rivam, and this distinction should therefore be considered.
