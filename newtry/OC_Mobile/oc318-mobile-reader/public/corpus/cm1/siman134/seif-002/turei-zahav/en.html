(Seif 4). “But when he claims that the artisan,” etc. According to Rambam as explained at the end of siman 133, the case must involve witnesses who saw the article in the artisan's hands. This is also the implication of the baraita in Bava Batra 45: if one sees his slave in an artisan's possession or his garment in a launderer's possession and asks why it is there, the answer “you sold it to me” is ineffective, but “you told me in my presence to sell it” is believed. The Gemara establishes the latter clause where the article comes from a third person's possession, as the Tur writes here. The clause still refers to an article that had been seen in the artisan's hands. Therefore, if the third person says, “In my presence you instructed the artisan to sell it,” he is believed through a migo that he bought it directly from the owner and that the artisan delivered it by the owner's command. If he says merely, “The artisan told me that you authorized the sale,” we are witnesses that the artisan sold it wrongfully. This supports my explanation of Rambam in siman 133: where there were no witnesses of possession against the person who lacked ordinary possessory credibility, the purchaser from him may say that his seller would have been believed, and we advance that claim for a purchaser. The Tur's words do not conclusively reject this. The present source also contradicts Beit Yosef's explanation, cited in siman 133, that a possessor who says another person stole and sold the item has made no legal claim, for according to the claimant that is precisely what occurred here.
