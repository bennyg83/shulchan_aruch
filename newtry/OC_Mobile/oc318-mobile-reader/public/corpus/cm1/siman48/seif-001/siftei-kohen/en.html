And he may write everything there, including the borrower's name, etc. According to Rashi, this is forbidden. The scribe must leave blank the names of borrower and lender, the amount, and the date; in a sale deed he must leave blank the buyer, seller, price, field, and date. We decree concerning the essential text of other deeds because of gittin. This appears correct, for the plain Mishnah indicates that Rabbi Eliezer does not dispute the first Tanna on this point. Tosafot and the Rosh object from the Gemara's challenge based on Rav Pappi's refusal to write an authentication endorsement before the judges signed because it appears false. One may answer that if apparent falsehood is a concern there too, writing the body of a deed for an anticipated transaction also appears false, even if names and amounts are omitted, just as under Tosafot's own view unsigned writing may appear false. Alternatively, the Gemara's implication is that the Mishnah invalidates only because of the decree from gittin, and otherwise apparent falsehood would not matter, contrary to Rav Pappi. The Gemara could have asked the same even under the Sages, but then one could answer that perhaps the law follows Rabbi Yehudah; once Rav ruled like Rabbi Eliezer the challenge was stronger. The Gemara instead gives the broader answer that Rav Pappi's concern is rejected. The Ran in Kol HaGet and the Raavan also rule like Rashi. A scribe preparing loan forms must leave blank the borrower, lender, amount, and date; for sale forms, seller, buyer, price, field, and date. [Citation reference: 30.] [Citation reference: 520.]
Since its lien was waived, one cannot borrow again with it, etc. The Ir Shushan first says collection remains possible from unencumbered property, then suggests that even that may fail. The Sema rules that a deed whose lien was waived still collects from unencumbered property. His proofs are unpersuasive. The Rosh's discussion in Nedarim concerns a borrower who denies nothing and a deed transferred according to an original condition, or, in the Rosh's conclusion, money expressly treated as a gift if payment was not made by the deadline. That is why the Mechaber and Rama in siman 55 allow collection there. Indeed, the Rosh's conclusion implies the opposite here, because he says that acquisition of documentary rights ordinarily requires writing and delivery. Without both, a deed whose lien was waived cannot collect even from unencumbered property. Here payment was absolute and the parties later attempted a new loan on the same deed. The deed was entirely nullified, and the borrower may claim payment. The Gemara in Chezkas HaBatim 32, the Rashbam, Rambam, Semag, Baal HaTerumot, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Tur, and Mechaber at the end of siman 57 all indicate that a repaid deed cannot be reused, for its lien has been waived and it is no more than an oral loan. The Ramban, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef similarly ask how Rabbi Yose can allow collection on a partially paid deed and answer that the initial arrangement made the payment a gift if the condition failed. Their question proves that an ordinary waived deed collects nothing, even from unencumbered property. The Rivash, siman 382, also states that such a deed is mere pottery. This is the primary ruling. A handwritten obligation differs. Since it serves only as evidence and never encumbers purchasers, the Ir Shushan and Sema correctly hold that its signer may reuse it. A formal witnessed deed became void when its lien ended and requires new testimony. A handwritten instrument is renewed by the debtor's own redelivery, as though he signed anew. Delivery of a witnessed deed can likewise create effect under the view that delivery witnesses validate, and a litigant's admission of delivery is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. If the handwriting says that any bearer may collect, or it is one of our commercial notes, all agree that it may be reused. The Sema limits reuse to the same day because otherwise the old date might create an improper priority. This requires qualification. A handwritten obligation has no priority merely from its date unless delivery at that time is proved, because of possible collusion. If the new loan occurred before witnesses, it may be reused even on another day. If redelivery itself occurred before witnesses and delivery witnesses create a full deed, then the original deed's lien was waived and the instrument cannot be reused even that day. Regarding commercial notes, the amount and maturity should be written near the signature so that the note cannot be cut and transformed. It need not be precisely opposite the signature, but neither may it be positioned so far above or below that alteration becomes possible. A signature at the bottom with space above is dangerous because one must not sign at the head of a blank sheet. The Ir Shushan also discusses whether a person holding a commercial note as deposit or pledge may claim up to its value through a migo of return. His distinction from siman 64 requires study. Since the note is payable to its holder, he may say he returned the original and that this is another note purchased or lent to him, unless identifying marks prove otherwise. See simanim 64 and 69 for the fuller law.
