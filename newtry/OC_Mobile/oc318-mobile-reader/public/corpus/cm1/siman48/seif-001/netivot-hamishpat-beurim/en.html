Its lien was forgiven. Sema holds that once a document has been paid and its lien extinguished as against purchasers, it may also lose document status as against the borrower himself. The Shach in siman 43 cites Rivash, who sought to prove from this that an antedated document is likewise wholly invalid even against a total denial. In my view that proof can be answered. The dispute about an antedated document concerns a borrower who denies ever authorizing or undertaking the debt and thereby contradicts the witnesses. The stringent view says a document not written according to the Sages' required form has no testimonial status and is testimony from writing; the lenient view says it still contains valid witness testimony against a total denial, although the borrower may plead payment because it lacks full document status. But if the borrower admits authorizing the document and merely says he never actually borrowed, that the document fell from him, or that it was an accommodation, even the lenient view agrees he can be believed through a migo of payment. The dispute concerned contradiction of the witnesses, not every defense that defeats the document. Accordingly the Gemara can properly ask, regarding a document whose lien was extinguished, that it is in any event antedated. The second loan was not witnessed by the original signers, so the borrower's defenses concern the later use of the old document and do not contradict the original witnesses. Those defenses are available even under the opinion that an antedated deed retains testimonial force against direct contradiction. This also clarifies the debate between Sema and Shach. The lender's claim must be that after the first debt was repaid and the document returned to him, the borrower borrowed again using that same document, and the borrower answers that it fell from him, was left in trust, or raises another defense. Ran explains that although we rule that delivery witnesses create the legal act, signed witnesses can function as delivery witnesses when the instrument is found in the recipient's possession, because that possession demonstrates delivery. Therefore the old signed witnesses can still support the fact of renewed delivery between the direct parties. The problem is collection from purchasers. The publicity created by the first loan was extinguished upon repayment. A second loan using the same document did not recreate that original public notice unless the relevant delivery was itself witnessed in a way that generated new publicity. Sema therefore has strong grounds to say the document can still be used against unencumbered property, even though it cannot collect from purchasers. The opposing view says that once document status was destroyed with respect to encumbered property, the document is wholly weakened even against the borrower and he may plead payment or other defenses. This also answers the Shach's discussion of Nedarim. Even a reused document can remain a valid instrument for acquisition where the relevant migo is absent, for example within the due date or under a credibility clause. The signed witnesses are treated like delivery witnesses, and once the borrower's contrary migo is blocked the instrument can function like an ordinary written obligation. The Gemara in Nedarim can therefore be established with a document lacking property responsibility, or one expressly limited from collecting against purchasers, so the problem of an antedated lien does not arise. The proof from the guarantor case in siman 57 is likewise not decisive. At minimum renewed obligation requires actual acquisition and hand-to-hand delivery of the document. Although we do not require a fresh writing and delivery formula in every case where the instrument is already in the lender's name, delivery itself is necessary; without it there is neither acquisition of the document nor the equivalent of delivery witnesses. A handwritten obligation can also fail when reused after payment because later presentation of the original delivery witnesses could create purchaser loss, making it analogous to an antedated instrument. For practical law, Sema's view is primary: because signed witnesses can serve as the equivalent of delivery witnesses, a properly redelivered document may support collection from unencumbered property, but it cannot reach purchasers after the original publicity was extinguished. The Shach's further limitation requiring witnesses to the second loan is understood where there is no remaining presumption against payment; if the second loan is still within its term or other circumstances rule out payment, the priority analysis changes accordingly.
And he delivered it to him before witnesses. The Shach requires that the witnesses to delivery be the same persons who signed the document. At first this appears difficult. If their original signatures attested only the first loan, why should their later presence cure the second loan? But after analysis his view is correct. Collection from purchasers through signed witnesses depends on treating the signers as though they testify that their document created the publicity upon which purchasers rely. If those same signers witness the second delivery, then when the court later relies on their signatures it is true to say that these witnesses also generated public notice of the renewed loan. If entirely different persons witnessed the second delivery, the later court would be relying on the old signers as though their signatures proved publicity for the second loan, which would be false. The original publicity had already ended with payment, while the new publicity came from different witnesses who may not then be before the court. The document would thus be internally misleading. Accordingly the same signers should serve as the delivery witnesses for the renewed loan.
