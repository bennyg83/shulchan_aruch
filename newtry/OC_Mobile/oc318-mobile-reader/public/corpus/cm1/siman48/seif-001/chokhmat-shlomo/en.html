Seif 1. A scribe who comes to write a document. At the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, Rabbi Yochanan discusses one who finds a promissory note in the marketplace. At first glance the sugya is difficult to understand. Surely the questioner knows that even something highly unusual can occasionally happen, while Rabbi Yochanan said only that if it actually happened that repayment occurred on the very day of the loan, a particular consequence follows. What then is the basis of the Gemara's question? One possible explanation is that the passage depends on the dispute whether we apply the presumption that a person does not pay within the due period, and whether a standard thirty-day loan is considered within its term. The questioner held that a person does not pay within the term and that the thirty days are included. He therefore asked why Rabbi Yochanan must discuss repayment on the same day. Even within the term repayment is ordinarily presumed not to occur. Yet when a document is found and thereby impaired, we nevertheless suspect even an unusual repayment. Thus, if repayment on the same day is possible at all, even though rare, we should likewise suspect it once the document fell and became impaired. The fact that Rabbi Yochanan singled out same-day repayment would seem to show that such repayment is normally impossible. The Gemara answers that it is merely rare, and even after a document falls we do not go so far as to suspect that extreme case. The concern within the ordinary term is different because payment within the term, though uncommon, is not virtually nonexistent. A better explanation is based on the rule that in monetary matters we generally do not follow a majority, but we do disregard an exceptionally remote minority. A promissory note that was used for a loan and then repaid ordinarily loses its lien only if the money was given as repayment. If the money was given merely as a deposit, the lien is not waived. Now where payment on the same day is extraordinarily uncommon, we might say that even if the borrower handed money to the lender, he surely intended it only as a temporary deposit and planned to receive it back shortly, but did not say so explicitly because he feared the lender would refuse to accept it. This resembles the rule concerning one who pays for land he knows does not belong to the seller, where the money is treated as a deposit because a person knows the land is not the seller's and therefore does not truly intend a gift. If same-day repayment were a virtually nonexistent occurrence, the minute possibility that the borrower actually intended repayment could be ignored even in monetary law as an exceptionally remote minority. The lien would therefore not be treated as waived. Even if the lender mistakenly thought the payment was final and intended to waive the lien, the borrower did not intend to acquire that waiver, and it would also be a mistaken waiver, for had the lender known the money was only a deposit he would not have waived the lien. Thus Rabbi Yochanan's rule would prove that people sometimes do repay on the same day. The Gemara answers that we do not say same-day repayment never occurs. It is a minority occurrence but a recognizable one. Ordinarily we still follow the majority and presume no payment where that preserves the strength of an existing document, much as a migo can sometimes be used to collect when supported by a document. But where we know that payment was in fact made on this very note, the note has already been impaired. This resembles the rule in siman 58 concerning a creditor who claims money received was for another debt: once we see that money was given, the note's strength is weakened. Here, all the more so, when payment was made on this document, it is as though there were no longer an unimpaired note. We therefore cannot follow the majority to extract from purchasers and must take account of the possibility that the lien was waived. This, with Heaven's help, appears to me the correct explanation of the sugya. In my third edition to Choshen Mishpat 287, in my responsum to Kalimaya, I explained the passage differently.
