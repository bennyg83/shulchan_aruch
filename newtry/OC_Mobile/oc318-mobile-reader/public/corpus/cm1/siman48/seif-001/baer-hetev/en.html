The Ir Shushan rules that where a document signed by witnesses can no longer be used to collect from encumbered property, it cannot be used even to collect from unencumbered property, because once the testimony is void regarding the lien, part of the testimony has been voided and therefore all of it is void. The Sema disagrees and brings proof from the Rosh concerning one who repaid part of a debt and deposited the document with a third party: if the stipulated time passed and payment was made, the creditor may again collect from unencumbered property. Although the Rosh appears to hold that a document whose lien has already been extinguished cannot collect even from unencumbered property without a new writing and transfer, the Mechaber and Rama do not rule that way in siman 55, seif 5. He gives an additional proof from the Gemara concerning a claim based on an authenticated document where the defendant says it was a trust document or was paid. This resembles an antedated document, concerning which the Rif and Rashi rule that one cannot collect from encumbered property but may collect from unencumbered property. Even authorities who disagree there do so because of a penalty for antedating, but where there is no improper priority they should agree. The Ir Shushan also writes that in contemporary promissory notes, the amount and due date should be written on the reverse directly opposite the signature, lest one cut away the rest and write whatever he wishes next to the signature. The Sema says 'directly opposite' is not literal. If the amount is written a line or two above the signature, cutting it away would leave only one blank line, and a document written on a single line is invalid. Likewise, if it is written immediately below the signature, cutting it away would be evident, since people do not ordinarily sign a note at the very bottom edge of the paper. Thus it is enough that the amount not be written far above or below the signature. The Shach, however, agrees with the Ir Shushan that once a document's lien has been extinguished it cannot be used even against unencumbered property. He rejects the Sema's proofs and cites the Tur and other authorities, as well as the Bach and Rivash, who treat such a document as a mere shard. He also rejects the Sema's statement concerning writing the amount below the signature, because one could cut it off and write new text above the signature, turning the signature into an ordinary signature rather than that of a promissory note. See also siman 69 concerning a blank note.

The Ir Shushan rules regarding a promissory note that if witnesses testify that it was deposited or pledged with the holder, the holder cannot claim up to its value through a migo that he returned it, because he would be afraid to claim that he returned it. At first glance this is difficult from siman 64, where one who holds another's document is believed through a migo that he returned it or that it was lost by unavoidable accident. One may answer that there he does not seek to collect through the document, since he lacks writing and transfer; he merely holds it so that he can claim that he seized it during the debtor's lifetime. There is therefore no comparable fear. The Shach nevertheless concludes there that even in that case he is not believed through a migo of return or unavoidable loss. Still, the Ir Shushan's ruling requires study. Here the note is written in a form that can be used by its possessor, so the holder can claim, 'I returned the deposited note; this is a different note that I originally lent upon or purchased from another,' and could thereby collect from the debtor or sell it to others. It is strained to assume that the witnesses would identify this particular note by some distinguishing mark. The Baer Heitev adds that even if witnesses cannot identify the actual note, if they know its amount this may be comparable to a receipt attested by witnesses, because witness testimony creates publicity and prevents resale. See the Rama to siman 51 and the Shach there. The Taz writes that where a person holds a promissory note stating that a certain object was deposited with him and a dispute arises over the identity of the object, the creditor is believed with an oath through the migo that he could say the object was lost through unavoidable circumstances, like a lender holding collateral, as stated in siman 296. Even a custodian who received a deposit under a written instrument is believed to say that he returned it through the migo of unavoidable loss. The Taz therefore considers this clear and notes that some judges err in this matter.

The Shach writes that the Mordechai's wording implies that this works specifically where the document was redelivered before the witnesses who originally signed it, or where there had been no signing witnesses at all and only delivery witnesses, and it is now redelivered before other witnesses. But if there were signing witnesses and it is now delivered before different witnesses, the new delivery does not help. The reason appears to be that the signed witnesses testify that the debtor owes the creditor, whereas in truth that document had already become void. It is therefore intrinsically false, and even Rabbi Eliezer, who generally validates a document through delivery witnesses, agrees that delivery witnesses do not validate an instrument that is false on its face. According to this, the Rama stated the rule too briefly.
