Since its lien was forgiven, etc. Ir Shushan writes that with a document signed by witnesses that can no longer be used to collect from encumbered property, one cannot even collect from unencumbered property. Since the testimony has been nullified regarding the lien, part of the testimony is void and therefore the whole testimony is void. In my view one can still collect from unencumbered property. A proof appears from the Rosh in the fourth chapter of Nedarim, concerning one who paid part of his debt and deposited the document with a third party. The Rosh writes that if he failed to come at the appointed time and later paid, the creditor may return and collect from unencumbered property. Although in Perishah and Derishah to siman 55 I wrote that the Rosh appears to hold that a document already paid cannot later be used to collect from unencumbered property without writing and delivery, Rama and the Mechaber there in seifim 5 and 6 do not rule that way and state generally that collection may be made. Another proof comes from the Gemara and from the Tur and Mechaber at the end of siman 83. If one sues another with an authenticated document and the defendant says, 'This document is a trust document or has been paid,' and the claimant says, 'That is true, but I had another valid document against you and it was lost,' his claim is ineffective because he himself admits this document was never held as proof of the loan. Here, by contrast, he claims that after repayment the borrower again borrowed on the same document and instructed him to retain it as proof for collection. This resembles an antedated document, where Rif and Rashi rule that one cannot collect from encumbered property but may collect from unencumbered property, and the borrower cannot say, 'I paid,' as explained in siman 43. Even Ri and the Rosh, who are stricter with an antedated document, do so because they penalize the creditor for allowing a document to be written with an improper earlier date, lest he collect from purchasers from that date. Here the document was originally written and delivered lawfully, so no such penalty applies. He should therefore be able to collect from unencumbered property and the borrower should not be believed to say, 'I paid and did not borrow again on it.' If so, why is the document still in the creditor's hand? There is a presumption that a person does not leave an unpaid witness-signed document in another's possession. Ir Shushan further writes that if the borrower issued a handwritten obligation, which serves merely as proof and cannot collect from encumbered property, he may reuse it for a later loan. This is correct. In my view, however, that applies specifically where the second loan occurred on the same day. Otherwise, even though the writing cannot collect from encumbered property, an earlier oral loan can have priority over a later documented debt when collecting from unencumbered property, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 104:13. Even according to those who disagree there, everyone agrees that an earlier oral loan precedes a later oral loan. Reusing the old writing on a later date could therefore create an improper priority. Ir Shushan also discusses the commercial promissory notes commonly used in our time, saying that the sum and due date should be written on the reverse directly opposite the signature, lest someone cut away the original reverse writing and write against the signature whatever amount he wishes. What he means is that the holder could write opposite the signature that the signer became obligated for double the amount originally written below it. The words 'directly opposite' are not literal. If the amount is written slightly above the signature on the second line, there is no concern it will be cut off, because then only one blank line would remain above the signature, and we require enough space above a signature to write a valid document; a one-line document is invalid, as explained in simanim 45 and 46. Likewise, if the amount is written immediately below the signature, there is no concern he will cut it off, because the forgery would be evident. People do not sign commercial notes at the very bottom edge of the paper. Therefore one need not be exact that the amount appear directly opposite the signature, only that it not be far above or far below it. Ir Shushan further writes that if Reuven produces a commercial note signed by Shimon, while the entire reverse side is blank and there is no amount or due date written above the signature, the note has a weak presumption because people do not ordinarily trust a lender with such a completely blank instrument. The borrower is therefore believed to say, 'It fell from me and you found it.' He states this as obvious, but I have great doubt. The halachah follows Rava in chapter Get Pashut, that we do not ordinarily suspect loss of a document. Nor is this presumption that a person would never trust another with a large blank instrument so strong. The Gemara and poskim discuss a borrower who claims a document was merely a trust document, and they do not distinguish even where the stated amount is enormous. Although the borrower is not believed, that is not because people never trust one another with such documents, but because he lacks the power to undermine a document held by the opposing party. The Mechaber appears to distinguish between trusting a friend generally and giving a creditor a blank instrument by which he could demand an enormous sum. In my humble opinion the opposite is more plausible. When a person urgently needs money and the lender refuses to advance it unless given a blank commercial note so that he can later write the actual amount of his damage or other details that cannot presently be specified, a borrower may indeed trust him and voluntarily bind himself. This too falls under the principle that the borrower is servant to the lender in a time of great pressure. Ir Shushan further writes that even if the borrower admits he borrowed such-and-such amount on the note but says he repaid, he is believed. In my view that does not follow even from his own reasoning. Once he admits that he originally delivered this blank note to the lender and borrowed on it, he himself testifies that he trusted the lender. How can we believe his claim of repayment through a migo that he could have denied ever borrowing, when according to his own admission he is outside the supposed presumption that people do not trust another with such a note?
