And he may write everything, and so forth. Gittin 26 teaches that the first Tanna holds that even in loan and commercial documents one may not write the operative portions in advance, while R. Elazar validates them all except divorce documents. Rashi explains that R. Elazar validates only the standard form, not the operative particulars, and that even the standard form of a get is invalid. The Gemara later challenges the ruling that the halachah follows R. Elazar from the concern that such writing appears false, and Rashi explains the challenge as applying to the standard form of other documents. Tosafot ask how merely writing a blank standard form can appear false, and therefore explain that R. Elazar validates even the operative text of ordinary documents, which is why the Gemara asks that it appears false. Shach attempts to defend Rashi by saying that even the standard form can appear false, but his words are strained, as the later authorities note. What falsehood is there in writing words that do not yet identify any borrower or lender? In truth, Rashi can be explained simply. There are two possible reasons to prohibit writing the operative portion of ordinary documents: either we decree ordinary documents because of a get, or the writing is independently invalid because it appears false. The prohibition against writing even the standard form of a get is itself a decree lest one come to write the operative portion. Therefore, if the operative portion of ordinary documents is prohibited only as a decree because of a get, then their standard forms remain permitted, because extending the decree from get to other documents and then from operative text to standard form would be a decree upon a decree. But if the operative portion of an ordinary document is independently invalid because it appears false, then just as Chazal prohibited the standard form of a get lest one write the operative text, they could equally prohibit the standard form of ordinary documents. The Gemara's question is therefore precise: if the reason is that it appears false, the standard form too should be prohibited as a protective decree. The answer is that we are not concerned with appearance of falsehood. Thus the operative part of ordinary documents is prohibited only by decree because of a get, and we do not extend that decree to the standard form. Shach concludes that one should be stringent like Rashi. Even aside from his argument, however, I am astonished that the great Rambam was overlooked. Rambam, Hilchot Gerushin 3:7, rules that one may not write the operative portion of a get in advance, but the standard form was permitted for the benefit of scribes. Maggid Mishneh and Kesef Mishneh explain that he follows the first Tanna of the Mishnah. According to that, in all other documents too, even loans and commercial instruments, the operative portion may not be written in advance. Raavan likewise rules like the first Tanna, and Baal HaIttur at the beginning of Ma'amar Shishi brings proof from the Mishnah that one does not write a deed for a purchaser until the seller is present. If writing the operative part in advance were allowed, what novelty would there be? Rather, even the operative text itself may not be written because it appears false. It is therefore surprising that Mechaber set aside these Geonim, especially since his general method is to follow Rambam. One must say that Mechaber understood Rambam differently. Rambam never states anywhere in Hilchot Malveh or Hilchot Edut that the operative portion of ordinary documents may not be written in advance. Indeed, in Hilchot Malveh chapter 23 he says only that a document is not written for the lender unless the borrower is with him. Maggid Mishneh explains that the lender might say, "Write and sign it, and let it remain with you," and so forth. If Rambam held that even the unsigned operative text itself was prohibited, that would have been the more basic law to state. It therefore appears that Rambam understood the Gemara as follows. When Rav said the halachah follows R. Elazar, there was uncertainty whether he meant the first version of R. Elazar or the second version, for two Tannaitic formulations were given in R. Elazar's name. The Gemara attempted to prove that he meant the first formulation, since according to the second there would be the problem of appearance of falsehood, but rejected the proof by saying that we are not concerned with such appearance. The matter therefore remains uncertain. With a get, where extending the prohibition from operative text to standard form is merely rabbinic, Rambam rules leniently and permits the standard form. Conversely, with ordinary documents, once we rule that appearance of falsehood is not itself a concern and the only issue is the rabbinic decree of ordinary documents because of a get, we again rule leniently and follow the second formulation of R. Elazar, validating even the operative portion. This accords with Tosafot. Thus Rambam's silence concerning ordinary documents is deliberate. Nevertheless, the matter requires study.
Because once its lien was extinguished, and so forth. Sema cites Ir Shushan as holding that once the lien of a paid note has been extinguished, the note cannot be used again even against unencumbered property. Sema disagrees. Rosh, Nedarim 27, discusses one who deposited his note with a third party and stipulated that if he failed to appear by a certain time the note should be returned to the creditor. Rosh writes that although the lien of the note had been extinguished, the creditor can nevertheless collect from unencumbered property. Sema therefore holds that, as between creditor and borrower, the note remains a valid written instrument: the borrower cannot simply claim, "I paid," although the note no longer reaches encumbered property. In this respect it is even stronger than an antedated note, concerning which Tosafot and Rosh hold the borrower may claim payment. Taz, and especially Shach, argue at length against Sema. Shach says Rosh's case is different because the borrower does not deny the underlying facts. Moreover, Rosh ultimately explains that the borrower had effectively gifted back the money already paid; otherwise a written obligation cannot be reacquired merely by handing over the document without the required writing and transfer. Shach also cites the guarantor case in Bava Batra 32b: a guarantor who paid the lender and held the lender's note against the borrower was met with the borrower's claim that he had already reimbursed the guarantor. The guarantor replied that he had lent him the money again. Rashbam treats the original lien as extinguished and the borrower as believed. Baal HaTerumot and Rabbenu Yerucham state this expressly, as does Rivash, responsum 382. Ramban, Ran, and Rashba also worked hard to explain the deposited-note case by assuming that the first payment was then given back as a gift. Had a paid note remained enforceable against unencumbered property, that entire discussion would have been unnecessary. Although Shach's position initially appears compelling, Sema's words are those of Rama his teacher, and Rama himself appears below in siman 55, and in Darkei Moshe there, to hold that one may collect from unencumbered property. We must therefore clarify their reasoning. In the present gloss, Mordechai and Rama rule that if the borrower redelivers the old note before witnesses, it can be effective anew. If so, as against the borrower personally, even private redelivery should function as an admission by a litigant, which is like one hundred witnesses. Shach himself writes similarly in seif katan 2 from Tosafot Gittin and Baal HaTerumot. Public delivery is required for the publicity needed to affect purchasers, but as between the parties the borrower's own delivery should suffice. Otherwise, even a note with no property warranty could never be reused after payment, because its lien too would have been extinguished. Yet the Gemara's reason that one may not reuse a paid note is precisely the extinguished lien. Why not say instead that it would be antedated? The novelty must include a note without recourse to encumbered property, where antedating creates no such problem. This shows that, as between the parties, the written obligation itself is not wholly destroyed. Shach responds by distinguishing a note with a property lien: because it is invalid in part, against purchasers, it is invalid entirely, even against the borrower. Rama and Sema reject that principle. Why should invalidity against purchasers destroy it against the borrower? An antedated note, according to Rashi and Rambam, proves the opposite: it is invalid against purchasers but valid against the borrower. Rosh's case in Nedarim therefore remains evidence for Sema. Shach says there the borrower did not deny the debt, but that does not resolve the acquisition problem. If the returned note were mere worthless paper, handing it over could not transfer the repaid money or recreate a debt. The fact that returning it can have legal effect shows that the note retains operative significance at least against the borrower. Similarly, Rivash's powerful question on those Rishonim who validate an antedated note against unencumbered property is resolved. Rivash asks why the Gemara needs the doctrine of an extinguished lien at all, since a reused note would in any event be antedated. The answer is that these Rishonim also hold that extinguishment invalidates only the purchaser-facing lien, not every aspect of the obligation, so the Gemara still needed that reason. The guarantor case proves nothing against this. There, the lender's note against the borrower was handed to the guarantor after the guarantor paid it. Even if the borrower himself handed to the guarantor a note originally written to the lender, why should that make the borrower newly obligated to the guarantor? The document was never written or signed as an obligation to the guarantor. A note made by Reuven to Shimon cannot simply be handed by Reuven to Levi and thereby become a written obligation to Levi. Therefore, in that case the original lien is wholly extinguished and the guarantor has no renewed written claim. Likewise, Ramban, Rashba, and Ran's explanations of the third-party case do not necessarily show that a reused note is void even against the borrower. Their discussion follows views according to which delivery witnesses do not create operative force in ordinary documents. Rav in Nedarim says the law does not follow R. Yehudah because asmachta does not acquire, implying that absent asmachta the third party could return the note. But Rav in Gittin 87b follows R. Meir with respect to documents and does not treat delivery witnesses as creating the instrument. R. Yehudah too holds that signature witnesses are decisive. Therefore they needed a different mechanism, such as treating the repaid money as a gift. According to our ruling like R. Elazar that delivery witnesses are effective in ordinary documents, however, the borrower's redelivery can renew enforceability against himself. The same observation answers an otherwise obvious question. In the third-party case the custodian normally returns the deposited item in the presence of witnesses. If delivery witnesses alone could recreate the lien, why did all those authorities struggle to avoid the problem of extinguishment? Because the sugya itself follows those who do not apply R. Elazar's doctrine to ordinary documents. There is therefore no proof from Baal HaTerumot either, especially since at the end of Shaar 13 he inclines toward the view that delivery witnesses do not create ordinary documents. Under that approach, an extinguished note is indeed ineffective even against the borrower. But once we rule that delivery witnesses do create the document, Sema's logic returns. Even so, I would qualify Sema. Rosh's statement that the note can collect from unencumbered property may mean only that it prevents a denial that the renewed loan ever occurred. If the borrower says, "I never borrowed from you again," the creditor may be believed, because the returned note still has enough legal character to support the claim, perhaps together with a migo that the original loan was never paid. But if the borrower admits a second loan and claims that he later paid it, he may be believed. Most authorities hold that a document incapable of reaching encumbered property is susceptible to a payment claim. All the more so here. Thus Rosh's phrase "collects from unencumbered property" need not mean that the borrower can never plead payment. This is in fact necessary within Rosh itself. His explanation assumes that when the custodian returns the note, the borrower becomes bound again. But that renewed liability arises only at the later time when the condition is breached and the note is returned. If the old date were fully operative, the note would be antedated. Rosh elsewhere follows Rabbenu Yonah that an antedated note is invalid for collection from purchasers and allows a payment plea, although it still has sufficient character to defeat a claim of "I never borrowed." Accordingly, Rosh's point is exactly that the returned note is not worthless paper; it supports a renewed oral-level obligation and defeats complete denial, but it need not bar a payment defense. This removes the supposed conflict between Rosh and Baal HaTerumot. Baal HaTerumot says that after the lien is extinguished the borrower may claim payment. He does not necessarily say he may deny the renewed loan altogether. The two positions can therefore stand together. The Yerushalmi at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia sheds further light. It gives two explanations why one may not reuse a note after payment, even when all occurred on the same day: one says the property lien did not attach properly; another says the witnesses did not sign concerning this later loan. Under the first reason, Sema's view is plausible, because the defect concerns the lien against property. Under the second, even collection from unencumbered property is problematic, because there is no signed testimony at all concerning the later loan. Which rationale does the Bavli adopt? It appears to follow the first. The Bavli's language is explicitly "because its lien was extinguished." Moreover, under the second reason the note would remain invalid even if it had no property warranty, yet the Gemara asks why we need the extinguishment rationale when the reused note is antedated. The question is meaningful only if the essential defect is the property lien. One must still understand the second Yerushalmi explanation. Witnesses who sign a loan document do not necessarily see the actual loan, since a note can be written for the borrower even when the lender is absent. Their testimony is inherently conditional upon future delivery. The same is true of a get: witnesses who sign do not testify that divorce has already occurred. Their signatures testify that when the instrument is properly delivered, it can take legal effect. It is therefore possible to say that their signatures refer not to one unique loan but to any loan made that same day through delivery of that document. If the borrower borrows, pays, and borrows again on the same day by redelivering the same note, the original signatures can still relate to the later loan. The defect then lies only in the extinguished lien. This logic cannot extend to a later day. The witnesses who wrote that the loan was made today cannot possibly have intended to testify to a loan occurring tomorrow. They would thereby be making themselves witnesses to falsehood and creating an antedated instrument. Therefore, if the renewed loan occurs on a later day, the second Yerushalmi rationale is certainly correct: the signatures do not address that loan, and the document is effectively without witnesses. The Bavli's question, "why not say it is antedated?" means precisely this. If the second loan occurred later, the note is invalid because the witnesses never testified to it. The Gemara's answer therefore establishes the discussion as concerning a second loan on the same day. In that setting the signatures can relate to any same-day delivery, and the sole issue is the extinguished lien. This neatly resolves Rivash's question. It also explains why the Rishonim discussing the third-party case did not simply rely on delivery witnesses. There the renewed effect ordinarily occurs after the original day, when the signatures no longer relate to the new obligation. Even if delivery witnesses are present, the signed text is internally false as to the timing of the new loan and resembles a document forged within itself. The solution had to be that the original payment was converted into a gift or some equivalent mechanism, rather than treating the old signatures as testimony to a later loan. Accordingly, Sema's law should be limited to payment and reborrowing on the same day, as in the Shulchan Aruch's case. There the signed witnesses can still relate to the renewed loan, and only the lien against purchasers has been extinguished. Against the borrower, however, the note retains enough force to defeat a claim that there was no renewed loan, although he may still be able to claim later payment. This also accords with Rashi, Bava Metzia 17, who describes the effect of extinguishment primarily as loss of the purchaser-facing lien and implies that, as between the parties, a basic loan obligation remains. There is a further issue. If a note is written with a later date, for example the loan occurs in Nissan and the note is deliberately dated Sivan, may the borrower pay in the interim and then borrow again on the note before Sivan? Since the lien against purchasers does not begin until the written date, perhaps there is nothing yet to extinguish. On the other hand, perhaps the lien has already begun in a contingent form and can be extinguished even before its future operative date. At first glance one might prove from the Gemara's inference that people sometimes repay a loan on the very day it is made. R. Yehudah says that a paid note cannot be reused. The Gemara asks: if the second loan were on another day, it would simply be antedated; therefore it must mean the same day, proving same-day repayment occurs. Why not say the original loan was in Nissan, the document was intentionally postdated to Tishrei, the borrower repaid in the interim, and wished to borrow again before Tishrei? The note would not be antedated for the later borrowing, because collection from purchasers begins only in Tishrei, yet its first lien could still have been extinguished. This seems to show that even before the written date, extinguishment already applies. One can answer that in R. Yochanan's era, when receipts were written, a deliberately postdated document was not ordinarily valid unless the note itself stated that it had been postdated. Bava Batra 171 explains that later practice changed after safeguards concerning receipts were instituted. Thus R. Yochanan's statement cannot ordinarily be established as an unmarked postdated note. Still, there remains a question regarding a note dated only by year or month. Such a note does not create purchaser liability until the end of that period. Perhaps one could repay and reborrow within the period without any antedating problem. Ritva in Ketubot indeed says R. Yehudah's rule can arise where the document states only the year and the loan is repaid and renewed within that year. How then can Bava Metzia infer same-day repayment? The answer may be that the Gemara's initial question assumes ordinary documentary practice, in which a precise day is normally written. A document recording only a year or month is unusual, and the Gemara would not explain an unqualified statement by an atypical case. Alternatively, the Gemara infers from the language "because its lien was extinguished" rather than "because there are no witnesses": if same-day reborrowing were so rare that witnesses would never contemplate it, the document would simply lack testimony to the second loan. The answer teaches that same-day repayment is sufficiently possible that the original signatures can be understood as supporting another same-day delivery, yet not sufficiently common to create concern every time a document is found. In any event, a handwritten obligation, which Shach himself agrees can be reused, and a formal note containing no lien on encumbered property, can certainly be reused on the same day. On the analysis above, a postdated note or one dated only by year may likewise be reusable during the relevant period, subject to the distinctions stated.
And he delivered it to him separately, and so forth. Shach cites a version requiring that the very witnesses who signed the document also serve as the delivery witnesses, and explains that otherwise the document would be internally false. I do not understand this. If the signatures relate only to the first loan and not to the renewed one, then even if those same signatories later witness delivery, the signatures themselves still speak falsely with respect to the second loan. Consider a person who borrowed from one Yosef ben Shimon, repaid him, and had the note returned. Could he then borrow from another Yosef ben Shimon merely because the second delivery occurred before the original signatories? Of course not. The signatures never attested to a debt owed to the second person. We must therefore say, as I explained at length in discussing the Yerushalmi, that the witnesses initially attest in general to every loan made that day through delivery of this instrument. If so, the document is not internally false. Ordinary documents rely upon signing witnesses even though the actual delivery may occur before different witnesses, and no one calls that internally forged merely because the signatories do not know the delivery witnesses. The same applies here. The signatories gave general testimony concerning the loan embodied in the instrument, and later delivery witnesses establish the operative delivery. If that general testimony does not suffice, even use of the same witnesses for delivery would not cure the defect. The correct reading is therefore the Krakow printing of Mordechai, which requires only that there be delivery witnesses, whoever they are. Rama adopted this reading, and Knesset HaGedolah likewise concludes that it is primary.
