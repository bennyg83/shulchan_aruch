And he may write the entire document, etc. We do not fear the appearance of falsehood merely because the scribe prepares the operative language before the debtor's final instruction. Shach is stringent and says the core operative portion should not be written without the debtor's knowledge. See Tumim for my discussion. Nevertheless, widespread practice is to prepare it without concern, and the custom has solid authorities upon whom to rely.
Since its lien has been waived, etc. Sema holds that although the lien of the document has been waived, the creditor may still collect from unencumbered property, and the borrower cannot plead payment. Shach and Taz hold that the document is entirely ineffective in every respect. In Tumim I discussed this at length and concluded as follows. If the borrower denies that he borrowed again on the same document, while the lender claims that he lent him a second time, and the lender has a migo that he could have said, "You never repaid me at all," then the lender is believed. We do not reject that migo on the ground that the old document is mere worthless pottery. In that respect it still has the force of a document. But if the borrower pleads payment, he is believed, as with a handwritten obligation. All this applies where he borrowed, repaid, and borrowed again on the same day. If he borrowed again on a later day, there is a substantial question whether the instrument has become completely invalid because it would then function as an antedated document. This remains an unresolved legal doubt. If the original deed itself was postdated and the debt was repaid and reborrowed before the date stated in the deed arrived, or if the document stated only the year generally, for example the year 5508, and repayment and reborrowing both occurred within that same year, its law is like repayment and reborrowing on the same day. See Tumim. Sema and Shach agree that with a handwritten obligation one may repay and then borrow again on the same writing. It appears that the same is true of a deed containing no property responsibility at all, because it has the same basic law as a handwritten obligation. See Tumim at length. Shach limits this to a case where the handwritten obligation was not originally delivered in the presence of witnesses. Otherwise, according to the view that delivery witnesses effectuate a deed, it has the full status of a formal instrument and the rule of a waived lien applies as with other deeds. Sema adds that if the second loan occurs on a later day, even a handwritten obligation raises a concern of antedating, because an earlier oral loan can take precedence over a later dated written loan. Shach qualifies this to a place where there is no concern of collusion through writing the note today and antedating it. I discussed this issue above in siman 43 and, with Hashem's help, will discuss it again in siman 65. The further discussion by Sema, Shach, and Taz concerning the customary Polish *mamranot* belongs properly below in siman 69, and I address it there.
And he delivered it in the presence of witnesses, etc. See Tumim. I conclude that if the renewed borrowing occurs on the same day, it is unnecessary that the delivery witnesses be the same persons as the witnesses signed on the deed. Other witnesses to delivery suffice, and the deed is not considered internally forged. If, however, the renewed borrowing occurs on a later day, the document becomes antedated. In that situation there is room to say that everyone agrees it is entirely invalid as internally defective. See Tumim, where I discuss how this may reconcile Rivash, and the matter still requires study. See also siman 55, with Hashem's help.
