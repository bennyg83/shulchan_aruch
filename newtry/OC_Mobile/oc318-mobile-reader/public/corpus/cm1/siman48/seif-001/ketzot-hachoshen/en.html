Its lien was waived. Ir Shushan suggests that a document whose lien was waived may not collect even from the borrower himself. The Sema objects from the Rosh in Nedarim, while the Shach, Taz, and Rivash bring support that such a document is mere pottery and cannot collect at all. Yet the Rosh's language suggests that where the document is transferred through writing and delivery, it can once again become effective. The Shach in siman 55 argues that this cannot be the Rosh's intent, because acquisition of a debt instrument requires the formula, "Acquire it and all the liens contained in it," and once the lien has been waived there is nothing to transfer. I do not find that compelling. When a debt is paid or waived, the original lien is not retroactively revealed never to have existed. Rather, the debtor reacquires or redeems the lien. The lien embodied in the original document can therefore be transferred back through writing and delivery, much as an acquisition document can be transferred through writing and delivery even after it already performed its original function. The transfer can operate upon the document and whatever legal lien can again be made to arise through it. This may require saying that even the personal obligation can be reconstituted where the debtor himself participates in the writing and delivery. In an ordinary sale of a debt instrument, the seller cannot transfer the personal obligation because it has no tangible substance and therefore retains the power of waiver. Here, however, the debtor himself can create a new personal obligation, and the same act that transfers the document can renew the property lien. Thus the Rosh's straightforward language need not be displaced. The proof from the guarantor case in Bava Batra, where the document did not support collection after waiver, does not refute this. There the holder had not acquired the renewed right through proper writing and delivery. Mere possession is not enough. Moreover, as I wrote in siman 83, a document that no longer collects from encumbered property does not support a migo to extract, because its strength as something already collected is gone. Therefore the guarantor could not rely upon it even if some underlying claim remained. The correct conclusion is that ordinary collection cannot proceed on a document whose lien was waived. But where the debtor properly recreates the obligation through writing and delivery, there is substantial basis in the Rosh that it can again have effect.
He cannot borrow again on it. I was uncertain whether witnesses could trace over their old signatures with ink after the first lien was waived, thereby creating new signatures. Gittin discusses a get written not for its intended purpose and then traced over with ink. Rabbi Yehudah treats tracing as new writing in the context of sanctifying Hashem's Name, while the Sages reject it there because it lacks the preferred form of writing. Rav Acha says the get context may differ. At first this suggests that tracing signatures could create fresh signatures. There is also a dispute concerning witnesses unable to sign who write over an outline prepared for them. Yet Tosafot distinguishes this from the rule on Shabbat that writing over writing is not considered new writing. Where the first writing was legally ineffective and the second tracing makes it valid, the second can count as writing. Here the original signatures were fully valid when first written. The later waiver of the lien does not erase their status as prior writing. Once valid writing existed, tracing over it is ordinary writing over writing and does not create a new signature. Therefore retracing the signatures after the lien was waived is ineffective.
The lien was waived. Ir Shushan and the Sema hold that where the debtor's own handwritten obligation was paid, he can later borrow again on that same handwriting. Bach disagrees, and the Shach favors the Sema because the person who originally signed can say that he now obligates himself again through that handwriting, as though he signed anew. The Shach compares it to a creditor's document that is redelivered with valid delivery witnesses. In my view there is no fundamental difference between a debtor's own handwriting and witness signatures. Once the original obligation has been discharged, the original written act has finished its function. A later redelivery of a witness signed document works only because the new delivery occurs before proper delivery witnesses who create the new transfer. A debtor's old handwritten document, merely handed over again without a new operative act of writing or witnessed delivery, is no better than another person's handwriting. The debtor's later admission that he delivered it does not itself transform the old writing into a fresh written obligation. Therefore the Bach's position appears primary. The Shach suggests that even the Bach would agree concerning bearer promissory notes. I do not see why. If the lien was extinguished, the bearer wording itself was already exhausted. One could perhaps justify recurring bearer notes only if their language initially undertook a continuing obligation to whoever holds them each time, much like a conditional recurring obligation. But that depends upon the instrument's wording and cannot be assumed merely from the fact that it is payable to bearer.
For that portion. The Sema, following his view that personal handwriting can be reused, says this works only on the same day, because otherwise the instrument becomes early dated. The Shach argues that an early dated handwritten obligation can still create problems of collusion, and he rejects a responsum that treated such writing as carrying ordinary priority. The Shach is correct that collusion remains a concern. Yet I also agree with the Sema that an early dated handwritten obligation should be considered defective even though it ordinarily does not collect from purchasers by priority. Tosafot concerning a get in the husband's handwriting treats the absence of a reliable date as a substantive defect because the woman might seize property and claim the divorce occurred from the written date. The same kind of concern exists here. Even where movables ordinarily have no priority system, a handwritten obligation may later be combined with clauses or collateral arrangements that create priority consequences. If the writing states an earlier date than the actual renewed loan, the holder may seize and claim the debt arose at that earlier time. Therefore an early dated personal writing cannot simply be reused as though the date were harmless.
To borrow again. The Sema discusses a blank signature. The Shach and Taz rule that a mere blank signature cannot create an obligation merely because a sum is later written above it. Tumim says that if the amount is filled in before the document is brought to beit din, it can be effective. In my view this is incorrect where no prior loan or obligation existed. Even in a witness signed instrument, if the witnesses sign first and the body of the document is written afterward, the writing is invalid. The same applies to a person's own handwriting. His signature preceded the text and never created a written obligation. One cannot say that he trusted the holder to write whatever he wished and thereby authorized the later writing, because such a broad undertaking itself requires a kinyan when it is not made at the time of a loan. The case where a son in law says, "I do not know what is written," and is bound by what he signed is different. There the complete instrument already existed when he signed, and he chose to bind himself to its contents. A blank signature has no contents at the time of signing. However, if a known loan already exists and the debtor gives a blank signature specifically to cover expenses, charges, or related amounts arising from that loan, his authorization can be effective without a separate kinyan because it accompanies the underlying loan. If there is no known loan and the claimant merely produces a blank signature that he filled in later, it creates no obligation under strict law, absent a governing communal enactment.
Before witnesses. The Shach understands Mordechai as requiring that the renewed delivery occur before the original signed witnesses, apparently because otherwise the old signatures would be internally misleading and resemble a forged instrument. I do not see the distinction. If an internally false appearance disqualified the document, even delivery before the original signers would not cure it. A get signed not for the proper purpose remains invalid because its own signatures are defective, even if delivered before witnesses. The better reading follows another version of Mordechai, which says that delivery witnesses are required. Knesset HaGedolah likewise emends the passage to mean that if the document is reused on the same day, the renewed loan can take effect through delivery witnesses. The old signatures then do not falsely attest to the new loan at all. They testified only to the earlier transaction, which has already been discharged. Since no current testimony is attributed to them, there is no concern of an internally forged document in the technical sense. Delivery witnesses create the new operative act. Thus Rama's concise formulation is defensible and need not be restricted to the original signers.
