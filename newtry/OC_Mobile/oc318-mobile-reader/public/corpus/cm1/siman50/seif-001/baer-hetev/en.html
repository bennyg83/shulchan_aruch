I borrowed. If witnesses signed below the writing not merely to authenticate the handwriting but to attest to the substance of the transaction, the debtor is not believed to say "I paid," as explained in siman 69, seif 3. Shach.
I paid. The Shach writes that this implies it is obvious that against a simple handwritten acknowledgment the debtor may claim payment. The Mechaber rules so in siman 69, seif 2. Rama there cites an opinion that he is not believed, so it is somewhat difficult that here Rama states the Tur and Mechaber's rule without dissent. For practical halacha the Shach concludes there that the Mechaber's position is primary. The Bach writes that the debtor cannot claim that the handwriting was merely a memorandum, a joke, or written to avoid appearing wealthy. The Shach says this requires study. The Taz writes that the debtor is believed, and the law is like other handwritten obligations in siman 69. Whenever he has a migo that he paid, he may say the writing was merely a memorandum or sample form, provided he does not deny that the handwriting itself is his.
Judgment. This follows the opinion that one is not believed to claim payment against a written judgment, as explained in siman 39, seif 4. Otherwise he should also be believed to say he never owed the money through a migo that he paid, just as with a handwritten acknowledgment. Sema.
Receipt. The Sema writes that this rule concerns a document that names no particular lender. If it states that he owes money to a named person, or to that person or whoever produces the document, then a later holder must derive his rights from that named person. In such a case, the Sema holds that a receipt in the borrower's hand does not necessarily discharge the debt even if the borrower says that the person who gave him the receipt was acting under the lender's authority and that he paid on the basis of that receipt. Indeed, even if the receipt itself says expressly that the person who gave it acted under the lender's authority, the Sema says this is insufficient, because one person's admission cannot impose a loss upon another and weaken the lender's written claim unless it is independently known that the person actually had authority from the lender. The Shach explains that the Sema's statement applies where the receipt was written by the collector himself in his own handwriting. If witnesses wrote the receipt and expressly attested that he came with the lender's authority, there can be no greater proof than that. The Shach further explains that a receipt is effective where the later purchaser of the document knew at the time of purchase that such a receipt had already been issued. The novelty is that even though the document is payable to whoever produces it, and the receipt was not written on the document itself, once payment was made and a receipt issued the debt was waived. If, however, the purchaser says he did not know of the receipt, the case is treated as though the debtor originally incurred an obligation directly to him. The debtor who paid the original lender caused his own loss. The Shach says this is also the contemporary custom with transferable promissory notes. Moreover, if the purchaser truly lacked knowledge, the case resembles one who bought an object and later discovered it had been stolen: under takanat hashuk he must at least be reimbursed what he paid, as explained in siman 356. Although takanat hashuk ordinarily does not apply to documents, the Shach writes that with commonly traded promissory notes of his era, which are bought and sold even more frequently than movable goods, the rationale plainly applies. Therefore, if the debtor proves that he paid the original lender before the later purchaser acquired the note, the purchaser swears how much he paid and recovers that amount. If he knew of the receipt, or if the receipt was attested by witnesses so the defect was public, he recovers nothing. See Responsa Rashdam, simanim 20 and 22. The Shach further cites Mabit that if the debtor paid part of the debt to the current holder and afterward the original lender himself produces the instrument, the debtor is exempt from the amount already paid even though the holder had no formal authorization. The Taz writes that if the document is payable to a named person "and to anyone who produces it," and the producer had no written assignment from that named person but received the instrument from him, payment made to the producer on the strength of a receipt appears effective. Siman 66, seif 17, records a dispute whether payment to a purchaser of a document discharges the debtor where the purchaser lacked full written assignment and delivery, and where the law is doubtful money cannot be extracted. All the more so here, where the document itself states that payment may be made to whoever produces it. According to the Rosh, no additional authorization is required at all.
Below. Although the Mechaber himself writes the related rule there, Rama adds a novelty here. Even where the obligor never owed anyone anything and merely undertook a new obligation to a future holder, as in siman 40, he becomes bound. The Mechaber in siman 61 did not expressly state that case. It is explicit in a responsum of the Rosh, which compares it to Shaul's promise in the episode of Goliath, where Shaul obligated himself and his property to give great wealth to whoever struck the Philistine, and David later acquired rights based on that undertaking. The Shach notes that Rama's wording, "he became obligated toward him, as explained in siman 40," precisely indicates an obligation voluntarily created where no prior debt existed.
