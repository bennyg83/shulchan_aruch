Since he is believed to say, I paid, etc. This assumes that on a handwritten obligation the debtor may claim payment, as the Mechaber rules in siman 69, seif 2. It is somewhat difficult that the Rama there cites an opinion denying that defense while here he follows the Tur and Mechaber without qualification. In practical law I concluded there in seif katan 8 that the debtor may claim payment. [Citation reference: 60.]
He is believed to say, “I did not borrow,” etc. He cannot, however, claim that the matter never occurred. We do not say that perhaps he did not write the instrument as an obligation, but merely as a memorandum, in jest, or so as not to appear wealthy. The Bach writes this, though his words require study. See what I wrote below in siman 69, seif katan 16.
The same applies to a judgment, etc. This is because one is not believed to claim payment against a written judgment, as explained above in siman 39, seif 10. See what I wrote there in seif katan 26 and 28.
A deed stating that Ploni owes Ploni, or anyone who produces it, etc. This expression is part of the wording of the deed. Therefore the ruling applies specifically where the producer derives his right through the named person. If, however, no person is named and the deed states generally that the obligor became liable to anyone who produces it, he must pay the present producer even if that person does not derive title from the original lender. See siman 61, seif 4, seif katan 6, and siman 66, seif 1, seif katan 10. The Mabit, part 2, siman 266, writes that if the debtor paid part of the debt to a person who produced the instrument, and afterward the original lender produced it, the debtor is exempt to the extent already paid even though the first producer had no authorization.
And if a receipt was written, etc. This refers to the first case, where no lender is named in the instrument. Where it states that the debtor owes Ploni or anyone who produces it, the Sema holds that a receipt in the debtor’s possession does not defeat the instrument unless it is known that the person issuing the receipt had authority from the named lender. If the receipt itself was written by witnesses and expressly states that the recipient acted with that authority, there can be no greater proof. In my view, a receipt issued on a bearer instrument cancels it against a later purchaser only where the purchaser knew of the payment or of the receipt. The Nimukei Yosef means only that a debtor is not liable repeatedly to every successive producer. Once one lawful producer was paid, the original obligation is discharged. But a later purchaser who bought without knowledge of the earlier payment stands as though the debtor had undertaken directly toward him, and the debtor caused his own loss by paying without having the payment written on the instrument itself. This is especially so where the receipt is merely in the original lender’s handwriting, since collusion is possible and a private writing has no publicity. Even payment before witnesses does not necessarily charge an innocent purchaser with knowledge. By contrast, a receipt executed before witnesses has publicity, and the purchaser is deemed to have known of it. This accords with the rule that a debtor may use a witnessed receipt against purchasers of the debt. It is also the commercial custom regarding transferable notes. If such notes were defeated by any hidden payment, no one would purchase them. Moreover, a good faith purchaser is protected by the market regulation, just as one who unknowingly buys stolen movable property is reimbursed. Although the Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch state in siman 60 that market regulation generally does not apply to ordinary debts, transferable notes are commonly traded and cannot be waived by the original creditor, so the regulation does apply. Accordingly, where the debtor proves that he paid the original lender before the present purchaser acquired the note, the purchaser swears how much he paid for it and recovers that amount under siman 356. If he knew of the prior payment, or a witnessed receipt existed, he recovers nothing.
And if he wrote in the deed, etc. The same rule applies where a person obligates and encumbers himself by deed to pay a stated amount to anyone who lends money to Shimon. See responsa Maharan Sasson, simanim 122 and 175, Maharam Alshich, siman 97, and Maharan Chaim, siman 21.
See below, siman 61, seif 10. The Sema says that the Rama merely repeated what the Mechaber himself wrote there. This cannot be correct, especially since the Rama himself directs the reader there. Rather, the Rama teaches that the obligor may bind himself even though he previously owed nothing to anyone and no one had lent him anything, as in siman 40. The Mechaber does not state that point in siman 61. The Rosh, responsa 68:9, proves it from Saul, who bound himself and his property to grant great wealth to whoever struck the Philistine. David struck him, and Saul thereby became obligated to David. Sanhedrin, chapter 2, says that David betrothed Saul’s daughter with that claim. This is exactly reflected in the Rama’s language that he became obligated toward him, as explained in siman 40.
