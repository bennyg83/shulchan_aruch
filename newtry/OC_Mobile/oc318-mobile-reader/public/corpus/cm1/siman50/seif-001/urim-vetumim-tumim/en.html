I borrowed from you one maneh, and so forth. One should not object based on what I wrote above in siman 49, seif 7, that the witnesses to a document can testify which Yosef ben Shimon they meant because they can produce another corrected document. Maggid Mishneh expressly says that absent internal identification their testimony still does not transform the defective note into a written loan. If ordinary documents require the relevant identity to be evident from within, how can a note saying merely "I borrowed from you" be valid? It does not identify the lender internally. Likewise, how can two people named Yosef ben Shimon in one city collect from an outsider if the document itself does not distinguish which of them is the creditor? One cannot simply answer that possession of the note itself identifies the lender. Tosafot in Gittin 24b say that if a man has two wives with the same name, according to R. Meir the get is invalid because it does not identify from within which wife is divorced. Why not say that the wife holding the get is thereby identified? Why would R. Meir require further distinction? The proper explanation is that according to R. Meir, who holds signature witnesses create the legal act, the document must be self-identifying at the time of writing and signing. Later clarification cannot cure that. According to R. Elazar, however, delivery witnesses create the legal act, and therefore it is enough that the identity be clear at the moment of delivery, because that is when the essential force of the document takes effect. Thus, where there are two wives with the same name, delivery of the get to the particular wife makes her identity clear according to R. Elazar, while R. Meir still finds the written text deficient. Likewise in a loan note, since we rule like R. Elazar concerning other documents, delivery to the lender identifies him: the person holding the instrument is the lender to whom it was delivered. But when the problem is the debtor's identity, as where there are two Yosef ben Shimons and the note does not distinguish which one borrowed, even at delivery to the lender it remains unclear which borrower created the obligation. Therefore, even R. Elazar's doctrine cannot cure that deficiency. This distinction fully resolves the matter. I later found that R. Shmuel Kaidanover in Tiferet Shmuel raised this difficulty and did not answer it. According to what I have written, there is no problem at all. This is exactly the difference between R. Meir and R. Elazar. Those authorities who rule like R. Meir regarding monetary documents, yet nevertheless accept the validity of the formula "from you," must presumably follow Ran's view that even according to R. Meir absolute internal identification is not required in ordinary monetary documents.
Whoever produces it may collect, and so forth. Bach writes that if the holder admits that he was not the original lender but says another person lent the money and he later acquired the note from that lender, he may collect without authorization and without written transfer, like a document explicitly payable to whoever produces it. The great Maharit, Choshen Mishpat responsum 23, disagrees. He holds that the formula "from you" is different. It indicates an obligation to the single person to whom the document was initially delivered at the time of the loan, not to every later holder. It is unlike a note saying expressly "to whoever produces it," which explicitly extends the obligation to every holder. Maharit derives this from Tur's wording: the holder says, "You borrowed from me, and I had no need to specify my name." This implies that if he admits someone else made the loan and the note only later came into his hands, he cannot collect without a proper transfer. Tur and Shulchan Aruch also say that the borrower is believed through a migo of payment to claim, "I did not borrow from you; perhaps it fell from the true lender and you found it." If this means only that the borrower does not actually know it fell, but merely says, "I know I never borrowed from you, so presumably you found it," that strongly supports Maharit against Bach. For according to the view that documentary rights can be acquired by mere delivery and that the holder need not prove the transfer, why should the borrower prevail? The creditor could answer, "True, you did not borrow from me. Another person lent to you and transferred this document to me." His possession would suffice. One could force Bach's approach by saying that the borrower asserts with certainty, "I know the true lender lost this note and you found it." He is then believed through his migo of payment. But this is very strained. It is unusual for the borrower to know with certainty that another person's note fell and was found by this claimant. Moreover, the underlying source is Baal HaTerumot in the name of Ramban, and Ramban plainly means even a doubtful claim. Ramban holds, as Ran cites in the first chapter of Gittin concerning the phrase "I obligate myself to you and to whoever comes through you," that because we rule that there is no bereirah in Torah law, a person cannot create an open-ended Torah obligation toward an as-yet unidentified future holder. Under Ramban, proper written transfer is certainly required. Thus the source itself cannot mean Bach's construction. Therefore, practical halachah follows Maharit. A note saying merely "I borrowed from you" does not create an obligation to every later holder. The holder who admits he was not the lender must show the legal basis by which he acquired the claim.
Since he is believed to say, and so forth. R. Shmuel Kaidanover in Tiferet Shmuel writes that Rashbam disagrees because he interprets the phrase "from you" even regarding a handwritten obligation, like the case where one produces the debtor's handwriting. This is no difficulty. Rashbam writes in Bava Batra 176b that a debtor is not believed to say "I paid" against his own written acknowledgment. Therefore there is no migo in his system. Nimukei Yosef, although he himself holds a payment claim is accepted against handwriting, often follows Rashbam's wording in his commentary and may simply have repeated the formulation. Alternatively, as Knesset HaGedolah suggests, the handwritten obligation may contain an express credibility clause. There is therefore no proof of a dispute from Rashbam.
And it fell from him, and so forth. Although we ordinarily do not suspect that a document fell from its owner, that presumption is not so powerful as to defeat every migo. Therefore, where a person produces a note saying "I owe Yosef ben Shimon one maneh," and the borrower says, "I owed the other Yosef ben Shimon and this note fell from him into your hands," he may in some circumstances be believed through the migo that he could have claimed payment. But if there are exactly two people named Yosef ben Shimon in the city and one of them produces against Reuven a handwritten acknowledgment saying, "I owe Yosef ben Shimon one maneh," Reuven cannot use a payment migo to say, "I owed the other Yosef ben Shimon and the note fell from him." A fall from one identified person into the hands of the only other same-named person is not suspected by anyone. This is a sufficiently strong presumption that we do not say a migo against it. This explains Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 33, part 4, law 3. He rules that two same-named creditors can collect from outsiders and the debtor cannot push off the claimant by saying, "I owe the other one; his note fell and you found it." Commentators ask why he specifies a fall from one person, since according to Rava we do not even suspect broader loss in the case of the phrase "from you." According to what I have written the distinction is precise. Baal HaTerumot includes even a handwritten acknowledgment. Against the broader possibility of an ordinary lost note the borrower can sometimes use a migo, but against the highly specific possibility that the only other same-named creditor lost this very instrument to the present claimant, there is no such migo. I later found Sefer Bnei Shmuel says the same, and this is correct practical law.
Specifically when he comes through that particular person. Below in siman 66, seif katan 8, Sema appears to understand that the holder must present authorization or a written transfer from the named creditor. If he merely says he acquired the note by delivery, that is insufficient. Sema therefore asks from Rama below, siman 66, seif 1, who holds that a document of this form can be acquired by delivery alone, and answers that where there is proof of the acquisition, delivery suffices; here the holder lacks proof and therefore needs authorization. Shach questions this, but I do not understand Sema's approach at all. If, according to Rama and Sema, this document is indeed acquired by delivery alone, why should the holder need independent proof? At the end of Bava Batra Abaye and Rava dispute whether one who claims ownership of a document acquired by delivery must prove the transfer. Rava holds no proof is needed. Ramban and Rosh explain that according to the view that delivery alone transfers documentary rights, possession of the document itself is the proof. We require additional proof only because we rule that ordinary documentary transfer requires both writing and delivery. But where delivery alone is legally sufficient, the holder need not bring separate evidence. Thus, if this note is one of the exceptional types transferable by delivery alone, the holder should be believed to say, "I acquired it," merely because it is in his hands. Why then require authorization or written transfer? The correct explanation is different. If the holder says, "I acquired it from the named creditor," then he indeed "comes through" that creditor. Since the document is in his possession and this type of document is transferable by delivery, he is believed that it was transferred to him. That is legally equivalent to having authorization or written assignment; Rava does not require proof of the transfer. The limitation applies where he does not claim to have acquired the claim from the named creditor at all. Suppose the note says, "I owe Reuven and anyone who comes through him," but the present holder says, "Reuven never lent you anything. I myself lent you the money from the outset and used this note." He has no document lien. The note was written only for Reuven or someone deriving rights from Reuven. It does not say that some entirely different person can make the original loan and thereby obtain the document's lien. The present holder therefore has at most an oral loan. If, however, the note expressly says that the debtor is obligated "whether through Reuven or not through Reuven," meaning to anyone who will make the loan and hold the instrument, then the document itself extends to every possible lender. If Shimon, rather than Reuven, actually made the loan and the note was delivered to Shimon, he may collect under the document without deriving title from Reuven at all. The wording expressly encompasses him. This resolves Sema's question without difficulty. It also revives Ramban's conceptual challenge: how can a person obligate himself to an unidentified future person when Torah law rejects bereirah? Ran and Nimukei Yosef answer regarding "to you and whoever comes through you" that there is no bereirah because the obligation can attach successively to every holder in the chain; the first creditor can transfer to a second, who can transfer to a third, and so on. But where the note means, "I obligate myself to whichever person will eventually lend me money," only one person can be the original lender. Once the loan is made to one, another cannot also become that original lender. The successive-transfer answer does not fit. Even so, the problem can be resolved. Without a kinyan, no document lien arises until the debtor actually delivers the note to the lender. At the moment of delivery the lender is already identified. There is therefore no retroactive bereirah at all. This resembles Rosh's case where Shaul promised riches to whoever would strike the Philistine. Maharit himself explains that the obligation took effect only when a specific person came forward and said, "I will strike him," at which point the identity was already clear. The same is true here. A second answer is that once the document is in the claimant's hands and he says, "I made the loan," we presume he tells the truth. Torah law itself creates a property lien at the time of the loan; the document is required only because Chazal removed purchaser-facing collection from oral loans for lack of publicity. In rabbinic matters we apply bereirah. Therefore, even if the documentary mechanism involved a form of future identification, it can be effective. There is thus no inherent dispute in this law, and Ramban too can accept it in this form.
And if a receipt was written. Sema and Shach understand this only where the note says either "whether through him or not through him," or uses an unqualified bearer form. If the holder can collect only by proving that he comes through the named creditor, then before he establishes that chain, a receipt issued by him should not prejudice the true owner of the note. Even if the receipt itself says that he acquired from the named creditor, he is not believed merely through his own statement if the witnesses do not independently know that fact. All of this follows their understanding. According to what I explained above, however, if the document is in his hands he is believed to say that he acquired it where delivery is a sufficient mode of transfer. Therefore, if the note is not in his possession, obviously his receipt does nothing. If the note is in his possession, his receipt does work, because the very fact that he holds the note supports his claim that he acquired it. This explains Rama's unqualified language that whenever the instrument is in the holder's hands, his receipt is effective. Taz also rules this way, though for a different reason. Shach further discusses a bearer note. Suppose the present holder gives the debtor a receipt and later sells the note to another person. If the receipt was executed before witnesses, it has publicity, and the later purchaser should have known of it; he therefore loses through his own negligence. But if the receipt is merely in the creditor's handwriting and has no publicity, Shach distinguishes. Where the purchaser can claim that the receipt's date was collusively antedated to defeat him, that claim is certainly available. Even where the handwriting was established earlier and there is no suspicion of antedating, Shach says the purchaser can still collect because a bearer note transferred to him is like a fresh obligation created directly in his favor. The former holder's private receipt does not defeat the new obligation. I do not understand this. Once the underlying debt has been paid, the bearer note is mere paper. How can a later transfer recreate a debt? In an ordinary witnessed loan note, once the debtor borrows and repays, he cannot simply borrow again using the same instrument because its lien has been extinguished. Shach himself above in siman 48 is stringent even against the original creditor. All the more so here, where the debt was already paid and the prior holder simply hands the dead instrument to another person. With a handwritten bearer obligation, Shach elsewhere allows the debtor himself to reborrow on the same writing, because by redelivering his own handwriting he personally creates a renewed undertaking. But here the debtor does nothing. The former creditor, after being paid, merely transfers worthless paper. What act creates a new obligation by the debtor to the purchaser? Perhaps Shach intends to rely on commercial custom governing transferable bearer instruments. If so, the matter depends on a valid communal enactment. Beit din and the city's recognized communal leaders may establish such a rule for the benefit of commerce. But absent such an enactment, I see no halachic basis to extract money after the underlying debt was already discharged. This requires study.
And if he wrote, and so forth. Shach explains the novelty as follows: even if the debtor never borrowed money from anyone, but simply writes, "I obligate myself in one hundred to whoever produces this document," whoever later produces it may collect even though he never lent the debtor anything. Maharit, in the responsum cited above, writes that according to Ramban and Ran, who apply the laws of bereirah to such open-ended obligations, this is problematic. Here Ran's answer that the debtor can become obligated successively to one holder after another does not help, because the obligation is supposed to arise in favor of whichever unknown person ultimately produces the note. Maharit therefore treats the law as dependent upon the dispute whether bereirah applies in Torah matters. It appears to me that a distinction is necessary. If the debtor makes a kinyan now obligating himself one hundred to whoever will later produce this document, then indeed at the time of the kinyan no creditor is identified. That is a true case of bereirah, and Maharit's difficulty applies. But where there is no kinyan, the holder acquires no right merely because the writing exists. The obligation takes effect only when the debtor actually delivers the note to a particular person. At that moment the creditor is known and the debtor's lien takes effect toward him. There is no bereirah because the relevant fact is already clarified at the moment of legal effectiveness. This is directly analogous to the case of Shaul, "whoever strikes the Philistine, the king will enrich him," cited by Rosh and by Maharit himself. The obligation does not become fixed upon an unknown person at the original declaration. It takes effect when one person steps forward and says, "I will strike him," at which point the identity is known. So too here. Without a kinyan, there is no difficulty at all.
Gloss relating to simanim 49 and 50. I previously challenged the rule that two people named Yosef ben Shimon can collect from outsiders on the ground that the document does not identify its subject from within. That question follows Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer simanim 130 and 136, who writes that according to R. Meir, if there are two same-named husbands, or even one Yosef ben Shimon who has two wives bearing the same name, delivery witnesses do not help because the get must be self-identifying from within. Beit Shmuel therefore questions Shulchan Aruch. In truth, one can explain Rambam differently. The Gemara is commonly printed with Rava, but Tosafot in Bava Batra and Yevamot read Rabbah. Rabbah does not accept the presumption "here found, here always" in the same way and can require proof like Abaye. Therefore, in the case of two same-named husbands, we can indeed fear that one wrote a get, changed his mind, discarded it, and the other found it, as Rashi explains. Rabbah must answer that R. Elazar's delivery witnesses are needed to identify the proper husband and wife. But according to Rava, whose view we accept that possession itself creates the presumption "here found, here always," we do not maintain this defect at all. The get in the wife's possession is presumed to have been delivered to her by the proper husband. Delivery witnesses therefore need not later appear in beit din merely to identify it, and this explains why Rambam omits such a requirement. The same possession presumption can also supply sufficient identification for purposes of the document. If we permit an eshet ish based on such a presumption, as Ramban writes, it is certainly strong enough to count as evident identification in a monetary document. This also answers why Rava in Gittin says, "This teaches that two Yosef ben Shimons can collect from others," when the Mishnah in Bava Batra already implies that they can. The Bava Batra Mishnah, because it says outsiders cannot collect against the two same-named people since a note may be written for a borrower alone, can be understood as discussing kinyan documents. In a kinyan document the underlying transaction is completed by the kinyan itself, even if no note is ever written. The document merely proves the kinyan and therefore need not identify its subject internally in the same way. The get Mishnah proves something more: even where the document itself creates the legal effect, the possession presumption can supply sufficient identification. Consequently, two same-named creditors can collect from outsiders even with non-kinyan loan documents. Tosafot in Yevamot 120 and Bava Batra ask why Rava can prove this. Perhaps we should suspect that the document was transferred from the other same-named person, and documentary rights require writing and transfer. According to what I have written, that concern is already excluded by Bava Batra's rule that same-named people collect from outsiders; the remaining question is only the internal-identification requirement in a non-kinyan instrument. The get proves precisely that point. Beit Shmuel inferred that Tosafot must reject this explanation, because if the possession presumption itself supplied internal identification, why did Tosafot need to change the text from Rava to Rabbah? But another reading is possible. Tosafot may hold that for R. Meir a possession presumption can never create "evident from within"; therefore they indeed needed Rabbah. Yet Rambam can disagree and follow the reasoning I have set out. Rambam's omission of a requirement to bring delivery witnesses in the duplicate-name get case strongly indicates that he does. I once explained further that the phrase "this teaches" in Gittin can be understood even according to the view that R. Meir requires internal identification in monetary notes lacking a kinyan. If a loan note is based on a kinyan, the kinyan itself creates the lien and the note is only evidence, so internal identification is unnecessary. Thus the Bava Batra Mishnah proves only that same-named creditors can collect where there was a kinyan. Rava needs the get case to prove that even without a kinyan, where the instrument itself creates the lien, possession is enough to identify the creditor. Abaye answers that the get Mishnah is R. Elazar, for whom delivery witnesses suffice, so it proves nothing concerning R. Meir. There is also a useful explanation of the Gemara's phrase "but the first can divorce" in the case of two Yosef ben Shimons. Rashi says perhaps the second wrote a get, changed his mind, discarded it, and the first found it. Maharsha asks why the ordinary concern that "I am not the borrower" is not enough. I suggested above that the Mishnah's statement that outsiders cannot collect against two same-named people must teach more than the obvious rule that money cannot be taken from one of two uncertain debtors. It includes a case where one is guarantor for the other. The guarantor then says that perhaps the supposed borrower saw the inadequately identified note, refused to complete the loan, discarded it, and it was found. That same scenario makes Rashi's discarded-get explanation natural. This also fits the discussion in Yevamot 115. The Gemara asks whether we fear loss and transfer when names match. If the concern were merely an ordinary deposit, one could ask why Rashi in Gittin needs the unusual case of throwing the document away. The answer is that Rashi treats custody between two persons of identical names as uncommon enough not to generate ordinary concern. A discarded instrument, by contrast, can enter another's possession without any entrustment. The phrase "one does not deposit with another of the same name" is therefore not literal proof that no such deposit ever occurs, but reflects the general rule that a particularized one-person loss or deposit is not ordinarily suspected. The question then arises: why not say the same in the case of two wives bearing the same name? Perhaps the husband wrote a get for one wife, changed his mind, threw it away, and the other wife found it. One answer is that a man who divorces discreetly can ordinarily do so without public embarrassment. But where there are two same-named husbands, the enactment requires the divorce to occur in the other's presence, creating publicity and embarrassment; the first husband may therefore be especially likely to abandon the instrument and prepare a properly distinguished one. Still, that explanation is somewhat strained. A better explanation is that we generally do not suspect that a husband changes his mind on the very day the get is written. Perek HaMevi Get implies that concern for reconciliation arises where the get remained with him for some period. But people do not normally delay a document of personal misfortune, since "a person does not advance calamity against himself." In an ordinary case, therefore, we need not suspect that he held the get for days and then discarded it. When duplicate names exist, however, the written get does not clearly identify which husband is divorcing until delivery. Before delivery there is no visible "calamity" tied specifically to him, because the document could refer to the other Yosef ben Shimon. Therefore the normal reluctance to keep an unexecuted get is weaker, and it is plausible that he retained it, later reconciled, and discarded it. This also helps explain why Tosafot in various places say that if the reading is Rabbah, the proof from the get is needed even though the Bava Batra Mishnah seems enough. Bava Batra can be explained through kinyan documents or other special structures, while the get establishes the stronger rule in an operative instrument. According to Rava, however, the possession presumption itself may make later proof unnecessary. I further suggested that the phrase "no outsider can collect against them" in the Mishnah includes cases such as where one same-named person became guarantor for the other's debts. This appears explicitly in the Yerushalmi, which discusses "so-and-so and so-and-so are guarantors" in connection with the Mishnah of two Yosef ben Shimons. If one is guarantor, why can the creditor not collect whichever way one looks? If the person sued is the borrower, he owes directly; if the other is borrower, this one owes as guarantor. The answer must be that the document itself is suspect: perhaps no loan ever took place because the borrower saw the defective identification and abandoned the note. This gives a strong basis for Rashi's discarded-get explanation and for the Gemara's comparison. There is another issue in Tosafot Yevamot. They explain Abaye's requirement that the holder prove his acquisition by concern for a document lost among many people, rather than simply by concern for a deposit. Why not say that custody by another same-named person is the problem? Apparently, Tosafot understand Rashi's phrase "because his name is like his" as only one reason deposits are uncommon, not as the exclusive concern. They treat an ordinary single-person deposit as too remote to suspect. This too aligns with the analysis above. Let us now return to the requirement that same-named husbands divorce in one another's presence. Tosafot ask why this is needed if, in any case, delivery witnesses must identify the parties. One can distinguish two formulations of the rule. Perhaps the scribes and witnesses are instructed not to release the get to the husband until the divorce occurs in the other's presence. Or perhaps no such custody duty rests on the witnesses, and the rule is only that the divorce is legally ineffective unless both same-named persons are present. If the first formulation is correct, Rashi's concern that one husband wrote a get and later discarded it cannot arise, because the instrument never entered his hands before the supervised divorce. The witnesses are careful custodians and will not casually lose it, as the authorities say concerning documents with recourse. Rashi must therefore follow the second formulation: the husband can possess the get, but actual divorce requires the other's presence. Under that formulation Tosafot's question indeed arises: if delivery witnesses must scrutinize the identities anyway, what additional role does the presence requirement play? They answer that ordinary delivery witnesses do not necessarily examine the duplicate-name problem unless the second same-named person is actually present. This analysis also reconciles the textual variation between Rabbah and Rava. If the reading is Rabbah, Rashi's discarded-get concern fits well because Rabbah does not use the strong "here found, here always" presumption. The rule must be the second formulation, and delivery witnesses must identify the parties. If the reading is Rava, however, the possession presumption already removes ordinary suspicion of loss. Rashi's explanation becomes less natural, and a different explanation of the Gemara is preferable, such as the issue of internal identification discussed above. Tosafot's difficulties therefore depend greatly on which name is read in the sugya. One can now also understand why a same-name wife does not automatically create the identical lost-get concern. On the day of writing, absent a delay, we do not suspect abandonment. If the get remained for some time, the ordinary principle that one does not advance personal misfortune makes such prolonged retention unlikely. The duplicate-husband setting weakens that principle because the document is not yet uniquely associated with one person. Therefore the husband's decision to abandon it is more plausible there than in the case of two wives of one man. There is still the earlier question: why does Rava in Yevamot bring proof from everyday collection of notes bearing common names, rather than from the explicit Bava Batra Mishnah that same-name creditors collect from others? According to Tosafot's explanation of the formula "from you," perhaps a document can be drafted as an obligation to whoever will later hold it. In such a case, even if we fear the document came from another same-named person, that may not matter: the debtor obligated himself to the holder. The Mishnah may therefore not prove that we reject loss. Everyday notes, by contrast, do not all contain such a special bearer formulation and can therefore demonstrate the general presumption against loss. The same point explains why the get case is useful: a get certainly cannot function as an open bearer instrument. One might object that this bearer-obligation explanation works only where there was a kinyan. Without a kinyan, the obligation does not arise until delivery, and if the document later moves to another person the original undertaking does not automatically recreate itself. Precisely so. That is why Rava seeks proof from actual practice and from the get: he wishes to establish the broader rule that even without kinyan we do not suspect such transfer. Now consider Ramban's question why Rabbah did not immediately prove from the Bava Batra Mishnah that we do not suspect loss. According to what has been said, that Mishnah may be explainable through authorization. Tosafot themselves say that perhaps same-name creditors can collect only through mutual authorization. Abaye's explanation of the receipt case then avoids the proof as well. Yet if the receipt is held by the borrower and one creditor authorizes the other, Rashbam says the borrower can still answer, "I intentionally took one receipt because it was sufficient against both." That explanation is accepted only at the conclusion. At the earlier stage, one can say the Mishnah concerns notes with identifying witnesses, while the receipt issue concerns a different configuration. Thus Bava Batra is not a decisive source for the general no-loss presumption. A further difficulty arises for Abaye. He does not apply the strong possession presumption and therefore needs the possibility that the instrument came from the other same-named person. Yet he also holds that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the lender. If the document is insufficiently identified from within, how can the signing witnesses acquire on behalf of an unknown lender? Tosafot Gittin imply that Abaye's doctrine works only where the beneficiary is identifiable. The answer is that Abaye need not interpret the Mishnah as involving signature-based acquisition. He can explain the no-collection rule through the payment-and-return defense of Rashbam instead. The Mishnah's statement concerning outsiders can then be understood to include a guarantor case, as above, where the concern of abandonment and later finding applies. This also explains Rashbam's reason for the Mishnah that the same-name parties cannot collect from one another: the defendant says, "I paid you and you returned the note to me." The Gemara itself elsewhere explains the Mishnah through the rule that a note can be written for the borrower without the lender. Why does Rashbam give a different reason? According to Abaye, who does not limit writing to kinyan documents through the same mechanism as Rava and who is more willing to suspect loss and transfer, the payment explanation is necessary and appropriate. According to Rava, the possession presumption removes that concern, so the Gemara emphasizes the original-writing defect. Each explanation addresses the sugya from the perspective of the Amora whose assumptions are operative. One might say the Mishnah itself concerns outside witnesses who identify the parties. If so, why can the claimant not collect? According to R. Meir, their oral testimony cannot cure a document whose internal identification was defective. According to R. Elazar, delivery witnesses could suffice. This is precisely why the Gemara introduces R. Elazar in the get parallel. Once we rule like R. Elazar, any witness present at actual delivery can identify the party, and we need not insist upon the document's internal wording. This returns us to Maggid Mishneh's statement above that if witnesses other than the document witnesses identify which Yosef ben Shimon borrowed, the claim is only an oral loan. That makes sense if those witnesses merely saw the underlying loan or heard an admission, not if they actually witnessed delivery of this document to the identified creditor. If they saw delivery, they are delivery witnesses and should confer full documentary force under R. Elazar. Thus the law must be read narrowly. The Yerushalmi's statement concerning a same-name guarantor also resolves the otherwise obvious question why the Mishnah bothers saying that no outsider can collect from them. Of course one cannot extract money from one of two possible debtors. The novelty is that even an apparent whichever-way-you-look-at-it arrangement, where one is guarantor for the other, can fail because the document itself may have been abandoned as unusable and later found. This is the precise bridge to the get case. There is a final point concerning the wording of the same-name divorce enactment. If the rule means merely that the divorce must occur in the other's presence, then after the fact, where the get is now in the wife's hand and the other same-named husband was not present, the divorce may still be invalid because the identification safeguard was not satisfied. If the rule instead governs the witnesses' custody before delivery, the result may differ. This may explain some of the disagreement among the authorities in Even HaEzer. Rambam's omission of a requirement to present delivery witnesses in beit din suggests he treats possession and the ordinary presumption as enough after the fact, while others are stricter. In sum: the apparent contradiction between the monetary law of two Yosef ben Shimons and the requirement of internal identification in gittin depends on several separate axes: R. Meir versus R. Elazar, signature witnesses versus delivery witnesses, kinyan documents versus documents whose force arises only through delivery, the presumption "here found, here always," and the special possibility that a defective same-name instrument was intentionally abandoned. Once these are separated, the sugyot can be reconciled. Rambam can omit the later production of delivery witnesses because we rule like Rava that possession creates a strong identifying presumption, while the passages that require delivery witnesses operate according to Rabbah or under a stricter view of internal identification. The same analysis explains why Rava seeks proof from gittin and from ordinary practice rather than relying solely on the Bava Batra Mishnah, and why Rashbam's explanation through payment and return remains meaningful alongside the Gemara's concern that the note may originally have been written for the borrower alone.
