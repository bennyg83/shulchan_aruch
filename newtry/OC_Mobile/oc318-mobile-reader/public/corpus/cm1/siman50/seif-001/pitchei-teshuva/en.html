See Baer Heitev in the name of the Shach: If the person who acquired the document claims that he did not know of the receipt, it is as though the borrower became obligated to him originally, and by paying the first creditor the borrower caused his own loss. Responsa Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 91, discusses a case where a man lent another person in a different city one thousand gold coins on a note payable to whoever produced it. To save travel expenses he gave the note to Shimon, instructing him to travel to a city near the borrower on market day, collect the money, and return home. Shimon came there, demanded payment in the name of the creditor, and said that he had merely been appointed as an agent so as to reduce expenses. The borrower paid him eight hundred gold coins, and Shimon wrote a receipt on the note. A few days later the creditor met the borrower at an inn and demanded the remaining two hundred. He said that he had sent away the note and did not have it with him. The borrower trusted him, paid the two hundred, and received a handwritten receipt stating that the sum was received toward that note and that nothing remained due. Shimon then demanded the remaining amount from the borrower, claiming that he had actually purchased the note and that the borrower should not have paid the original creditor. Panim Meirot rules in accordance with the Shach in siman 50: payment to the first creditor was the borrower's own loss because he paid without having the receipt written on the note itself. Accordingly, the borrower must pay the present holder and then recover from the original creditor. However, the holder must swear that he did not tell the borrower that he came only as the creditor's agent. If he did say that, the borrower may answer: You never told me that you bought the note; rather, you said you were doing the creditor a kindness and acting in his place to reduce expenses, so I reasonably believed the note still belonged to the first creditor and paid him. I do not wish now to be forced into litigation with that first creditor. See there. Regarding the Shach's further citation of the Mabit, that if the borrower paid part to the person producing the note, see Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6. He explains that the Shach stated the matter too briefly. In the Mabit's case the note said that the borrower was obligated to a named person and to anyone who produced the note. According to the Rashba, the producer needs neither authorization nor power of attorney, while the Rosh writes that because of custom he does require authorization. The Mabit explains that their dispute concerns a borrower who says to the producer, You are not my litigant. According to the Rosh, prevailing custom permits that claim if the producer lacks authorization. But if the borrower went ahead and paid him, the original creditor cannot later say that he should not have paid without authorization, because the note expressly stated that payment could be made to anyone who produced it, and not everyone is versed in commercial custom. The creditor therefore has no claim against the borrower. The Mabit happened to speak of partial payment because that was the actual case, but the same applies to full payment. Moreover, ordinarily when the producer is paid in full he returns the note to the borrower. Chatam Sofer further discusses a note written payable to the creditor or to his 'ordre' and doubts whether this is equivalent to 'whoever produces it.' The term 'ordre' may mean only a person whom the creditor actually sent and instructed to collect. If so, the creditor can say, I did not instruct that person and gave him no order, and the borrower cannot answer that not everyone knows commercial custom, because the note itself expressly limits payment to the creditor's order.
