I, so and so, etc. The witnesses sign below to attest to the substance of the obligation, not merely to verify his signature. If they sign only to verify the signature, it has the status of a full document even though the text is phrased, "I, so and so."
Whoever produces it may collect with it. We do not suspect that another person was the lender, that the document fell from him, and that the present holder found it, Gemara. See Tumim for my discussion of why collection may be made from encumbered property when the identity of the lender is not apparent from the document itself.
May collect with it. Bach writes that if the holder says, "I bought it from the lender named in the document," he may collect without a power of attorney and without written assignment and delivery. See Tumim, where I wrote that Maharit disagrees, and I established the law in accordance with Maharit.
Because he is believed to say, "I paid." This means a handwritten obligation without a clause of trust. Rama here does not add any gloss to the Mechaber, which implies that he agrees the debtor is believed to say, "I paid," although below in siman 69 he cites two opinions. Perhaps he relied on the later discussion, Shach. Specifically the debtor is believed to say, "I paid," or, "I owe someone else," but he is not believed to say, "There never was such a debt, I wrote this merely in jest," Bach. Although Shach writes that this requires study, Bach's ruling appears correct in practice.
And it fell from him, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that this does not mean that the debtor claims to know with certainty that it fell and was found. Rather, he claims with certainty, "I never borrowed from you. Since you have no power of attorney or written assignment and delivery, perhaps it fell from the lender and you found it." See there. According to Maharit, who holds that such documents are not acquired by mere delivery, the debtor may similarly say, "Perhaps it was delivered to you, but delivery alone does not transfer ownership." The Mechaber mentioned only one of these possibilities.
And you found it. See Tumim regarding two people with the same name, Yosef ben Shimon, where one of them produces a handwritten obligation against Reuven. Even though Reuven has a migo because he could claim payment, he is not believed to say, "Perhaps it fell from the other Yosef ben Shimon," because such a specific fall is extremely unlikely, making this a migo against a strong presumption. The case is one where he also cannot say, "Perhaps it was delivered to you and delivery does not transfer it," such as where the document says, "and to every bearer," which below we rule is transferred by delivery alone.
The same applies to a judgment of beit din. This is according to the opinion that one cannot claim payment against a beit din judgment. According to the opinion that one may claim payment, the debtor has a migo and is believed just as with a handwritten obligation, Sema and Shach, and this is obvious.
Specifically when he comes through that named person, etc. See Tumim. Sema explains that mere delivery is insufficient and that he requires either a power of attorney or written assignment and delivery. This applies where he cannot prove that he purchased it from the lender. But if he proves the purchase, mere delivery also suffices. Likewise, if the document says, "whether through him or otherwise," mere delivery suffices. I wrote that since such a document is acquired by delivery, no proof of the acquisition is required at all. Rather, the meaning is this: the holder must claim that he acquired it from the person named in the document. If he says instead, "Reuven named in the document never lent you anything. You later came to me and I lent you money on the strength of this document," that does not help, and his claim is only an oral loan, because the document's lien is based on Reuven, from whom his rights must derive. But if the document says, "whether through him or otherwise," the holder may say, "I myself lent you the money," because the document creates a lien in favor of any person who lends on its strength. See there. If the document was in the second holder's possession and he received partial payment, and afterward the first lender came with the document, the first lender loses the amount already paid to the second holder, Responsa Mabit.
If a receipt was written, etc. Sema and Shach write that this applies specifically where the document says, "whether through him or otherwise," and certainly where it says, "to every bearer." But if collection must derive specifically through the named lender, then without a power of attorney the second holder's receipt is ineffective and the borrower loses unless he proves that the holder had authorization or the witnesses to the receipt clearly testify that he had it. I wrote in Tumim that according to my conclusion, where possession of the document itself makes the holder believed to say he acquired it without needing further proof, he may also issue a valid receipt. If he did not possess the document, it is obvious that he has no authority to issue a receipt. Why should he be able to give a receipt for another person's debt? This is obvious.
No person can collect, etc. Shach writes that with a bearer document of the type commonly used in Poland, if the borrower paid the lender and the lender later sold the document to another, then if the borrower has a receipt signed by witnesses the purchaser receives nothing. But if the receipt is in the lender's own handwriting, even where it is known that the receipt was made before the sale and there was no collusion, the purchaser may nevertheless collect the document. See Tumim, where I wrote that this ruling has no root and is entirely unreasonable as ordinary law. It might possibly rest on a local governmental regulation that beit din may adopt as an emergency measure, but as strict law I know no basis for it.
If it is written in the document, etc. Even if the obligor never previously owed anyone and never borrowed from anyone, once the document says, "I obligate myself to the bearer of this document," and the document is in the holder's possession, he must pay. See Tumim. I wrote that this applies specifically where the obligation takes effect through delivery of the document, because at that moment he knows to whom he is becoming obligated. But if he made a generic kinyan saying, "I obligate myself now to whoever will do such and such," or, "I obligate myself now to whoever will later possess this document," it is ineffective according to many authorities, because we rule that bereirah does not apply. See there.
