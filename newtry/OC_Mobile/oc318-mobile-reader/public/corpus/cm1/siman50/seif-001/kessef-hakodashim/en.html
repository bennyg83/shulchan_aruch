Gloss. Against the bearer of this promissory note, see Urim VeTumim, seif katan 7. Urim VeTumim writes in siman 50 that according to Ramban, who says regarding maamad sheloshtan that because there is no bereirah in Torah law an obligation cannot be created in favor of whoever may later derive title from the present creditor, the same problem applies to a note written "to whoever presents it." At first glance one could distinguish the cases. In an oral loan the lien arises only when the money is received, so a lien in favor of a later unidentified person could work only by retroactive clarification that this person was included from the outset. With a written note delivered to the lender and payable to any bearer, however, no retroactive effect seems necessary. Each person who later receives the note could acquire the creditor's power at that moment, functioning as the creditor's agent. According to the Shach at the beginning of siman 66, who requires writing and delivery even where the note says "to any bearer," there would be more reason to compare the note to maamad sheloshtan. Yet Urim VeTumim himself writes that such a bearer note does not require writing and delivery. Even according to the Shach, no formal authorization is needed to collect from the borrower. Without a special authorization the successive bearers can be viewed as agents of one another. Writing and delivery concern the transfer as against the original creditor, not the borrower's duty to pay, and the problem of bereirah therefore need not arise in the same way. Still, Urim VeTumim later writes that a lien cannot arise through later delivery unless the note was originally written with such a transfer in mind. If there is no bereirah, then an original intention that the obligation should run to whoever later presents the note is ineffective as to a person who was then unidentified. All the more so where there was no such intent at all. One therefore cannot simply say that the lien successively arises through the document as though each transfer were a sale by an agent. Agency from the original lender might itself require authorization, and the authorities' statement that no authorization is needed may perhaps be only a rabbinic rule, comparable to Ramban's explanation of maamad sheloshtan. This requires further study. It may be that only in maamad sheloshtan did the Sages establish a special transfer despite the Torah rule of no bereirah. Perhaps the reason no authorization is needed in an ordinary bearer note is that by handing over the note the borrower himself effectively appoints the bearer to receive payment. If the issue is preventing the original creditor from retracting, writing and delivery are required. This may be the Shach's reasoning and would answer Urim VeTumim's objection. It would also explain why, according to the Shach, if the original creditor dies before the borrower pays the bearer, the borrower need not pay the creditor's heirs, since the bearer's agency derives from the borrower rather than from the deceased creditor. This reasoning fits those who hold that bereirah does apply in this context, as the Rosh sought to prove from Shaul's declaration, "the king will enrich him." One might answer for those who reject that proof that the biblical condition was not actually fulfilled in the manner assumed. David was not first enriched, for he later said that he was poor and lightly esteemed. Only after he struck two hundred Philistines was Michal given to him, and that arose from the later episode. Had a binding monetary condition already arisen, it would seem improper to impose an additional dangerous undertaking before satisfying it. Even though a father cannot transfer his daughter by such a condition and could retract from arranging the kiddushin until actual kiddushin, it would still have been proper first to provide the promised wealth so that David would possess money for kiddushin. On the other hand, perhaps because Shaul fixed no time for enrichment, the usual thirty day period of an unspecified loan has no application, and delay in performance did not show that the condition was void. The public announcement of the condition to David and to the people also suggests that it had legal force. Although the promise concerning the king's daughter did not itself create a fixed acquisition, the promised wealth and freedom must have been meaningful, otherwise the entire condition would have been empty. The rule of "you acquire and the donkey acquires" is not directly relevant, because Shaul had the ability later to arrange his daughter's kiddushin and the promise itself could have the character of a preliminary obligation. Those who disagree with the Rosh may instead understand Shaul's obligation as wages for an undertaking in which David exposed himself to danger, comparable to one who engages a worker through an agent, or to the rule that one who says "feed him and you will not lose" becomes liable. The king's men announced the terms to David on Shaul's behalf, and Shaul later spoke with David himself. Even if David's own motive was entirely for the sake of Heaven, he may have inquired about the condition merely to avoid the appearance of arrogance and the resentment that can arise when others believe that salvation should have come through them. The king's public formulation, however, was that the promised benefit would induce someone to confront the Philistine, which is language of hiring. A person may initially be stirred by an ordinary motive and yet come to act for the sake of Heaven. The Rosh, however, may have understood that everyone recognized David's intent as entirely for the sake of Heaven, and that he did not press for fulfillment afterward, making the analogy to hired labor less compelling. Alternatively, perhaps those who told David of the condition were not legally appointed agents of Shaul. This whole proof therefore requires further study. Another possible basis is the Ran's principle that bereirah is not problematic when the object can belong successively to many persons. Urim VeTumim cites this. On that basis, even a lien on encumbered property could pass under a note written to whoever presents it. This does not contradict the Rosh's proof, because Shaul's promise could ultimately be fulfilled only to one person. The Rosh's proof would then rest on another reason. The Shach's principal discussion, however, concerns collection from the borrower, where formal authorization is unnecessary because no retroactive transfer is needed. There is room to say that the Rosh, whose language appears to dispense with writing and delivery, follows his own view that bereirah operates here, so it is retroactively clarified that the note was written for its present holder. The Shach, requiring writing and delivery, can follow Ramban that there is no such bereirah, while still holding that authorization is unnecessary against the borrower because the bearer's right to collect operates prospectively. Perhaps the same applies even against encumbered property, because a note written with an existing lien and intended for delivery to a bearer is not comparable to a handwritten note that was never intended to create public reliance. This too requires study. This also answers the question cited by Urim VeTumim at the beginning of siman 69. Maamad sheloshtan there concerns an oral loan whose lien derives only from the money itself, and is therefore unlike a kinyan made now to take effect when future property comes into existence. In the latter case the kinyan is made over property, whereas money lent for expenditure no longer exists as a specific object. This explains why Rashi discusses a loan rather than a deposit. A deposit can be acquired prospectively when the later condition occurs. With a loan, if there is a document one must consider the law of an antedated lien, though Urim VeTumim concludes that where some lien exists throughout, it need not be considered antedated. If there is no document, it is difficult to see how a new lien can later arise by words alone, especially when nothing is then said. Even in a written case the transferor can retract before the future event, and if he dies before it, the later transfer may fail. Similarly here, if the original creditor retracts before the note reaches the bearer, formal authorization may again be necessary. Whether witnesses to delivery are required for the later transfer also needs study. Urim VeTumim questions the Sema's statement that one may not deliver such a note to a non-Jew. Perhaps the Sema means that the non-Jew may collect contrary to Torah law, failing to allow the borrower the period and exemptions required by the law of creditors, and may demand expenses or interest for delay. If the debt is secured by a mortgage, transferring it to a violent non-Jew may also resemble bringing a lion to one's neighbor's boundary. This requires further study. According to the Ran's principle that successive ownership avoids the problem of bereirah, one can also explain the Gemara's statement concerning persons not yet born in the law of maamad sheloshtan and answer the question of Maharam Schiff cited by Urim VeTumim. The Gemara need not say that maamad sheloshtan should work even for those later born, because if the original declaration cannot include people not yet in existence, the right is not potentially available to every person and the Ran's basis for bereirah falls away. Finally, the Gemara did not explain maamad sheloshtan as liability created through the benefit of forgiving a loan, even though a woman can be betrothed through such benefit. The reason may be that this formula requires an explicit statement that the consideration is the benefit of forgiveness. Mere transfer language does not imply that intent. Perhaps the special halachah of maamad sheloshtan causes ordinary language to operate as though such benefit had been specified, but that explanation is difficult because the rule also applies to deposits, where forgiveness of a specific deposited object does not function like forgiveness of a loan. Moreover, the benefit of forgiving a loan is ordinarily worth only the value of that benefit, not the full amount of the debt. Thus maamad sheloshtan remains a distinct rabbinic transfer and not merely an application of benefit from forgiveness. The matter requires careful study.
