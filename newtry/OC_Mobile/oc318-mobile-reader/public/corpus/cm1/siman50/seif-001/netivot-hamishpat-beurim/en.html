Whoever produces it may collect with it. See Shach, seif katan 2. The producer must say, 'I myself lent you the money.' If he says instead, 'I bought this document from another person,' ordinary assignment law still requires writing and delivery. The Gemara concludes that the word 'from you' means only the person to whom the instrument was delivered at the original transaction. The debtor became obligated to that original holder, not automatically to every future holder. Therefore sale of the claim still requires the normal mode of transfer. Maharit's responsum, cited by Urim VeTumim, says the same. This differs from a document expressly stating liability to 'whoever produces it,' as explained below.
Rather, it fell from another and you found it. Mechaber implies that the borrower is believed through a payment migo only when he claims the instrument fell from another person, but not when he says, 'It fell from me and you found it,' even where he otherwise has a migo of payment. At first this seems difficult from siman 46, where Sema writes that with a forgery migo the borrower can say the document fell from him. One distinction is that there the deed names a specific creditor. A claim that it fell from the borrower would require the unlikely coincidence that precisely the named creditor found it. Such a one-person fall may be disbelieved unless reinforced by a strong forgery migo. Here the document says only 'from you' and could be collected by many possible people. Still, one might think the borrower's claim should be stronger because the possibility of a random finder is broader. It appears Mechaber holds that a borrower is normally careful not to lose this kind of bearer-like document, because any dishonest finder could collect with it. By contrast, a named-creditor document is dangerous only if the specific named creditor finds it. Moreover, where the borrower has a forgery migo he may not guard even the named document carefully because he expects to answer any later claim with, 'It is forged.' Here a mere payment plea is different. If a stranger first sues him orally and the borrower truthfully says, 'I never borrowed from you,' the stranger may then produce this writing and render him an established denier. He therefore has strong reason to guard it even after payment. Consequently a plea that it fell from the borrower is weak. It is also possible that Mechaber states the greater novelty: he is believed even to say the document fell from another person, although a valid document is ordinarily guarded carefully. All the more so he may sometimes be believed to say it fell from himself because he regarded the document as ineffective after payment. The precise law in that case therefore requires study; see also what I write in siman 69.
Specifically when he comes through that named person. Meaning that he claims he purchased the instrument from that person or acts as that person's agent. But if he says, 'That person did not wish to lend on the document, so I myself lent the money,' this does not work. A document stating, 'to so-and-so and to whoever produces it,' is like one written, 'to so-and-so and his authorized representative.' The holder must therefore come through the named person's right. Whether a formal authorization or writing and delivery are needed is discussed in siman 66. Shach cites Mabit. The apparent case is that a person who came through the named creditor held the instrument, the debtor paid him part in front of two witnesses, and received a receipt. One might have thought the original creditor only authorized complete collection and surrender of the document, because in that case the representative would have to return either the money or the document. Perhaps he was not authorized to accept only part and issue a receipt, since the principal could not later compel him to prove whether he issued such a receipt. The ruling teaches that a partial receipt can nevertheless be effective. After I later saw Mabit, I found that he indeed validates a partial receipt. This is difficult because Shach in seif katan 7 holds that even a receipt for the whole amount may be ineffective in some such circumstances. Shach in siman 121 also leaves Mabit's ruling requiring study. Accordingly, in the case described, the creditor can plausibly say, 'I did not authorize him to accept payment merely against a receipt.'
Unless it explicitly says, whether he comes through him, etc. Then the holder may collect whether he says he himself made the loan, bought the claim from another, or is acting as another's agent, even without a separate authorization or ordinary assignment writing. There are several resulting rules. If the holder says he himself made the loan and the borrower pleads payment, the holder must swear if the borrower says, 'Swear to me.' If the holder says he bought the claim from another and the borrower admits the original debt but says he paid the seller, Shach holds the borrower can still be liable because payment to the seller may not discharge a claim expressly made payable to every holder. Under Urim VeTumim's contrary view, where payment to the seller before the purchase would extinguish the lien, the seller must swear if the borrower demands it. If the seller refuses, the purchaser can swear that the seller did not tell him of payment and then collect. This differs from siman 66, where the seller's refusal can defeat the purchaser because the seller can still waive the claim and is therefore believed. In a document payable to whoever produces it, the seller cannot waive after transfer and therefore is not believed merely to destroy the purchaser's right. If the holder is only an agent, the original creditor must swear when the borrower demands an oath and there is no credibility clause. If the holder says he personally lent the money while the borrower says, 'You did not lend; another person did, and I paid that person,' the borrower is asking only for an oath. Even if the holder admitted the borrower's version, he would merely have to swear. Therefore the borrower cannot force more than a ban where appropriate. If the holder says he purchased the document and the borrower says the seller owes him an offsetting debt, this does not defeat the purchaser. An offset against the seller is not equivalent to payment of this debt. At the time of sale the document's lien remained intact; once the borrower obligated himself to every holder, the purchaser stands as though directly obligated to him from the beginning. If the borrower merely says, 'Perhaps you did not buy it and are only the seller's agent; I paid the seller and therefore the seller must swear,' while the holder says, 'I own it,' the holder faces at most a ban because the borrower's allegation is uncertain. If the borrower says the document simply fell from the seller but admits no payment, there is not even a basis for a ban. A document expressly payable to every holder makes an ordinary fall claim ineffective, as Tosafot imply in Bava Batra. A plea that the seller was first paid and only afterward lost the document is different, because payment may have extinguished the lien before the holder acquired it. The rule therefore depends on the actual plea.
If a receipt was written in the name of some person, etc. This means the witnesses wrote in the receipt that, when it was issued, the debt document for the stated amount was physically in that person's hands; the debtor paid him part and received a receipt. Such a receipt is effective because at that moment the document payable to whoever produces it was actually in the payee's hands, so he was unquestionably the creditor against the debtor and had authority to accept payment and forgive that portion. Once that part of the lien was extinguished, a later purchaser acquires only what remained. But if the receipt does not say that the person actually produced the document before the witnesses at the time of payment, it is ineffective. Certainly, if the current holder says the document was always his, the ordinary presumption is that property found in a person's hand was there all along, and another person's receipt does not defeat it. Even if the holder says he later bought the document, the receipt witnesses still did not establish that payment was made to the person then entitled to receive it. Perhaps the document had already been sold to someone else, in which case the debtor was no longer obligated to the named payee but only to the actual holder. Shach, seif katan 7, at first writes that the debtor can say, 'I did not know of the purchase; since I obligated myself to whoever produces the document and you now produce it, it is as though I was obligated to you from the beginning.' Later he relies on the rule that a witnessed receipt has publicity. His words appear contradictory. If the original named creditor's receipt or witnessed payment always extinguished the debt even after transfer, there would be no need to say the debtor became newly obligated to the purchaser. The explanation is that a document naming the original creditor differs from one expressly payable to every holder. In an ordinary named document, the debtor may pay the original creditor even after sale in many circumstances, because the seller can still waive the claim and payment to him can discharge the borrower. The debtor need not protect later purchasers in the same way. In a true bearer-style document, however, once the debt is transferred the original creditor no longer has power to waive or accept payment on behalf of the claim. The debtor is obligated to the current holder. Therefore he may not pay someone else in a manner that prejudices purchasers. Why then does a witnessed receipt help? Because a witnessed receipt has publicity. The Sages permitted partial payment against such a receipt precisely because its public nature allows later purchasers to discover that the lien was reduced. An unwitnessed payment, even before witnesses but without a formal receipt, does not have equivalent publicity and should not prejudice a later purchaser of a bearer-style document. If the debtor pays improperly and thereby causes loss to a purchaser, the purchaser may still collect. Urim VeTumim disagrees and holds that even ordinary witnessed payment can extinguish the lien, provided the witnesses saw the document in the payee's hands at the time of payment. On that view the crucial point is not publicity alone but proof that the person who received payment was then the actual holder and creditor. I incline to this approach. But if the witnesses did not see the document in his hands at that moment, no receipt or handwritten acknowledgment is enough, because perhaps he had already transferred the document. Tzemach Tzedek appears to validate a receipt where the document had earlier been in the payee's possession and was later lost; in my view that is not enough. Nimukei Yosef's language indicates the instrument must actually be produced at the time of payment. Likewise, I do not know the source for the proposition that if such a bearer-style document is lost, the debtor must nevertheless pay upon receiving an indemnity bond against future harm. The ordinary rule is only that a receipt may sometimes replace the document. If no effective receipt can be written against this type of lost instrument, the debtor can say that an indemnity is not sufficiently secure for him.
