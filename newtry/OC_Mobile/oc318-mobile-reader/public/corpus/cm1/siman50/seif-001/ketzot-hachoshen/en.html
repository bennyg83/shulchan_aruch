A document with witnesses. Taz, citing Maharik, explains Rashbam as discussing a document entirely in the borrower's own handwriting. Maharik therefore says that the borrower is not believed through a migo to claim that the document fell from him, because people are very careful with their documents and we do not ordinarily suspect an accidental fall. This is effectively a migo against witnesses. Bnei Shmuel, citing Maharshal Chayon, similarly infers from Rashbam concerning a document reading, "I, so and so, borrowed from you a maneh," in the borrower's handwriting, that even without witnesses the borrower is not believed through a migo of payment. These writers overlooked Rashbam's own position. Rashbam holds that concerning an obligation in the borrower's handwriting the borrower is not believed to claim payment at all, as he states at the end of Perek Get Pashut and as Beit Yosef cites in siman 69 from Rosh together with Baal HaMaor and Raavad. According to our practical law, which does permit a claim of payment against personal handwriting, the borrower can likewise be believed through an appropriate migo. The Rashbam cannot be used to prove otherwise.
A receipt from some person. The Shach writes that a receipt helps specifically when the later purchaser of the bearer instrument knew that payment had already been made to the original creditor. If the purchaser says he did not know, then although the debtor was never obligated for more than one maneh, it is as though the new holder became entitled from the outset. The Shach distinguishes further. If payment is known only from a handwritten receipt of the first creditor, there is concern for collusion because handwritten material has little publicity. Even if the handwriting was validated in beit din, the purchaser can say he did not know. But if the receipt was executed before witnesses, it creates publicity. A purchaser who later buys the instrument should have known and loses through his own negligence. The Shach compares this to the rule that a debtor can defeat purchasers of a debt instrument through a witnessed receipt, and further invokes the market overt enactment for commonly traded bearer notes. If the debtor proves payment to the original creditor before the purchaser bought the instrument, he should, according to the Shach, swear how much he paid to acquire the paper and recover that amount, unless he knew or there was a sufficiently public receipt. I do not accept these distinctions. First, the difference between a witnessed receipt and oral witnesses to payment is weak. The Gemara distinguishes loans from sales regarding publicity because people may borrow privately but ordinarily sell publicly. Payment likewise may be public. Second, as I explained at the end of siman 46, if oral witnesses establish actual payment, the debtor need not pay the purchaser. The concern for purchasers applies to liens on the debtor's property, where the underlying property belongs to the debtor and hidden repayment can affect later purchasers of land. But if the creditor sold something that he no longer owned because the debt had already been paid, the owner can recover it from the purchaser like any other property sold by one who lacked title. The purchaser may have a claim for reimbursement under the market overt enactment in appropriate cases, but that does not preserve the extinguished debt against the debtor. Nor is the Shach's distinction based on publicity sufficient. Even a witnessed document is not so public that every later purchaser is conclusively presumed to know it. We see this from the Rosh's rule that even when movables are encumbered together with land by a public document, purchasers of the movables are protected by the market overt enactment. Therefore, if market overt protection applied here, even a witnessed receipt would not necessarily defeat it. In truth, however, the market overt enactment does not apply in this case at all. Maharam Alshich rules that where a stolen item after sale comes back into the original owner's own hands, he need not pay the purchaser the purchase price under the market overt enactment. The Shach in siman 356 accepts this in such a setting. Here the debtor already possesses his own assets. Once the debt was paid, the later purchaser of the bearer paper seeks to remove the debtor's property from him. This resembles the stolen object already returned to the owner's hand, where the market overt enactment does not operate. Bearer notes can sometimes be treated as though already collected when they remain payable, but a note already discharged before the sale no longer stands ready for collection. The debtor therefore remains the possessor of his assets. The Rosh and Tur's discussion of market overt protection for instruments in siman 60 concerns encumbered documents or movable rights sold onward, where the purchaser is already in possession of the relevant subject. It does not govern a case where the debtor's own property remains in his hands. Therefore, when witnesses establish that the bearer obligation was paid before the purchaser acquired it, the debtor is exempt and no market overt enactment requires him to reimburse the purchaser. Regarding a handwritten receipt, I wrote in simanim 69 and 48 that if the debtor is already in possession he can sometimes say that it was written on its stated date, even though public collection from purchasers would be unavailable. Nevertheless, where the very fact of payment remains doubtful, the special status of a bearer instrument may still affect the burden of proof. That point requires further study. But where payment before purchase is established by witnesses, the debtor is certainly exempt, and the market overt enactment does not revive the debt.
