Unless the witnesses to the document came and testified. The Beit Yosef below, siman 63, explains that their signatures must be authenticated from another source; otherwise, since they do not remember the loan, their testimony is not testimony, as the Rambam rules. This is not persuasive, for what benefit is there in recognizing their signatures from another source if this document is not authenticated through another document? The Beit Yosef in Kesef Mishneh offers another explanation, that they remember the loan. One might have thought that because they do not testify directly that they signed, but only remember the loan, perhaps they err in thinking the signatures are theirs, and the matter would remain merely an oral loan. The ruling therefore teaches that when they remember the loan there is a strong indication of truth and it is treated as a documented loan. This too is difficult. If there is a real concern that they err, remembering the loan cannot convert it into a documented loan. Rather, the Rambam holds that when the witnesses themselves recognize their signatures we do not suspect error, as is also implied by the Ran. Nevertheless, they must remember the loan, for otherwise this document is no better than any other document. Ordinarily, it is presumed that they remember it. The Ramah disagrees and follows the Ran, as explained below in siman 63. See also what I wrote there in disagreement with Responsa Maharan Sasson, siman 279.
