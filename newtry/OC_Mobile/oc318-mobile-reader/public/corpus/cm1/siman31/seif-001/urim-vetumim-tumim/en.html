Those who contradict each other, etc. Shach, seif katan 1, cites the sages of Brisk, who were uncertain where one witness says that a loan occurred and another denies it. Do we say they may not combine together in a later testimony because one of them is lying, or, since neither was contradicted by two witnesses, do they remain fit to testify together? Shach decides from Ran and Ramban that they may not combine, and this is also supported by Raavan and Tosafot. Nevertheless, the sages of Brisk have substantial support, because Rashi and Rambam appear to follow their view. In Perek Kol HaNishba'in the Gemara challenges R. Chisda, who says that false witnesses are unfit for other testimony, from the baraita: one says the new moon was three staff-lengths high and one says five; their testimony is void, yet they may join other testimony. Rava answers that "other testimony" means another witness can join one of them concerning that same testimony, for then there are two against one and the single contradiction is disregarded. That answer is needed according to R. Chisda. According to Rav Huna, whom we follow, two contradictory sets remain fit for other testimony because each set can appear separately. Tosafot therefore object to Rashi for retaining Rava's explanation even according to the halachah. Rambam likewise writes in Hilchot Kiddush HaChodesh that one of the two may join two others who testify like him. Their wording can be explained according to the Brisk position. The Tosefta says explicitly that the two contradictory witnesses do not join one another, but each can join other witnesses. If even a one-against-one contradiction left both witnesses fully fit to testify together later, why say only that each can join others? They should be able to testify together in another matter. Thus, at the initial stage of the Gemara, the baraita itself indicates that one-against-one contradiction prevents the two from joining together, though each can join a different valid witness. This creates the challenge to R. Chisda. Rava's answer is then a practical legal clarification, even though he himself follows Rav Huna. "Other testimony" means the same lunar testimony: one of the contradictory witnesses joins two others who agree with him. The original pair cannot testify together because they contradict each other, but where one is supported by two, the single opposing witness is disregarded. For a wholly unrelated matter, however, Rav Huna allows even the two contradictory witnesses to testify together, because neither has been disqualified by two witnesses. This explains why Rashi and Rambam preserve Rava's interpretation even though the halachah follows Rav Huna. Rashi and Rambam also support this from the case where one witness says a woman was divorced and another says she was not. Rashi does not limit the case to a dispute whether the get was nearer to him or nearer to her. Rambam likewise writes that if a woman had been presumed unmarried, one witness says she became married and was divorced, and another says she was not divorced, if she married she must leave because their testimony first established her as married. Rambam does not adopt Ran's limitation that both agree a get was given and dispute only whether it reached her. This indicates that, in an ordinary one-against-one contradiction, neither witness is automatically disqualified from all testimony. Nevertheless, for practical halachah Mechaber in Even HaEzer siman 152 cites Ran's rule without dissent: where the witnesses dispute the very fact of divorce, if she married she need not leave. This shows that we treat one-against-one contradiction as a genuine contradiction for purposes of combining the witnesses. Accordingly, although Rashi and Rambam provide support for the sages of Brisk, the practical ruling follows Ran and the authorities cited by Shach: two witnesses who directly contradict each other cannot join one another in one testimony, even though neither was contradicted by two witnesses and each may remain fit separately according to the applicable rule. The wording of the baraita itself sharpens this point. It does not say merely that each witness remains fit for some unrelated testimony. It says, "they combine for another testimony," and Rashi explains the verb "combine" as each one joining other valid witnesses. The Tosefta is even more explicit: they do not combine with one another, but each combines with others. This is difficult if the dispute between them has no legal effect at all. According to the initial understanding that "another testimony" means an unrelated monetary matter, the baraita therefore proves against the broad Brisk position. If one-against-one contradiction left them fully fit to join each other, why exclude their later joint testimony? The wording indicates that although neither is personally disqualified from all testimony, the two cannot form one pair after directly contradicting each other. Rava's answer redirects the phrase to R. Chisda's case: the "other testimony" is the very lunar testimony, where another two witnesses support one of them. Then the supported witness joins those two, and the opposing single witness cannot contradict two. This is a precise use of the word "combine." For Rav Huna, by contrast, in an unrelated matter the two contradictory witnesses can each appear within a different evidentiary unit because we do not know which is false. Whether they can themselves constitute the same pair in the unrelated matter is exactly the issue under discussion. Rashi's persistence in reading the baraita through Rava's answer is therefore not an accidental retention of an answer needed only for R. Chisda. It preserves the distinction that even according to Rav Huna, the two witnesses whose mouths directly contradicted one another should not themselves be joined into one testimony in that contradictory context. Rambam's formulation in Kiddush HaChodesh is similarly exact: one of them joins two others who testify like him. This also explains why Tosafot's objection to Rashi is meaningful. Tosafot understand that once Rav Huna is accepted, the phrase can be read more broadly and need not be limited to Rava's same-testimony case. Their disagreement with Rashi therefore touches the very legal question raised by the sages of Brisk. The case of divorce strengthens the issue. Ran explains that where one says, "She was divorced," and another says, "She was not divorced," the two cannot simply be treated as a valid pair whose disagreement is ignored. He therefore limits the case in which the woman must leave to one where both agree a get was given and differ only whether it was closer to him or to her. In that limited case their common testimony establishes the act of giving the get, while the contradiction concerns its legal completion. Rambam does not state that limitation and can therefore be read like the sages of Brisk. But the practical ruling in Even HaEzer follows Ran without a recorded dissent. Accordingly, the best practical distinction is this: direct one-against-one contradiction does not necessarily brand either witness as globally disqualified, because no two witnesses have established which one lied. Each can therefore testify elsewhere according to Rav Huna's principle. But the two contradictory witnesses should not themselves be treated as a coherent pair in the matter in which their contradiction remains operative. Where another valid witness or pair joins one side, the numerical rules of testimony determine the result.
For certainly, etc. Beit Yosef cites Meisharim, Netiv 2, part 6: if witnesses contradict one another regarding only part of the testimony but agree regarding another part, the agreed portion is established. Kenesset HaGedolah notes that this is explicit in Perek Chezkas HaBatim. One litigant brings witnesses that the property belonged to his ancestors and that he held it for the years of chazakah; the other brings witnesses contradicting only the years of chazakah. We say that although they contradict one another regarding the chazakah years, they did not contradict the ancestral ownership, and that part remains valid according to Rav Huna. That is correct, but I am troubled by Raavan. Raavan writes that where one witness says one hundred and another says two hundred, neither becomes disqualified because their disagreement concerns subsidiary examinations. He argues that if the witnesses became disqualified, then where two witnesses say one hundred and two say two hundred, Beit Hillel could not award even one hundred. Even Rav Huna would agree the testimony is invalid where all concern one borrower and one lender, because one of the sets must be false. At first glance, why is this different from the ancestral-ownership case? Regarding the first hundred, no one contradicts anyone. The contradiction concerns only the additional hundred. We should preserve the common hundred. Moreover, the Gemara itself says that with one borrower and lender but two separate documents, one for one hundred and one for two hundred, the claimant receives only one hundred because the burden of proof rests on him. So why does Raavan stress one borrower and one lender? Raavan must be addressing Tosafot's question in Sanhedrin 31a. On the first hundred, all four witnesses testify. If one set is false, then among the four who jointly testify to the common hundred there is a disqualified witness, and ordinarily where one of a group is related or disqualified the entire testimony fails. Raavan evidently treats Beit Shammai's position, where one witness says one hundred and another two hundred and the testimony does not combine, not as a personal disqualification of either witness. Rather, the testimony fails because Scripture requires two witnesses giving matching testimony. This avoids saying the common hundred is ruined by a disqualified participant. Now the distinction from the ancestral case is clear. There, regarding ancestral ownership, only one set supplies that testimony. We preserve that set in its presumption of fitness according to Rav Huna. The opposing set contradicts only the chazakah years, not the ancestral ownership. But if all four witnesses themselves testify concerning the common hundred, then if contradiction creates personal disqualification, the rule of a related or disqualified participant threatens the entire common testimony. The same distinction applies to two documents. Two witnesses testify on one document and two on the other; not all four testify together concerning each document. Therefore one valid document can remain for one hundred. This is likely what Beit Yosef intended in citing Meisharim. His rule is especially significant where all the witnesses testify regarding the uncontested portion. Even then, Meisharim holds that contradiction does not invoke the rule of "one found related or disqualified" so as to destroy the agreed portion. He follows the Tosafot answer that contradiction is not treated like an intrinsically related or disqualified witness. That is a substantial novelty. One Tosafot answer indeed preserves the testimony this way, but Raavan does not accept that approach. I am therefore uncertain whether practical halachah fully follows Meisharim in this broader form.
