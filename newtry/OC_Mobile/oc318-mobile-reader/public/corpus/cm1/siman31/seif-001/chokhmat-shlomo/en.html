Seif 1. Note: See the Shach, seif katan 1, and the proof he brings from the Ran to resolve the doubt of the sages of Brisk. I saw in Gaon Tzvi that, based on a responsum of Maharik, he argues that where one witness testifies that a slaughterer slaughtered improperly, that witness may later eat from the slaughterer’s slaughter. The person is not permanently established as disqualified on the strength of one witness, and perhaps he repented. Gaon Tzvi therefore says that the Ran’s case, which concerns the very same occasion, proves nothing about a later occasion. In my view the Shach is correct. A slaughterer from whose hand an invalid slaughter occurred is different from a witness who is known to be wicked. With slaughter, the principal concern is that the slaughterer was careless and some technical failure may have occurred. He does not thereby become a deliberate apostate whose slaughter is carrion even when performed correctly. Thus, if the witness later personally sees him slaughter properly, the meat is certainly permitted. Even without seeing the entire act, several doubts may combine: perhaps he repented, and perhaps no defect occurred this time. But with testimony, the Torah says, “Do not place a wicked person as a witness.” Even truthful testimony of a wicked person is disqualified, and another witness who knows him to be wicked may not join with him. A doubtful repentance does not remove a certain legal disqualification. More precisely, the original question of the Brisk sages likely concerned matters in which formal testimony is indispensable, such as gittin and kiddushin. There, if the witnesses are not legally valid, the transaction cannot be established merely because the underlying facts are true. A doubtful repentance cannot uproot a definite prior disqualification. In cases where testimony serves only to clarify a factual reality that could exist and be accepted independently, there is more room to combine doubts and perhaps rely on the later testimony. The Ran’s proof concerns a case in the same setting, where there was no possibility of intervening repentance at all, and therefore shows at least the underlying principle. There. “Two groups of witnesses who contradict one another.” I have a further doubt. Suppose Reuven and Shimon each swore against the other in a case such as the shopkeeper and workers, where both sides swear and collect from the employer even though one oath must be false, since we do not follow Ben Nannas who objects that both cannot be brought to a vain oath. The same occurs in siman 72, seif 28, where lender and borrower make opposing claims and both may swear. If these two litigants later sign together on a document, is the document invalid because one of them certainly swore falsely and is therefore disqualified, or is it valid because we cannot identify which one lied? The scholars of Brisk and Urim VeTumim hold that where one witness contradicts another, each may later testify separately even though one of them certainly lied. The reason is that neither has been established as false by two witnesses. It would seem that the same should apply to contradictory oaths. Since neither individual has been proven by two witnesses to have sworn falsely, neither has been legally disqualified. The later document signed by both should therefore remain valid. This requires further study. Seif 3. The Sema writes that where a single witness has already operated to impose a monetary liability, that same witness cannot additionally operate to impose an oath. It seems to me there is proof from Bava Metzia 4a, where the Gemara asks regarding two witnesses, “What is unique about witnesses? They establish him as a denier.” At first glance this is backwards. If the witnesses establish him as a liar, that should be a reason to impose more liability, not less. Rashi explains that once he is established as a liar, we do not trust him with an oath. This supports the Sema’s principle: after the witnesses have already performed one legal function, namely establishing his denial as false and creating monetary consequences, they are not simultaneously used to create an additional oath obligation. By contrast, a person’s own partial admission or a single witness, where their primary function has not already been spent on another legal consequence, can generate the oath. This fits the Sema’s reasoning. Mechaber, Seif 3. “One lender.” I find difficulty with Bava Kamma 98 regarding one who burns another’s debt document. According to the view that judges dina degarmi, he pays the full value of the document. The Gemara asks: if witnesses know what was written in it, let them write another document; if there are no witnesses, how do we know what it contained? It answers that the burner admits its contents. Why not answer with a case involving two contradictory groups of witnesses, each of which may testify separately? Suppose Reuven’s original debt document was signed by one group. If the document remained, Reuven could collect with it, because that group may come independently and testify. Shimon then burned the document, and the second contradictory group had seen what was written in it. They cannot write a replacement document that combines their own testimony with the first group’s instrument, because collection on the replacement would necessarily depend on both contradictory groups together, while one of them is certainly disqualified. Yet they can testify against the burner that he destroyed a document that had real collection value, since for that purpose their testimony concerns the independent fact of damage. They are merely saying that he burned an asset which, while extant, could have been collected upon. That should be valid under the rule that each contradictory group may testify separately. The Gemara could then have illustrated dina degarmi with such a case. This requires substantial study.
