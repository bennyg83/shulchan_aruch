And he shall swear concerning the remainder. Shach already objects to Sema's explanation that this is a Torah oath with a sacred object, as I wrote in Urim. Shach's own answer, that the case is where the borrower has no land, is also strained, because such an essential condition should not have been omitted. In my view, the commentators momentarily overlooked Rambam in Hilchot Ishut, chapter 16. Rambam rules that where she says, "I married as a virgin and my ketubah is two hundred," and the husband says she was a widow and entitled only to one hundred, he pays one hundred and takes a Torah oath concerning the rest. Raavad objects that this is a claim secured by land. Maggid Mishneh answers that Rambam states the law after the enactment of the Geonim that movable property is also subject to collection, so the claim is no longer treated solely as a land lien. As I explain below in siman 88, Rosh also follows this approach. Therefore, just as Rambam states the ketubah law according to the later enactment, so too the present ruling is stated according to the current law that a creditor can collect from movable property of heirs. The lien attaches to movables as well, and there is no difficulty in requiring the Torah oath. I discuss the substance of this law at length in siman 88. Gidulei Terumah raises another case. Two documents have the same date, amount, and lien, the lender produces both and claims both, and the borrower denies both. Since one of the documents must be false, must the borrower swear regarding the second? There is no real doubt. We are not saying the underlying loan is certainly false; we are saying that one set of witnesses must be invalid. Thus there is no testimony regarding one of the two documents. The lender claims two hundred through two documents of one hundred each, while the borrower denies everything. One document is certainly valid. This is exactly R. Chiya's first rule: the claimant demands two hundred and witnesses establish one hundred, so the defendant must take a Torah oath concerning the remainder. It further appears that if the borrower swore and afterward the witnesses on the document concerning which he swore appear and testify orally that they truly signed a valid loan, the borrower becomes disqualified from testimony and oaths, because those witnesses now testify that he swore falsely. Their testimony is not defeated by the uncertainty created by the lender's possession of two conflicting documents. The court refused to extract the second hundred only because the lender himself produced two documents and thereby admitted that one set of witnesses must be invalid. That admission by the lender does not personally disqualify either set of witnesses. When one set later appears orally and testifies to the truth of its own transaction, their testimony stands separately. As I explain below in siman 34, if a defendant swears against one witness, that witness may later join another witness to disqualify him. Although the defendant prevailed monetarily through the oath, the witness's testimony itself did not become disqualified. All the more so here. This creates a further doubt. Suppose the witnesses signed a document for two hundred and the borrower admits the loan but claims he repaid. Is he believed through a migo that he could have denied the loan entirely? Perhaps not. He may fear denying the loan because if he swears and the witnesses later appear and testify orally to the loan, he will be disqualified for having sworn falsely. By claiming repayment, however, he does not contradict the witnesses. Tosafot make a similar point in Perek Chezkas HaBatim concerning the case of the metal bar of R. Abba, and according to Rosh we may not recognize a migo that requires one to contradict witnesses in a manner carrying personal disqualification. On the other hand, there is reason to support the migo. The usual reason a borrower cannot say "I repaid" against a document is the claim, "What is your document doing in the creditor's hand?" Here he may have left it there because he relied on the fact that one of the two documents necessarily contains invalid testimony and expected the court not to extract on both. Still, this is not convincing. The borrower did not know the lender would produce both documents together. The lender might have produced them sequentially or in different courts. Leaving a paid document in the creditor's possession remains suspicious. I therefore remain uncertain whether this migo exists where denial would expose him to later personal disqualification if the witnesses appeared. There is also a broader question concerning Rav Huna's rule that contradictory sets remain fit when each appears separately. May one such set testify that another person, previously presumed fit, is a robber or otherwise disqualified? The set itself is under a cloud because one of the two contradictory sets is false. Rav Huna preserves each through a presumption of fitness where it testifies to an ordinary matter. But here preserving the witnesses' presumption of fitness would require destroying the other person's presumption of fitness. Why prefer the witnesses' chazakah over his? Tosafot and Ran discuss analogous issues where two sets contest a person's qualification. I later saw Tosafot in Ketubot 22a write that according to R. Chisda, who treats contradictory witnesses as false, such a set obviously cannot testify that another person is disqualified, even if no one contradicts them. Their wording implies that according to Rav Huna they may be able to do so. Still, perhaps Tosafot chose R. Chisda only because his case is indisputable, while Rav Huna's case remains doubtful. The matter requires study.
And some say it is preferable for him to burn the smaller document, etc. See Urim, where I wrote that Sema's entire explanation is already stated in Bedek HaBayit by Beit Yosef. Sema evidently did not have that work before him and independently reached the same conclusion. There is nevertheless a basic question. How can the borrower impose an oath that the lender does not possess the larger document? The borrower has no definite claim. Even if he saw the larger document in the lender's possession earlier, perhaps the lender burned it a moment later. The answer must be that there are strong grounds for suspicion. Since the borrower saw the larger document in his possession, the ordinary assumption is that the lender retained it and may later produce it in another beit din. This resembles cases such as a coin seen falling from one of two people or an open chest, where even without absolute certainty the circumstantial basis can support an oath. Mechaber below in siman 75 does not accept the open-chest rule and generally requires an actual definite claim for an oath. He therefore omits the oath rule here. Rama follows the view in siman 75 that strong circumstantial grounds can suffice and therefore writes this here as "some say," reflecting the underlying dispute. It appears further that if the lender burns the smaller document immediately after the borrower raises the claim in beit din but before the oath is taken, that is sufficient. Ultimately, he then holds only one document and cannot cause the double loss that generated the concern. This explains Tur's advice: if the lender is clever, he will first claim in one court with the larger document and then produce the smaller document in another court. Bach asks: what cleverness is this? The borrower can demand an oath, and surely the lender will not swear falsely. According to what I have written, the tactic presents little downside. Perhaps the borrower will not remember to demand the oath, and the lender will collect both documents in separate courts. If the borrower does remember and demands an oath, the lender can immediately burn the smaller document and still collect the larger one. He loses nothing. If burning after the demand did not help, Bach's objection would be strong, because merely bringing both documents would create a risk that he would be forced to disclose both and collect only the smaller. Therefore the rule is as stated: burning the smaller document before the oath removes the basis for the oath and allows collection on the larger document.
So that the borrower should not make him swear, etc. This applies specifically where the lender first sues on the larger document, for two hundred, and the borrower denies it. There is then an immediate monetary issue: if the lender also possesses the smaller document for one hundred, he should not collect the larger amount in the same way. By concealing the smaller document, he may presently cause the borrower to pay an additional hundred. This is a current monetary claim and can support the oath. But if the lender first produces the smaller document for one hundred, what can the borrower say? "Produce the larger document"? Even according to his own allegation, he presently owes at least the hundred already claimed. The concern is only that the lender is now withholding the larger document and may later sue on it as well. That is a future suspicion, not a present monetary claim. Even regarding a suit first brought on the larger document, Rosh was uncertain whether an oath applies. Tur nevertheless decides that it does. We should not extend that ruling further to the case where the smaller document is produced first. This is also indicated by Tur's wording. He says only that when the lender produces the larger document the borrower can make him swear. Had the same rule applied whenever either document was produced, Tur should have written it generally. Why, then, did Tur advise the lender to burn the smaller document rather than simply collect on the smaller one first, thereby avoiding the oath? First, the borrower could still impose a general cherem, and who would advise the lender to place himself under possible cherem? Second, collecting the smaller document first is not necessarily safe. Before he can later collect the larger one, the borrower may lose his assets. Burning the smaller and collecting the larger is therefore preferable. Accordingly, the practical law is as stated. Sema further writes that according to Rosh, if both documents are presented in the same beit din, the court will not allow collection on the smaller after the lender already collected on the larger; only another beit din might later do so. One might ask from the general rule that a dispute among poskim is like two contradictory sets of witnesses. Rama below in siman 268 cites Terumat HaDeshen that where a person seized according to one authority and later according to another authority, if the first matter was already adjudicated we say the judgment stands. Even if one of the seizures must ultimately be wrongful, the first judgment can remain because of waiver or the power of beit din to render property ownerless. Why not say the same here? Once the larger document was collected, let that judgment stand, and then collect the smaller as well. There are two answers. First, in Terumat HaDeshen's case the litigant is in possession at the second stage as well. We therefore do not remove money from him because perhaps the first judgment created waiver. Here, when he comes with the second document he seeks to extract new money. Who says the first judgment created a waiver that authorizes the second extraction? Doubt cannot extract money. Second, in the ordinary "judgment stands" case, the court knew of the halachic dispute when it rendered the first ruling and nevertheless decided the case. That can create waiver or a valid beit din disposition. Here, had the first court known that the lender also possessed the smaller document, it would not have allowed him to collect the larger document in that manner. The original judgment was therefore issued through factual mistake, not through a conscious ruling despite a known legal doubt. No waiver or beit din forfeiture follows. Gidulei Terumah writes that where there were originally two lenders and two borrowers, and one lender later acquires the other's documents, or both documents are transferred to a third party, we still follow the original identity of the lenders and treat the case as two lenders rather than one lender with two borrowers. I do not know the basis for this. The present holder owns both documents and seeks to collect from one borrower in a situation where one of the two claims is necessarily improper. Why should it matter that originally there were two lenders? Nor can he say, "If you remain silent, I will restore the original arrangement," because the argument of "if you remain silent" is used to preserve possession, not to extract money from a possessor. Perhaps Gidulei Terumah intended only the unresolved Talmudic case of one lender and two borrowers, where the Gemara already leaves the matter doubtful and allows collection in certain sequential circumstances. One could then say the special uncertainty was stated only where the same lender existed from the outset. But where there are two lenders and one borrower, and the two documents are later acquired by one person, it appears that he cannot collect both. With one lender and one borrower everyone agrees he cannot collect two conflicting documents, because one is certainly false. A successor holding both documents stands in the same position: he cannot extract on both where one must be invalid. At minimum, doubt leaves the money with the borrower.
