He shall pay only one hundred. Since beit din does not know which of the two documents is invalid, the holder of the documents has the weaker hand. We therefore treat the larger document for two hundred as the invalid one, remove it from him, and burn it so it cannot later be used in another beit din. But if he produces the second document only after collecting on the first, he may collect on the second even in the same beit din, because the two were not simultaneously before the court. This too is included in the principle that each set comes separately and testifies. This appears to be the Mechaber's view following Rambam.
He shall swear concerning the remainder while holding a sacred object. Rambam and the Tur write this because he resembles one who admits part of a claim: the one hundred he pays through the first document functions like the admitted portion. Although ordinarily admission and denial established solely by documents do not generate a Torah oath, here the document is not certainly valid, so the matter is treated more like an oral claim and admission. Raavad and Ran disagree and require only hesset.
Some say it is better for him to burn the smaller one, etc. It is difficult why Rama phrases this as a dissent merely concerning good advice. It appears rather that the dispute concerns whether the borrower can initially require the lender to swear that he does not possess both documents. According to the Mechaber's unqualified rule, when the lender produces one document alone, it is treated as a separate valid appearance even if the same beit din later sees the second. The dissent holds the borrower may require an oath at the outset, and therefore the lender is advised to burn the smaller document before collection.
So that they will not make him swear, etc. Meaning a hesset oath. It appears specifically the borrower must demand this oath; beit din does not impose it sua sponte even according to this view. This applies when he is collecting on the first document. When he later brings the second in the same beit din, according to the dissenting view the court itself should not facilitate a second collection once it knows the first has already been collected.
He shall make him swear, etc. If the smaller document was burned, there is no point in asking whether he burned another document. Even if he admits that another existed and was burned, he can collect on the present document because there is no longer any possibility of collecting twice. It is as though only one document remains, and that one may be treated as valid.
This one swears and collects, and that one, etc. Each lender swears with a stringent oath while holding a sacred object that the debt is owed and then collects. Although one of them necessarily relies on an invalid document, their case resembles the shopkeeper and workers, where both sides may swear and collect despite the certainty that one account is false.
Even if a credibility clause is written in the documents. Since one group of witnesses is invalid, the document containing its credibility clause is likewise invalid. The rule that each of two lenders may collect on his own document does not allow the court to rely on a special credibility clause embedded in the document whose validity is itself doubtful.
