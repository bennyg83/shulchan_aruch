He must swear concerning the remainder while holding a sacred object. So rule the Rambam and Tur. Their reason is that he is comparable to one who admits part of a claim, namely the maneh that he must pay by virtue of one of the documents. An admission from his own mouth should not have greater force than testimony of witnesses. Although denial and admission concerning a written obligation do not ordinarily create a Torah oath, as stated below in siman 88, seif 28, that applies to a document certainly valid, whereas here no particular document is certainly valid, so the matter is treated like an oral claim and admission. So explains the Sema. His meaning is that because there is no certainly valid document, there is no definite lien on land. The Raavad raised this difficulty, and the Kesef Mishneh answered in this manner. I do not understand the answer, for regarding one maneh there is certainly a valid document, whichever it may be. Perhaps they intended the Bach's explanation that the smaller sum may be collected only from unencumbered property and not from purchasers, as the Baal HaTerumot writes in the name of Ri Migash. In my view, however, this is incorrect. The maneh may certainly be collected, even from encumbered property, whichever document is valid. The Baal HaTerumot there discusses one lender and two borrowers, as in seif 3, where collection is limited because had the borrower denied the claim it could not have been collected. Alternatively, he discusses our case with respect to the larger sum. Regarding the smaller sum, which is certainly due, his words imply that it may be collected even from encumbered property. The same is true where there are two lenders and two borrowers. Rather, it appears that the Rambam is not discussing land liens here. He means only that this case is analogous to Rabbi Chiyya's rule and to partial admission. The same question arises in every partial admission and in Rabbi Chiyya's case according to the view that liens are biblical even for an oral loan: how can a Torah oath ever arise? It must be where the debtor owns no land, as Tosafot, the Ran, the Rosh, the Tur, and the Mechaber explain. The Rambam too should be understood in that case. The Ran, who writes here that the defendant takes a hesset oath, does not necessarily disagree in law; he discusses a debtor who owns land. The Kesef Mishneh further asks that the defendant cannot deny the first hundred, whereas the Rambam rules that one who cannot deny is not liable through partial admission. This is no difficulty. Exemption where he admits what he cannot deny is based on the rule of 'here it is,' whereas here he does not admit the amount written in the document, as I distinguished in siman 75, seif 6.
