And they tear them. This is done so that the claimant cannot use the documents improperly by suing again before another beit din. If, however, the second document is produced only after the first claimant already collected, the second claimant may collect even in that same beit din, because the two documents were not before the court together. This too falls within the principle that each set of witnesses is treated as having come separately. This appears to be the Mechaber's view. The Sema further writes that the reason the defendant swears concerning the remainder with an oath administered while holding a sacred object is that the case resembles a partial admission, namely the maneh that he pays pursuant to the one document. Although an admission and denial appearing in a document ordinarily do not create a Torah oath, as explained in siman 88, seif 28, that applies where the document is certainly valid. Here, where neither document is certainly valid on its own, the matter resembles an oral claim and admission. The Raavad and Ran disagree and hold that only a shevuat heset is required. The Shach writes that he does not understand this answer, because one maneh is certainly supported by a valid document one way or the other. Perhaps the intent is as the Bach writes, that even the smaller claim may be collected only from unencumbered property rather than from purchasers. The Shach rejects that view as well, holding that one maneh certainly may be collected from encumbered property. He therefore explains that Rambam is not addressing liens on land here and is speaking where the debtor has no land. The Kesef Mishnah further objects that the defendant cannot deny the one hundred, while Rambam rules in Hilchot To'en that one who cannot deny an amount is not liable for a partial-admission oath. The Kesef Mishnah leaves this unresolved. The Shach answers that only where the defendant himself admits is he exempt by reason of "here it is"; where he does not admit the amount established by the document, the exemption does not apply, as stated in siman 75, seif 5, in the gloss.
He shall make him swear. Meaning a shevuat heset. It appears that this is specifically something the borrower may demand; beit din does not administer the oath on its own if the borrower does not raise the matter. This applies when the claimant seeks to collect with the first document. If he later brings the second document before that same beit din, they do not collect on it, because they know he already collected with the first. If he burned one document, there is no point in asking him to swear whether he burned another, because even if he admits burning one he may collect on the document he now presents, since he can no longer attempt to collect on both. The Sema questions why Rama introduces the advice to burn one document as "some say," since it is merely practical advice and need not represent a dispute. He suggests that all may agree to the advice. Rama writes it as a dissent because the Mechaber appears to hold that when the claimant first comes with only one document, the defendant cannot compel him to swear that he has no other document. Rama records an opinion that the borrower may compel such an oath, and therefore it is advisable for the lender to burn the smaller document. The Taz asks why Chazal would offer this advice, unlike siman 248 where one who advises a sale designed to defeat another's rights is called wicked. He answers that there the sale is prohibited even from the seller's own perspective, whereas here, according to the lender who knows the truth that the debt is genuinely owed, there is no prohibition.
