And the borrower denied both documents. This applies specifically where the borrower denied all the documents. If he admitted the smaller one, he would not incur a Torah oath for denying the other creditor's larger claim, because he could not have denied the amount he admits, since in any event there is a document supporting it. The law of partial admission therefore does not apply. But since he denies all of them, he is subject to a Torah oath under the first ruling of Rabbi Chiyya, even though he could not validly have denied them. Shach, and this is correct. See what I wrote above in siman 88.
And he shall swear concerning the remainder while holding a sacred object. Sema explains that this is not considered a claim secured by land because the creditor cannot collect from encumbered property, since it is unknown which document is valid. Shach correctly objects that at least with regard to the one hundred that must certainly be paid, there is necessarily a lien on land. Whichever of the two documents is valid, that document entitles the creditor to collect one hundred from encumbered property. It is therefore plainly a claim secured by land. Shach answers that the case concerns a debtor who owns no land, but this is strained. See Tumim, where I wrote that there is no difficulty because Rambam follows his own view that after the Geonic enactment concerning collection from movables of heirs, the category of a claim secured by land no longer applies in this way. See what I wrote at length in siman 88. If the borrower refuses to swear and admits that the document is genuine, Baal HaTerumot writes that the creditor still may not collect from purchasers, because the purchasers are not bound by his admission and may claim that the larger document is forged. See what I wrote above in siman 47. If the two documents are equal in amount, the lender claims both, and the borrower denies them, I likewise concluded in Tumim that according to Rambam he must take a Torah oath, just as where one document is smaller and one larger, unlike Gidulei Terumah, who remained uncertain. See Tumim, where I also left unresolved a case in which the borrower admits that the larger document is genuine but claims he paid it, while the witnesses to that document are alive. Since, had he denied the document, those witnesses could contradict him, is he believed through a migo that he could have said, "It never happened," or not? See there. I also discussed whether those witnesses may disqualify the borrower if he swore that the transaction never occurred and they testify that the document is genuine, since in this respect each document is considered independently. I further questioned whether they are believed to remove a person from his presumption of fitness when their own testimony is subject to a possible disqualification. See there. I likewise left unresolved a case where both groups of witnesses testify identically to part of the matter, for example that a person has ancestral title, but they contradict each other regarding the years of possession, one group saying that one person consumed the produce and the other that another person did. Does their common testimony establish the ancestral title, or is this comparable to a group in which one witness was found related or disqualified? See there.
He collects that one also, etc. In Bedek HaBayit, Beit Yosef states explicitly that Rambam's words imply that even before one beit din the creditor may collect on the two documents one after the other. For that reason he writes that the debtor cannot impose any oath upon the creditor at all, because if both may be collected before one court there is no basis for such an oath. The Mechaber therefore omitted the rule that the debtor may require the oath, because he understands Rambam not to accept it. Rama, however, records it as "some say," following the Tur and Rosh, who hold that before the same beit din the creditor may not collect the documents consecutively and who also hold that the debtor may require the oath. These matters are therefore disputed. Sema independently reached the same conclusions recorded in Bedek HaBayit, apparently without having seen that work, as is clear from seif katan 6. His analysis is correct. See Tumim.
Some say it is preferable, etc. Meaning it is preferable not to administer the oath. See Tumim, where I write that this follows the Rama below in siman 75, who allows an oath on a claim whose basis is uncertain. According to the Mechaber there, no oath is administered here either.
He may require him to swear, etc. See Tumim. This applies specifically where the creditor first produced the larger document, creating a concrete monetary concern, and therefore an oath may be required. If he first sued on the smaller document, the debtor may not require an oath, though he may impose a general cherem. If the creditor burned the smaller document, even if he admits doing so the debtor has no claim against him so long as he is suing only on the larger document. Sema in the name of Darkei Moshe.
That he has two documents, etc. According to this view, after producing the larger document and collecting upon it, the creditor may not then produce the smaller document before the same beit din. He must collect it before another beit din. So write Sema and Knesset HaGedolah. It appears to me that even another beit din helps only when the second court does not know that the larger document was collected before the first court. If they do know, what difference is there between this court and another? How can we allow him to retain money unjustly? If the reason is merely that he has already collected, then again it should make no difference which court hears the second claim. Yet according to this, I do not understand why the debtor may not require him to swear that he did not previously collect the larger debt in another beit din. If he admits that he did, he would lose. This requires study. See Tumim concerning Gidulei Terumah's discussion of one person who purchased both documents by assignment, where the case may no longer be treated as one lender and two borrowers. This requires study. Gidulei Terumah further writes that where there are two lenders and two borrowers, there is not even an oath, and each document is governed like an ordinary document.
This one swears and collects, etc. This is analogous to the grocer on his ledger. Strict law would require payment to each claimant without an oath, but because the debtor is being compelled to pay one of them in a manner that may not be legally justified, each must swear while holding a sacred object before collecting. So too here: because the borrower will pay one creditor on a document whose witnesses may be disqualified, the creditor must swear before collecting. This is the Rif's explanation, and it is correct. Sema combines Rashi's and Rif's explanations, which is not correct. See Tumim, where I wrote that if the two lenders assigned their documents to one person, the case becomes one lender against one borrower, and he cannot extract money from the borrower in a doubtful manner.
Even if the document contains a clause of credibility, etc. The reason is explained below in siman 82. A credibility clause helps only against a plea of payment, but it does not prevent a plea that the underlying transaction never occurred.
