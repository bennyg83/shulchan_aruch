And they divide. The Shach infers that where the holder admits he owes one maneh to one of two uncertain claimants, the parties divide because neither claimant makes a definite claim and there is no liar. According to Tosafot and Rosh, however, the rule of deposit does not depend chiefly on whether one party is lying. It depends on whether the proposed division can be true. Where two people each claim the same found garment, division can be true because both may own half. Where one specific third maneh belongs wholly to one claimant, division cannot reflect the true ownership. In such a case the proper rule is to leave the money deposited rather than divide, even if neither claimant is lying. Rosh's wording confirms that for the Sages the key criterion is whether both parties can truthfully own what division assigns, while Rabbi Yosei emphasizes the presence of a liar. Therefore, in the present case, the statement that they divide should be understood as applying only where the parties themselves consent to division. A separate case of unresolved law can produce division because a legal doubt itself creates a genuine monetary uncertainty independent of the parties' claims. That is the rule of Sumchus-type monetary doubt and does not undermine the distinction.
