And he pays the stolen property. See Shach, seif katan 20, who says that the Mishnah differs because the robber knew from whom he stole and admitted it, and therefore he is not discharged merely by placing the stolen item between the claimants. I do not understand why the Gemara needs that reasoning. According to R. Tarfon, in an ordinary case where several people claim the stolen object, the robber may place it among them and withdraw even though the actual owner has not received it. Why should the mere fact that he admitted to one person change the rule so fundamentally? The better explanation requires distinguishing the legal effect of the admission itself and whether it created a new deposit relationship. If, after acknowledging the owner, he effectively accepted that person's property as a deposit in his hands, ordinary custodial obligations can arise. The Gemara's language must be understood in that framework rather than as a mere consequence of knowing the victim's identity.
