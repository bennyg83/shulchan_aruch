Knows. They too do not make a definite claim, each saying, "I am the one from whom you stole." Sema.
They divide. So write the Rambam and Rabbeinu Yerucham. It implies that by law they divide it, not merely if they both agree. Otherwise the ruling would be obvious, and the same should have been taught regarding a deposit or purchase. Moreover, the Rambam holds that wherever the Mishnah says "it shall remain," it remains with the holder. Why then does he write here, "he gives one hundred"? Rather, the law is that they divide it. Only in a sale or deposit where they demand it and one is certainly lying do we say the money remains. Here, since they themselves do not know that they were robbed, there is no liar. See the Gemara and Rosh at the beginning of chapter Shnayim Ochazin. Shach.
Heaven. In an analogous deposit he is exempt even from the obligation to satisfy Heaven, as stated in siman 300, seif 3, in the gloss, because the depositor should have been precise. The Rosh writes likewise. Ma'adanei Melech explains the distinction: one assumes the robber is a violent man and is unlikely to return, so the victim did not demand payment. This is difficult, because the law necessarily includes a case where only after the robber says, "I robbed one of you," each says, "you robbed me," and the rule depends on whether the claimant now asserts certainty. Rather, "I robbed one of you" is taught like "the father of one of you deposited," meaning they themselves do not know. Therefore the Mishnah does not say "one of you deposited," because where one deposited he certainly knows and should demand it now. Accordingly, if he certainly knows he robbed one of them in their presence but does not know which, and they do not now demand with certainty, he places the stolen item between them and withdraws, and is exempt even from the obligation to satisfy Heaven.
