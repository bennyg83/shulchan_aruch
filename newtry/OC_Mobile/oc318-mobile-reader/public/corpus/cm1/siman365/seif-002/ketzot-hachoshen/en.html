In an analogous deposit case, where the depositors themselves are uncertain and the custodian had no reason to distinguish their property, he can be exempt even from heavenly liability. Rosh explains that if the deposit was made in one bundle, the custodian had no duty to keep track separately. My grandfather, Maadanei Yom Tov, explains why theft differs. A victim of theft may not pay close attention at the time because he assumes the violent thief will never return the property. Therefore the victim's present uncertainty does not necessarily count against him. The deeper distinction is that custodial liability for confusion arises because the custodian should have been careful. If the depositor himself had equal reason to be careful and now makes only an uncertain claim, we do not impose even heavenly liability on the custodian. A thief, however, is liable not because he failed to keep records but because he committed a transgression. The Sages penalized him because of that wrongdoing. Therefore even where the victim is now uncertain, the robber can still bear a stronger duty to satisfy Heaven.
Regarding a deposit, liability where two deposits were given separately rests on the fact that the custodian should have been careful to distinguish them. If the depositors themselves now do not make definite claims, that consideration weakens sharply, because they too had reason to remember. Where one bundle was deposited, even heavenly liability is absent because the custodian never had a duty to distinguish. Theft is different. The thief's liability is not merely a consequence of failing to remember. The Gemara says the Sages penalized him because he committed a transgression. Therefore even where the victim cannot make a definite claim, the thief can remain liable under the special penalty. This explains why the Gemara gives two different reasons: for theft, because he committed wrongdoing; for deposit, because he should have been careful. Had the theft rule rested merely on failure to remember, the victim's own lack of care would weaken the claim as it does in deposit. The penal basis avoids that difficulty.
