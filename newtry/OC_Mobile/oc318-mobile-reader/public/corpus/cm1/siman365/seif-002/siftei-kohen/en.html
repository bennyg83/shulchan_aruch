And they divide it between themselves, etc. Rambam and Rabbeinu Yerucham write this, and it implies that as a matter of law they divide it, rather than leaving it until Eliyahu comes. One cannot say that the meaning is merely that they may divide it if both consent, because that would be obvious and teach nothing. Moreover, if so, regarding a deposit above in siman 300 and a sale above in siman 222, seif 2, why did Rambam and the Mechaber not teach that they may divide it if they wish? Rambam further holds that whenever the Mishnah says, "it shall remain until Eliyahu comes," it remains in the holder's possession, and Rabbeinu Yerucham writes likewise, as explained in siman 222. Why, then, do they write here that he gives one hundred? It must be that here the law is that he gives one hundred and they divide it, and we do not say that it remains until Eliyahu comes. Above, in simanim 300 and 222, where each makes a definite claim, one of them is certainly lying, and therefore we apply that rule. Here, since the claimants themselves do not know which of them was robbed, there is no liar, and therefore they divide it. See the Gemara and Rosh at the beginning of Perek Shnayim Ochazin and examine carefully.

If he wishes to fulfill his obligation toward Heaven, etc. In the parallel case of a deposit, he is exempt even from the obligation toward Heaven, as stated above, siman 300, seif 10, in the gloss, because the depositor himself should have been careful, as the Rosh writes in Perek HaMafkid. Maadanei Melech explains that the distinction between a deposit and robbery is that the victim assumed the robber was a violent man who had robbed him and that it was uncommon for such a person to return the money, and therefore he did not demand it. This explanation is difficult. Necessarily, even if he did not demand it initially, but only after the robber says that he robbed one of them each says, "you robbed me," the same rule applies. The law does not depend on whether he demanded it earlier, but on whether the robbed party or depositor now makes a definite claim, as is clear from the Gemara in Perek HaMafkid and the poskim. Thus, in the robbery case too, the case must be one where even now he does not make a definite claim, so it cannot be explained by saying that he believed the robber would never return it, because he now sees that the robber does wish to return it. Rather, the teaching "I robbed one of you" parallels the case "the father of one of you deposited with me," meaning, "I robbed one of you and I do not know which." The baraita therefore does not teach, "one of you deposited one hundred with me," because when a person makes a deposit he certainly knows whom he deposited with, and this is equivalent to making a definite claim now. Accordingly, where it is known to a person that he was robbed in his presence and he knew of the robbery, but the robber does not know which of them he robbed, and the claimant does not now demand it with certainty, the robber may place the stolen property between them and withdraw, and is exempt even from the obligation toward Heaven.
