And the moser denies the claim. Maggid Mishneh, cited by Beit Yosef, writes that Rambam appears to agree with Rabbeinu Tam that if the moser does not deny the event but says he does not know how much loss he caused, the person informed upon takes an oath and collects. Maharshal rules similarly in chapter HaKones, siman 90.
The moser says, 'I do not know.' This is Rabbeinu Tam's position in Tosafos. The Gemara's unresolved question whether the special enactment for one who was robbed applies to a moser concerns only where the moser makes a definite denial. If he says he does not know, the injured person certainly swears and collects. This is also supported by the difficulty raised by the early authorities: if dina d'garmi creates Torah liability, why should the moser differ from any other tortfeasor? Ramban in his novellae on dina d'garmi answers that the moser caused the damage merely through speech. But this is still difficult. Why should speech matter if the damage caused by the speech is treated like an act? And even according to the authorities who hold dina d'garmi rabbinic, once Chazal imposed liability for such damage he should certainly be within the tortfeasor enactment. According to Rabbeinu Tam the matter is clear. The Gemara never asked about a case where the injured person claims with certainty and the moser says he does not know. With an ordinary tortfeasor, the special enactment for a robbed person was not made where the tortfeasor makes a definite denial, and even regarding fire the enactment was not extended to every such case. The question is only whether, because informing is especially severe, Chazal treated a moser fully like a robber so that the injured party may swear and collect even against a definite denial, or whether a moser is no more severe in this respect than an ordinary tortfeasor. Therefore Rabbeinu Tam properly holds that where the moser says 'I do not know' there was never a question. Even if dina d'garmi is rabbinic, the moser remains a rabbinically liable tortfeasor and is included in the ordinary enactment for damages. The unresolved issue is only the case of a definite denial. See what I wrote in siman 386, seif katan 1.
The person informed upon shall swear and collect. The Shach, seif katan 36, writes that according to his general ruling like Rambam and those who follow him, even where the defendant had no reason to know, the rule is that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay. At first glance the same should apply here. He nevertheless raises another issue: Ramban holds that a Torah oath is not imposed for liabilities arising from dina d'garmi, and some poskim hold that dina d'garmi itself is only rabbinic, in which case perhaps no Torah oath applies at all. I have already written in siman 386, seif katan 4, that Ramban's words are not conclusive. He states only the Talmudic rule that no Torah oath is imposed concerning promissory notes. That can be understood where the documents themselves still exist. Where the documents no longer exist, such as where they were burned, the matter depends on the dispute between Rambam and Raavad. Rambam follows the original character of the damaged object, so destruction of land remains legally a claim about land, and destruction of documents remains a claim about documents. Raavad looks to the final monetary loss, in which case an oath can apply after the documents were burned. Even if we assume no Torah oath is imposed when one burns a document, that is because the claim is still fundamentally about an object excluded from the oath laws, since the Torah oath concerns objects whose body has monetary value. A claim against a moser is entirely different. Once dina d'garmi makes him liable, the claim is a direct monetary claim for the loss he caused, not a claim upon a document whose body lacks monetary value. The Shach also suggests that if dina d'garmi is rabbinic, a Torah oath cannot apply. But I wrote in siman 87, seif katan 32, that even on money whose underlying obligation is rabbinic, a Torah oath can apply, together with the rule that one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay. Still, Rabbeinu Tam's statement that the injured party 'swears and collects' cannot be based on that rule. If it were, no oath would be needed, because in the classic case of fifty known and fifty unknown, the claimant collects without taking an oath. Rather, Rabbeinu Tam's oath is the enactment made for a robbed person. Tosafos also hold that where the defendant had no reason to know, the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear does not apply. The injured party swears because of the special enactment. The Gemara's uncertainty is whether this stronger enactment was extended to a moser even against a definite denial, not whether it applies where the moser says he does not know. The Rosh writes that the question remained unresolved because a moser causes loss through dina d'garmi and mere speech. That difficulty arises because the Rosh understands the question to include even a moser who says he does not know. According to Rabbeinu Tam, however, the case of a custodian presents no difficulty because it too concerns a defendant who says he does not know. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 47, cites the Rosh and asks that although a moser causes loss through speech, he is still more comparable to a tortfeasor than to a negligent custodian. A moser pays from his best property, as appears at the beginning of Bava Kamma, while a negligent custodian supposedly does not. I am astonished by this question, because the same opening sugya of Bava Kamma that includes a moser among tort categories also includes the four bailees among the principal categories of monetary liability, and Rif and Rosh explicitly state that the four bailees pay from their best property. Maharshal in chapter HaKones, siman 30, likewise writes that the case of a moser concerns a definite denial. Although an enactment was made regarding one harmed by fire, the ordinary tortfeasor is not wicked like a robber; only a moser is sufficiently severe to raise the possibility that he should be treated like a robber. This supports Rabbeinu Tam. Where the moser says 'I do not know,' he is no less liable than a custodian or ordinary tortfeasor. This also appears from the plain wording of Shulchan Aruch, which speaks of 'the moser denies,' implying that if he merely says he does not know, the injured party certainly swears and collects. Rama did not bring a dissenting opinion, which likewise suggests he accepts the Shulchan Aruch's ruling.
If he causes him to be imprisoned. The Shach discusses this at length and concludes that no distinction should be drawn between informing against a person's property and informing against his body. So long as the moser performed no physical act and caused the result only through speech, his liability is only through dina d'garmi. He writes that he would not have elaborated but for the fact that Mahari Weil, followed by Maharshal, Maharam Lublin, and other later authorities, ruled otherwise, and in his opinion their conclusion was an error. Mahari Weil, siman 86, ruled that since Rabbi David was a full moser, Rabbi Yishai may swear and collect from him whatever loss he knows he suffered through the final imprisonment. Although the early authorities treated the unresolved question whether the robbed-person enactment applies to a moser leniently, one who directly damages another's money is subject to the enactment used for fire. Here, Mahari Weil says, the injury is treated as direct because informing against a person's body is like direct physical damage. He relies on Rabbi Ephraim at the end of chapter HaGozel Kama, that one who informs against another's body is a direct tortfeasor, comparable to one who wounds him. Mordechai similarly writes that if one informs against another's person, even the Sages who exempt one who merely points out another's property because the injury is only indirect agree here. It resembles one who wounds another and pays medical costs, which are treated as though he directly deprived the victim of money. Likewise, handing over the person's body is treated as directly handing over his property. Mordechai proves that bodily damages differ from property damages in this respect because one who confines a person in a room and prevents him from working is liable for lost labor, whereas one who confines an animal and prevents its work is exempt. The reason is that when one damages property and thereby causes a secondary monetary loss, the loss is indirect and exempt, even according to Rabbi Meir. The Shach explains Mordechai as speaking only where the moser physically seizes his fellow and forcibly delivers him to imprisonment, not merely where he informs verbally. In that case, even though he does not personally seize the victim's money, because he physically delivers his body and monetary loss follows, the loss is treated as though he directly damaged the property. I find this explanation difficult. The ordinary term moser does not mean physical delivery, but informing by speech. Moreover, what would it mean for him personally to seize and imprison the victim? The matter depends upon the ruling authority. If the authority did not wish to imprison him, the informer's physical act could not accomplish it, and if the authority did wish to imprison him, the informer's escort added nothing. The real causative act is the speech. It therefore appears that Mordechai distinguishes between indirect monetary damage to property and indirect damage to a person. We find that one who injures another person pays for lost work and medical expenses even though these are themselves indirect monetary consequences. By contrast, one who pushes another's coin into the sea is exempt for expenses needed to retrieve it, because property caused another monetary loss. Thus Torah law recognizes some forms of garmi in person against person that it does not recognize in property damage. If one physically confines another in a room, he must pay for lost work even though he did not damage the body itself, but merely caused monetary loss by preventing work. On the same reasoning, one who causes the authorities to confine him should also be liable. Mahari Weil's ruling follows: the Gemara's uncertainty concerning a moser is relevant to ordinary monetary informing, which some understand as only rabbinic liability under dina d'garmi. But informing against the body can fall under Torah bodily-damage liability even according to the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Meir, and then the robbed-person type enactment certainly applies. I nevertheless hesitate about Mordechai's broad principle that person against person is liable for all garmi damages. The Torah's liability for lost work and medical costs may apply specifically where the defendant first performed a direct physical injury to the body, and the resulting medical and lost-work costs follow from that direct act. Where the initial act itself is only indirect, perhaps even person against person is exempt. The Mishnah that exempts one who frightens another or blasts into his ear because the damage is indirect appears to prove this. Perhaps Mordechai distinguishes between ordinary grama and the more immediate category of garmi, holding only garmi liable in person against person. Yet Rashi at the beginning of chapter HaKones does not appear to make that distinction. Rashi explains the exemption of one who frightens another because he holds indirect damage exempt, implying that according to Rabbi Meir, who judges dina d'garmi, even that case might be liable. Since Rashi does not distinguish between grama and garmi as the Shach notes in siman 386, seif katan 1, this suggests that according to those who reject dina d'garmi, even person against person can be exempt. Nevertheless, regarding the practical ruling, I agree with Mahari Weil, Rama, Maharshal, and Maharam Lublin. As I wrote above, the principal view is Rabbeinu Tam's, and Maharshal also adopts it. The Gemara's unresolved question concerns only a moser who makes a definite denial. If the moser says he does not know, no special question arises, because whether garmi is Torah law or rabbinic, he is at least a tortfeasor and the injured party can use the applicable enactment. The only question is whether a moser is so severe that even against a definite denial he is treated like a robber. That distinction is relevant to informing against property, because an ordinary tortfeasor does not face the stronger enactment against a definite denial. But informing against the body is comparable to bodily injury, and Chazal already enacted that an injured person may swear and collect even where the alleged injurer makes a definite denial. Since under Rabbeinu Tam there is no distinction for this purpose between direct damage and garmi once the category of bodily injury applies, a person whose body was handed over may swear and collect under the enactment for bodily injury. Thus the ruling of Maharshal and those who follow him is the principal one.
One who says to his fellow, 'You informed against me.' Rashba writes in a responsum that if a moser admits part of the claim, he pays what he admitted but does not pay the remainder under the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear, because he had no reason to know the amount of the other person's loss. The Shach writes that according to his ruling in siman 72, that even where the defendant had no reason to know we apply the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear, the same should apply here. This appears to contradict what the Shach himself wrote in seif katan 36, that even according to Rambam there may be no Torah oath here because the obligation arises from dina d'garmi. However, as I wrote above in seif katan 5, a Torah oath can apply even to the monetary claim against a moser.
He was not discharged from the first act of informing. The Shach writes that if there is a doubt whether the first moser was already discharged from the consequence of his first informing, we do not extract money because of the doubt and the later moser is exempt, citing Maharam. This requires study from Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchot Chovel, who rules that if two people together kill an animal or break an object they divide the payment. If five people placed loads upon an animal and it did not die, and a final person added his load and it died, then if the animal had been walking under the first loads and stopped only after the last was added, the last person alone is liable. If it had already stopped before he added his load, the last person is exempt. If it is unknown which situation occurred, all pay equally. Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 383, rule this way. Thus where multiple people cause damage and it is not known through whose act the damage was completed, they divide the payment. Why not say the same here? Moreover, why does the Shach say the later moser is exempt while apparently leaving the first liable? Perhaps the damage was actually caused only by the later informing. One can answer that the first informing was certainly sufficient to cause the damage, so the first moser was already like one who placed the victim into a trap. The only uncertainty is whether he was later discharged from the consequence of that first act. That is comparable to one who says, 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' This requires study.
