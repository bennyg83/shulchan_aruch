Ketzot HaChoshen similarly writes that Ritva in Kiddushin explains the rule that there is no agency for a transgression not as resting primarily on the agent's duty to obey the Master rather than the sender, but as a scriptural decree. If so, even where the agent is known to commit such acts, there is still no halachic agency for transgression. Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 164, however, follows Rama and writes that Shach's objection is not compelling. He further discusses one who sent a non Jew to shame another Jew publicly, and the non Jew did so. He rules that the sender is liable to penalty as though he had shamed the person himself. First, the agent is not subject to the same halachic liability. Although humiliating another is compared to bloodshed in Bava Metzia 58, and descendants of Noach are prohibited from actual bloodshed, public humiliation is not literal bloodshed for which they incur that liability, as is evident from Sanhedrin 57a. Second, a non Jew of that sort is established in performing such acts, and where the agent is established in the transgression, even if technically subject to liability, the sender can be held responsible. Beyond that, the sender is treated as a moser, one who hands another over to harmful coercion, because he delivered his fellow's person to humiliation and suffering. Even if the non Jew had initiated the act independently, there would be a mitzvah to save the victim from humiliation under the duty not to stand by another's blood. All the more so, instructing the humiliation is a severe form of handing him over, and beit din should impose an appropriate deterrent penalty. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 185, likewise writes that Rama is correct in practice, though for a different reason than Tashbetz. The sender himself is a moser because he delivered his fellow into the power of a coercive person. It makes no essential difference whether the coercive person is Jewish or non Jewish. When one hands property to a non Jewish coercive authority, even property alone can readily endanger life, while handing a person's body to such a system is certainly severe. Even if the first person hands him only to an intermediary Jew who then hands him to the non Jewish authority, the first remains a moser despite the chain of indirect causation and agents, because this is how informing to authorities ordinarily operates. Chatam Sofer's failure to mention that Tashbetz himself already gave essentially this reasoning is surprising. Shach, seif katan 65, cites Tashbetz that one may hold the sender liable as though he handed the victim directly to the coercive authority, without needing agency doctrine, because one who hands another to a person established as an informer is effectively handing him to the authority itself. It therefore appears that Shach did not object to this reasoning, but only to the particular case before him involving documents. See there. The matter requires study.
