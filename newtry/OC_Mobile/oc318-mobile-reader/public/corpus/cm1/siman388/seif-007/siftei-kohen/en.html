One against whom there are witnesses, etc. See Responsa Maharashach, volume 3, simanim 86 and 88, for an extensive discussion of the laws of an informer.
Or one who was compelled and physically took and handed over, etc. The Rambam indeed writes this, and the Levush likewise says that the question remained unresolved. This is difficult to me. How can one say that the case of a person compelled to take and hand over property is an unresolved question? The Gemara’s question concerns an informer according to the view that adjudicates dina de-garmi. One who physically takes and hands over the property, however, directly damages it and is not merely liable through garmi. It is obvious that the evidentiary ordinance for a robbed party should not apply, because the actor was compelled. If the victim makes a definite claim and the actor definitely denies it, the actor swears that he was compelled and is exempt. He is not disqualified from taking an oath, since he acted under compulsion and may truthfully swear to that fact. This appears entirely clear to me. The words of the Rambam, Mechaber, and Levush require great study.
And he personally handled and transferred it, etc. If he personally took and delivered the property without coercion, it is obvious that the enactment made for one who was robbed applies, for he is an actual robber. Maharshal likewise wrote this in Perek HaKones, siman 30, in the name of a responsum of Mahari Weil, which is the responsum I cite below in seif katan 36, and in the name of other authorities; he concurred with them. This is straightforward.
And the informer denies it, etc. The Maggid Mishneh, cited by Beit Yosef, writes that these words of the Rambam imply that he follows Rabbeinu Tam: when the informer does not deny the act but says that he does not know how much loss he caused, the victim swears and collects. This is the opinion introduced below by the Mechaber as ‘there is one who says.’ Maharshal likewise understood the Rambam this way. I do not agree. Rabbeinu Tam’s rule is novel, and had the Rambam intended it he should have stated the converse explicitly, namely that when the informer does not deny the act, the victim swears and collects. Rather, the Rambam follows his own position that one who admits part of a claim and says regarding the balance, ‘I do not know,’ is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and therefore must pay, even where he was not expected to know, as explained at length above in siman 72, seif katan 51. The Rambam therefore had to state here that the informer denies the claim. If he instead said, ‘I do not know how much,’ he would be obligated to swear but unable to do so, and the opposing party would collect without an oath. The Rambam had no need to state that converse, because it is independently obvious and is not a special law of an informer. This appears clear to me in the Rambam’s view.

There is one who says. The Tur, Tosafot, the Rosh, Mordechai, Semag, and other authorities cite the dispute between Rabbeinu Tam and Ri. Rabbeinu Tam is the ‘one who says,’ while Ri disagrees, and the Rosh and Tur rule like Ri. One may ask why this dispute is needed at all. Since the informer admits that he caused a loss but does not know its amount, he has admitted part and says ‘I do not know’ regarding the balance. The established rule is that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay, and the opposing party need not even swear. Responsa Maimoniyot, however, writes that in a claim of one hundred where the defendant says, ‘I know of fifty and do not know of fifty,’ the opposing party must at least swear before collecting, and derives this from Rabbeinu Tam’s ruling regarding an informer. I do not agree. In the ordinary case the claimant collects without an oath, as is agreed in many places, and the very authorities who cite the dispute here rule elsewhere that he collects without an oath. Moreover, according to that approach, Ri’s reason for disagreeing with Rabbeinu Tam is unexplained. The same responsum’s discussion of a supporting witness is also difficult, but this is not the place to elaborate. One answer is that Rabbeinu Tam and Ri may hold, as cited above in siman 386, seif katan 10, in the name of Ramban, that Torah oaths do not apply to any liability of dina de-garmi. Alternatively, Ri and other Tosafists may hold that dina de-garmi is rabbinic, in which event the rule of one who cannot swear paying does not apply, as Maharshal writes. This answer cannot explain the Rosh and Semag, who hold that dina de-garmi is biblical. One could also say that the informer offers immediate payment for the amount admitted, or has an offsetting sum in the victim’s hands. The clearest answer emerges from Rashba and the Rosh: because the informer was not expected to know the amount of the loss, he is not treated as one obligated to swear. Rashba writes that where she admits part of the damage caused by her informing but says she does not know the rest, this differs from the ordinary ‘fifty I know, fifty I do not know’ case, where the defendant was expected to know. Here she was not required to know, and the matter resembles the sealed sack discussed in the Jerusalem Talmud. Rashba further rejects the view that the victim swears and collects, because the Gemara leaves unresolved whether the enactment for one who was robbed was extended to an informer, and money cannot be extracted from doubt. Although in the sealed-sack case the claimant collects with an oath, that is because the special enactment was made for a deposit. With an informer, whose liability is dina de-garmi and whose damage was caused by speech alone, the Gemara remained in doubt whether such an enactment was made. The Rosh states the same point expressly. Even according to the Rambam and those who hold that inability to know does not prevent the rule of ‘obligated to swear but unable,’ the dispute can concern a case where the victim himself concedes that the informer does not know; in such a case all agree that the informer is not made to pay under that rule. Bach argues that Rabbeinu Tam is correct because the informer’s admission creates the ordinary rule of one who cannot swear, and that Rabbeinu Tam required the victim’s oath only as an added stringency. This is not persuasive. Bach appears not to have considered the end of the Rosh’s discussion, which expressly says that no oath obligation arises because the informer was not expected to know. Darchei Moshe and Sema also cite Rashba without reservation, and Bach himself elsewhere records the dispute whether the rule applies where the defendant was not expected to know. Nor is it reasonable to say that Rabbeinu Tam added an oath merely as a stringency, for if the victim ordinarily collects without an oath, there is no basis to burden him more in the case of an informer. As practical law, one might initially rule that the informer must pay because I concluded in siman 72 that even where he was not expected to know, he is treated as one obligated to swear but unable, unless the claimant admits that the defendant does not know. Yet the matter still requires study because the Rambam holds that Torah oaths do not apply to dina de-garmi, and other authorities hold dina de-garmi is rabbinic, according to whom a Torah oath does not apply at all.
One who says, etc. At first sight the Mechaber is difficult. He already quoted the Rambam’s phrase, ‘and the informer denies,’ which the Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef understand to imply that if the informer does not deny but says he does not know, the victim swears and collects. Why then repeat this ruling as ‘there is one who says’? The answer is that the converse was not expressly stated. The phrase ‘the informer denies’ could be read non-literally to include one who says, ‘I do not know.’ The Mechaber therefore states explicitly that according to one opinion, where the informer says he does not know, the victim swears and collects. Semag can be explained similarly. Alternatively, the first ruling excludes a case where the informer says ‘I do not know’ about a matter he could have known, for then he is obligated to swear but cannot and must pay, as explained above. The later dispute concerns a case where the victim himself admits that the informer does not know. In that circumstance no oath obligation rests on the informer, and the sole issue is whether the enactment for one who was robbed was extended to an informer. Maharshal’s preference for Rabbeinu Tam is not persuasive. The plain sense of the sugya supports Ri, as the Rosh explains, and so conclude Semag, Nimukei Yosef, Rashba, and several responsa of Maharam. Maharshal also appears to have relied on his understanding of the Rambam, which I rejected above. Ri’s position therefore appears primary. My earlier statement that the practical ruling requires study referred to a case where the informer may know. Where it is certain that he does not know, or the victim admits this, the informer has no oath obligation, and the dispute of Rabbeinu Tam and Ri alone remains. In that case Ri is primary, because the Gemara itself remained in doubt whether the enactment was made, and money cannot be extracted from doubt.

All this concerns one who informed against another’s property, but if he informed against the person of another, etc. Although Mahari Weil ruled this distinction, it does not appear correct to me. Since Maharshal and Maharam of Lublin followed his responsum, I must discuss it at some length. Mahari Weil ruled that where Rabbi David caused Rabbi Yishai’s final imprisonment, Rabbi Yishai swears and collects all loss clearly caused by that imprisonment. He reasoned that although most authorities rule leniently from the unresolved question whether the enactment for one who was robbed applies to an informer, that applies only to informing against property, which is dina de-garmi, such as merely pointing out another’s property. One who directly damages another’s property is subject to the enactment, as in fire. Here, he argued, informing against the victim’s person is direct damage, comparable to bodily injury, and therefore the victim swears while holding a sacred object and collects. Mahari Weil further sought to reconcile responsa attributed to Maharam by distinguishing between informing against property and against the person. In my view, this distinction is incorrect where the informer did no physical act and caused the imprisonment only through speech. Whether he informed against property, against the person, or caused imprisonment, his liability is still only dina de-garmi, and it falls within the Gemara’s unresolved question. I searched the editions of Mordechai and found no ruling of Maharam like Rabbeinu Tam; rather, Maharam rules leniently because the question remained unresolved. If a contrary text exists, it must be a scribal error or require another explanation. Mahari Weil’s reliance on Rabbi Ephraim is also misplaced. Mordechai’s statement that handing over a person is like direct injury refers to physically seizing the person and delivering him by force to the authorities. In that case the informer acts directly upon the victim’s body, and the resulting monetary loss is treated like direct bodily damage. Mordechai contrasts injury to the body, where medical costs and loss of work are recoverable, with damage to property that indirectly causes other monetary loss. His proof from one who locks a person in a room and causes loss of labor confirms that he speaks of physical restraint. Mahari Weil’s case, however, involved only speech, as do the ruling of Rama and the responsum of Maharam of Lublin, where the ruler imprisoned the victim because of the informer’s words. Such conduct is grave and may require atonement, as Maharam writes concerning one whose informing led to a Jew’s death, but that does not transform the resulting monetary loss into direct physical damage for purposes of this enactment. Therefore, unless the informer himself physically seized and delivered the victim, both informing against property and informing against the person remain dina de-garmi. The Gemara’s unresolved question whether the victim may swear and collect applies equally. I have elaborated because Mahari Weil was a leading authority and Maharshal, Rama, Maharam of Lublin, and other later authorities followed him, but in my humble view their ruling here is an error that issued from great rulers.
One who says to another, ‘You informed against me,’ etc. Rashba writes that an informer who admits part must pay what he admits but does not pay the remainder. Even if he says, ‘I know of fifty and do not know of fifty,’ we do not apply the rule that one obligated to swear but unable to swear must pay, because he was not expected to know the amount of the loss. Rashba compares this to the sealed sack in the Jerusalem Talmud and follows the Rosh’s understanding of that case. According to the Rambam and Mechaber, however, who rule in simanim 90 and 298 that the sealed-sack defendant is treated as one obligated to swear but unable even though he was not expected to know, the same should apply here. Bach likewise rejects Rashba’s responsum, and I concluded above in siman 72 that the rule applies in every case unless the claimant admits that the defendant does not know.
He must perform this exculpatory oath only upon the testimony of one witness, etc. Darchei Moshe writes that this appears from Maharam’s responsum in Mordechai and from the rulings of Mahari. In truth, those sources do not establish it. Hagahot Ashri, Mordechai, and Agudah record a case of two Jews living together where no witnesses were available. One accused the other of informing against him to the authorities, and the accused denied it. Rabbi Eliezer of Metz ruled in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that the accused must swear before the ruler that he had said nothing harmful about the claimant. This expressly shows that even without one witness he must perform this exculpatory oath. Rama’s statement should therefore be understood only to exclude reliance on gentile witnesses to require him to swear before them. If the victim states with certainty that the accused informed against him, he must swear before the authorities even without any witness. The Rabbeinu Tam case involved such a definite claim. Mahari Weil’s responsum likewise supports this reading.

Some say that a person who is being beaten, etc. Darchei Moshe cites Maharam of Rothenburg that if one habitually strikes others, it is permitted to have his hand cut off by gentile authority, to cause him monetary loss, or to save the victim through one of the assailant’s limbs. Rama questions this because even the property of a confirmed informer may not be directly destroyed. I do not understand Rama’s objection. Maharam writes that it is a mitzvah to report the assailant to the judge so that he will not continue. If the judge imposes a false charge and confiscates all his property, the reporter is exempt, for otherwise no one would save another from an attacker. If the victim can be saved by injuring one limb, he may do so, as Rav Huna cut off a hand; perhaps it is preferable to report him and cause monetary loss rather than maim him. This is not comparable to ordinary informing, because the report is necessary to rescue the victim. If the only means of rescue is to cause the attacker monetary loss, doing so is permitted and is a mitzvah. Moreover, the property of an informer may not be destroyed directly, but it may be caused to be lost through indirect damage.
