Seif 2. "One who informs on another's money, etc." Note: See Sema, seif katan 7. His words must be understood so that they do not conflict with the law of personal injury, where a husband need not pay after his wife's death even though the injured party did not cause his own loss. The point is as the Shach writes: bodily injury is uncommon, whereas informing to the authorities is common. See there. The Shach writes that the Ramban's answer appears to disagree. The Ramban distinguishes between a liability that existed before marriage, for which the Sages took account, and one arising after marriage, for which they did not. It appears that his reasoning is that when the liability existed before marriage, the husband never entered the rabbinic status of purchaser with respect to that liability at the time of marriage. The Sages protected him against his own loss because the ordinance was made for his benefit and not to weaken him. But a third party's loss that arises only after marriage is different. At the moment of marriage the wife was not liable, and the husband already became a purchaser by rabbinic enactment. The Sages did not later strip away his rights merely because another person subsequently suffered a loss. Yet the Ramban's position is very difficult. If so, why does the Gemara in the chapter Yesh Nochalin need to explain, regarding Rabbi Yose bar Chanina, that we are not concerned for the purchasers' loss because they caused their own loss by buying from a woman who remained married? Rabbi Asi's case concerns a sale after marriage, and according to the Ramban any loss arising after marriage should not override the husband's purchaser status even where the buyers did not cause their own loss. Why, then, does the Gemara supply a different reason? I have tried several approaches to reconcile the Ramban and have not found a satisfactory one. This requires study.
