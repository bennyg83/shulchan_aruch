And he does not know what he damaged, etc. This implies that if the tortfeasor knows what he damaged and denies the claim, the injured party does not swear and collect. See what I write below, siman 418, seif 13, and Responsa Maharshal, part 3, siman 81.
And he threw it into the water, etc. Mordechai in Perek HaKones cites Raavyah that whenever one causes another monetary loss through culpable conduct, and witnesses establish that the loss occurred through him, the injured party swears how much he lost and collects. Maharshal limits the robbed party ordinance to intentional direct damage, but I agree with Raavyah. His reference to negligence means culpable intentional conduct, even where the loss was not caused by literally throwing the object by hand, such as striking it so that it rolled or fell into the water. The Gemara's case of kicking the chest supports this. In inadvertent damage, however, Maharshal is correct, and Raavyah would also agree.
Or he is presumed likely to have deposited them. He must clarify his claim by identifying who deposited them with him. Maharshal writes this.
He was negligent toward himself, etc. The wording might suggest that the reason is that the tortfeasor could not have anticipated such contents. The proper meaning is that the claimant is not believed and his oath does not help, because people do not ordinarily place such items there. If there are witnesses, however, several authorities hold that the tortfeasor is liable. The phrase that the claimant was negligent means that people will not believe he acted in such an unusual manner.
If he snatched a skin bag, etc. Maharshal writes that in such a case it is obvious that he is not believed. The Gemara's unresolved question concerns a chest in which money is commonly placed where the claimant says that pearls were inside.
He is not believed. This appears to mean that without witnesses he is not believed. If witnesses testify to the contents, Rashba, Rambam as understood by the Maggid Mishneh, the Rosh, Tur, Raavyah, and other authorities hold that the tortfeasor is liable even though such items are not ordinarily placed there. This appears to me the primary ruling. Their view also fits the Yerushalmi, under which the dispute between Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages concerns the robbed party ordinance when there are no witnesses, whereas with witnesses liability is established. This also explains the Gemara's discussion of concealed property in fire damage. I later found this approach in Sefer HaMilchamot.
And if he seized. Even if he seized in the presence of witnesses.
And some disagree. They hold that seizure in the presence of witnesses is ineffective in an unresolved question of law. Without witnesses to the seizure, however, he is certainly believed. I discussed this at length in Tekfo Kohen.
And if the tortfeasor knew, etc. See Sefer Ateret Avi, page 104, and above, siman 90, seif 10. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 143.
And some say that the injured party swears and collects, etc. Their reason is that where the defendant could not have been expected to know, we do not apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. I discussed this at length above, siman 72, seif 12, seif katan 51, and concluded that the rule applies in all cases. Therefore, the primary ruling here follows Rambam and the Mechaber. Rabbeinu Yerucham also states Rambam's view without qualification.
