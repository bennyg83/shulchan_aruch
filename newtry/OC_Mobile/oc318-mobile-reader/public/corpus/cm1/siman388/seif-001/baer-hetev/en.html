Knows. This implies that if the tortfeasor knows the extent of the damage and denies it, the injured party does not swear and collect. See below, siman 418, seif 13, and Responsa Rashakh, part III, siman 81. Shach.
And threw it. Maharshal cites Raavyah that one who causes another monetary loss through negligent conduct, where witnesses establish that the loss occurred through him, must pay after the injured party swears how much was lost. Maharshal disagrees, holding that the enactment for one whose property was robbed applies only to a direct intentional tortfeasor, not mere negligence, and perhaps not even to direct accidental damage. The Shach agrees regarding inadvertent damage but explains Raavyah's "negligence" as intentional damaging conduct, such as throwing an item to the ground so that it rolls into the water, or knocking it from another's hand so that it falls into the water. That is the Gemara's case of kicking a chest so it fell into the river. The Rambam's wording "and threw it" can likewise be understood this way. Shach.
With him. Maharshal writes that the claimant must clarify who entrusted the property to him. Ibid.
Was negligent. The wording seems to mean that the tortfeasor was not expected to anticipate such contents, as the Ir Shushan explains. If so, why does the ruling later say the claimant is not believed? The Lechem Mishneh also leaves this difficult. It seems the meaning is that he is not believed and his oath does not help, as the Rambam concludes: we do not administer an oath about such contents because people do not ordinarily place them there. The later rule that if he seized, etc., is because this too is included in the unresolved question whether the conduct is considered negligence and whether an oath helps. The Maggid Mishneh, however, understands that the Rambam agrees that if there are witnesses to the unusual contents, the tortfeasor is liable. Maharshal states this explicitly, and the Rama's wording also appears so. Accordingly, "he was negligent" means he caused his own evidentiary problem, since people will not believe he placed such things there. Ibid.
A skin bottle. Maharshal writes that in such a case it is obvious the claimant is not believed. The Gemara's question concerns a chest, where money is ordinarily placed, and the claimant says it contained pearls, as Rashi explains. Ibid.
Seized. Even if the seizure was witnessed. Others hold that witnessed seizure does not help in a legal doubt, although unwitnessed seizure certainly does because of migo. I discussed this at length in Tekfo Kohen. Ibid.
Knew. See what I wrote in siman 90, seif 10; Sefer Imrei Emet, p. 104; and Responsa Rashdam, siman 143. Ibid.
And collects. Where the defendant was not expected to know, some do not apply the rule that one who must swear but cannot do so must pay. I discussed this at length in siman 72, seif 12, and concluded that in every case he is treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear. Accordingly, here the Rambam and Mechaber's view is primary. Rabbeinu Yerucham and Semag likewise rule so. Ibid.
