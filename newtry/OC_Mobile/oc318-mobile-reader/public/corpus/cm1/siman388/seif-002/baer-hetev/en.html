The informer. The law of an informer applies to land as well, as above, end of siman 371. Ibid.
In judgment. He holds that an informer's liability is based on rabbinic dina d'garmi, as I wrote at the beginning of siman 387. Therefore, if the informer actually took and delivered the property by hand, even the dissenting opinion agrees that collection is made from his heirs even if judgment was not entered during his lifetime. See what I wrote in siman 385, seif katan 3, and Responsa Rabbi Moshe Alshich, siman 134.
Her life. The Sema asks why this differs from a woman's liability for injuring others, where the Tur, Rama, and Mechaber require her to sell the contingent value of her property and pay. Perhaps informer liability, where she did not damage with her own hands, was treated more leniently because it is a penalty. The Shach answers that there is no difficulty: here too, if the victim wishes, she must sell the contingent value of her melog property, as stated generally in siman 424. The present ruling teaches an even stronger point, that the property is immediately regarded as belonging to the victim, subject only to the husband's right to fruits, and after her death payment is made. The Shach further cites Maharshal, chapter HaChovel, siman 30, who rules that in both injury and informing the husband is treated as a purchaser, not an heir, and is not liable after her death; rather, beit din compels her to sell the contingent value of her ketubah if a buyer can be found.
How. This refers back to the case above of an informer acting without compulsion and therefore liable. See Responsa Mabit, part I, second questions, siman 57; Responsa Ibn Lev, part II, siman 54; Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 66; and Responsa Rashdam, simanim 344 and 358. Shach.
To bring. This refers to a situation not authorized by dina d'malchuta, as I wrote in siman 369, seif 8. Ibid.
Liable. The Tur indeed writes so, but I do not know his source. Reason would suggest exemption, because he can say: I thought they would torture me to reveal money, and perhaps that is what truly happened. At minimum it is a doubt, so why should he pay? The Gemara says simply that a Jew compelled by non-Jews who reveals another's property is exempt, without specifying that the compulsion was to reveal money. The Beit Yosef's proof from the case of the silver cup is not convincing, since there he actually took and delivered it. The Bach also objects. Nor is the Bach's proposed proof from the Gemara conclusive. It appears that the Tur speaks where circumstances clearly show that the initial torture was not for the purpose of locating money. Ibid.
Money. Responsa Rashba, siman 980, does not imply this but rather that one who merely reveals the property is exempt even under financial compulsion. I likewise found this in Sefer HaChinuch of the Re'ah, whose teacher was the Ramban. Thus Rashba, Ramban, and Re'ah hold that revealing property under financial compulsion is exempt, and one may say kim li like them. Ibid.
To another. The Sema cites the Nimukei Yosef that once the harm has already fallen upon a person, he may not remove it from himself by causing harm to another. Responsa Maharshal, siman 19, discusses a case where Reuven bought a ring from a non-Jew knowing it was stolen and pledged it to Shimon. Shimon later sold it to another official, after which an accusation arose that it was stolen. Shimon saved himself by identifying Reuven as the seller, forcing Reuven to flee and lose his home and property. Maharshal ruled that Shimon must pay all damage caused, as assessed by beit din. Even if Shimon denies being warned that the ring was stolen, he had no right to sell another's pledge without the owner's or beit din's permission. Even if he claims he bought it from Reuven knowing it was stolen rather than receiving it as a pledge, once the accusation fell upon him he may not save himself at another's expense. If, however, Reuven truly sold it to him without disclosing that it was stolen, Shimon appears exempt because he may say: had you told me it was stolen, I would not have displayed it before the non-Jew.
