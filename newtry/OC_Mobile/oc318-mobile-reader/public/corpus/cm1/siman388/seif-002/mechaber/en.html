One who delivers another person's property to a violent person, whether a non-Jew or a Jew, must compensate from the best of his property for everything the violent person took, even if the informer did not physically carry or hand over the property but merely caused the seizure. If the informer dies, the claim is collected from his heirs like the claim against any other damager. Some say this applies only if judgment was entered against him during his lifetime; if he died before judgment, his heirs are exempt. If a married woman acted as an informer, she is placed under a ban. If she owns property over which her husband has no rights, she pays from it. If she has usufruct property, her husband continues to receive the produce during her lifetime, but upon her death he must compensate the victim, just as any heir is liable for damage inherited from the deceased. These rules apply when the informer acted voluntarily. If a non-Jewish or Jewish violent person compelled him to point out the property and he merely pointed it out, he is exempt. If he was compelled to identify his own property and instead identified both his own and another's, he is liable for the other's property. If he physically carried the property and handed it over, he is liable even under coercion, because one who saves himself with another person's property must pay. Thus, if the king ordered that wine, straw, or similar goods be brought to him, and a person said that so-and-so had a storehouse at a certain place, causing the authorities to take it, that person must pay. Rama: Even if he was tortured without being told what to reveal and he then revealed another person's property, he is liable. Coercion means physical blows or torture, not merely financial pressure. If a person sees a loss approaching him, he may save himself even though this causes the loss to fall upon another, where he does not directly hand over the other's property.
