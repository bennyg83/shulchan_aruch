Whether, etc. Although the Bavli and Yerushalmi speak of a gentile official, that is merely the usual case. The Tosefta likewise discusses robbers who enter by force and distinguishes one who personally takes and hands over another's property.
From the best land, etc. The Gemara on 5a asks why this category is not listed separately, because all such liabilities are treated like primary damages. See Tosafos 116b and the parallel passages in Bava Metzia and Nimukei Yosef.
Although, etc. Bava Kamma 116b, 117a, and 117b.
Rather, one who regularly causes, etc. Liability may arise even through speech, as the Gemara says regarding verbal direction, and as with one who sells a debt document and then forgives it. Mordechai.
(Collected note) Rather, one who regularly causes, etc. This parallels one who issued a wrongful judgment and one who shows a coin to a money changer; see siman 25 seif 4 and Tosafos Bava Kamma 100a. (End of note.)
And if he dies, etc. This follows the view that dina d'garmi is ordinary law. The Rashba also understands the conclusion that way.
And some say, etc. According to Riva, cited in siman 386 seif 3, liability is a fine, and a fine is not collected from heirs. See Moed Katan, Gittin, and Bechoros. In siman 66 seif 32 the ruling is stated without qualification like the first opinion.
After he stood in judgment. At that point the liability has become a monetary debt imposed by beit din. See Kesubos 42 and Tosafos there.
And a woman, etc. As the Gemara there concludes regarding excommunication, etc.
And if there is, etc. This is implied there 89a.
And if there is, etc., and if she dies, etc., and the husband does not, etc. The point is not only that she may sell the value of her future interest, as stated at the end of siman 424, but that collection may be made from the property itself. Although the Gemara invokes the enactment of Usha, Tosafos Bava Batra 139b distinguishes the case. The Ramban follows the Rashba's approach, as stated at the end of siman 424, and so rule the poskim. See Shach.
The husband consumes, etc. The Gemara's statement that she should sell nichsei melog is not literal as an immediate absolute sale, since a child similarly cannot sell property during the parent's lifetime in the relevant case. Rather, it means a sale effective upon her death, and the same applies to the injured party.
And if they coerced him to show, etc. See Tosafos there concerning the alternate reading, and the end of siman 371.
Even though he, etc. The Gemara distinguishes one who was coerced merely to show from one who actually took and handed over property. The Rif explains at length that even where the person was coerced, physically handing over another's property can create liability, rejecting Rav Hai Gaon's broader exemption. Other Rishonim distinguish whether the officials came specifically for the other person's property, whether they could have taken it themselves, and whether the coerced person merely identified or actually brought the property. The Rambam and Shulchan Aruch follow the Rif's basic approach, as reflected in seif 4.
For one who saves, etc. This rejects the Raavad's distinction between financial coercion and bodily coercion. Even where life is endangered, one may not save oneself with another's money without repaying it. Tosafos, the Rosh, Ramban, and other poskim say likewise, as stated in siman 359 seif 4 and in the related cases.
How so, etc. The following clauses explain the rule. See Bava Kamma 116b and 117b.
And even if they tortured him, etc. Rabbah's statement there concerns precisely a coerced case, because voluntary disclosure is already covered by the Mishna. The Rashba explains so.
And this is not called, etc. Where the act is mere grama and liability is only by rabbinic fine, true coercion may remove the fine. But financial coercion can still leave the actor blameworthy. The Rishonim disagree about one coerced to reveal his own property who instead points to another's; Tosafos and the Rashba generally impose liability, while the Raah is more lenient. See Shach.
If he saw, etc. The Yerushalmi teaches that before an advancing danger has actually entered one's property one may divert it elsewhere, but once it has entered one may not redirect it onto another. It applies the same principle to royal tax collectors and billeting soldiers: before they arrive one may lawfully arrange matters, even if this indirectly increases another's burden, but after they have arrived one may not shift the imposed burden to another. See Sema, Hagahos Ashri to Bava Batra, and Nimukei Yosef.
