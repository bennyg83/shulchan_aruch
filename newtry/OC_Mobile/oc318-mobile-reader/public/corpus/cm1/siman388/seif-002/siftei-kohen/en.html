One who informs on another's property, etc. The law of a moser applies to land as well, as explained above at the end of siman 371.
From the best of his property. Meaning from superior land, like any other tortfeasor. Although liability for causative damage is rabbinic, the Sages modeled it upon Torah law. This applies where he has no movable property. If he pays with movable property, he may satisfy the judgment even with low quality items, because all movable property is considered superior payment. The Maggid Mishneh writes this, and it is straightforward.
Only one who caused it to be handed over. Meaning that he showed the property or spoke to the coercive official and thereby caused the property to be delivered into that official's hands.
And if he died, collection is made from his heirs. This follows the view that a moser's liability is substantive law and not merely a rabbinic penalty. Therefore, even if judgment was not entered before his death, collection may be made from his heirs. According to the dissenting view, which treats this as a rabbinic penalty based on causative damage, collection from heirs requires that judgment first have been entered. If the moser actually took the property and handed it over, however, even that view agrees that the heirs are liable although judgment had not yet been entered.
And some say, specifically if judgment had already been entered, etc. Darchei Moshe cites Maharam to this effect, but this disagrees with the Maggid Mishneh's understanding that liability of a moser is law rather than penalty. According to Ramban's law of causative damage as well, one collects from heirs even without prior judgment. Maharam follows the approach that the liability is penal in character. This distinction is clear.
The husband consumes the produce, etc. The Sema asks why this differs from a wife's liability for injuring others, where she must sell the contingent value of her property and pay. There is no real difficulty. Here too, if necessary, she must sell the contingent value of her property. The novelty here is greater: the injured party has an immediate claim against the corpus, while the husband continues to consume the produce during her lifetime, and on one view the husband must pay after her death. Tosafos and Ramban discuss whether the husband is treated as purchaser or heir under the enactment of Usha. Maharshal ultimately rules that both in personal injury and informing, the husband is treated as a purchaser and is not liable after her death; rather, beit din compels sale of the contingent value if a buyer can be found. This appears to be the stronger conclusion.
And the husband, for this purpose, is only like an heir, etc. I already wrote that some disagree and hold that the husband is treated as a purchaser for this purpose and is exempt after her death. Maharshal rules this way.
And he showed his own and his fellow's. Even if he showed his fellow's property together with his own, he is liable, as explained above at the end of siman 371.
How so, etc. This refers back to the rule of a voluntary moser who acts without compulsion. For example, the ruler imposed a demand and did not coerce this person to identify another's property, yet the informer stood up and identified it. See the cited responsa.
If the king decreed that he bring him, etc. This concerns a decree that is not valid under dina d'malchuta, such as a decree directed at a particular individual, as explained above, siman 369, seif 8.
And even if they tortured him without telling him why, and he showed his fellow's property, he is liable. The Tur writes this, but I do not know its source. Logic suggests exemption, because he can say that he thought they were torturing him in order to make him reveal property. At minimum, this is doubtful, and one should not impose liability from doubt. The case of the silver cup is no proof, because there the person actually took and handed it over. Nor is there proof from the Gemara's phrase that he merely pointed it out. It appears that the Tur speaks where the circumstances demonstrate that the torture was not originally imposed for money.
But not monetary compulsion. Beit Yosef, citing Responsa Maimoniyot, says that exemption applies only to bodily compulsion. Responsa Rashba, Ramban as cited by the Chinuch, and Raah indicate that even monetary compulsion exempts one who merely points out the property. Since these authorities hold that any showing done under compulsion is exempt, it appears that one may plead that the halachah follows them.
