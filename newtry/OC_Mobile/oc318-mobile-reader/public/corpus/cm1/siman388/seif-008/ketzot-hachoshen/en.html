Likewise, if he admitted that he informed. Maharshal writes that an informer who voluntarily admits is liable because *dina de-garmi* is not a penalty from which one is exempt by confession. He cites Rabbi Yitzchak of Corbeil. The Shach objects that self-admission exempts only from Torah penalties, not rabbinic ones. Shitah Mekubetzet, however, records an opinion that even a rabbinic monetary penalty can be lost through voluntary confession. In discussing one who snatched another's mitzvah, it distinguishes a true penalty from a remedial enactment precisely because confession would exempt from the former but not the latter. This resolves a question of Pnei Yehoshua. If nonapparent damage were only a rabbinic penalty, why does the Gemara need a separate reason to exempt an accidental actor so that he will report what happened? If confession to a rabbinic penalty would itself exempt him, he could simply report it and escape payment. That consideration supports treating the liability there as an enactment rather than a classic penalty, or otherwise distinguishing the cases. There remains some tension from the Talmudic case of burning a document where liability can exist even on the plaintiff's trust without external witnesses. If *dina de-garmi* were a penalty fully cancelled by self-admission, such a case would be difficult. See siman 38.
