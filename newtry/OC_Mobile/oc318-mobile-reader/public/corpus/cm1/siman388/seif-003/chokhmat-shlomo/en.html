Ibid., in the gloss. "Similarly, if he admitted that he informed and there are no witnesses to the matter." Note: See Shach, seif katan 51, and see my novellae to Pesachim in the sugya concerning an acquisition made in error, written in 5604. It now appears that this dispute is explicit among the early authorities and is cited in Yoreh Deah, Hilchot Avadim, siman 267, seif 20. The Mechaber rules there that this fine is not collected in Babylonia, while the Shach cites the Rosh, who disagrees on the ground that since the fine is rabbinic it does not have the ordinary law of a Torah fine and can be collected in Babylonia. The same distinction should apply to admission, because both questions turn on the same principle. Likewise the Rama writes here that seizure is effective, while the Shach cites the Raavad that seizure is ineffective. That dispute too depends on this issue. The Raavad's reason that admission to a fine does not help is that an admission ordinarily exempts the defendant from the fine, and for the same reason seizure cannot create liability. According to the Rosh and Tur, however, seizure certainly helps here. According to those who hold that this fine is not collected in Babylonia, the matter depends on the broader dispute between the Rambam and Raavad whether seizure helps with a fine. In practical law it appears that seizure certainly helps because there is a double doubt: perhaps the Rosh is correct, and even if the opposing authorities are correct that this fine is not collected in Babylonia, perhaps the Rambam is correct that seizure nevertheless helps with fines. The laws here can therefore be illuminated by the dispute there. Examine carefully.
