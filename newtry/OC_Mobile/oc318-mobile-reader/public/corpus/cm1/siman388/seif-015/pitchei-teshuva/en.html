Ketzot HaChoshen likewise argues that the principle that there is no agency for a transgression is, according to Ritva in Kiddushin, not based principally upon the rationale "the words of the Master and the words of the disciple, whose words should be obeyed," but is a Scriptural decree. Accordingly, even where the agent is habitually prepared to carry out such acts, there is still no agency for a transgression. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 164, however, agrees with the Rama and writes that the Shach's objections are not conclusive. He also discusses one who sent a non-Jew to publicly shame another and the non-Jew did so, asking whether the sender is fined as though he had personally inflicted the shame or whether there is no agency for a transgression. He concludes that the sender is liable. First, the agent is not personally subject to the same halachic liability. Although public humiliation is compared to bloodshed in Bava Metzia 58, and a Noahide is commanded against murder, humiliation is not actual murder for which a Noahide is liable, as is evident from Sanhedrin 57a. Second, a non-Jew of this sort is established as one who carries out such acts, and where an agent is known to do so the sender is liable even if the agent is otherwise legally responsible. Beyond this, the sender should be regarded as a moser, for he delivered his fellow's person into the hands of one who would torment and publicly shame him. Even where the non-Jew acts on his own, there is a mitzvah to rescue another from public humiliation under the prohibition against standing idly by one's fellow's blood. All the more so one who orders the humiliation is comparable to a moser who hands over another's person to suffering and public disgrace. His law is severe, and beit din may fine him as required to safeguard the community. See there. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 185, likewise writes that the Rama's ruling is correct, though not for the reason given by Tashbetz. The sender himself is a moser because he placed his fellow in the hands of a coercive person. What difference is there whether the coercive intermediary is a Jew or a non-Jew? One who directly hands a Jew to a non-Jewish coercive authority is liable even when he hands over only property, because such a situation can naturally develop into mortal danger. By contrast, when he sends a Jew, he is a moser specifically if he delivers the victim's person to a coercive authority. Here he handed the person to a Jewish informer who would hand him to a non-Jewish authority and thereby cast him into danger, so the first sender himself is a moser even if the resulting harm occurs through multiple indirect stages and agents. Most informers to foreign authorities do not themselves perform the final act; the consequences unfold through officials and their agents, yet the original informer remains a moser. Chatam Sofer's omission is surprising, because Tashbetz himself gives essentially this reasoning, as cited by the Shach, seif katan 65: "There is further reason to hold him liable as though he handed him directly to the coercive authority, and we need not reach the law of agency, for since the elder was established as an informer, one who hands a person to him is as though he handed him to the coercive authority itself." It appears that the Shach's objection concerned only the particular case there, which involved delivery of documents. The matter still requires analysis.
