Through indirect causation. Such as lowering him into a pit or acting through gentiles. This is the Tur’s formulation in the Rosh’s responsum. Maharshal disagrees, arguing that using gentiles is equivalent to killing him oneself because agency for sin is recognized stringently in such a case. He permits only lowering into a pit. His arguments are not compelling, as I explain in the following seif katan.
Although it is forbidden to kill him. The Tur indeed cites this from the Rosh in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel. It is surprising that Rama states it without qualification, for the Mechaber above, seif 11, plainly rules that the informer may be killed. As I showed above, this is the position of Rambam, Rashba, and many other authorities. Sema writes that Baal HaIttur supports Rabbeinu Chananel, but in my view Baal HaIttur speaks only about the informer’s property.
If the agent is established as one who will carry it out, the sender is liable, etc. This ruling of Rama and Rashbatz does not appear correct. Rashbatz argues that the principle that there is no agency for a transgression applies only to a Jew, whereas a confirmed informer and violent extortionist is not treated as a Jew, since Rambam calls informers worse than gentiles; therefore the sender cannot claim that the agent should have obeyed God rather than him. This is not correct. According to Rav Sama, followed by Ramah, Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, and many others, there is never agency for a transgression whenever the agent could choose whether to act, even if the agent is not personally liable. Here too the informer could choose not to act. Those authorities say that the only agency for a transgression is through one’s courtyard, which also proves that a habitual informer is not an exception. Even according to Ravina, who makes the sender liable where the agent is not subject to the prohibition, this informer remains a Jew and is subject to the prohibition. Rambam calls informers worse than gentiles only regarding disqualification from testimony. All authorities state the rule without distinguishing whether the agent is habitually criminal. Rama follows his view in siman 182 that everything depends on the agent’s personal liability, but I disputed him there. Rashbatz himself appears to have relied on additional reasons: that the case resembles unauthorized use of a deposit, which creates liability through an agent, and that handing property to a known informer is equivalent to handing it directly to the oppressor. These reasons are also unpersuasive. Unauthorized use through an agent applies to taking a deposit for oneself, not here; moreover, documents are excluded from theft liability. Since, as I explain next, even directly handing one’s own paid documents to a gentile does not create liability, certainly handing them to an informer who then gives them to a gentile does not. This appears clear.
Likewise, if he handed over a paid document, etc. This too is from Rashbatz, and I do not agree. Even if he handed it directly to the ruler, he is exempt, because this is not sufficiently direct causation. Rashbatz also writes that if Reuven was the creditor and Shimon handed Reuven’s document to an informer, thereby destroying the debt, Shimon is liable as an informer. The opposite is more reasonable. In that case alone Shimon is liable, but not because he is an informer. Since Reuven entrusted the document to him and he delivered it to an informer, he effectively burned the document and is liable under dina de-garmi like one who burns another’s documents. By contrast, one who gives his own paid documents to a gentile has caused only indirect damage. Rashba expressly rules that one who returned such a document to a gentile is exempt because a person who does not create an immediate acquisition causes only grama. Mahari ben Lev, Maharam Alshich, Maharam of Lublin, and Masat Binyamin rule accordingly. Darchei Moshe notes that some disagree, apparently referring to another responsum, but it is therefore especially surprising that Rama rules here unqualifiedly like Rashbatz. As practical law, Rashba’s view appears primary.
