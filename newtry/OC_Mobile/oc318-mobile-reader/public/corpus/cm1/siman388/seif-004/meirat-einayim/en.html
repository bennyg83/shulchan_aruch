Although the king compelled him to bring it, etc. This differs from the Gemara's law, recorded by the Tur and Mechaber in siman 292, seif 8, concerning one who holds another's deposited food or money and is not known to be wealthy. If bandits come upon him and he gives them the deposit in order to save himself, he is exempt, because since he is not presumed wealthy we assume the bandits came only because of the deposit. All the more so, one might think, where they expressly compel him to hand over his fellow's money. The cases differ. There, because he is not wealthy, the accident that befell him presumably arose only because of the deposit; had the deposit not been in his possession, they would not have come against him. Here, however, the coercers demand that he bring Reuven's money from Reuven's own house. His own bad fortune caused them to impose this task upon him. Otherwise, why would they approach him and force him to bring money that is not in his possession? Therefore his own field was struck by misfortune and he is liable even if they also coerced him to perform the act of bringing. So writes Ran. Ra'avad disagrees with Rambam even here and writes that although he physically took and delivered the money, since he was coerced regarding the bringing there is nothing that stands before danger to life. Once they specifically designated the other person's property, he is not required to die for the sake of his fellow's money and consequently is also exempt from later payment. He cites this in the name of a Gaon. See Nimukei Yosef in the final chapter of Bava Kamma and Darkei Moshe, and see the Derishah.

Since the coercer was standing there, etc. The Tur says that regarding what he merely pointed out, he is exempt because he was coerced and did no physical act. Regarding what he afterward took and handed over, he is also exempt, because once the coercer had seen it and could take it himself, it is regarded as though the coercer already possessed it. See the Derishah, where I discuss the phrase "even in this my master the Rosh wrote." Darkei Moshe cites Maharam of Rothenburg concerning Reuven who took an object from Shimon to sell to an official and afterward returned it to Shimon in the official's servant's presence. The official later seized the object from Shimon without payment. Reuven must reimburse Shimon, because he should have returned it privately. His claim, "I did not intend to harm you," does not help.
