Seif 15, in the gloss. "One who sent an agent to inform, if the agent was established as one who would do so, the sender is liable." Note: See my work on Even HaEzer, ninth edition, siman 13, in my responsum to the community of Zamosc, where I reconciled the difficulty from Bava Kamma by a new approach. At present, however, I find this view difficult from Bava Kamma 51, where the Gemara asks how a pit jointly owned by two people can be found. Why does it not answer that the two appointed as their agent a person known to perform such conduct, to dig in the public domain, in which case agency might apply even to a transgression? Even if one argues that only with an established informer is there agency for wrongdoing, because the Rambam describes informers as worse than non-Jews, but not with other established sinners, the Gemara could still answer that they appointed a notorious informer or an apostate. It therefore appears that even in such a case we still say that there is no agency for a transgression. Yet this can be reconciled according to those who hold that an apostate is not included in the covenantal community for purposes of agency and is legally like a non-Jew. The Gemara could not answer with such a person because a non-Jew is not an agent in the first place. This, however, creates a serious difficulty for the Tashbetz and Rama, who hold that there can be agency here because the Rambam says that informers are worse than non-Jews. Either an informer has the status of a non-Jew, in which case although the rule "there is no agent for a transgression" may not apply, ordinary agency also does not apply; or he is better than a non-Jew and can serve as an agent, in which case the rule against agency for transgression should apply. It appears that the intention of the Tashbetz and Rama is different. The rule that there is no agent for a transgression is based on the reasoning, "Whose words should be obeyed, the master's or the student's?" By contrast, the rule that there is no agency for a non-Jew is a scriptural decree without that rationale. Liability here can arise through dina de-garmi. If the ordinary rule against agency for transgression applied, there would be uncertainty whether the agent would carry out the wrongful instruction, and the damage would not be certain enough for dina de-garmi, which requires predictable damage. But once the ordinary rule against agency for transgression does not apply because this person is an established informer, the sender can know that the informer will act. Even if formal agency is unavailable because an informer is treated like a non-Jew, the sender is liable under dina de-garmi because the resulting damage is certain. This also answers the Shach's difficulty. In the Bava Kamma case, by contrast, the Gemara could not answer with an informer or apostate because formal agency is unavailable, and the doctrine of certain damage cannot help in the case of digging a pit, since perhaps nothing will ever fall into the pit. There is therefore no dina de-garmi. This supports the conclusion that an apostate and informer cannot serve as formal agents. See also my work on Even HaEzer, ninth edition, siman 87, on Even HaEzer siman 141, in my responsum to the community of Odessa, where I brought another proof that an apostate cannot serve as an agent. Examine carefully.
