The Maggid Mishneh writes that the Rif’s proofs are especially sound and that the Rashba reached the same conclusion. The Rif indeed discusses the matter at length. The Baal HaMaor agrees with him, and the Ramban in Milchamot adds further proofs for the Rif. The Raah, Rosh, Semag, Mordechai, Tur, Mechaber, and other authorities likewise follow the Rif. Maharshal rules this way in HaGozel Batra, siman 47, as does the Bach. In my own work, however, I discussed the matter at length, rejected all the proofs of the Rif, Baal HaMaor, and Ramban, and concluded as follows. If the oppressors coerce a person regarding unspecified property, then even though they force him to bring property, he is liable if he physically takes another person’s property and hands it over. This is considered saving himself with another person’s money and resembles a pursued person who breaks vessels, discussed above in siman 380, seif 3. The present case differs where the oppressors explicitly compel him to bring a particular person’s property. Their coercion was directed from the outset at that identified property. The coerced person is not choosing to save himself with another’s money. He is forced to do exactly what the oppressor commands. If he refuses, he would in effect preserve another person’s property at the cost of his own life, and nothing stands before preservation of life. In the incident in which the oppressors later told the informer, “Carry it and bring it with us,” they did not then coerce him anew. At first they had compelled him only to point out the property, and afterward they merely told him to carry it. Examine the incident carefully, and you will find that this distinction answers all the proofs of the Rif and Ramban. See also the Baal HaItur, page 48b, and the Beit Yosef. The Lechem Mishneh’s answer concerning the baraita about “this bundle” or “this sheaf” is entirely incorrect, and it appears that he overlooked the words of the Baal HaItur and Nimukei Yosef. The Baal HaItur answers that the baraita involved no coercion because the oppressor and the property stood on opposite sides of a river and the Jew could evade him. The Beit Yosef, Shiltei Gibborim, and Maadanei Yom Tov follow this answer. It does not appear convincing to me. According to that explanation, the questioner thought the baraita involved coercion, and the answer’s central point, that there was no coercion, is omitted from the text. The Gemara gives an unnecessarily long answer and should simply have said, “The case is where there was no coercion.” Furthermore, if the questioner thought the baraita’s “bundle” or “sheaf” referred to a person whom the oppressor compelled, why did he challenge only the reason that the oppressor could have burned it? He should have challenged the baraita itself: why is the person liable, since he was compelled concerning specifically designated property and nothing stands before preservation of life? In my humble opinion, the Baal HaItur and those who followed him were forced into this interpretation unnecessarily. Both the question and the answer concern a case in which the oppressor did not coerce the person at all, but merely spoke to him. The baraita says, “An oppressor said to him, ‘Hand me this bundle,’” not that the oppressor compelled him to hand it over. The Gemara asks that according to the view that burning the item would not itself constitute taking its value, then even where the oppressor merely says, “Hand it to me,” without any coercion, perhaps the Jew should be exempt because the oppressor could have burned it. The answer is that the property stood on the opposite side of the river, so the oppressor could not take it without the Jew’s assistance, as Rashi explains. The case is straightforward. Even though they stood on opposite sides of the river, the oppressor could have threatened or coerced him, but in fact did not do so. The baraita teaches, according to both the question and answer, that the mere request of a violent person is not itself legal compulsion. So long as he does not explicitly threaten him, such as by saying, “If you do not hand it to me I will kill you,” but merely says, “Hand it to me,” the person who complies is liable. This appears entirely clear. Nevertheless, where they explicitly coerced him to bring a particular person’s property and he physically brought and handed it over, he is exempt. Rav Hai Gaon and Rabbeinu Chananel hold this view. It is obvious that one should not disagree with the Geonim absent a clear proof from the Gemara, and here the law itself appears to support them. Rabbeinu Ephraim and the Baal HaItur likewise hold this way. The Nimukei Yosef writes that the Raavad’s words appear very correct. The Rashba rules similarly in responsum 980. Although I do not accept the Rashba’s statement there that the passage concerns specifically monetary compulsion, his agreement with the Raavad on the practical law appears correct. The Yerushalmi at the end of HaChovel likewise supports this. The Ramban in Milchamot and the Rosh interpret it through forced explanations, but its plain meaning is that one compelled to bring specifically designated property is exempt, in accordance with Rabbeinu Chananel. I also found in Rabbi Avraham Stein’s commentary to the Semag, page 148b: “In the Raavyah, siman 1040, he cites the Rif’s words with all his proofs. He then cites Rabbi Shevet, who refutes the Rif’s proofs, and also cites the Yerushalmi and Rabbeinu Chananel’s explanation, all of which disagree with the Rif. He discussed the matter at great length, and I have abbreviated,” end quote. I likewise saw in Maharam of Rothenburg’s rulings collected by Maharam Recanati, siman 473, that he writes so in the name of the Raavyah and Rabbi Shevet. Maharashdam also writes in responsum 55 that because this matter is disputed by early and later authorities, money cannot be extracted. The Shiltei Gibborim in HaGozel Batra likewise treats it as a dispute among the authorities. In my view, it is not merely doubtful. The principal law follows those who exempt, as I have written in my work. See also the Rama’s responsum, siman 11, page 23b.
