The Shach agrees and concludes that liability for physically handing over property applies only when the coercers demanded money generally, or even when they demanded that he bring property but did not target a particular person's money. In that case he is saving himself with another's property, comparable to a pursued person who broke another's utensils, as stated in siman 380, seif 3. By contrast, if the coercers specifically ordered him to bring a particular person's money, the coercion was from the outset directed at that person's property. He is then not considered to be saving himself with another's property, but rather is compelled to do what the coercer commands. If he refuses, he would be saving another's property with his own life, and nothing overrides danger to life. The Shach cites several major early authorities who hold this way. Rashdam likewise writes that since the matter is disputed among earlier and later authorities, money cannot be extracted. Shiltei Gibborim also treats it as a dispute. The Shach concludes that in his view it is not merely doubtful: the authorities who exempt are correct. See Responsa Rama, siman 11.

In the presence of. Reuven took an object from Shimon to sell to an official. He later returned it to Shimon in the presence of the official's servant, and afterward the official forcibly took the object from Shimon without payment. Reuven must reimburse Shimon, because he should have returned it privately. His claim that he did not intend to cause harm does not help. This ruling of Maharam of Merseburg is cited in Darkei Moshe at the end of the siman. Sema.
