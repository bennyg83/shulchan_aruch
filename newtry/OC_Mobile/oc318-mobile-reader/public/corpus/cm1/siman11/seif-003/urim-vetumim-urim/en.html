When he is in the metropolis, etc. This means a large city. Even so, the court does not rely on the messenger having informed the defendant's neighbors. The neighbors may assume that the messenger encountered the defendant on his way and told him directly, and therefore they may not pass on the message. Sema.
Even a woman, etc. The presumption is that the neighbor conveyed the summons, and if the defendant did not appear he is treated as refusing. Perishah asks that elsewhere we are uncertain whether an agent carries out his mission, and in Torah matters we rule stringently that perhaps he did not. Here, should we not be lenient to the defendant? Perishah answers that no money is extracted on this presumption; only excommunication is imposed. According to him, however, the plaintiff should bear the fee for writing the excommunication, because money cannot be extracted on the basis of the presumption. This is not implied by the Shulchan Aruch or the authorities. It is better to distinguish a messenger acting under the direct order of beit din. We presume such a person fulfills the court's command, because people do not ordinarily refuse the instructions of beit din. The court messenger tells the neighbors in the name and by command of beit din to notify the defendant. In this setting we may rely on the presumption that the mission was carried out.
They excommunicate him in the evening. Sema explains at length that "in the evening" means on the next day, corresponding to seif 1. The point is that no additional waiting period is required. See there.
Likewise, if he was in the city, etc. Sema asks that the beginning of the seif already discussed one who is in the metropolis and said the messenger must find him personally. A better explanation is that Rama now clarifies the reason. We refrain from excommunicating because perhaps the neighbors never conveyed the message. If the neighbors say that they did convey it, he is excommunicated. One might otherwise have thought that because he is physically in the city and the messenger could easily find him, the defendant himself may disregard the neighbors, reasoning that if the summons were genuine the court messenger would have come directly. Rama therefore teaches that the only concern is whether the neighbors transmitted the message. Once they confirm that they did, the defendant is treated as refusing.
