Or he did not wish to come. At first glance this is difficult. The beit din officer need not testify that the litigant failed to appear; if the person does not come on the appointed day, the court itself sees that fact and places him under ban. Nor does the officer ordinarily need to prove that he delivered the summons, because we rely on the presumption that a beit din officer performed his mission, as Rama and Sema explain later. Likewise Mechaber's concluding requirement of two witnesses that the litigant refused to come seems unnecessary, because the court can see whether he came. The correct interpretation is that the officer or two witnesses testify that the litigant affirmatively responded that he refuses to come. Such a response triggers immediate excommunication. Because the resulting monetary expenses include writing the writ of ban, two witnesses are needed for that affirmative response. If instead the court merely sees that he failed to appear, they excommunicate him the next day and issue the writ without concern that perhaps the officer failed to notify him, because a beit din officer is presumed to have performed his mission. Although the ordinary presumption that an agent performs his mission does not suffice to extract money, a beit din officer is different. His uncorroborated word is insufficient only to prove the litigant's particular reply where monetary expenses depend on it. Regarding performance of his assigned mission, the presumption is sufficient even for expenses. This is why notices are customarily sent through a single beit din officer.
