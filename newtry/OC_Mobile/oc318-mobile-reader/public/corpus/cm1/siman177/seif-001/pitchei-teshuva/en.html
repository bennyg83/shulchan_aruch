And some say the reverse. Baer Heitev cites the Shach, who considers the Mechaber's view primary. Chacham Tzvi, in the additional responsa printed at the end of the Lemberg edition, sharply rejects the Bach's defense of the Tur, but also disputes the Shach's explanation that the Rif and Rambam relied on what they wrote concerning a widow. Ramban's language indicates that absent an express tannaitic distinction, one could have differentiated between brothers and a widow on substantive grounds. Brothers are equal members of the family estate, so even a fixed medical expense could reasonably be paid from the middle because one brother may be ill today and another tomorrow. A widow is different. Therefore, the omission of the Tosefta by the Rif and Rambam suggests that they did not accept its distinction between fixed and unfixed medical expenses in the case of brothers. Chacham Tzvi accordingly identifies three approaches. Tosafot, Rosh, Ramban, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Beit Yosef, and Shach hold that where the illness was unavoidable, medical costs are paid from the common estate whether fixed or not; where the illness resulted from negligence, the distinction between fixed and unfixed expenses may matter. The Tur, Sema, and Bach hold instead that negligent illness is always paid by the sick brother himself, while in unavoidable illness one distinguishes between fixed and unfixed expense. The Rif and Rambam, who omit the Tosefta, distinguish only between negligence and accident: negligence is borne individually and unavoidable illness from the middle, without any fixed-cost distinction. Pitchei Teshuva therefore concludes that one should certainly follow the Mechaber insofar as unavoidable illness with fixed medical expense is paid from the common estate, because the majority and weight of authorities support it. But the Mechaber's implication that even negligent illness with an unfixed expense may be paid from the middle should not be treated as certain, because Rif and Rambam disagree and the Tur, Sema, and Bach join them on that point. Chacham Tzvi further discusses Ramban's qualification that before treatment the other brothers may demand division rather than continue bearing future unfixed medical expenses. He suggests that this applies specifically to an unfixed expense, which functions like ongoing maintenance and arises day by day. A fixed medical expense is a present, defined obligation imposed because the sick brother labored for the family estate, and once the obligation exists it may be treated as though the cure had already been obtained. Pitchei Teshuva notes that the plain wording of the Shulchan Aruch does not clearly reflect this distinction. Regarding a widow, the Shach appears to hold that where the estate must pay her unfixed medical expenses there is no distinction between negligent and unavoidable illness. Bach limits the estate's obligation to unavoidable illness, and Netivot HaMishpat finds Bach's distinction reasonable. Chacham Tzvi nevertheless leans toward the Shach, explaining that a widow relies on the full force of the marriage conditions and may be reluctant to litigate over whether she was personally negligent. He stops short of an absolute ruling because he did not find this stated explicitly by earlier authorities. See also Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer, siman 78.
