Specifically when they are not supported. I am troubled by this formulation. If seif 3, dealing with the one brother who alone conducts the business, concerns brothers who are not maintained from the partnership, and Mechaber and Rama there record the dispute of Tur and Rambam concerning negligence, ones, treatment with a fixed expense, and treatment without a fixed expense, the result is difficult. The distinction between medical treatment with a fixed expense and without a fixed expense is that treatment without a fixed expense resembles ordinary maintenance. If the brothers are not supported from the partnership at all, why should treatment without a fixed expense be paid from the partnership? Likewise, Shach writes that Rif and Rambam relied on the rule that treatment without a fixed expense has the same law as for a widow. But here the brothers are supposedly not maintained together. Tosafot and the other poskim likewise explain the rule on the ground that such treatment resembles sustenance. It therefore appears that the brother who alone conducts the enterprise in his city, such as one selected for royal service whose gain goes to all the brothers, is himself supported from the common estate because he is working on their behalf. For that reason, according to Tosafot, treatment without a fixed expense is included in his sustenance. The distinction in this seif is between such a person, whose sole labor for the group entitles him to support, and ordinary partners who are not maintained from the partnership. Even if ordinary partners promised one of them sustenance in exchange for undertaking a particular task alone, and he becomes ill while performing it, he does not receive medical treatment from the partnership, even without a fixed expense. This resolves another difficulty. Siman 60, seif 3, rules that where one obligates himself to provide sustenance to another, medical treatment is not included. Shach there explains that only a widow supported by a condition of beit din has medical care included, not one supported by private undertaking. If partners are supported only by their own agreement, why should medical treatment be included? Perishah at the end of siman 176 answers that partners who actually live and eat together benefit reciprocally from one another. That rationale does not fit the brother who alone works and is supported separately. We must therefore say that where he labors alone while the others remain idle, yet is legally required by the Mishnah to share his profit with them, the Sages correspondingly imposed on the partnership the obligation to provide treatment without a fixed expense. In this respect his support resembles one imposed by beit din. According to Mechaber he receives even treatment with a fixed expense because he works while the others remain idle. Mechaber's words, "When does this apply, that he is treated from the middle," therefore refer to one who is maintained by force of the legal partnership rule. But ordinary partners who neither live together nor benefit reciprocally from one another in sustenance, and who merely hire one member for a particular task and promise him food as compensation, are like one who privately undertakes to support another, and medical treatment is not included. Where partners actually live and eat together, however, treatment without a fixed expense is included for the reason Perishah gives. This also clarifies Tosafot's discussion of the word "ones." Our Gemara establishes the case as one where a single brother alone conducts the business, and on that basis even fixed medical treatment can be paid from the common estate according to Mechaber. The Tosefta, by contrast, deals with brothers or partners working and being supported together, so it distinguishes between treatment with and without a fixed expense because the right to treatment there arises only through sustenance. Tosafot therefore say that "ones" in our Gemara is not subject to that distinction. From this it follows that the opening rule of the siman, where one brother was taken for royal service and his wage belongs to all the brothers, applies even if they are not generally supported from the inherited estate. Taz rules like Mechaber and Rama, and this is primary, unlike Sema, seif katan 5. Shach's categorical statement that a widow's case does not distinguish between negligence and ones has already been disputed by Bach. Bach's view is more reasonable.
