But with ordinary partners, both of whom are obligated to conduct the business, if one exerted himself more than the other he does not receive extra compensation unless he first notified his fellow. The other can say, "Had you told me, I would also have worked." Only where a partner became ill or died, so that the other could not rely on him to perform the work, does the active partner receive compensation for his labor. An ordinary partner who simply worked more cannot later demand wages. He may charge for use of his animal or shop, as explained above in siman 176, seif 44, but not for his own unannounced labor. The same principle is evident from siman 287. Where adult heirs improved property in a manner that the minors could also have performed had they been informed, the improvement is shared because the adults did not notify them. Although that opinion differs from Rambam, Sema explains that siblings normally waive claims against one another. Even so, where the minors could have performed the work, failure to give notice is treated as waiver. The same applies to ordinary partners. Bava Metzia 69, concerning R. Elazar of Hagronia, may appear to imply that without the rule that a sharecropper is subject to the landowner, the extra labor would be compensable. That case is different. There the parties had always conducted the enterprise on the basis of payment for labor. When one later continued working without a new stipulation, he plainly acted on the original basis and expected the same compensation. The Gemara therefore needed the special reason that a sharecropper is already obligated to the landowner and that the prior payment had been given only because of ribbis concerns. Once that concern disappeared, no labor payment was due. An ordinary partner who simply works more, however, is treated as having waived extra wages by failing to notify his fellow.
