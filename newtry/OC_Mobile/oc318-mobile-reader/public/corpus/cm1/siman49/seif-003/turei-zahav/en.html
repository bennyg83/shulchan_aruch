Seif 8: "But if they are distinguished by a third generation," etc. Rama writes this, following Ramah, whose reasoning is that the holder of the document can say, "Since I acted carefully and distinguished the names in this document, you should likewise have distinguished them in the receipt." At first glance this appears difficult from the case below of one lender who lent to two men both named Yosef ben Shimon and whose names were distinguished in the loan documents, where a nondistinguished receipt is effective. One cannot answer that there the receipt was found among torn documents, because the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia shows that this is necessary only when the receipt is in the lender's possession, not when it is in the borrower's possession. The answer is that Ramah's stringency applies specifically when one borrower borrowed from two lenders both named Yosef ben Shimon and the loan documents distinguish them. Then there is an adverse indication if the receipt is not similarly distinguished, because it is known that the borrower has two creditors with identical names. Here, however, the borrower's name is Yosef ben Shimon and he has only one creditor. There is no reason to require the receipt to distinguish which Yosef ben Shimon borrower it refers to. What difference does it make that the lender also lent to another Yosef ben Shimon? Two men with the same name may each produce their own documents against another person, and all the more so the Yosef ben Shimon who holds the receipt may rely upon it. Thus there is no adverse indication even according to Ramah. Tur objects: what benefit is there in distinguishing the loan documents with respect to a receipt? The borrower can say: you, who seek to extract money, must produce a clear proof, whereas I merely seek to retain what is mine and may rely upon an ambiguous formulation, because the burden of proof rests upon the one who seeks to extract. Tur further asks: why is it necessary for the loan documents to be distinguished at all? Meaning, even if one accepted the argument that the lender may say, "Since you were careful, you should have been equally careful," still there is no particular need to distinguish the loan documents; the borrower can say he did not know this had been done. Even if he sometimes saw the document, perhaps he did not notice the additional identifier, for a matter that is not incumbent upon a person is often not retained in his mind. Beit Yosef questions Tur's first objection from Tur's later ruling that distinguishing documents can matter for a receipt, apparently referring to seif 9 concerning two documents that were distinguished. That is no proof. There, the distinction does not function merely to affect the receipt; rather, without it the instrument would not be a valid document at all, because another person could not produce a document against Yosef ben Shimon. I already wrote in siman 42, seif 8, that the halacha should be fixed in accordance with Tur here, that an undistinguished receipt is valid.
I later saw in Masas Binyamin, siman 95, that he writes that the Gemara proves Ramah's position and that Tur failed to notice it. Abaye explains the Mishnah's rule that if one finds a receipt stating that the document of Yosef ben Shimon was paid, both documents are treated as paid, as a case where two men named Yosef ben Shimon lent to one borrower. The Mishnah then says, "What should they do? They should distinguish," meaning, according to Masas Binyamin, that there is a remedy for the two identically named lenders: if the borrower pays one and receives an ordinary receipt, the other Yosef ben Shimon would otherwise lose because the borrower could use the receipt against him too; therefore they should distinguish the loan documents, so that a receipt written without the distinction will be ineffective, in accordance with Ramah. I say this interpretation is impossible. If you create a remedy for the lender, what remedy have you given the poor borrower? He may pay one of the distinguished creditors and receive an ordinary receipt that, on this view, has no effect. If you answer that the borrower is obligated to have the receipt distinguished, then rather than institute the needless additional measure of distinguishing the loan documents, the Mishnah should simply have required the receipt itself to be specific. Ramah's own law is not difficult, because he does not say that the lender should deliberately distinguish the loan document as a corrective enactment. He says only that if the document happened to be distinguished, then the borrower too should distinguish the receipt on the principle of taking the safer course. But to interpret the Mishnah's phrase "What should they do? They should distinguish" as an enactment directing the lenders to distinguish their documents for this purpose is unnecessary and cannot be correct.
