(Seif 8: But if they are distinguished by a third generation, etc.) The Ramah’s reason is that the holder may say, “Since I took the precaution of distinguishing the names in the document, you should have done the same in the receipt.” At first sight this conflicts with the later case of one lender who lent to two namesakes whose names were distinguished, where an unspecified receipt is effective. The difference is that where one borrower owes two lenders with identical names and the loan documents are distinguished, there is a defect in an undistinguished receipt because the borrower knows he has two such creditors. But where one lender lent to two borrowers with identical names, each borrower has only one creditor and an unspecified receipt plainly identifies the debt; the fact that the lender also dealt with the other namesake creates no defect. The Tur nevertheless objects that the lender, who seeks to extract money, must identify his right precisely, whereas the borrower needs only retain what he possesses and may rely on an ambiguous receipt. He further argues that distinguishing the loan documents serves no necessary purpose from the borrower’s perspective, so the borrower may not even have noticed it. The Beit Yosef’s proof from the later case is inconclusive, because there the distinction is what gave the documents legal validity in the first place. The correct practical ruling is therefore the Tur’s: an unspecified receipt is valid. The Masat Binyamin’s attempted proof from the Mishnah fails, because a rule requiring distinguished loan documents cannot justly render an ordinary borrower’s unspecified receipt worthless after payment. The Ramah’s doctrine operates, if at all, only where the documents happened already to be distinguished, not as a general corrective enactment.
