Where he relied, etc. Sema explains the case as one where the identity was not otherwise known and the witnesses either relied upon a woman or relative, or independently thought they recognized a Torah scholar. Because it is not customary to scrutinize a Torah scholar closely, he is believed to say that he relied on others who deceived him. By contrast, ordinary people are presumed to have independently checked carefully rather than relied on a woman or relative, and therefore cannot later say they were deceived. See Tumim. I wrote there that this explanation is not persuasive. Why should ordinary witnesses be disbelieved when they say they relied on a woman or relative? Rather, according to Nimukei Yosef, even if they actually relied on a woman or relative, they cannot say afterward that those people lied to them, because such informants are afraid to lie in a matter that will quickly be exposed. According to Rashbam, however, even those informants can later admit that they lied. The distinction is then this: a Torah scholar who was deceived by them may later correct the matter and admit the truth. An ordinary person, once he has already said that those informants denied the identification, cannot later reverse himself and say that he himself was mistaken. See Tumim.
