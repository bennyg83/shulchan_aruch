(Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 1) Furthermore, if the lender wished, etc. Note: One can say that where he directly hands the receipt to the debtor of the second person bearing the same name, that debtor will fear using it to exempt himself from the second lender, because he fears the forgery will be exposed by the witnesses to the receipt, who know that another person of that name handed it to him. But if he schemes by first handing it to another person, who then gives it to the debtor of the second lender, there is genuine concern. When the second lender sues, the debtor can say that the receipt came to him from that lender, and he will not necessarily be caught as a forger even if the witnesses testify that the first person of that name gave the receipt to a third party. The debtor can claim that he knew nothing of the forgery and blame his own lender for misleading him with a receipt that originated with the first person of the same name, as part of a collusion between the two lenders to induce him to pay in reliance on that receipt. Since his own lender gave him the receipt, he paid the debt. Even if his claim is not believed sufficiently to exempt him from the second lender's demand, he still will not be established as a liar and forger. This differs from where the witnesses saw the first person hand the receipt directly to him, for then, if he later says he received it from his own lender, the witnesses themselves contradict him because they saw him receive it from the other person of that name.
