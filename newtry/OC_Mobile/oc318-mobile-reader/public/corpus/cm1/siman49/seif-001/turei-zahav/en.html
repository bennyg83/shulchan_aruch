(Seif 7, gloss: His statements contradict one another.) The Rama’s difficulty is resolved by analyzing the sugya. Two men named Yosef ben Shimon living in one city cannot collect from one another. The Gemara explains that the Mishnah permits writing a loan document for the borrower without the lender present, so one namesake may have had a document written as though he were borrowing from the other and later use it fraudulently. The baraita that allows them to collect follows the view that a document is not written without the lender present. Rashi and the Tur add another defense: the defendant may say, “I was the lender, you were the borrower, and after you repaid me I returned the document to you.” Their reason is not independent of the Gemara’s. Even if both namesakes happened to be present when the document was written, under the Mishnah’s rule the defendant could explain that the witnesses delivered the document to him as borrower and he later returned it after payment. Because documents may ordinarily be written for the borrower alone, he was entitled to rely on that legal background and need not have torn the paper. According to the baraita, however, a document is never written without the lender present. If the defendant had truly been the lender and later returned the document, he should have torn it; leaving it intact creates a presumption that the present holder is the genuine lender. Thus the two explanations are interdependent. Since the Mishnah’s permission to write for the borrower alone applies only to a document involving a formal acquisition, the Mechaber correctly makes the law depend on whether there was a kinyan. The Rama’s presentation of the reasons as competing opinions is therefore mistaken; all agree to both elements.
