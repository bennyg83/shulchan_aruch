Since two people with the same name were not established, etc. Sema explains that this refers to the first case. If two people with the same name had already been established, the lender could not collect merely because the borrower says, "It was not I who became obligated; it was the other person." But in the later clause, even if two people with the same name were established, the borrower cannot say, "I do not owe you, I owe the other person," because we rule that two people with the same name may enforce debt instruments against other people. Shach explains differently. He says the case concerns a document not presently in the lender's possession; if two identical names had been established, collection would require authorization, as stated below in the gloss to seif 7. A simpler explanation is possible. Suppose two people with the same name are established, and the borrower admits, "I am the one who owes the debt, not the other namesake, but I do not owe it to you. You merely assumed the other lender's name." He is believed through a migo, because he could have denied being the debtor altogether, and where there are two identical names a deed cannot ordinarily be used against either one without clarification. He therefore can say, "I do not owe this person," and this is not a migo against an overwhelmingly strong presumption. The presumption from identifying the parties is not so strong, since the Sages were willing to rely even on a woman or relative, who could have erred. Thus the clause can indeed refer to the later case and teaches an important migo. This is a satisfactory reading.
