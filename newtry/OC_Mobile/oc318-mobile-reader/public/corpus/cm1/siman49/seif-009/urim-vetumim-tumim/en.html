Perhaps the case can be explained as one where the lender himself found the receipt and tells beit din that this is how he found it, as Tur and Shulchan Aruch's wording implies, while he claims that only one of the two same-named borrowers actually paid him. In that situation he has a migo: he could have concealed the fact that a receipt was in his possession at all. Therefore, two factors are needed against him: the receipt must be validated, and the note must have been found among cancelled documents. Without the cancelled-document circumstance, he can say, "True, I possess a receipt, but it relates to the other Yosef ben Shimon," and he is believed through the migo that he could have denied possessing a receipt altogether. Although this is a migo used to extract money, we do apply such a migo where it supports an otherwise unimpaired document, because the note itself has not been weakened if he says the receipt does not relate to it. But where the note was found among cancelled documents, the note itself has been impaired and a migo cannot be used to extract money against that defect. Conversely, if the note was found among cancelled documents but the receipt was not validated, perhaps Tur follows Tosafot that one may extract money through a double doubt. Here there is a double doubt: perhaps no payment ever occurred and the receipt was merely prepared in anticipation of payment, since it is not validated; and even if the receipt is genuine and payment occurred, perhaps the other Yosef ben Shimon paid rather than this one. A double doubt can support collection even from a possessor. Therefore validation is also required, leaving only a single doubt and eliminating the lender's migo. This explains why both conditions are necessary.
