His words contradict one another, etc. This difficulty is not unique to the Shulchan Aruch. It is equally difficult in Rashbam, who explains the Mishnah's rule that two people named Yosef ben Shimon cannot collect from one another on the ground that the defendant can say, "You repaid me and I returned your document to you." The Tur gives the same reason. This appears to contradict the Gemara explicitly, for the Gemara explains the Mishnah through the rule that one may write a loan document for the borrower even when the lender is not present. The Rama likewise records that reason. How could Rashbam and the Tur invent a different reason? The Sema, Bach, and Shach all addressed this, but their explanations are strained. I will first explain how Rashbam can be understood. Rashbam can maintain that the Gemara's statement, "they dispute whether we write a document for the borrower even when the lender is absent," does not mean that there is a genuine tannaitic dispute over the general law. We do not find any dispute that a shtar haknaah may be written for the borrower without the lender. Rather, the Mishnah can be speaking of a document containing a kinyan, while the baraita speaks of one without a kinyan, as Rashba's language cited by the Maggid Mishneh also somewhat implies. According to Abaye, however, who holds that witnesses acquire rights for the creditor through their signatures, documents may always be written for the borrower. How then can he explain the baraita that allows the two Yosef ben Shimon to collect from each other? It is difficult to say that the tannaim dispute Abaye's principle, for no such dispute is known. Rashbam can therefore say that according to Abaye the special case of two people with identical names differs. Either all agree that witnesses do not write without the lender present in this case, because the witnesses can see the obvious possibility of deception: the borrower who receives the document might later reverse the identities and claim that he was the lender and the other Yosef ben Shimon the borrower. Or, according to Tosafos in Gittin 17, Abaye's principle operates only where the creditor's identity is evident from the document itself. In a document involving two identical Yosef ben Shimon, until the document is actually delivered to the lender it is not evident from the text which of them is lender and which is borrower. The signatures therefore cannot create rights before delivery. A document written in Nisan and delivered later would then be antedated unless the lender was present at the writing. One cannot solve this by saying that the Mishnah simply concerns kinyan documents, because according to Abaye the Mishnah was not framed around kinyan documents at all. The unqualified language "they cannot collect" should cover ordinary documents. According to one who disagrees with Abaye, however, the Mishnah's ordinary loan document can be understood as a kinyan document, just as the Mishnah elsewhere says that a document may be written for the borrower without the lender only in such a case. This is one approach. Another approach follows the rule in Yevamot 116 concerning documents issued in Machoza in the name of Chavi bar Nanai. Rava, as Ramban explains in Milchamot Hashem, relies on the clear presumption that an instrument found in a person's possession was there from the outset. We do not ordinarily assume that it passed from one person to another. Thus, even though letters are not acquired merely by physical delivery, a document found in this person's hand is presumed not to have come from the other Yosef ben Shimon. Under that reasoning, Rashbam's defense, "I repaid you and returned your document to you," would ordinarily not be accepted, because the same presumption would tell us that the document was in the present holder's possession from the witnesses onward and did not pass to him from the other party after repayment. Abaye, however, disagrees with Rava there and allows the possibility that the document passed from one person to another, while he solves the case by holding that documents are acquired through delivery. According to Abaye's position, the defense that the document was first in the other Yosef ben Shimon's hands and came into the plaintiff's possession after repayment is therefore possible. Hence the Mishnah can deny collection because of repayment, as Rashbam says. The baraita can follow Rava's presumption that what is found here was here from the beginning and therefore does not fear that defense. Abaye rules like the Mishnah and therefore rejects Rava's presumption. Rava, by contrast, cannot say that the Mishnah and baraita simply dispute that presumption, for then the halachah would follow the Mishnah against him. He therefore explains the dispute through the separate question whether documents may be written for the borrower without the lender, and because Rava does not follow Abaye's theory that signatures alone acquire rights, the Mishnah can be limited to kinyan documents. This fits the flow of the Gemara. A second explanation is possible. Rashbam's repayment defense works only after the due date. Within the loan period there is the strong presumption that a person does not repay before the due date, a presumption strong enough to extract even from heirs. If the only reason for the Mishnah were repayment, the Mishnah's unqualified rule, "they cannot collect," would be incorrect within the due date. According to Abaye there is no difficulty, because Abaye holds at the beginning of Bava Batra that a person may repay within the due date. The Mishnah can therefore follow Abaye and deny collection because of repayment even within the term, while the baraita can follow Reish Lakish's rule that a person does not repay early. But for us, who rule that a person does not repay within the due date, Rashbam's reason cannot explain every case. We therefore need the Gemara's reason that perhaps the document was written for one Yosef ben Shimon as borrower before the lender was present and afterward placed in his hands, so that he now reverses the identities. Under our ruling, which does not generally follow Abaye's signatures theory but requires a kinyan for advance writing, the Mishnah can concern kinyan documents. A third explanation is simpler and allows Rashbam's reason to complement, rather than replace, the Gemara. The Gemara's reason by itself raises a question: why did the Sages respond by invalidating collection between two people with the same name? They could instead have enacted that in such a city witnesses may not write a loan document for the borrower unless the lender is present. We find an analogous special enactment in gittin, where two men with the same name may not divorce their wives except in each other's presence. Why not impose the same safeguard for loan documents? Rashbam supplies the answer. Even such a rule would not remove the problem, because the defendant could still say, "I paid you and returned your document to you." That concern ordinarily applies only after the due date, but that is the common situation in which claims are brought. The Sages did not institute a cumbersome special procedure merely for the unusual claim brought within the due date. Thus both reasons are true: the Gemara identifies the drafting concern, and Rashbam explains why a simple drafting enactment would not cure the problem. A fourth explanation is that returning the document after repayment may seem like a poor defense because the party harmed himself by returning a document that could later be used against him. If he trusted the other man, why should that change the law? This answer may suffice between the original parties, but purchasers cannot be deprived merely because their seller trusted someone. They can raise Rashbam's concern that the document was repaid and returned. The Gemara therefore gives the rule of writing for the borrower as the reason why the Mishnah says broadly that no collection is made even from the debtor himself. Once collection from encumbered property is unavailable, writing for the borrower creates no concern of antedating, because only unencumbered property can be collected. The two reasons are therefore complementary. None of these reconciliations, however, fully explains the Tur. The Tur states Rashbam's repayment reason and does not mention the Gemara's explicit reason at all. If he follows Abaye that signatures alone acquire rights, he should have said so. If he follows the distinction that Abaye's rule does not operate here because the identity is not evident from the document, why give a reason absent from the Gemara? Nor can we use the earlier Abaye and Rava approach, because we rule like Rava that the presumption is "here it was found and here it was from the beginning," and we also rule that one does not repay within the due date. The better explanation of the Tur is this. I wrote above that the rule permitting witnesses to write for a borrower without the lender requires that they know the parties. The Sages were strict even for a receipt, requiring identification of both parties because of possible deception. Here, where there are two people with the same name, what meaningful identification exists until the witnesses have clarified with the other Yosef ben Shimon who is truly lender and who borrower? The device is obvious: if they hand the document to this borrower he can later claim that he was the lender and the other the borrower. If the Sages worried that a person might even change his name to exploit a document, all the more should witnesses guard against an obvious manipulation in a city where two identical names are known. Therefore, in an ordinary document naming both parties, the witnesses will not write it for the borrower alone until they investigate and ascertain the parties. The Gemara's concern must instead apply to a different form of document, one saying simply, "I, Yosef ben Shimon, borrowed one hundred from you," without naming the lender. Such a document is valid, as the Gemara itself says in this discussion, and even a kinyan can be expressed that way. The witnesses may know only the borrower's name and may not know there is another Yosef ben Shimon in town, since ordinarily they have no duty to investigate whether an additional person of the same name exists. They may therefore prepare and deliver to him a document saying, "I, Yosef ben Shimon, borrowed from you." He can later use it against the other Yosef ben Shimon by claiming that the latter was the borrower. Rashbam's repayment concern, by contrast, applies to the form that explicitly says, "I, Yosef ben Shimon, borrowed from you, Yosef ben Shimon." In that case the witnesses would not have written the document for one party alone without clarifying the identities, so the Gemara's drafting concern does not apply. Yet after the real debt was repaid, the lender might return the document to the borrower because he trusts him not to brazenly reverse the parties and claim that he was the lender. This is a plausible human assumption, and many halachic rules rely upon the fact that a person does not ordinarily brazenly deny his benefactor to his face. On the other hand, the repayment explanation does not fit a document saying merely, "I, Yosef ben Shimon, borrowed from you." A lender should not return such a document intact after repayment, because the borrower could hand it to another person, perhaps in another city where the duplicate name is not known, or that person could seize property and claim upon it. More importantly, just as one may not return an ordinary unextinguished loan document to a borrower because he might later borrow again with it and create an antedated lien, so too this generic document must not be returned intact after repayment. A new lender who did not know of the first loan might accept it and thereby gain an improper earlier date. Tosafos in Bava Metzia says we normally do not fear that a paid loan document will be reused because the original lender knows that its lien was extinguished and will not lend again upon it. That reasoning fails in a generic "I borrowed from you" document, because it can be presented to an entirely different lender who knows nothing of the prior loan. Thus the two explanations address two different document forms. The Mishnah says without qualification that the two Yosef ben Shimon cannot collect from one another. For the form naming both parties, the reason is Rashbam's repayment and return defense. For the form naming only the borrower and saying "from you," Rashbam's defense does not apply because the lender would never return it intact, but the Gemara's reason applies: the document may have been prepared for the borrower before the lender was present. The baraita that allows collection holds that such a document is not written for the borrower alone. There is no need to fear collusion with some empty person who poses as the lender. Once the witnesses properly deliver the document to a lender, we do not continue to invent collusion, for otherwise there would be no end to suspicion. Ramban explicitly says this concerning identification of names in Bava Batra 167, and I demonstrated the same above regarding receipts. This reconciles the Gemara, Rashbam, and the Tur. It also helps explain the passage in Bava Batra that asks why we do not suspect that a document found in one Yosef ben Shimon's hands fell from the other. Tosafos ask that another baraita explicitly permits collection between them, so the tannaim necessarily already dispute the concern of loss. That argument is strong according to Abaye, who distinguishes a single person's loss from a public place loss. According to Rava, who does not make that distinction, the baraita itself indeed proves that the issue is whether to fear loss. Thus the Gemara's detailed answers are directed primarily to Abaye. This is why it gives answers that fit his system rather than Rava's. There is a further way to reconcile the Tur with Rava. Rava's presumption that a document found in a person's possession was there from the beginning answers the concern that it came from another person. Rashbam's defense here does not necessarily contradict that presumption, because the defendant claims that the document was in the present plaintiff's possession at the beginning as well, only then in his capacity as borrower, after having been written for him, and later returned to him after repayment. The document has thus remained in the same person's physical possession in the relevant periods, so the presumption "here it was found and here it was" does not identify which legal role he originally had. That makes Rashbam's defense consistent with Rava. In any event, the Tur's use of Rashbam is best understood as stating a reason applicable to one document form, while the Gemara's reason applies to the other. This also explains Rambam's unqualified ruling. Rambam does not follow Abaye that signatures alone acquire rights, yet he states simply that two people named Yosef ben Shimon cannot collect from one another without distinguishing a kinyan document from an ordinary one. The Maggid Mishneh cites Rashba's distinction between a document with a kinyan and one without, but Rambam himself does not explain his general wording. The answer may follow the principle in Bava Metzia that an ordinary document not weakened by loss is not presumed paid, because if it had been fully repaid it would have been torn. If only part had been paid, both sides would guard it. Thus loss itself creates the defect that leads us to suspect repayment. Here, by contrast, Rashbam's concern that the document was repaid and then returned can itself supply the needed basis in the forms where writing for the borrower is not the concern. The Mishnah and Rambam may therefore speak broadly. Another reconciliation, especially for the Shulchan Aruch, depends upon Rambam's wording that they cannot collect from one another until the witnesses to the document themselves come and testify, "This is the person about whom we testified as lender and this the person about whom we testified as borrower." The Perishah correctly infers that general testimony that the transaction occurred in the presence of both men is not enough if the witnesses no longer remember which was which. If they do point to the parties, the document is almost irrelevant, for their present testimony itself establishes the debt and they could write a replacement document with clarified identities. Why is general testimony that both parties were present insufficient? Because even if that removes the concern that the document was originally written for the borrower alone, Rashbam's repayment defense remains. The defendant can still say, "I paid you and you returned the document to me." Ordinarily one might respond that no lender would return such an intact document because it could be used to reverse the identities. Yet where the document contains a kinyan and therefore could have been written for the borrower alone, the lender may have thought: if the witnesses later do not remember the transaction, the document cannot be enforced against me because the plaintiff will face the defense that it was originally written for him as borrower; and if the witnesses do remember, they will likely remember who was lender and who borrower. He therefore may have returned the paper after payment merely for its material use. It is unlikely that the witnesses will remember that both parties were present yet forget which was which, and one need not fear such a remote case when deciding whether to tear the paper. Therefore, even testimony that both men were present does not defeat Rashbam's defense. Only testimony identifying this one as lender and that one as borrower does. This explains why the Shulchan Aruch says specifically where the document contains a kinyan: the possibility that it was written for the borrower alone makes the repayment defense plausible. Without a kinyan, witnesses may not write until the lender is present, so there is no such background concern, and returning the intact paper after repayment would be too dangerous to be believable. This is a fitting explanation that I offered in 5478 while learning in the fellowship of scholars before my teacher, the gaon Rabbi Shmuel of Krakow, of blessed memory, in Prague, and he praised it greatly. Nevertheless, because Rambam, Rashbam, the Tur, and Bartenura use unqualified language suggesting that the defendant may always maintain the defense and prevent collection, even without a kinyan, a possessor can certainly plead that he follows these great authorities. However, where the document contains no kinyan and the claim is brought within the due date, there is no repayment defense because of the presumption that a person does not repay early. Even though the Tur's broad language does not distinguish, that may be because he follows Abaye that witnesses' signatures acquire rights, in which case the independent concern of writing for the borrower remains. Since we do not rule like Abaye, I see no clear authority allowing the possessor to defeat collection within the term in the nonkinyan case. Finally, commentators struggled with Rashbam's phrase that a person may think he can deceive his fellow through this document "and he does not know what we learned, that one cannot collect from another whose name is the same." The text is likely corrupted. The word printed as "what" should be read as an abbreviation for "therefore." The sentence means: he intends to deceive his fellow and collect through this document, while the fellow knows nothing of the witnesses' writing; therefore they taught that one cannot collect through a document against another person with the same name. This reading is simple and correct.
Unless the witnesses to the document themselves come, etc. The Shach cites the Maggid Mishneh and Responsa Rivash, siman 323, who bring a view that even then the obligation is only that of an oral loan because the identity is not evident from the face of the document. I am surprised. Gittin 24b explicitly teaches that according to Rabbi Elazar, who holds that witnesses to delivery effect the instrument, the necessary identity need not be evident from the text. If our case concerns a document with a kinyan, the issue is even easier. Remove the document entirely: a kinyan itself creates collection from encumbered property because a kinyan has publicity. Whether or not this fact was written in the document is immaterial. Necessarily, then, the disputed view must concern a document without a kinyan. But in that case witnesses are not allowed to write until they actually deliver the instrument to the lender, as explained above in siman 39, seif 13. There could be no clearer witnesses to delivery than the very witnesses who write and then deliver it to the lender. According to Rabbi Elazar, identity need not be evident from the face of the instrument when there are delivery witnesses. One must therefore say either that this view follows what I wrote above in siman 40 regarding Mordechai, that delivery witnesses help only when specifically designated for that role; or that it rejects Rabbi Elazar's rule for ordinary monetary documents; or that it follows Abaye's signatures theory, so even a nonkinyan document may have been handed to the borrower alone without any delivery witnesses to the lender. But according to our rulings that witnesses to delivery validate ordinary documents, that signatures alone do not acquire rights, and that we have not adopted a requirement of special designation, there is little room for this view. Where delivery witnesses exist, the document should be fully valid even though the parties' identities are not evident from the face of the text. There is an interesting parallel in Gittin 24b regarding a man who has two wives with identical names. According to the view requiring the parties to be evident from the document, even a minor wife holding the get would not be divorced, because the instrument does not identify which wife is meant. The Gemara compares this with two people named Yosef ben Shimon who possess documents against others. See Even HaEzer, simanim 130 and 131. How then can those Yosef ben Shimon collect if monetary documents also require evident identity? It may be that the Gemara's comparison runs in only one direction: if the get itself is sufficiently identifying, then certainly the monetary document is; but at the conclusion, the monetary document can be valid because its principal lien begins at delivery and present possession creates a presumption equivalent to evident identity, whereas a get requires the identity to be evident at writing and signature, and the fact that the woman later holds it cannot retroactively cure the lack of identification in the instrument. This also explains why Rava says there, "This teaches that two Yosef ben Shimon may collect from others." Why was the Mishnah in Bava Batra itself not enough, from which Rava and Abaye already infer the same rule? The Bava Batra Mishnah concerns kinyan documents, where no evident identity is necessary because the kinyan independently creates rights. The get teaches that the instrument is sufficiently individualized even in ordinary documents, and therefore permits collection against others more broadly. This also answers Tosafos's question in Yevamot 120 and elsewhere how Rava can derive the rule, since perhaps the monetary document reached the holder through physical transfer and letters are not acquired through mere delivery. That concern had already been rejected in Bava Batra by the rule that they may collect from others. The remaining issue was only whether an ordinary nonkinyan document lacks evident identity on its face, and the get comparison resolves that.
It has only the law of an oral loan, etc. This wording implies that when the original witnesses themselves identify the lender and borrower, the debt has the status of a loan by document and may be collected from encumbered property. I am puzzled. As explained, we are discussing a nonkinyan document. Why should the original date create a document lien once the original instrument, as written, was legally insufficient to identify the parties? Even if the witnesses can now write a replacement document, it would be dated from now. If witnesses were supposed to write a document on the loan date but wrote it only the following day, it would be antedated if dated with the earlier date. The same should apply here. The original paper was effectively worthless as a document of lien. The fact that the witnesses now have authority to clarify the identities should not retroactively create a lien from the earlier date. Perhaps the Maggid Mishneh and Rivash hold that because liens are Torah law, even an oral loan would by strict Torah law collect from encumbered property, and the Sages prevented that only for lack of publicity. Here the witnesses did create publicity identifying this Yosef ben Shimon as borrower, even if their document was textually ambiguous, and once they now testify which person it was, the original public notice can support collection from the original date. But this does not adequately explain the Mechaber, who elsewhere rules that where a document was defective, even though the underlying transaction and publicity were clear, the lien does not operate until there is a valid document. For example, above in siman 46, where two blank lines were improperly left and only on the following day filled with relatives, the document is treated as antedated even though the witnesses certainly publicized the transaction. Once the Sages limited collection from encumbered property to a valid document, publicity alone does not restore the lien. It is therefore difficult to justify retroactive document status here. If the Mechaber did not rely on the Maggid Mishneh's reason that the witnesses can reissue the paper, but on my explanation that evident identity is unnecessary when there are witnesses to delivery and that the original signers were themselves delivery witnesses, then the original document was always valid and there is no difficulty. But if other witnesses were present at the loan or the borrower admitted it, without being the witnesses who actually delivered the document to the lender, the original paper should not gain document status merely because the fact of the loan is now known. Accordingly, the law requires study. At minimum we must distinguish between true delivery witnesses, who can validate the original instrument, and other witnesses who merely know that the loan took place.
And if one of them was a minor, etc. It appears that this also affects the preceding case where two Yosef ben Shimon cannot collect from one another. If the plaintiff was a minor at the date of the document and the defendant was an adult, the plaintiff may collect. A lender can be a minor. Once he reaches the age at which a child's transactions in movable property are effective, he may lend his money and have a document written for the loan. But the borrower cannot be a minor, because his obligation would have no legal force. The present plaintiff must therefore have been the lender. If he had been the borrower, why would competent witnesses have written a binding loan document against a minor? We rely on the presumption that witnesses act properly. I saw Knesset HaGedolah think otherwise and cite Sefer Moreh Tzedek for the claim that Rashi in Ketubot and Rashbam in Bava Batra imply both parties must be adults. He did not examine their context closely. They discuss a seller of land and a purchaser. There both must be adults because a minor cannot sell land and a minor purchaser likewise does not acquire land through the ordinary adult transaction. A monetary loan differs. A minor who has reached the age of transactional competence may lend movable funds and take a document. This is clear.
They should write some identifying mark. The Mishnah says that they should write whether one is a kohen or a Levi. Riaz, cited by Shiltei Gibborim, was uncertain about the following case. One of the two Yosef ben Shimon is a kohen and the other is an ordinary Israel. The document simply says "Yosef ben Shimon" without adding "kohen." Do we infer that it refers to the Israel, because had it referred to the kohen the witnesses would have written his priestly status? Or should they have written explicitly "Yosef ben Shimon the Israel," and since they did not, perhaps the generic name can refer even to the kohen whose full designation was omitted? Rashbam's formulation, "They should write: I, Yosef ben Shimon the kohen, borrowed from Yosef the Israel," does not decide the issue. Where one Yosef ben Shimon borrowed from the other and one is identified as kohen, it is obvious that the other is the nonkohen, since there is only one kohen. Rashbam's word "Israel" can therefore be ordinary phrasing. Riaz's doubt, however, is resolved by a definite ruling of the gaon Mahari in Pesakim Uketavim, siman 6: if the husband is an Israel and the other person with the same name is a kohen or Levi, there is no need to add another identifying mark, even though the distinction is not explicit from the get. This clearly shows that if the document does not say "kohen," we identify it with the Israel, for had it referred to the kohen the witnesses would have specified that status. One should not object that perhaps the document really concerned the kohen and the witnesses did not know that another Yosef ben Shimon existed, so they saw no reason to write "kohen." Where two people with the same name are established in one place, the matter becomes known publicly and the witnesses investigate. This must also be Tosafos's meaning in Ketubot 24. Tosafos says that although witnesses ordinarily do not investigate whether a person called kohen truly has priestly lineage, and therefore one cannot establish lineage from ordinary documents, when two Yosef ben Shimon exist the witnesses do investigate and the designation "kohen" then serves as an identifying mark. One might ask: perhaps the witnesses did not know there were two, relied upon the borrower's own statement, and thereby allowed an Israel to manipulate the document against the actual kohen. We must say that the existence of two identical names has publicity and the witnesses take special care. The Shach similarly infers below that witnesses know when the duplicate name is established. Tosafos in Bava Batra 167 appears at first to say that witnesses do not investigate whether another Yosef ben Shimon exists. That can be distinguished. In the time of the Mishnah, witnesses did not routinely write "kohen" unless needed to distinguish two identical names. Thus, if "kohen" appears, it itself demonstrates that they knew of the duplicate and investigated. In the Amoraic period, when priestly status was often written routinely, it might be added casually and could not by itself establish lineage. Even then, however, where two identical names are known, witnesses investigate carefully. This resolves the sources.
And some say that the city name is not written in documents, etc. This is the Beit Yosef's ruling, inferred from the end of siman 42 and below in siman 61, that the location is not ordinarily written in monetary documents. Bach and Sema object that those sources discuss the place where the document or loan was executed, which matters for determining the currency, not the borrower's city of residence. Rama was asked about this in Responsa, siman 95, and answered that Maharik held that if the borrower's residence was written, the loan presumably occurred there, since transactions ordinarily occur where the borrower lives, citing Ran at the end of Ketubot. I do not understand this. The presumption is rather that the loan occurs where the lender resides. If the borrower lives in Chevron and the lender in Jerusalem, the loan will ordinarily occur in Jerusalem, because the borrower goes to the lender. Ran's language about bringing proof from the place where "the lender or borrower lived" refers to the ordinary case in which both lived in the same place. His point is only that whichever party alleges a special value of the currency bears the burden of proof. The operative location is the lender's place. Here, however, writing the lender's city does not establish which of two Yosef ben Shimon borrowers is meant. The borrower can say, "I am not the one who borrowed; another Yosef ben Shimon from a different city did." Still, the Beit Yosef's interpretation of the Tur is correct in one respect. When the Tur says that the city of residence should be written, it is a direction for proper drafting in the first instance. If the city was omitted, the document is not automatically invalid unless the borrower raises the specific identity defense. This is compelled by the Tur's conclusion that there is no difference between monetary documents and gittin. In Even HaEzer, siman 128, the Tur follows the Rosh that originally people wrote the residences of husband and wife because of the concern of duplicate names, but in our exile, because people move constantly, we rely on the rule that omission of the place does not invalidate the get after the fact. If monetary documents were intrinsically invalid without the borrower's residence, there would indeed be a difference. Although one might distinguish that in a get the husband and wife are personally identified before us and the concern is only collusion with another couple, while in a loan the defendant can simply deny being the borrower at all, the Tur explicitly equates the two. Moreover, even in gittin, where two identical husbands are actually established in the same place, omission of needed identification can invalidate the get unless delivery witnesses clarify it. Thus the Tur's broad language demonstrates that writing the city is initially advisable, not an absolute condition. This also explains why the Tur recommends writing the lender's city, which the Perishah found difficult. The reason is practical protection of the lender. We rule that two people named Yosef ben Shimon may collect from outsiders, but if the borrower holds a receipt from one person with that name, the creditor may be unable to collect. Without residence identification, every creditor's document would be vulnerable: the borrower could search the world for another person bearing the same name and obtain from him a receipt stating that the debt was paid or forgiven. It is therefore good practice to write that the lender is from a particular city. Then the receipt must likewise identify the same lender and city, in accordance with Ramah's view below. Even though the Tur does not accept Ramah in every detail concerning three people of the same name, here he agrees that once the practice is to identify the parties' residences in loan documents, a receipt intended to defeat that document must contain the same identifying information. This is the correct understanding of the Tur, and it shows that all these requirements are stated for proper drafting initially. The Shach writes that the Beit Yosef's position is explicit in Ramban, cited by the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 24 of Hilchot Malveh, who holds that one does not write the residences of lender and borrower in loan documents at all. In truth there is a major practical difference. The Beit Yosef understands the Tur to say that the borrower's residence should ideally be written and that if omitted the document is valid unless the borrower pleads, "I am not the person named; perhaps it was someone from another place." Ramban, however, as cited by the Maggid Mishneh and as he writes in Bava Batra 164, asks why we do not fear that a person called Yosef ben Shimon might have a document written in his own name and then travel to another city where another Yosef ben Shimon lives and give the document to a lender to use against that person. Ramban answers that if we feared this there would be no end to suspicion. His answer implies both that residence is not required even initially and that the defendant's identity plea does not help, for otherwise there would be no conspiracy to fear: the local defendant could simply say, "I am not the borrower; perhaps it was a Yosef ben Shimon from some other city." According to Ramban, the document can be collected even without a residence. This is a major dispute. I find Ramban's view difficult to comprehend. If a document names no city, and the defendant does not live in the place where the document was written so that there is no presumption he resided there at the time, how can one extract from him merely because his name happens to match? There are many people throughout the world with identical names. A person could go to India, obtain a document from an impoverished Yosef ben Shimon, and then bring it to France to collect from a wealthy man of the same name. What basis identifies the wealthy man as the debtor? This differs from a get that omits residence, where the husband cannot simply plead that it belonged to another husband unless there was collusion, and we do not ordinarily suspect such collusion. Here no collusion is needed at all. The defendant merely says, "I never incurred this obligation." The Rosh in Perek HaZorek infers that if the city of the husband or wife was stated incorrectly the get is invalid, but if no city was stated the get is valid. Since the Mishnah there is according to Rabbi Meir, who generally requires the parties to be evident from the document itself, one may ask how a get without any place of residence is sufficiently identified, for in principle it could refer to anyone in the world with that name. Perhaps the Rosh and those who follow him hold that even Rabbi Meir does not require this kind of evident identification, as I explained above. Ramban himself in Bava Batra appears to agree with Raavad that residence in a get is essential. I therefore have not merited to understand this opinion fully, and it requires study.
Nevertheless, certainly, etc. One may ask: if there is no need to write the city at all and no need to fear another Yosef ben Shimon living elsewhere, what does it accomplish if the document does state that the person is from a particular city? Since the information is not required, perhaps the witnesses did not testify carefully to that detail. The Rosh says explicitly in Perek HaZorek that if a birth place was written incorrectly, the witnesses are not disqualified because they were not required to record it and therefore are not regarded as having testified to every such detail. Why should residence be different? The answer is that if this creditor seeks to collect from a Yosef ben Shimon who currently lives in Jerusalem while the document says that the borrower lives in Chevron, the Jerusalem resident should not be made to pay. Even without the contradictory residence, he could have raised the question whether he is the person intended or another Yosef ben Shimon from elsewhere. Ramban's position allows us to presume identity only where there is no defect in the document. Once the document itself points to a different place, there is at least affirmative reason to think another Yosef ben Shimon exists, and the matter returns to doubt. We therefore do not collect from the Jerusalem resident. Ramban can still hold that we may collect from the Yosef ben Shimon who actually lives in Chevron, because even if the city had not been written at all the document would have been collectible from him under his approach.
"May he live long" or "may his light shine," etc. See what I wrote in Urim. One can object that since we rule that a postdated document is valid, the defendant may still say, "I am not the borrower. The document refers to the other Yosef ben Shimon. At the real time of the loan his father Shimon was still alive, which is why the document says 'may he live long'; the document was later postdated, and in the meantime the father died." One might use this to explain the Gemara at the beginning of Perek Kol HaGet, where the inference is that although the second of two identically named husbands cannot divorce, the first can, which would show that two Yosef ben Shimon can collect from outsiders. Rashi explains that perhaps the second husband wrote the get, reconsidered and discarded it, and the present wife found it. Maharsha asks why we need to fear that scenario. In our loan case, by contrast, how can the creditor collect if the debtor says, "I am not the one"? In the study hall I once suggested a sharp answer: the Gemara certainly knew there can be concern that a husband reconsidered after writing a get, but ordinarily he would not discard it because his wife might find it. Even if he trusted her not to claim divorce during his lifetime because they reconciled, after his death she could first collect as a widow through witnesses of marriage and afterward present the get and collect again under the laws of one who produces a get. Even if a receipt would ordinarily be required, there is a practical difference where she lacks witnesses that she was his wife: without the get she may have a migo problem, while with the get in her possession the document itself proves the marriage. With two husbands of the same name, however, the first husband may indeed discard the get after reconciliation, because if she later lacks witnesses of marriage and produces the get, the heirs can say that the other husband with the same name divorced her. Thus the Mishnah shows that two identically named people may collect from outsiders. This is an exercise in dialectic. More simply, the Gemara asks what novelty is taught by saying that one cannot collect against the two identical persons. Obviously we do not extract money from either when we do not know which owes it. The novelty must be that even where some distinguishing phrase such as "son of the living Shimon" appears, collection still fails because perhaps the document is postdated. If so, a get bearing the same identification would not be self identifying under Rabbi Meir's initial assumption. Perhaps the woman had formerly been married to the other Yosef ben Shimon, was divorced by him, then married the present one, and the present document is a later dated get from the former husband. This would apply according to the view that a postdated get takes effect from delivery; the laws of postdated gittin are disputed, so the matter requires study. In the ordinary case, however, we do not suspect postdating merely because it is theoretically valid. Since one should not postdate in the first place, we presume that a document was written at its stated time. See Urim, where there is also the separate difficulty of extracting from a possessor.
But if he wrote, etc. The Shach writes that many authorities disagree and he inclines to Ran's approach, as stated in the name of Ramban, that when we say the witnesses did not testify to every detail, it means only that they relied upon an existing presumption. We do not say that they testified to something affirmatively false. Even so, this is not enough to extract from the possessor, because he can plead that he follows Rashba and Raah and that the burden of proof is on the claimant. Chelkat Mechokek in Even HaEzer, siman 3, asks that here it appears that if the status is written in the witnesses' own language it is effective, whereas in Even HaEzer even witness language does not establish lineage because we say the witnesses were not testifying to that point. Beit Shmuel answers that although they did state it, they may have relied upon the person's presumptive status, and one does not establish lineage from a presumption. That answer is insufficient. Rashba distinguishes between a formulation in which the borrower speaks in the first person and one in which the witnesses themselves speak because he found it difficult to say that witnesses would write a falsehood, and because Tosafos asks how "kohen" can distinguish two Yosef ben Shimon if the witnesses do not testify to priestly status. Once we adopt Ramban's answer that the witnesses are not lying but merely relied upon a presumption, there is no reason to distinguish the grammatical form. Even if the document is written in the first person, the witnesses' signatures approve the statement and they are not signing falsehood. Ramban himself explains the Gemara's first person wording, "I, Yosef ben Shimon the kohen, borrowed one hundred," through reliance upon presumption. Since the Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer follows Ramban, it should not distinguish here based on whether the language is that of the witnesses or the borrower. It should rule that the designation serves as an identifying mark in either form. We must therefore say that Rama remained uncertain which explanation is primary. In lineage law he is stringent for Ramban's answer: even if written in witness language, the witnesses may merely have relied upon presumption, and one cannot establish lineage from that. In monetary law, however, a possessor may plead that he follows Rashba and Raah, under whom where the document speaks in the borrower's first person the witnesses did not carefully verify the descriptive title at all, so even the presumption is absent. Yet this creates another difficulty from the Rosh in Perek HaZorek. The Rosh rules that if witnesses wrote the wrong birthplace in a get, the get can still be valid, and the witnesses are not disqualified as liars because anything they were not required to write was not part of the matter to which they attested. According to Rashba there should be a distinction depending upon the grammatical form, while according to Ramban this is not a proof because the witnesses may have relied upon a presumption. It seems the Rosh necessarily follows Tosafos, that ordinary witnesses do not scrutinize an unnecessary descriptor except where two identical names are known. Rama in Even HaEzer 128 rules that if the place of birth was altered, one may be lenient in an agunah situation, indicating that he too principally follows Tosafos and the Rosh rather than Ramban and Rashba. How then can he rule here that where the designation was written in the borrower's language it may not help? One answer is that a get is itself written in the first person of the husband, "I, so and so son of so and so," not in the witnesses' language. Thus Rashba's principle itself can explain why the witnesses do not scrutinize an unnecessary birthplace. But then Even HaEzer 136, where Rama rules that where there are two husbands named Yosef ben Shimon the description "kohen" can distinguish them, is difficult. According to Rashba, if the get speaks in the husband's first person the witnesses do not scrutinize the title at all, whether or not duplicate names exist. I therefore think the better explanation is that Rama did not place Rashba's qualification in the earlier general rule that an identifying sign such as "kohen" distinguishes the two. Rather, he mentions it specifically regarding honorifics such as "may he live long" and "may his light shine," whose value as identifying signs is independently doubtful, as the Beit Yosef questioned and as I wrote in Urim. Combining that doubt with Rashba's theory that a first person descriptor was not carefully verified is sufficient to prevent extracting money. For the stronger identifier "kohen," however, where only Rashba's theory stands against the mainstream Tosafos and Rosh approach, Rama did not rely on it. Gidulei Terumah at the end of gate 33 also tends this way. Examine carefully.
But if there is no document, etc. These are the words of Nimukei Yosef in the name of Ritva: if a person admits that he owes one hundred to one of two people named Yosef ben Shimon and then dies, while his heirs do not know which one, neither claimant receives anything until he brings proof, and the heirs may reject each. It appears that while the debtor is alive, if he says that he owes one of them but does not know which, he must pay each claimant, as explained below in siman 76, seif 2, because he was negligent in failing to know. If neither claimant demands payment, he is at least obligated to satisfy his duty toward Heaven. However, if the loan was documented and the lender lost the document, or the document was entrusted to a third party who does not know which lender deposited it, the borrower is exempt. He was not required to remember independently, because he reasonably expected to pay whichever person later produced the document. Since the lender lost his document, the lender caused his own loss.
He cannot extract, etc. This is so even if each claimant says, "The one hundred you owe belongs to me." It appears analogous to two people who deposited together, one one hundred and the other two hundred, and afterward each claims the two hundred. Since one claimant is certainly lying, the disputed one hundred remains until Eliyahu comes. Here as well two people demand the same one hundred, while the debtor certainly owes only one Yosef ben Shimon. One of them is therefore lying, and the money should remain until Eliyahu comes. Ritva must hold that when the Mishnah says the money remains until Eliyahu comes, it remains in the depositor's or debtor's possession, which is why he says that neither claimant can extract it. According to the authorities cited by Rama below in siman 300, who hold that the money is removed from the custodian and deposited with beit din, the same should apply here. At minimum the money should be taken from the borrower or his heirs and held by beit din. Rama's unqualified statement that neither claimant can extract from him therefore requires study according to those authorities. Rabbi Moshe Galante, in his short work of novellae, comments on the rule that recovery is possible through an authorization and writes that authorization helps only where there is no liar among the claimants. Where one of them is certainly dishonest, beit din does not involve itself, based on Tosafos in Bava Batra 35, s.v. "we do not extract." His words are difficult. Tosafos wrote that only according to the Nehardeans, who in the case "this one says it belonged to my fathers and this one says it belonged to my fathers" hold that if a third person seized it we do not remove it from him. We rule, however, like Rav Ashi, who explains the matter differently and does hold that we remove it, as stated below in siman 139, seif 2. The same should apply here: an authorization enables collection. A mistake escaped the pen of that great authority.
