Contradict. The Shach writes that there is no contradiction. In a document without a kinyan, which may not be written without the lender present, the borrower cannot plausibly say, "I repaid you and you returned the document to me." Since the document was originally written with both parties' knowledge, once the debt was paid the borrower should have been concerned that the lender might later use it against him. He should not have returned it intact, but should have torn it. In a document containing a kinyan, however, the borrower can explain why he did not tear it: he did not fear a later claim because he knew such a document could be written without the lender present and therefore, standing alone, was not reliable evidence that a loan had actually taken place. If asked why he accepted such a document in the first place, he may answer that at that time he did not know the law and thought it could not be written without the lender. The same explanation must in any event be given according to the Tur and those who allow the borrower to say that the document had originally belonged to him. Why would he have taken such a document initially? One must answer as above. This also explains the Tur. At the end of siman 39 the Tur rules that even a document without a kinyan may be written without the lender, and therefore here he does not distinguish between documents with and without a kinyan; in every case the document alone proves nothing. The Mechaber, by contrast, rules in siman 39, seif 13, that a document without a kinyan may not be written unless the lender is present, so he must distinguish between documents with and without a kinyan. According to our ruling that returning a deed of sale or gift does not itself reverse the sale or gift and that a new kinyan is required, it may be that a claim, "This deed was originally mine and I returned it to you," is ineffective in those cases, because there was no reason to return it. In a loan, returning the document makes sense once the debt is extinguished. In a sale or gift it achieves nothing unless the claimant says, "You originally wrote the document for yourself." The Sema's explanation, that in a document without a kinyan the borrower would be afraid to lie by saying the document had been his, is very strained, because we see that he is in fact making precisely that claim, and certainly it may be true. Likewise, the Bach's proposal to emend the Mechaber's text to read "if it does not contain a kinyan" is not convincing, especially since all printed texts read otherwise and the Mechaber's wording appears derived from the Maggid Mishneh, who expressly makes the distinction based on a kinyan. The Shach concludes that his interpretation is clear and primary. See the Taz, who likewise discusses the Mechaber at length and expresses surprise at Rama.
Orally. The reason is that if the identities of lender and borrower cannot be determined from the document itself, the document is effectively nonexistent. Nevertheless, the original witnesses have not completed their agency and may write another document identifying the parties clearly, even by adding third identifiers to their names until they are distinguishable within the text. Even if they did not actually write a new document, the testimony is treated as though the identities were clear within it. So writes the Maggid Mishneh. Sema.
From the other. The Sema explains that we presume witnesses do not sign unless they have ascertained that the persons identifying themselves are adults of sound mind.
And some say. The Beit Yosef also writes this and brings proof from the Tur in siman 61. The Sema writes in Derishah that the proof is not conclusive. The Shach writes that the Maggid Mishneh in the name of the Ramban certainly disagrees and holds that a loan document need not state the borrower's city. The Bach likewise writes that Ramban disputes this requirement. Perhaps this is the authority Rama intended by writing "and some say," contrary to the printed marginal attribution to Rambam. See the Taz's discussion.
A distinguishing sign. The Beit Yosef writes that concern still remains that the honorific "may he live" could refer to the living Yosef rather than to his father Shimon. Darkei Moshe rejects this because if the honorific referred to Yosef it should have been written before the father's name. The Sema reports this. The Shach agrees instead with the Bach and rejects Darkei Moshe's proof. It is common not to interrupt a signature in that way, and many parallels appear in Scripture and the words of Chazal. The Shach brings several proofs.
Were precise. The Shach discusses at length and proves from the Gemara and poskim that we rely on the assumption that witnesses took care with these identifying details and testify to the entire matter. See there for his practical conclusion.
In his possession. We do not fear that the document fell from someone else. See below, siman 66, seif 1, and Responsa Maharit, siman 120. Shach.
