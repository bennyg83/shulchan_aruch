The Rashbam, Nimukei Yosef, and Bartenura all explain like the Tur. The rule applies particularly to a deed containing an acquisition, which may be written for a borrower without the lender present. Otherwise, if the borrower had fully paid, why would the creditor return the intact deed rather than tear it, allowing the very claim now made? The Mishnah's Tanna holds that a deed may be written for the borrower without the lender, so the creditor can say: I returned the intact deed because I knew such a deed proves nothing. One may ask why he accepted it originally. He can answer that at first he mistakenly believed such a deed valid, following the opposing Tanna. The error is plausible because the Tannaim dispute whether a deed may be written without the lender. If the deed lacks an acquisition and cannot be written without the lender, he cannot plausibly say he returned it intact after payment, since he should have feared a later claim and torn it. The Tur follows Abaye that signatures acquire for the beneficiary, so even a nonacquisition deed may be written without the lender and he makes no distinction. The Mechaber follows Rav Asi in siman 39, seif 13, and therefore distinguishes deeds with and without acquisition. This resolves the matter. In a sale or gift deed, the claim that it was mine and I returned it when you transferred the property back may not help, because returning the deed does not reverse a sale or gift without a new acquisition. The Sema's explanation that the borrower fears lying is strained. The Bach's proposed textual correction reversing the Mechaber is also untenable and contradicts the Maggid Mishneh and the Gemara. The Rashbam's words simply mean that a deceitful borrower may have written such a deed because he did not know the Mishnah's rule that a person cannot collect from another merely because they share a name. May he live long, or may his light shine, etc. The Rama treats this as an identifying mark. The Beit Yosef says it is not, because the blessing may refer to Yosef, not to his father. The Darkei Moshe objects that custom applies the blessing to the father and otherwise it would have been placed before the father's name. I agree with the Beit Yosef. The ordinary practice is to bless the person presently under discussion, namely the son; the deed has no concern with his father. Placement after the father's name proves nothing because writers avoid interrupting the signature. Scripture and rabbinic literature often attach a title to an earlier person rather than the immediately preceding name. Mahari ben Chaim, responsa 28 and end of 43, likewise upholds the Beit Yosef. Although the rules of gittin say not to write Ploni son of Ploni, called such and such, because the appellation may appear to describe the father, but instead Ploni called such and such son of Ploni, it is not impossible for a title to refer beyond the immediately preceding name. Tosafot note that in Yehoshua son of Yehotzadak the High Priest, Yehotzadak was not the High Priest; the title refers to Yehoshua, just as king of Israel refers to David in David son of Yishai, king of Israel. One therefore cannot extract money merely from the placement. The Rashba's case may have included a blessing after Yosef as well, though he did not reproduce every word. A blessing such as may his light shine or may he live long naturally refers to the person being praised, the subject of the deed, like High Priest referring to Yehoshua and king of Israel to David. This also differs from an appellation in a get. An appellation is part of the person's name and therefore directly joins the signature, such as Meshullam called Vayfesh son of Uri. Writing Yosef, may his light shine, son of Shimon would instead interrupt the signature, because may his light shine son of Shimon is not a name. Mahari ben Chaim's broader explanation would make it difficult why Mahari Weil invalidated a get that placed the son's appellation after the father's name. Perhaps he invalidated only where another get could be written, as the German responsum's wording implies. The Rama's unqualified statement in Even HaEzer 129, seif 9, that it is invalid is therefore imprecise; the Levush says only that it should not be written. Another possibility is that Uri itself was commonly called Vayfesh, so the placement actually suggested the father's appellation. In all events, a blessing written at the end does not necessarily refer to the immediately preceding father. At minimum the matter is doubtful, and money cannot be extracted through doubt. The Beit Yosef's view is therefore clear and primary. But if the deed states. If it says, I, Yosef son of Shimon, may he live long, or I, Yosef son of Shimon the Kohen, the Rashba and Rama imply that this does not identify which Yosef is intended. The Giddulei Terumah's attempt to limit the Rashba to may his light shine is incorrect. Nevertheless, the Rashba and Rama reason that the witnesses may not have scrutinized every word because they testify only to the principal transaction. In my view the second chapter of Ketubot proves the opposite. The Gemara asks whether one may establish priestly lineage from deeds. It explains the case as a deed reading, I, Ploni the Kohen, borrowed a maneh from Ploni, signed by witnesses, and asks whether they testify only to the money or to the entire recital. Rav Huna and Rav Chisda disagree. The question is not whether the witnesses signed without any basis for calling him a Kohen. Rather, it is whether they testify merely that the person publicly presumed a Kohen borrowed, or whether they personally know him to be a Kohen. Witnesses certainly read the deed and would not sign a false designation. Proof is that the Gemara treats elevation to lineage as doubtful but elevation to terumah as certain. The Rambam in chapter 20 of Issurei Biah, the Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer 3, and the Maggid Mishneh all say that deeds certainly establish eligibility for terumah. If witnesses might ignore the word Kohen, even terumah would remain doubtful. Rather, they certainly attest that he is publicly established as a Kohen. That public presumption suffices for terumah, though it may not prove lineage. This cannot be explained as mere innocent speech, because the authorities speak where the witnesses themselves write Ploni the Kohen borrowed. The Ran cites the Ramban that even in witness language the doubt remains because scribes rely upon names and statuses as locally established; seeing him eat terumah and called a Kohen, they call him so. This itself proves that they would not write Kohen unless he was publicly known as such. The Rambam and Tur do not distinguish between first person and witness wording, because the Gemara's first person formulation is not exact. This also resolves Tosafot's question about two residents named Yosef son of Shimon, one a Kohen. The witnesses will not sign unless the borrower is publicly established as a Kohen, so Kohen is a valid monetary identifier. Yet their signature does not prove that the presumption is correct enough for lineage. Tosafot's distinction that where two such names are established the witnesses attest to everything is unnecessarily strained. The Bach's claim that monetary law was treated leniently is also wrong, since extracting money is a stringency against the defendant. The correct distinction is between sufficient public status for monetary identification and proof of pedigree. Thus the Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, Tur, Ran in the Ramban's name, Tosafot, and Giddulei Terumah disagree with the Rashba's suggestion that witnesses may not scrutinize such terms. Witnesses read and examine the deed and sign only where the description reflects the person's established status. The practical conclusions are these. If two men named Yosef son of Shimon live in one city, and a deed states Yosef son of Shimon, may his light shine, or may he live long, borrowed from Ploni, while only one man's father is living, the deed cannot be collected from either because the blessing may refer to Yosef himself. In a get, if only the son has an appellation, it should be written Ploni called such and such son of Ploni. If written after the father's name it is invalid according to some, though where no replacement can be written and there is concern of agunah some validate it. If the deed says Yosef, may his light shine, son of Shimon, may his light shine, it may be collected from the man whose father is alive. Even where the wording is first person, I, Yosef son of Shimon, may his light shine, borrowed from Ploni, and the witnesses sign that the transaction is true, it may be collected from the person whose father is alive, because they would not sign after reading the blessing unless it matched the known facts. The same applies to I, Yosef son of Shimon the Kohen, and all the more to witness language stating that Yosef son of Shimon the Kohen borrowed. Such deeds also establish eligibility for terumah, but no deed alone establishes priestly lineage, because witnesses may rely on public reputation rather than verified ancestry.
