The witnesses to the document. Rambam writes that where two men in one city are both named Yosef ben Shimon, neither can collect against the other by means of a document, and an outsider likewise cannot collect from either one unless the witnesses to the document themselves come and identify the particular borrower and lender. Maggid Mishneh notes that some commentators hold that even then the claim has only the force of an oral loan because the document does not identify its parties from its own text. Rambam appears to hold that if the original document witnesses themselves identify the parties, it retains the law of a written loan, whereas if other witnesses identify them it has only the law of an oral loan. Maggid Mishneh explains that the original witnesses can still specify the names more fully because they have not yet completed their agency. This is difficult. How does their ability to prepare a better document now make the existing document a written loan? The borrower may now protest and refuse a new writing. The matter requires study. At minimum, the case of an oral loan is clear when witnesses testify directly to the loan itself, as I wrote above.
The name of the city. Beit Yosef cites Rabbi Yeshayah that although a document lacking the city name is valid, if the defendant says, "It was not I who borrowed from you, but another Yosef ben Shimon from another city," his claim is valid. Ramban, in his discussion of wine found in an orlah vineyard, explains that grapes found in their very place can be governed by the principle, "Here they were found and here they were," which can outweigh an ordinary majority. Rosh, however, appears to hold that even something found in its actual place can still follow the majority. Torat Chaim therefore explains that where only one Yosef ben Shimon lives in this city and the lender sues him, while he says the borrower was some other Yosef ben Shimon elsewhere, the local man should be liable because the local proximity is a strongly demonstrated proximity while the worldwide majority is not concretely established. Even if many people of that name probably exist elsewhere, the local individual is the clearly proximate candidate. Nevertheless, this is not decisive in monetary law. Even if such demonstrated proximity can outweigh a majority in prohibitory law, monetary extraction does not follow a majority, and the same should apply to proximity. The burden of proof remains upon the claimant.
It is no worse than an identifying mark. This is Rama's own position. Tosafot in Ketubot asks whether a person can be elevated to priestly lineage from a document reading, "I, so and so the kohen, borrowed from so and so," signed by witnesses. If the witnesses do not testify to the whole text, how can the Mishnah in Bava Batra solve the problem of two men named Yosef ben Shimon by adding the designation "kohen"? What does that help if witnesses may sign without knowing whether he is truly a kohen? Tosafot answers that where two men of the same name are already known to exist, the witnesses certainly pay attention to the entire identifying description, and in that case the document can establish priestly lineage. But where there is no known ambiguity, perhaps they attest only to the monetary obligation and not to every identifying detail. Accordingly, if two Yosef ben Shimon live in different cities and the city name is written merely as an unnecessary identifying detail, perhaps the witnesses did not carefully verify it, especially according to authorities who hold a mistaken city designation does not invalidate the document. The Tur, citing Rabbi Yeshayah, therefore writes that the city names of both lender and borrower should be written, because otherwise the defendant can say the borrower was the man of that name in another city. This is not merely because the city name serves as an identifying mark. It must be part of the information to which the witnesses actually testify. Rama can answer according to his own preferred reading of Rashba: the distinction is not whether two same named people were previously known, but whether the text is written in the witnesses' own declarative language or in the party's own first person language. Where the witnesses themselves write the identifying information, they attest to the whole matter. Therefore the city name can serve as an identifying mark even if there was no known same name problem.
I, Yosef ben Shimon. I have already explained Tosafot's distinction between a known same name problem and an unknown one, and Rashba's distinction between language spoken by the witnesses and language spoken by the party. Even according to Rashba, if the document is written in the party's own language, "I, Yosef ben Shimon the kohen," and the lender seized property, we do not remove it from him. Rashba explains the Gemara's unresolved question concerning whether one may elevate to priestly lineage from such documents. One Amora says yes and one says no, and the halachah was not definitively decided. It is therefore an unresolved legal doubt. This also explains Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer: one may not elevate for lineage, where doubts are treated stringently, but one may elevate for rabbinic terumah, where a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. This uncertainty concerns only information relevant to the substance of the transaction, such as the borrower's identity. Details that are not relevant to the transaction are certainly not treated as part of the witnesses' monetary testimony. Ran writes that the dispute concerns specifically the person's own identifying name, because the loan depends on it. Other descriptive matters are not part of the operative monetary testimony. Rashba proves this from the Mishnah concerning one who challenges a field after having signed as a witness to an instrument describing the adjoining boundary. The fact that he signed does not necessarily prove the adjoining property belonged to the named person, because that detail did not affect the transaction. The later authorities learn from this that whenever a detail does not affect the transaction itself, the signed witnesses do not necessarily attest to it. Ritva, however, citing his teacher, gives a deeper explanation. Strictly, the witnesses may indeed be attesting to the boundary detail, but in monetary law we require oral testimony rather than testimony through writing. The signed document is effective as a document only for matters written with the consent of the person whose rights are affected. The boundary owner's title was not being transferred by that instrument, so the writing concerning him remains testimony through writing and is ineffective against him. I had already raised this very point in siman 28: even if the witnesses attest that the borrower is a kohen, how can their written statement establish lineage when that fact is not itself part of the monetary instrument? I thank Hashem that the principle accords with the teaching of Ritva's master. His wording also indicates that the strict rule of oral rather than written testimony is a monetary evidentiary requirement in this context. Lineage and other prohibitory matters may have different rules.
Except through authorization. Tumim cites Chiddushei HaGershuni, who says that authorization works only where there is no fraud. Where the parties are fraudulent, beit din does not attend to them, based on Tosafot in Bava Batra 35. Tumim objects that Tosafot says this according to the Nehardeans, who hold that where two people each claim ancestral ownership and a third person seizes the property, it is not removed from him. We, however, rule like Rav Ashi that the third person is a robber, so Tumim says authorization should work. I do not see an error in Chiddushei HaGershuni. The Nehardeans say that where a third person from the marketplace seizes the disputed property we do not remove it because a robber of the public is not called a robber. Tosafot asks why the two claimants cannot write authorization to one another and thereby convert the case into robbery from one individual. Tosafot answers that because the claimants themselves are wicked or fraudulent, beit din does not assist them with this rabbinic device. The reason is that authorization is not fully effective by strict law concerning property one cannot transfer, such as stolen property or property outside one's judicial control. It is a rabbinic enactment. The Sages did not institute that benefit for fraudulent litigants. Rav Ashi disagrees with the Nehardeans only on the third party's status and calls him a robber of the public. He does not discuss the law of authorization at all. Therefore there is no basis to say he disagrees with their rule that authorization is unavailable to fraudulent litigants. Even according to Ramban, who explains Rav Ashi differently and says the third party should return the property to the place from which he took it, the ruling does not rest upon authorization. A debtor may sometimes deflect among competing creditors, but a donee, partner, or robber holding property actually belonging to others cannot simply deflect them in that manner. Thus Rav Ashi's rule is independent of authorization. Since the Nehardeans explicitly hold that the rabbinic remedy of authorization is not provided to frauds and Rav Ashi does not disagree on that point, Chiddushei HaGershuni has substantial basis. Nevertheless, the general silence of the decisors in many places suggests that this limitation may not have been accepted as practical law in all cases.
