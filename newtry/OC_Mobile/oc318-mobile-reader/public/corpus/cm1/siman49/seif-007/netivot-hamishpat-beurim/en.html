This too is difficult. Shach also writes that with a deed of sale or gift one cannot claim, "I returned the document to you." His apparent intent regarding a deed of sale is a writing that simply says, "I sold my house," without identifying the house, where it is known that the seller owned only the house in which he lived, and the holder seeks to take that house by virtue of the writing. Shach would deny the claim that the writing itself belonged to the defendant and was returned. If the defendant can claim, "I did not know of this document. He could therefore say truthfully, "I am the seller. If he could even claim that it fell from him and the other person found it, or that it was merely an amanah writing, as explained in simanim 69 and 62, all the more so he should be able to make this claim. In my humble opinion, the matter can be explained simply. Shach's underlying question is extremely strong. Why would the debtor have returned the document intact after repayment? If he knew that the document could not be used for collection, why did he accept it at the beginning? If he did not know and believed it could be collected upon, why did he return it whole? Yevamot 116 expressly says that where another person bears the same name, a person would not ordinarily entrust such a document to him. The words of Shitah Mekubetzet are difficult for the same reason, as Tumim notes. If the witnesses did not know the law and thought such a document could be collected upon, how could they have acted improperly and delivered such a writing to the borrower alone, relying upon the rule that a document may be written for the borrower without the lender? Did they not realize that he could later claim to be the lender himself because the document is in his hand and the names match? If, on the other hand, they knew that such a document could not be collected upon, why did they write a useless instrument and cause him needless scribal expense? Furthermore, the Gemara itself gives this concern as the reason one cannot collect. According to the simple understanding, if such a document were collectible, witnesses would obviously never hand it to the borrower alone in the first place. The Gemara's concern would then never arise. It therefore appears that the concern is that the witnesses themselves may err and think that even when the document is directed against other persons, the holder cannot collect because of the possibility of loss or transfer, as the baraita on Bava Batra 173 discusses. One might ask why they would write such a document and cause the borrower needless expense if they thought it could not be collected. The answer is that the borrower may intend to borrow upon the document through delivery witnesses, who will know the identity of the borrower. Still, if the witnesses thought collection was possible only with delivery witnesses, the lender would eventually have to bring those witnesses before beit din, so they could identify who the borrower was and recognize the very document they saw delivered. Otherwise there would be concern that this was a different document. If those delivery witnesses must appear orally in any event, the signatures on the document seem to contribute nothing. Even without any signatures, a writing delivered before valid witnesses can have full documentary force, as explained at the end of siman 51. See Tosafot in Gittin 22, in the passage beginning "but regarding documents." Why, then, would the witnesses sign and thereby cause the scribal fee where their signatures appear to serve no evidentiary purpose? The Gemara can be understood as follows. The opinion that allows persons with identical names to produce documents against one another follows the view that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the creditor from the time of signing. Thus, it is entirely possible that the signing witnesses believed the document could not later be collected without delivery witnesses, but there was still reason to sign it. For example, the borrower may have intended to borrow in Tishrei but had the document signed in Nisan in order to acquire lien priority from Nisan through the rule that the signing witnesses acquire for him. Likewise, if the document contains a kinyan, the kinyan may have occurred before the signing witnesses, the date of the kinyan was written in the document, and the loan itself was not advanced until Tishrei. In that situation the signatures preserve the date from which the lien takes effect, once delivery witnesses later establish that this particular document was delivered by this borrower to this lender. There is no concern that the document was antedated because it had previously been delivered and repaid, and is now being reused, because so long as the borrower has not already incurred the scribal expense in a way that clearly serves a legitimate purpose, we do not suspect that he deliberately seeks to use an antedated or extinguished document to cause a major loss to purchasers. Tosafot make this kind of point in Bava Metzia 13, in the passage beginning "this is the reason." Even a found document can sometimes be returned without concern for such remote fraud, all the more so a document that was never lost. Once later delivery witnesses identify the borrower, beit din can rely upon the date in the signed document for lien priority. The signatures therefore have a real function. The debtor can consequently claim that the witnesses wrote the document for the borrower alone because of the above legal mistake, and can also claim, "I returned it to you when you repaid me." There is no difficulty that he should have feared a later suit upon the document. He could have known that the writing itself was not collectible without delivery witnesses who knew which person was the borrower, because of the Gemara's concern that perhaps the document had been written for the borrower alone. His original willingness to lend on such a document is explained by the fact that there were delivery witnesses. The signing witnesses were added only to establish the starting date of the lien, either because the signing witnesses acquire rights from the signing date or because the kinyan date needed to be preserved. According to the view that signing witnesses do not acquire rights from the signing date, and in a document without kinyan, there is no similar concern that the borrower came alone to have the writing prepared deceptively. The reason is the one stated above. If the witnesses believed that the document itself, without delivery witnesses, could be collected upon, they would not write it for the borrower alone. If they believed it could not be collected without delivery witnesses, then their signatures would serve no purpose at all and there would be no reason for the borrower to incur the cost. Thus, where signatures were in fact obtained, we can assume that the witnesses believed the document itself would be collectible, and therefore they would not have written it until they saw delivery to the lender. On that basis, there is likewise no concern that the lender later returned the document intact after payment. If he believed it could be collected upon, why would he return it whole and risk a later claim? This explains why the Gemara itself states the earlier concern, that perhaps the document was written for the borrower alone, rather than the later concern that perhaps it was returned after payment. The latter concern depends upon the former possibility. Without the possibility that the witnesses had written it for the borrower alone under the mistaken framework described above, there is no reason to suspect that the creditor later returned it intact. The Shulchan Aruch, however, rules that such a document cannot be collected upon until witnesses come and say, "This is the person regarding whom we testified in the loan," meaning that they must know specifically who the borrower was. It is not enough for them merely to remember that both persons of the same name were present without remembering who borrowed. Rashbam explains that in the latter case the specific concern stated in the Gemara may no longer apply, and therefore Shulchan Aruch needs the additional concern that perhaps the creditor returned the document to the borrower after payment. There is still no difficulty as to why it would have been returned whole. The lender may have thought collection was impossible without delivery witnesses who could identify the borrower. The signing witnesses were needed only to establish the initial lien date according to the view that signing witnesses acquire rights from the date of signing, or to preserve the date of the kinyan. Accordingly, with a document that contains no kinyan and in a framework where there is no concern of deceptive prior preparation by the borrower, the creditor who establishes his claim can collect even from encumbered property. This is evident from the baraita, which says that just as persons with identical names can produce documents against outsiders, they can produce them against one another. When such a person produces a document against an outsider, he can collect from encumbered property. Therefore, when one identical name holder validly produces it against the other, he likewise can collect from encumbered property. This is contrary to Tumim's conclusion. It also appears that even where the debt is still within its stated term, the debtor can claim, "I returned the document to you when you repaid me," despite the presumption that a person does not ordinarily pay before the due date. He can say that he intentionally paid early specifically in order to recover the dangerous document and prevent a later claim. Furthermore, where the document contains a kinyan, or according to the opinion that signing witnesses acquire rights from the date of signing, and the document could have been written for the borrower without the lender, the debtor can say: "You prepared this document yourself on the date stated in it. Later, when you came to borrow from me, I refused to lend on that date and fixed an earlier due date. You gave me another writing to that effect, and when you repaid me I returned this document to you." In an ordinary debt instrument held by the creditor and still within its stated term, the debtor cannot make such a claim, because he cannot contradict the date appearing in the document held by the creditor and say that the true due date was otherwise. Even if both persons were present when the document was prepared and it was certainly delivered before the witnesses, the defendant can still say, "I prepared it in order to borrow from you and delivered it to you intending to borrow upon it. I trusted you, but in the end I never borrowed, and you later returned it to me." Where he has a migo, he can even make a payment claim within the term. If he does not make this particular claim but instead says, "It is true that I was the borrower, but I repaid you and left the document in your hand," he is likewise believed through the same migo. This resembles one who admits that he wrote a document but is believed regarding certain defenses through a migo. Even where there is some possibility that the document did not reach the creditor on the day it was written, we do not assume that it was delivered only much later. The purchasers can demand proof, as explained in Bava Metzia 16. All the more so here, where the witnesses must appear in beit din in any event. Once it becomes known that the document was written for the borrower alone, the creditor will be unable to collect from encumbered property from the date written unless witnesses testify that the document was already in his possession on that very date, or at least from the date on which they saw it in his possession. The Sages do not ordinarily enact decrees for such remote circumstances. Thus, the original writing is valid to create the lien. Its deficiency lies only in the evidentiary identification contained in the writing. The original witnesses may therefore rewrite the document from the original date and add the necessary identifying details, because they have not yet completed their agency with respect to creating sufficient evidence, while the lien itself was already acquired on the original date. Other witnesses are different. Even where a kinyan unquestionably created a lien from the date of the kinyan, other witnesses who merely saw the kinyan cannot ordinarily create a new documented loan against the borrower's will, as explained in siman 39, seif 5. Specifically against the original witnesses to the kinyan, the borrower cannot simply claim payment, because those witnesses could still write a document, and therefore a prudent debtor should notify them at the time of repayment so that they do not later write one. Against other witnesses, however, the debtor can say that he repaid and notified the original witnesses, or that the original witnesses already wrote a document which he took back when he paid. The same principle applies here. Against the original witnesses, because they can still write a corrected document, the debtor is not believed to say, "I paid and left the original useless writing with you," for the witnesses could issue a new document. The situation resembles witnesses to a kinyan. Against other witnesses who merely saw delivery, however, he can say, "I repaid and notified the original witnesses." Here, however, the debtor does not wish to steal or keep the money for himself. He simply does not know to which claimant he must return it. This resembles one who says to two people, "One of you deposited this item with me." In such a case the property has not left the owners' domain in the same way as stolen property. A robber acquires certain legal powers over the object regarding physical change and related matters, and the property therefore leaves the victim's possession to a degree. Indeed, an act of robbery requires an act of acquisition such as lifting, pulling, or control through a domain. A deposit, by contrast, remains legally in the owner's possession. Ramban in the second chapter of Bava Metzia similarly writes that even a lost item can remain within the owner's domain in relevant respects. Accordingly, here the debt instrument is held by another person who has not acquired it through robbery. Even if one claimant were physically holding it while wrongfully seeking to take it, a victim can in some circumstances transfer the stolen object to the robber himself, as discussed by Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer. Since under strict law the true owner can transfer his rights in the debt instrument to the other claimant, no special rabbinic authorization enactment is necessary. Mutual authorization is therefore effective, as explained above.
