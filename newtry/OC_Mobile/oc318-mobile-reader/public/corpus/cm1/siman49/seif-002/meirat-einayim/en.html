That the borrower's name is so-and-so son of so-and-so, etc. But the Mechaber does not require recognition of the lender's name or purchaser's name. He holds this differs from a receipt, where both names must be known. With a receipt, the lender comes to give it to the borrower when payment is made. Even if the witnesses identify the lender's name, there remains concern that this lender will collude to hand the receipt to another debtor whose creditor has the same name, enabling that debtor to exempt himself wrongfully. They therefore must know the borrower's name as well. Once they know both names, we do not go so far as to fear that there is yet another lender and borrower with exactly the same names. By contrast, with a loan document, the borrower comes to the scribe and witnesses to obligate himself and says, 'Write a document that I owe so-and-so son of so-and-so one maneh.' Even if they do not know the named lender, they may write it, because there is little concern that another person bears this borrower's same name and that the borrower will hand the document to someone having the named lender's name so that he can extract money from that other person. If we feared that, even identifying the lender would not solve the problem. This is why the rule with two people of the same name is that no outsider may collect from either solely by such a document, as the Tur and Mechaber write in seif 7. Maggid Mishneh understands the Mechaber this way and Beit Yosef cites him. Rambam, however, requires recognition of the lender's name too, but according to Beit Yosef only in a document without a kinyan, for the reason explained shortly. In Perishah and Derishah I gave a strong reason that even in loan and sale documents both names must be recognized, even where there is a kinyan, which is the plain implication of Rambam and the Tur, and Rama rules so as well. Without a kinyan they must, etc. The reason, according to this view, is that a document without a kinyan should not be written for the borrower alone when the lender is absent. Otherwise the borrower may date the document today intending to borrow, while the lender does not actually lend until tomorrow or later, producing an antedated document. Therefore, lender and borrower must appear together before the scribe and witnesses. The witnesses recognize both names and the document is then delivered to the lender in their presence according to Rambam. Other Geonim hold that even if the witnesses do not actually see the delivery, we assume it is handed over immediately because the lender is present at signing, as explained in siman 59. But if the witnesses do not know the name of the supposed lender who is present, there is still concern that he is colluding with the borrower by assuming the name of the borrower's real lender, while the actual loan will occur only later and the document will be antedated. This concern applies specifically to a document without a kinyan, because the lien does not arise until the actual loan. With a kinyan, the borrower becomes obligated to the lender from the time of the kinyan and there is no antedating concern. This is Maggid Mishneh and the Mechaber's reasoning. As I wrote in siman 59, however, this cannot fit the Rosh, Tur, and those who hold that the witnesses' signatures acquire for the lender. According to them, even a document without a kinyan is not antedated if the witnesses sign before the actual delivery. Yet the Tur still requires recognition of both lender and purchaser. Therefore the reason must be what I wrote in Perishah to siman 39 and here. Under that reasoning there is no distinction between a document with or without a kinyan. Both names must always be recognized.
Even a woman or a woman close to him does not lie in her:
And if he had recognized himself or witnessed all of her as hers to say I was wrong and wrong
The author of the Mechaber wrote in Beit Yosef, Choshen Mishpat siman 29, that this is primary, so his contrary ruling here is surprising. In my view there is no difficulty for one who studies Beit Yosef carefully. There he cites the incident in chapter Get Pashut where a receipt bore the signature of Rava bar Rav Chanan, etc., and the Gemara says that a Torah scholar does not ordinarily examine closely. Beit Yosef writes that from Rabbeinu Yerucham's unqualified language one might think a Torah scholar is not expected to scrutinize carefully in any matter. Rashbam, however, explains specifically that a Torah scholar does not ordinarily stare closely at women so as to recognize them well and may rely on others for identification. Beit Yosef therefore concludes that the rule applies specifically to identification of a woman, not to other testimony, and that this is also implied by the Tur's placement of the rule in Even HaEzer 110. But one who studies Rashbam carefully sees that he did not mean only identification of a woman. The same applies to similar matters where a Torah scholar is permitted to rely upon another person's identification, even a woman or relative. This category includes identification of the names of borrower, lender, seller, and purchaser. A Torah scholar or judge may rely upon others who tell him their names. Ordinary judges who are not Torah scholars may also rely upon others, even a woman or relative, but when they write and sign simply, 'so-and-so son of so-and-so,' without stating that the identification came from others, we ordinarily assume they themselves investigated and knew the names. They therefore cannot later retract and say that others misled them, for that would be retraction from testimony. A Torah scholar, by contrast, is preoccupied with his learning and does not always undertake such investigation. We therefore accept his explanation that he did not investigate personally but relied upon others who misidentified the person. Rashbam says specifically that a Torah scholar does not stare at women in order to teach an additional leniency. Even where no substantial investigation would be required because the woman is standing before him and a simple glance would identify her, we accept his statement that he did not wish to stare at her. With a man, by contrast, he cannot say in such a circumstance, 'I refused to look at him and relied on others.' He is believed only where he did not recognize the man even after looking, so that further inquiry would have been required, and his preoccupation with learning explains why he relied on others instead. One should not say that the special rule concerning women means that even without relying on anyone else, if the scholar simply thought she was a certain woman, we accept his later claim that he erred. If so, Rashbam's words would be difficult. He begins by saying a Torah scholar does not ordinarily look closely at women and concludes that he may rely upon others, implying that even with a woman, if he did not inspect her, there must have been others whose identification he accepted. The reason the case is stated with a woman or relative is that in other contexts one ordinarily relies only on valid witnesses, and if the Torah scholar signed on the basis of valid witnesses he would not later be believed to say there was an error, because the valid witnesses themselves cannot retract. But where he relied on a woman or relative, whose statement is not full testimony, the Torah scholar may later say that they misled him. With this all the sources fit together. Beit Yosef in siman 29 cited the Tur in Even HaEzer 110 because that is where the case appears. Here too, when Beit Yosef rules that a Torah scholar may say he was misled by a woman or relative regarding the name of borrower or lender, he cites the same Even HaEzer source. Indeed, he expressly recalls what he wrote in siman 29, proving that when he wrote the present ruling he had not forgotten his earlier discussion. Moreover, the present rule is from Baal HaTerumot in the name of Raavad and is itself inferred from the Gemara. The case of reliance on a relative identifying a borrower or lender has nothing to do with unwillingness to stare at women, and no authority explicitly disagrees. We therefore should not reject their words. The explanation I have given is the correct reconciliation.
