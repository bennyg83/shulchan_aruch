And in a receipt, the names of borrower and lender, and so forth. This law is derived from Bava Batra 167b, which says that when a woman gives her husband a receipt for her ketubah, the witnesses must know the husband's identity. From there the poskim derive that in ordinary receipts the witnesses must identify both borrower and lender. At first glance the cases are not comparable. If a woman named Rachel comes to write a receipt to her husband Yaakov, even if the witnesses know that she is Rachel, they must still know that her husband is Yaakov. Otherwise perhaps another Rachel is married to Yaakov, and this woman will hand him the receipt and cause loss to that other Rachel. But in a loan, when Reuven the lender comes to write a receipt for Shimon his borrower, and the document itself says that Reuven releases Shimon, why must the witnesses identify Shimon? Obviously there is some Shimon to whom the receipt refers. If the concern is that this Shimon also owes another Reuven, recognizing Shimon does not help. He could still use the receipt against the other Reuven. One must therefore explain that "recognition" here means that the witnesses know clearly that this Shimon in fact owes this Reuven who is issuing the receipt. Once the receipt is being written for a genuine debt between these two identified people, the witnesses have properly performed their task. We do not then suspect every possible later fraud. Human beings can always devise new deception, and halachah cannot prevent every imaginable scheme. We are concerned only that the instrument not be false from its inception. The same is clear from the husband and wife case. Even if the witnesses know that Yaakov has a wife Rachel, perhaps he has another wife named Rachel overseas and could use this receipt to harm her. We do not suspect that. Since he truly has a wife Rachel and the receipt is properly written in relation to that marriage, we do not continue inventing remote possibilities of abuse. From this we learn a practical law. If a person comes to beit din saying, "My note was lost; write the borrower a receipt," and there is another lender in the same city with the same name, the receipt may not be written merely on the first person's word. The witnesses must know that this borrower in fact owes this particular lender, and where the note is lost that cannot be established merely by the claimant's identity. The other same-named lender must therefore be notified. Derishah raises this question and answers with difficulty. According to what I have written it is straightforward. This also resolves Baal HaIttur, who says that a note may be written for a borrower even if his name is not independently verified because people are not brazen enough to change their names. Maggid Mishneh calls this astonishing in light of the rule that both names must be recognized for a receipt. There is no contradiction. The receipt rule is not fundamentally a concern that someone changed his name. The names may both be genuine; the concern is that the receipt is being created from the outset for the wrong debt. In a get, by contrast, because of the severity of the marital prohibition the Sages were more stringent. Thus Baal HaIttur's position is coherent.
A loan document without a kinyan, and so forth. The wording implies that in a sale document, even without a kinyan, the purchaser's name need not be recognized. Shach asks why. We rule that a sale deed without a kinyan also may not be written for the seller unless the purchaser is present. If so, the same concern should exist: perhaps no true purchaser is present, and the deed will create an antedated claim. Rambam's ruling is understandable according to his own view that a sale deed may be written for the seller even without the purchaser, but why does Mechaber rule this way? I previously explained in siman 39 that there is a broader answer. Where a kinyan allows a loan document to be written without the lender present, if the borrower nevertheless arrives with someone claiming to be the lender, why should we suspect an elaborate ruse merely to obtain a document? He could simply make a kinyan before the witnesses and authorize them to write it without any lender. Mechaber can answer that the borrower may not want to make a kinyan, because a kinyan binds his property whether or not the loan is ultimately made. Rosh already calls it surprising that someone would knowingly bind his property before receiving the money. Therefore, the borrower might use deception to have witnesses write a note without making that binding kinyan. That reasoning applies to a loan but not to a sale. A kinyan made by the seller in a sale functions principally to bind him in warranty if the purchaser later loses the property. If the purchaser never actually buys the field, there is no warranty claim at all. Thus the seller loses nothing by making a kinyan now. Since he has a simple legitimate way to obtain the document, there is no reason to suspect the contrived deception of producing a false purchaser merely to get an antedated deed. Mechaber can therefore rule that in a sale deed the purchaser need not always be identified. Tur, who requires identification of both lender and borrower even though he follows Abaye that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the lender and therefore a note can generally be written for the borrower alone, can be understood as addressing a document whose wording affirmatively implies that both parties stood before the witnesses. As explained above in siman 39, that wording can create several forms of deception. But if the document expressly indicates that only the borrower appeared, then even Tur should agree there is no need to identify the absent lender. There remains, however, a difficulty from Mechaber's ruling below in siman 85 following Tur: where the borrower sold land to his creditor after the loan had already matured, the borrower may argue, "Had I really still owed you, you would have collected from the land when I sold it to you." According to that, one can imagine a fraud even in a sale document. Reuven owes Shimon one thousand gold coins on an overdue note. Levi comes before witnesses and claims to be Shimon. They write that Reuven sold land to "Shimon" for one hundred gold coins, though the land is worth only fifty. Levi then obtains witnesses who saw in beit din that a debt note was held by someone named Levi, and later has the sale deed validated as a deed showing that Reuven sold land to Shimon on a particular day. When the true Shimon later seeks payment, the debtor can produce those witnesses and argue: if you were really owed the money, how could you have bought my field while your loan note remained in your possession? Perhaps the sale deed has been hidden only so you can collect wrongfully now. In this manner the real Shimon could suffer loss without fault. This requires study.
Therefore it is astonishing, and so forth. Shach already answers this difficulty by saying that Mechaber too speaks of a case where the witnesses relied upon recognition supplied by a woman or relative, in a setting where a talmid chacham is not accustomed to scrutinize closely. But the very point remains difficult. The witnesses themselves did nothing other than rely on the woman or relative. If those informants were mistaken, why should the law differ between a talmid chacham and any other person? The talmid chacham did not himself investigate and misidentify the woman; he relied on the same informants as everyone else. Sema and Derishah therefore explain that it is not independently known that he relied on a woman or relative. Rather, he now says that this is what happened. A talmid chacham is believed to say so because he is not ordinarily meticulous in identifying women. An ordinary person, who is normally more careful, is presumed to have identified her himself and cannot later retract by saying that he relied on others. Sema himself concedes this is somewhat strained, because if halachah permits reliance on a woman or relative, why should ordinary people not be believed to say they followed the permitted procedure? According to Nimukei Yosef's text the matter can be understood differently. The case was a receipt signed by Rabbah bar bar Channah. The woman later came and said, "I am not that woman." Rabbah bar bar Channah said, "I too initially said it was not you," meaning that he had told the people who identified her that he did not think she was the person, but they insisted that she was, explaining that age had changed her voice or appearance. Abaye ruled that although ordinarily a witness cannot retract after testifying, a talmid chacham is different because he is not accustomed to scrutinize women carefully. Nimukei Yosef explains that his initial statement was not a formal retraction of testimony, and once he later agreed that the woman had been misidentified he was believed because his original identification had been based on others. Still, one must explain why this should differ from an ordinary person. Once the Sages gave a woman or relative credibility for identification, their identification has the force of two witnesses for this limited purpose. How can one later witness simply say they erred? Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 33, writes that where two people identify a woman and another witness later says they were mistaken, he is not believed. The answer may be that the credibility of a woman or relative here rests on the assumption that they are afraid to lie because others can investigate and expose them. With an ordinary person, who is likely to check carefully, they are indeed afraid. With a talmid chacham, however, they know he does not normally scrutinize women's appearances, so their fear is weaker. Therefore, if he later says the identification was wrong, we can accept his statement. Baal HaTerumot's discussion supports this approach. He writes concerning a talmid chacham who goes to identify a woman that he should take an ordinary layman with him. If he goes alone and later says he was mistaken, he is believed, because he could have been misled. But if an ordinary layman accompanied him, the identifiers fear that person's scrutiny and the later claim of mistake is no longer accepted. This shows that the difference lies in whether the original identifiers were afraid of detection. Rashbam appears to explain the Gemara differently. According to him, Rabbah bar bar Channah himself initially said that the woman before him was not the person named in the receipt. After others explained that age had changed her, he reversed himself. Ordinarily the rule "once he has testified, he cannot testify again" would prevent this retraction. A talmid chacham, however, is not expected to inspect a woman's face carefully, so his first impression is not treated as a fully considered testimony and he may correct it. On this reading, the Gemara does not establish a general distinction between talmidei chachamim and others who relied on a woman or relative and later discovered the identification was wrong. The entire distinction concerns the witness's own initial statement and later correction. Tosafot appear to follow Rashbam. It is therefore difficult to say that Mechaber rejected Rashbam and Tosafot entirely in favor of Nimukei Yosef. The wording of Mechaber can instead be read in line with Baal HaTerumot: where one relied upon a woman or relative and subsequently an error is discovered through the person's own changed recognition, a talmid chacham may be allowed to retract because his original personal identification was not exacting, whereas an ordinary person is not. Baal HaTerumot's precise phrase, "he returned and an error was found," supports this. Thus Mechaber's language can be reconciled with Rashbam, Tosafot, and Baal HaTerumot. For practical halachah, however, the matter still requires study, since Rama in Even HaEzer siman 110 appears to follow the first explanation based on Nimukei Yosef.
