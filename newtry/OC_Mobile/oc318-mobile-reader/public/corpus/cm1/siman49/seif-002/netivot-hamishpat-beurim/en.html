The borrower's name. Sema explains that without identifying the borrower properly, the borrower might later use the receipt to defeat a debt owed to another person with the same name. Urim VeTumim objects that even if the witnesses identify this borrower's name, perhaps he also owes the other same-named creditor. Moreover, a dishonest creditor would not need to change the borrower's name merely to perpetrate such a scheme. The proper case is where the witnesses already know that the man standing before them does not owe the second same-named creditor, for example because they asked that creditor and he denied having any claim against this man. In that situation no triple identification is otherwise needed. Nevertheless the witnesses must still know that the man's name is truly what he says. Otherwise he may actually be another individual who deliberately changed his stated name in order to obtain the receipt and pass it to a debtor who truly owes the second same-named creditor.
For a Torah scholar we accept it. The explanations of Sema and Shach are strained. Baal HaTerumot, cited by Urim VeTumim, gives the proper reason. A Torah scholar is not accustomed to scrutinizing facial or personal identification with ordinary worldly precision. A deceiver is therefore less afraid to mislead him, and the matter is not so clearly one that will be discovered. An ordinary householder, however, is expected to recognize the person carefully, and a deceiver fears that the falsehood will be exposed. Therefore the person is not believed to say the householder was mistaken. This can matter even where two witnesses signed the instrument and one is a Torah scholar. One such signer's uncertainty can undermine the document. The argument that each Torah scholar should be believed through a migo that he could deny the signature entirely is no answer, because a plea of, 'I was mistaken,' is not a valid migo in this context.
