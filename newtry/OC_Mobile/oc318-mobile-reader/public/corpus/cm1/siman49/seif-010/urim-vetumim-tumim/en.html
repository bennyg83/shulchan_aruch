Two men named Yosef ben Shimon. This ruling requires study. Its source is the Gemara in Bechorot 48. The case there is one whose wife bore two male children and the father died. Rabbi Meir holds that if the redemption money was given before the heirs divided the estate, it was valid, but if the estate had already been divided they are exempt. Rabbi Yehudah says that the property became obligated. In one explanation, the Gemara asks when the father died. If after thirty days, why would Rabbi Meir exempt them? Rather, he died within thirty days. But then why should division matter? If after division each heir can reject the kohen and say that the other is the firstborn, the same should be true before division. Rabbi Yirmiyah therefore says: this teaches that where two men named Yosef ben Shimon purchased a field jointly, a creditor may collect from them by an unavoidable alternative claim. Rava objects that a person's property is only his guarantor, and one does not claim from a guarantor before first claiming from the borrower. In another explanation the Gemara challenges Rabbi Yehudah: if the father died after thirty days, why would Rabbi Meir exempt them, and if within thirty days, why would Rabbi Yehudah obligate them after division, since each should be able to reject the kohen toward the other? Rabbi Yirmiyah therefore says: this teaches that where one Yosef ben Shimon bought a field from the other, the creditor collects from him through the same unavoidable alternative claim. Rava again objects that a person's property is only his guarantor. From these passages the authorities derived the rule stated here, that whether the two same named persons purchased a field jointly or one purchased a field from the other, the creditor cannot collect merely by saying that one way or another the field is bound to him. These are the words of Baal HaTerumot at the end of gate 33. Gidulei Terumah notes, however, that Rambam omitted this entire rule. This is significant, for why would Rambam omit such an important law concerning two men named Yosef ben Shimon and the inability of the creditor to reach the land by an unavoidable alternative claim? It appears that Rambam follows his own position. In chapter 11 of Hilchot Bikkurim he rules like Rabbi Yehudah that even if the father died within thirty days, the assets are obligated and the kohen collects even after the heirs divided them. The commentators already asked why, since the Gemara appears to say that when the father dies within thirty days the assets cannot be collected because the property is only a guarantor. Kesef Mishneh and Bach answer that Rambam follows the first version of the Gemara. There the Gemara's question, if he died within thirty days why does division matter, was directed only against Rabbi Meir, and it did not challenge Rabbi Yehudah, because according to Rabbi Yehudah when day thirty arrives the lien takes effect retroactively against the father. The difficulty for Rabbi Meir is only the alternative claim: either collection should work even after division or it should fail even before division. This answer still requires study. According to Rabbi Yehudah, where has the Gemara's reasoning gone that the property is only a guarantor and that the guarantor is not pursued first? It is true that Kesef Mishneh implies that once day thirty arrives while the child lives, the lien takes effect retroactively against the father who was alive at the birth. But this is difficult. Scripture says that redemption begins from one month of age, and if the father died earlier, what debt ever became personally binding upon him? Moreover, according to the Gemara's conclusion that the dispute concerns whether an oral debt may be collected from purchasers, Rabbi Meir presumably does not also disagree with Rabbi Yehudah about whether the lien attached retroactively. Otherwise they would be disputing two independent matters. Why then force the case into one where the father died after thirty days? We could say that the dispute applies whether he died before or after thirty days: Rabbi Meir would hold that after division the kohen cannot collect because an oral debt is not collected from purchasers, while before division the assets became obligated retroactively; Rabbi Yehudah would hold that an oral debt is collected from purchasers. The wording of the Gemara does not support such a forced reading. It therefore appears clear that the rule that a person's property is only his guarantor, and that one does not pursue the guarantor before pursuing the borrower, applies when property liens are not of Torah law. Under that view, the primary obligation rests only upon the borrower, and beit din compels him to pay. If he refuses, his property is reached, and the Sages further enacted collection from encumbered property. The property therefore has only the status of a guarantor. But if property liens are of Torah law, then as soon as the loan arises the debtor's property is directly encumbered to the creditor. It is a true lien upon the property, not merely the secondary liability of a guarantor. Indeed, one opinion in Pesachim holds that the creditor can even consecrate such property immediately. In that framework the rule of first pursuing the borrower does not apply. Accordingly, since we rule that property liens are of Torah law, the objection based upon the property being only a guarantor disappears. Rambam therefore properly omitted the entire rule concerning the two men named Yosef ben Shimon, because in his view the direct lien upon the property remains and there is no barrier of first pursuing the borrower. This creates a difficulty for the Shulchan Aruch. Here it rules without qualification contrary to Rambam, while in Yoreh Deah, siman 305, in the laws of redemption of the firstborn, it expressly follows Rambam and rules that even if the father died within thirty days the assets became obligated, without citing a dissenting opinion. There, the Beit Yosef follows his own different explanation, that the lien takes effect retroactively. In my view, however, the explanation just given establishes Rambam's position correctly. Therefore the present law is at least an unresolved question of law. The burden of proof lies upon the claimant, and if the creditor seized property we do not remove it from him. There is some support for this from the fact that we do not rule like Rav Papa regarding collection from minor heirs where there is no concern that the father left a bundle of money for payment. A minor is certainly not personally subject to collection, yet collection is made from his property. This indicates that the rule of Rav Papa, which treats the property only as a guarantor, is not our ruling, because property liens are of Torah law. I already wrote at the beginning of siman 39 regarding the dispute whether a man should first redeem himself or his son. The first tanna, who ordinarily is Rabbi Meir, and Rabbi Yehudah need not be understood as disagreeing whether a Torah obligation is considered like a written debt. The practical distinction may instead be whether an earlier oral debt precedes a later written debt, and this depends upon whether property liens are of Torah law. Rabbi Yirmiyah, who explains the dispute as one concerning whether a Torah debt is like a written debt, can be understood in either of two ways. He may hold that the other explanation, which states that they disagree under Rav Papa's rule, itself means that they disagree whether property liens are of Torah law. If so, why are two different disputes needed? One must say that Rabbi Yehudah teaches a further novelty: even where only encumbered property is available, redemption of the son takes precedence and one may seize from purchasers for that redemption. Alternatively, if Rav Papa's dispute is understood to concern a case where even Rav Papa concedes that collection from purchasers applies because otherwise lending would be discouraged, Rabbi Yirmiyah may hold that everyone agrees property liens are not of Torah law, and that the Sages nevertheless treated a Torah debt like a written debt once they enacted collection by written instruments. This is the point of Yam Shel Shlomo's discussion regarding the Gemara's question according to Rav and Shmuel whether damages differ because they are like a written debt. Shach objected that Rav and Shmuel themselves do not say so concerning heirs' obligation to bring their father's burnt offering. The answer is that Rav and Shmuel explicitly say that by Torah law neither an oral debt nor a written debt creates collection from encumbered property. Thus the phrase "like a written debt" cannot mean a Torah lien. Rather, the question is whether, after the Sages enacted collection for written debts, a Torah obligation was included in that enactment as well. There is no proof from sacrifices, where no such enactment applies. This can be used to explain the two versions of the Gemara. According to the first version, property liens are of Torah law. Rabbi Meir's position elsewhere indicates that he denies such a Torah lien, for otherwise there are circumstances where an earlier redemption obligation would precede a later written loan, contrary to his absolute ruling. Rabbi Yehudah, however, may hold that property liens are of Torah law, so the rule of the property being only a guarantor does not apply. Rabbi Yirmiyah can therefore derive his conclusion from Rabbi Yehudah without facing Rava's objection. Rava's challenge applies only to Rabbi Meir, whose position indicates that liens are not of Torah law. The Gemara then answers through Rav Papa's dispute, understood as the dispute whether property liens are of Torah law. The second version, by contrast, assumes that everyone agrees liens are not of Torah law, and the Rav Papa dispute is understood through the special concern of discouraging lending. Under that version even Rabbi Yehudah is subject to Rava's challenge that the property is only a guarantor. Since we rule that property liens are of Torah law, the first version is the primary one for practical law. Rambam consequently rules that even when the father died within thirty days the assets became obligated, and he omitted the same named Yosef ben Shimon rule because the guarantor objection has no place in our ruling. One should not ask that if Rabbi Meir certainly holds liens are not of Torah law, why the Gemara asks, if the father died after thirty days, why Rabbi Meir exempts after division. The heirs are like purchasers, and an oral debt is not collected from purchasers. At that stage the Gemara must have assumed that with heirs dividing an estate an oral debt is nevertheless collected, for otherwise every group of brothers could divide the estate and thereby evade their father's debts. Yam Shel Shlomo's rejection of a certain reading in Kiddushin can also be understood this way. Rabbi Yirmiyah says that Rabbi Yehudah's ruling teaches that two men named Yosef ben Shimon who purchased jointly can be charged, and Rashi explains that after division the heirs are like purchasers. One might ask how the kohen collects if each says that the other was the firstborn and that he purchased from him, since an oral debt is not collected from purchasers. But at the initial stage, as explained, the Gemara assumed that an oral debt could still be collected from heirs who divided. I also said in my novellae that one cannot simply establish the redemption case where there was a later written creditor and say that the son's redemption should precede it. We must understand how the Gemara can advise prioritizing the son's redemption in a manner that would cause loss to another creditor. It appears that the case concerns purchasers who bought without warranty. A person may knowingly buy land only for the present and accept the risk of later collection, and such a borrower is not considered one who wickedly borrows and does not repay. If the later written creditor had warranty, however, the problem returns, because the advice would cause direct loss. Tosafos in Bechorot asks that purchasers should return and collect from the unencumbered property that the kohen received for the son's redemption, which shows that Tosafos assumes purchases with warranty. Their reasoning appears to be the reverse: the Gemara specifically needs the reason that the mitzvah of redeeming his son rests upon him. Without that reason, one might say that by paying the son's redemption first he performs two mitzvot, his own and his son's, while otherwise he loses one mitzvah. The fact that the Gemara still needs a reason indicates that the competing value is the mitzvah of repaying one's debt, since by preferring the son's redemption he leaves purchasers with a claim against him and risks the status of one who does not repay. Thus the case can indeed involve purchases with warranty. See below, siman 117, where I wrote that perhaps Tosafos's question is not that the purchasers should recover from the kohen, but that they should tell him that he left available property from which he could collect. I have also written further resolutions in the following siman.
Since he cannot claim from the borrower himself, etc. In the case where one Yosef ben Shimon purchased a field from the other, the Gemara gives the reason that one must first pursue the borrower and not his guarantor. Why does it not use the simpler argument that the purchaser can say, "If you remain silent, fine, and if not I will return the deed to its former owner," according to Tosafos who holds that we do not say, "when you actually return it"? From the borrower himself, etc. This requires analysis in light of the Rama below, siman 129, seif 8, who rules that if the borrower denies the loan but the guarantor admits that the loan occurred, the guarantor must pay. Here, the creditor holds a document against one of two men named Yosef ben Shimon. He claims against the one who purchased the field, and that person denies having borrowed. By that denial he effectively admits that the other Yosef ben Shimon borrowed, because there is a witnessed document establishing that some Yosef ben Shimon borrowed. If he says expressly that it was not he, he is saying that the other borrowed and merely denies improperly. Why then should he not be liable as guarantor, even though the borrower denies, just as the Rama rules that a guarantor who admits the loan must pay? There is an additional point. Above, seif 7, it is explained that if other witnesses testify which Yosef ben Shimon borrowed, their testimony establishes an oral debt. Regarding his own liability, a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. Thus he admits that the other Yosef ben Shimon borrowed, and the witnesses in the document testify that a Yosef ben Shimon borrowed. The answer must be that this indeed creates only the equivalent of an oral debt. Since the document itself does not identify which man is the borrower, its lien upon encumbered property is ineffective. The purchaser may therefore admit that the seller borrowed and still say that the creditor has only an oral debt, which cannot be collected from property sold to him. In that case there is no unavoidable alternative claim. This works only where he purchased the field. If he inherited from the other Yosef ben Shimon, the creditor should certainly collect, because the heir admits that the other man was the borrower and the property became bound for that oral debt. We rule that an oral debt is collected from heirs. Consider Reuven who denies a loan, while Shimon his son admits that Reuven truly owes the money. The son's admission cannot obligate Reuven during his lifetime, but after Reuven dies and his property descends to Shimon, can there be any doubt that Shimon must pay what he admitted his predecessor truly owed? The same is true here. By saying that he himself was not the borrower and that the witnessed document does not refer to him, he necessarily admits that his predecessor was the borrower. As heir, he must pay. Likewise, if one Yosef ben Shimon acted as guarantor for the other, he should be liable. By saying that he himself did not borrow, he necessarily admits that the other man borrowed. The other borrower's denial does not help, because the Rama rules that where the borrower denies and the guarantor admits the loan, the guarantor must pay. Even an oral loan can obligate a guarantor. Therefore the earlier explanation does not apply in these cases, and in my view the rule stated here has no foundation with respect to an heir or guarantor. He must pay. Shach, seifim katanim 19 and 20, concludes that the same exemption applies if one inherited from his same named uncle or became guarantor for the other same named man. In my view this is incorrect, for the reasons stated, and his ruling requires study. With this I explained in my novellae the difficulty of Maharsha concerning the Gemara in Gittin. The Gemara infers from the statement that one cannot divorce with another person's get that he can divorce his own wife, apparently proving that other persons can produce documents against two same named Yosef ben Shimon. Maharsha asks that perhaps we simply do not fear that one reconsidered and discarded his document and the other found it, whereas in a loan case each same named person can say, "I never borrowed." The Gemara's proof can be understood as follows. The statement that outsiders cannot produce documents against them would otherwise be obvious, because the identity of the borrower is unknown. It must therefore include even a case where one same named person is guarantor for the other, and yet it exempts him. Why? One might have expected collection through the unavoidable alternative claim. The answer is that there is an inherent defect in the circumstances: why would a lender lend to one of two identically named men without adding identifying details to the document and thereby place his money into a doubtful litigation? It is plausible that one Yosef ben Shimon wished to borrow, but when he saw that the document had not been properly distinguished he refused to proceed, discarded it, and the other later found it and now presents it. Therefore both are exempt. The same reasoning applies to a get. Why would a man write a get that could be used only in the cumbersome manner of divorcing in the other's presence, thereby publicizing the matter unnecessarily? It is plausible that when he saw that the get was not distinguished he refused to divorce with it, discarded it, and the other found it. This answers Maharsha's difficulty. There is also an additional point from the familiar question that perhaps the get was written conditionally, in hope of reconciliation. The usual answer that a person does not hasten punishment upon himself is weaker in the case of two men with the same name, because the misfortune may be attributed to the other man. Examine carefully.
