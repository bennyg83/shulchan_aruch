Since your fellow has no unencumbered property. Urim VeTumim asks why the purchaser cannot simply say, 'If you remain silent, fine; if not, I will return the deed to its owner.' There is no difficulty. Returning the deed does not undo the first sale; it is effectively a new resale back to the seller. The case can therefore be one where the other namesake likewise has no other unencumbered property except this field. If the purchaser transfers it back, the creditor can still proceed against the other namesake from either direction. Moreover, even where the other namesake has other assets, inability to collect from him can resemble loss of the available property. Still, Mechaber's ruling itself is not entirely satisfactory. The purchaser was negligent by accepting a deed that failed to distinguish the namesakes. This resembles a document dated only 'Nisan' without specifying the day, where the creditor may be told that he left available property from which to collect because he created the uncertainty. The case in Bekhorot is different because there the claimant committed no such negligence.
And since he cannot sue the borrower. Shach, seifim katanim 19 and 20, extends this to a namesake who inherited a field from another namesake, and to a namesake who guaranteed another namesake. Urim VeTumim rightly expresses astonishment. Even putting the document aside, Rama in siman 129 rules that if the borrower denies but the guarantor admits, the guarantor must pay. Here the guarantor says, 'I did not borrow,' which necessarily means he admits his fellow borrowed. Likewise, an heir who admits that his father owed the money ordinarily must pay from the inherited estate. The correct distinction is where neither guarantor nor heir independently knows that the other namesake borrowed and the entire attempt to establish that fact depends on combining the document with the present party's admission, 'I was not the borrower.' Suppose witnesses testified only that one of two namesakes borrowed, and another set testified that one particular namesake definitely did not borrow. We would not combine the two sets to establish that the other borrowed, because the first testimony was only half a matter and has no independent legal effect. The same is true when the second element comes from the admission of one namesake. The original witnesses did not establish a complete liability against a particular person. This is especially important with a guarantor. A guarantor is not sued before the borrower. If the testimony cannot establish the borrower's personal liability, it likewise cannot establish the guarantor's secondary liability. Similarly, a lien on property depends on proof of the borrower's obligation. The case of the two male children in Bekhorot shows that even where beit din knows that one estate must be liable, property is not taken when the personal obligation cannot be established against a particular obligor. This reasoning depends on the doctrine of half a matter. Some authorities understand half a matter to depend on the evidence as it stood at the time of seeing, not merely at the time of testimony. On that approach one can argue that the first witnesses did observe a complete borrowing event but forgot which namesake borrowed, which may still leave a half-matter problem. In any event, Shach's rule can also be understood in a narrower guaranty case: the guarantor said, 'Lend to the namesake and I will guarantee him,' without personally knowing the loan later occurred. If he specified that the loan should be made by document, and the creditor accepted a document that cannot be enforced against the borrower, the guarantor can say he undertook only on the assumption that he could later recover against the borrower through an enforceable document. Even if he said generally, 'Lend to so-and-so before witnesses and I will be guarantor,' his undertaking naturally assumes proof sufficient to sue the borrower. If the loan was made without adequate identifying proof, the guaranty may fail. As for an heir, if the heir independently admits that his father borrowed, ordinary heir liability applies. The exemption applies where the heir's only reason for reaching that conclusion is the defective document combined with his statement that he himself was not the borrower. In that case the instrument never had effective documentary status against the father, and the heir can also say perhaps the debt was later paid; leaving such an unusable document in the creditor's hands is no proof of nonpayment.
