The Shach writes that the same applies where one Yosef ben Shimon guaranteed for another Yosef ben Shimon: just as the lender cannot collect from the borrower, he cannot collect from the guarantor. This is very difficult. Put aside the document. If the guarantor says, "I myself did not borrow," he is necessarily admitting that the other Yosef ben Shimon was the borrower. When the borrower denies the debt, the guarantor must pay, as explained in siman 129. The Tur's case, in which the guarantor is exempt, is where it is not known at all for which of the two men he guaranteed. Since the borrower cannot be identified and is exempt, the guarantor is likewise exempt. But if the guarantor knows which one borrowed, the guarantor is liable even if the borrower denies the debt. The same applies whether the borrower is Jewish or gentile. The Shach's further statement that an heir who inherited from his uncle Yosef ben Shimon need not pay more is also surprising. Even an oral loan must be paid from the inheritance when it is known that the decedent owed it. Tumim likewise expresses astonishment. The Shach can nevertheless be defended through the sugya in Perek Yesh Bekhor. Rabbi Yirmiyah initially says that if one Yosef ben Shimon bought a field from another, a creditor can collect from that field from whichever direction one views the matter. Rava objects that a person's property is only his guarantor. The underlying issue is that a document naming merely Yosef ben Shimon does not identify from its own text which person undertook the obligation. Even if witnesses later know which man signed, the instrument itself lacks internal identification and is therefore not a valid obligation document. The same is true of a deed of acquisition. Rabbi Yirmiyah thought that although the personal debtor cannot be identified from the instrument, a particular field later acquired by one Yosef ben Shimon can nevertheless be bound because the lien on that field can be identified from the instrument. Rava rejects this because property is only guaranty for the debtor. If no personal obligation was ever created by the defective instrument, the property lien cannot exist independently. Accordingly, a guarantor named in such a document can likewise be exempt even where his own guaranty is identifiable, because the principal borrower never became liable through the instrument. This applies where the guarantor does not know that actual money was lent and his entire liability would arise only through the defective instrument. If the guarantor admits that actual money was lent to the particular borrower, he is certainly liable even if that borrower denies it. Likewise, the implication in seif 7 that there can at least be an oral loan applies where witnesses testify directly to the actual loan. This resolves the Shach.
