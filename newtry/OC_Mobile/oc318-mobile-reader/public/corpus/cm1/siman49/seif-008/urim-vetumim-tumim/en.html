But if they are distinguished in the document, and so forth. Tur wonders where Ramah derived this law. The author of Mishpat Binyamin answers that he interprets the Mishnah's question, "What should they do?" as advice to prevent one Yosef ben Shimon from being harmed by a receipt issued by the other. The solution is to distinguish the parties in the original documents, so that an undistinguished receipt will not defeat them. Shach rejects the proof: perhaps the Mishnah's advice is simply that both the note and the receipt should contain the identifying distinctions. According to what I wrote above in seif katan 10, Tosafot in Bava Batra hold that witnesses do not ordinarily investigate whether there are two same-named people. If so, to whom is the instruction "what should they do? distinguish them" addressed with respect to the receipt? The lender who is being paid has no incentive to protect the other same-named creditor. The borrower would actually prefer a broad receipt capable of defeating both. And the witnesses themselves may not know that two Yosef ben Shimons exist. This strengthens Mishpat Binyamin's reading. Nevertheless, another difficulty remains. R. Yirmiyah interprets the subsequent case of a found receipt as referring to a receipt found in the lender's possession, not one in the borrower's. If so, his discussion is not about the borrower's ability to use a receipt against two same-named lenders at all. How can the earlier phrase "what should they do?" be interpreted as advice for that different problem? More naturally, as Rashbam explains, "what should they do?" means: if same-named parties wish to lend to one another or to another person, they should add identifying markers. Abaye can be explained similarly. Why then invent a new law from wording that does not compel it? Were I explaining Ramah himself, I would suggest that he disagrees with Rashbam's premise that a borrower can say, "I deliberately took a single receipt from one of you because it would serve against both," which Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 33, finds surprising. One would expect a receipt to say explicitly, "the notes of both Yosef ben Shimons are paid." Rashba defends Rashbam by saying that otherwise the Gemara should have answered that authorization permits collection. Maggid Mishneh and Gidulei Terumah discuss this. Yet Rashba's proof can be answered. Abaye, who gives the explanation that the receipt was found with the borrower, also holds earlier that the dispute whether identical-name parties can collect from others depends on whether letters are acquired by delivery. Even if documents are acquired by delivery, the baraita refusing collection can follow the view that writing and transfer are required. If so, even authorization would not always help against a receipt saying generally, "the notes of Yosef ben Shimon are paid." The borrower can claim that every note presently held by the second Yosef ben Shimon originally belonged to the first and was later transferred to him. What good is authorization from the second? The borrower says, "All of those notes were owned by the first Yosef ben Shimon, and he gave me a receipt releasing them before he transferred them." This is especially plausible if the receipt says in general, "all my claims are forgiven," or "my notes are paid," rather than speaking of a single note. Even if the wording uses the singular, that may not be exact. Baal HaTerumot itself asks why the receipt did not say "the notes of both Yosef ben Shimons," not merely why it failed to use the plural. Thus Abaye cannot simply answer that authorization always solves the problem, because in some document wordings it would not. This, however, is only Abaye's framework. We rule like Rava, who does not ordinarily suspect that a note passed from the other same-named person; rather, we apply "here found, here always." Under that approach authorization does help, and Baal HaTerumot's objection returns: if the borrower really paid both, he should have obtained a receipt expressly covering both creditors. Even so, the Gemara did not necessarily spell out every practical variant. There remains a stronger point. Even according to Abaye, if the original notes themselves contain identifying markers, the borrower cannot say that one creditor's note was transferred to the other; the marker shows whose debt each note represents. In that case authorization from both should certainly permit collection despite a generic receipt. If so, the Gemara could have taught at least that much. Ramah's inference may therefore be that once the original notes are distinguished, a later undistinguished receipt cannot defeat them. The burden shifts to the holder of the receipt to prove that it applies to the specifically identified note. This reasoning also offers a possible proof for Ramah from Bava Metzia 19. The Gemara discusses a found receipt and worries that perhaps it was written in Nissan but not delivered until Tishrei, during which interval the ketubah was sold. Abaye answers through his doctrine that the signing witnesses acquire rights immediately. The same answer must underlie the Mishnah's general rule that if the issuer says to return the found receipt, it is returned; Maharsha discusses this in Bava Metzia 13. But the sugya there is established in a context where duplicate names may exist, and Abaye in Yevamot 115 is particularly concerned for another same-named person even when not established. Why, then, may a found receipt be returned? Since the receipt itself was found, Abaye ordinarily suspects collusion. Perhaps the issuer wrote the receipt in Nissan but never delivered it, later decided to give it only now as part of a conspiracy against another Yosef ben Shimon who also holds a note. One cannot simply say that he could issue a new receipt now, because circumstances may have changed. The other same-named creditor may meanwhile have sold his note, in which case a new receipt would not defeat the purchaser if we reject Shmuel's power of forgiveness after assignment. But an old receipt dated Nissan would predate the sale and could defeat the purchaser. Or perhaps the other same-named creditor died between Nissan and now. A new receipt issued by the surviving creditor could not release the heirs' note, while an old receipt from the lifetime of the deceased might be used to create that false impression. Since the receipt was found and thereby impaired, why do we not suspect such collusion? Ramah's law answers this. Chazal instructed same-named creditors to distinguish their notes. If the other creditor did so, an undistinguished receipt cannot harm him. If he failed to do so, he brought the danger upon himself by ignoring the protective arrangement established by Chazal. Beit din therefore need not withhold returning an otherwise valid receipt merely to protect someone who could have protected himself by distinguishing the original document. This makes Ramah's ruling compelling. One might ask from the Mishnah concerning found divorce documents, slave emancipation documents, gifts, and receipts, which Rabbah and R. Zeira establish where duplicate names are known. If a receipt is found, why not return it under Ramah's principle? If the underlying note was not distinguished, the creditor harmed himself; if it was distinguished, the generic receipt cannot defeat it. The answer is that witnesses may later come and identify directly the borrower against whom the note was made. In that event the note could be collected even without a written identifying marker. A generic receipt found later could then improperly defeat that direct testimony. Therefore the receipt still cannot automatically be returned. The same reasoning applies to the other documents in their respective configurations. Ramah's law therefore appears well grounded. This also illuminates Rashbam's statement on the Mishnah, "What should they do? If they wish to lend to one another, to borrow from another person, or to purchase land, they should distinguish their names." Why does he mention purchasing land? If a deed of sale itself is in the buyer's possession, the buyer should own the field just as a lender holding a loan note can collect. The need for identification can arise where the land was acquired by money or chazakah and the deed serves only as evidence. Such an evidentiary sale document is not necessarily transferable even by writing and delivery. Under Rashbam's own view that documentary rights can be acquired by mere delivery, the Mishnah's statement that same-name holders may collect from others can be explained without relying on the presumption "here found, here always": even if the note came from the other same-named person, delivery transferred it. That logic works for a loan note or a constitutive sale deed whose rights can be transferred. It does not work for a mere evidentiary instrument that itself transfers nothing. The seller could say, "You are not my purchaser; the other Yosef ben Shimon bought the land and later handed you his evidence, which does not transfer ownership." Therefore Rashbam advises adding identification in evidentiary deeds as well. According to our ruling, however, which does not follow Rashbam that documentary rights pass by mere delivery and instead relies on the presumption "here found, here always" when same-name parties collect from others, even an evidentiary sale document should ordinarily support the person who holds it. The same possession presumption applies. Thus the need for special identification in such a sale record is reduced according to our halachic framework. One further point requires consideration. According to Rosh and Rashbam, the borrower may say to two same-named creditors, "I paid you both and deliberately took a single receipt because it was sufficient against both." If so, the Gemara's rule validating a postdated document because receipts are written without dates seems difficult according to Abaye. Abaye himself is concerned for duplicate names. Suppose Reuven borrowed from one Yosef ben Shimon in Nissan, and the document was postdated to Tishrei. He also borrowed from the second Yosef ben Shimon in Nissan. In Iyar he repaid both and, relying on Rashbam's rule, took a single undated receipt from one of them to serve against both. In Tammuz the second creditor died. In Cheshvan the first creditor and the heirs of the second both present their notes. The debtor now faces an unavoidable problem created by the postdating. If he says the undated receipt was issued after Tishrei so as to defeat the postdated first creditor's note, then it could not have been issued by the second creditor, who had already died, and therefore cannot defeat the heirs' note. If he says the receipt was issued while the second creditor was alive, then it necessarily preceded Tishrei and cannot defeat the first creditor's postdated note. He will have to pay one debt again despite having paid both. Tosafot repeatedly assume that Abaye accepts the institution of writing receipts, apparently from Rava. This difficulty can nevertheless be resolved, and I discuss it elsewhere.
