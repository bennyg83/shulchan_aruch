But if the names are tripled in the deeds, etc. The Beit Yosef defends the Ramah, and the Darkei Moshe says that since the loan deeds contain the third identifying names but the receipt does not, each creditor may reject the receipt and its holder must prove which debt it covers. Masat Binyamin, siman 95, argues at length for that position. In my view the Tur is correct that the receipt holder prevails. Masat Binyamin's proofs fail. Abaye's interpretation in Bava Batra 172, followed by the Mishnah's instruction that the parties add third names, means that they should add them in both deed and receipt. Nor does the claimed danger make lending impossible. We do not presume wicked collusion. Witnesses writing a receipt know that two residents share the same name and will add the third identifiers. If they omit them, they presumably know that both debts were paid and intentionally write a general receipt usable against either creditor. There is no concern that one Yosef son of Shimon will give a handwritten receipt, because his handwriting identifies him. The receipt witnesses can also later clarify for which creditor they wrote. Remote concerns that they may die or travel overseas do not justify changing the law, and a fraudster cannot rely on events outside his control. A lender concerned about fraud may lend only to trustworthy borrowers or against security. The Sema says the Derishah supplied a strong reason for the Tur. The Bach, Giddulei Terumah, and She'erit Yosef also tend toward the Tur. This is the primary ruling. See responsa Maharash Kohen, book 3, siman 93. [Citation reference: 50.]
