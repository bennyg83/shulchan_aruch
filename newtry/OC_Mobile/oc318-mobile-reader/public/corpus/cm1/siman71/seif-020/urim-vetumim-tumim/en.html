I would add that the dispute follows directly from the earlier disagreement about one witness. Raavad infers from the Mishnah's clause "I have no vow or oath against you" that ordinary credibility does not defeat a one-witness oath. His proof is that the Gemara identifies the relevant oath as the partial-payment oath rather than the oath generated by one witness. The same inference can be made regarding collection in the debtor's absence. Why not say the husband wrote the exemption precisely because he might later be overseas when the wife collects the ketubah? That is at least as foreseeable as partial payment. If the Mishnah nevertheless does not identify that case, perhaps ordinary credibility does not remove the special oath required for collection outside the debtor's presence. Baal HaTerumot and Ramban, who follow Raavad regarding one witness, therefore also tend to be strict here. Rosh follows his own opposing theory. He says that when the husband broadly exempted the wife from every oath beit din might impose, the waiver includes all such procedural oaths unless third-party rights intervene. Purchasers are different because the debtor cannot waive someone else's protection. But an oath imposed merely because the debtor is absent is still part of the debtor's own procedural protection, which he can waive. The Gemara's mention of partial payment is only an example from the Mishnah's ordinary sequence, not an exhaustive list. Thus Rosh's position is internally consistent. Above I showed that Mechaber in Even HaEzer siman 98 follows Rosh regarding the breadth of an ordinary oath exemption. If so, Mechaber should not have stated the stricter rule here as though Baal HaTerumot's position were undisputed. The matter remains a real dispute, and the possessor can invoke the relevant major authorities.
