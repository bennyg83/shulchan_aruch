To swear. The Tur explains this by the rule that ambiguity in a document is construed against its holder. Lechem Mishneh questions that the underlying reason elsewhere is that the defendant is in possession, and here too the borrower is in possession. The Shach answers that because the ne'emanut clause is written in the lender's document and the lender is producing it, the ordinary inference is that the borrower granted ne'emanut only to neutralize the document, not for an independent oral claim. The Taz questions why we do not instead say that the borrower granted it only against himself because the lender would not dare lie to his face, as explained in seif 20. He answers that such a reading would damage the borrower more than benefit him, since he might rely on the clause and pay without taking back the document, only to have to pay again to the lender's heirs after the lender dies.
Even. This is the Tur's wording and seems to imply, all the more so, that if the lender died an oath is required. The Shach says that this is not necessarily so. The Rif and Rosh may have spoken only where the lender is alive and asserts a definite claim, whereas after his death the heirs generally assert only uncertainty, upon which an oath is not imposed. Perhaps the Tur's phrase "even during his lifetime" means only where the heirs themselves make a definite claim. The matter requires study.
The heirs. This wording implies that even if the heirs make no claim, beit din claims payment for them. In seif 22, however, the Mechaber cites an opinion that they must pay unless they bring witnesses that their father said the debt was paid. That is the opposing view, and the Mechaber apparently relied on what he wrote there.
Collusion. This does not contradict siman 106, where it may be possible to collect from encumbered property with an unauthenticated document even though it has the status of a document only through the borrower's admission. Here the borrower was granted ne'emanut, weakening the lender's document, as though it had fallen. Alternatively, there the Torah-level rule does not require authentication, so the Sages left the matter at its Torah status. See Responsa Maharshdam 228.
Shevuat heset. The Sema explains that the ne'emanut clause exempts him from a Torah oath. The Bach and Gidulei Terumah elaborate that the Torah oath for a partial admission is based on the reasoning that the defendant would prefer to deny the entire claim but lacks the audacity to do so. Here, because he possesses ne'emanut, he does have that audacity. The Sema adds that the reason is not the rule that one does not swear concerning documents, because this is not considered a claim concerning the document itself. The Shach remarks that the latter point is irrelevant, since there the documents themselves are being claimed and their physical bodies are not money, whereas here the debt is being claimed. The Taz and Chacham Tzvi offer other explanations for exemption from a Torah oath.
