But he must swear, etc. The debtor was presumably granted credibility only to nullify the force of the document. The creditor nevertheless retains an oral claim against him, and ambiguities in the creditor's document are construed against its holder. The Tur and the authorities write this. Lechem Mishneh, at the end of chapter 14 of the laws of loans, questions this from the rule above in siman 42, seif 8, that ambiguities are construed against the holder because the defendant is in possession of the money; here too the debtor is in possession. In my view there is no difficulty. Here the very credibility clause appears in the creditor's document and the creditor is the one producing it. The ordinary construction is therefore that he trusted the debtor only to defeat the written instrument, not to eliminate the creditor's separate oral claim.
Even during the creditor's lifetime, etc. This is the Tur's wording, and it might imply that after the creditor dies the debtor certainly must swear. That is not necessary. The Rif and Rosh explain that a heset oath is required because the credibility clause merely cancels the document, leaving the creditor with the equivalent of an oral claim, "You owe me one hundred," which the debtor denies. That reasoning applies when the creditor is alive and makes a definite claim, but not necessarily after his death, when the heirs ordinarily make only an uncertain claim and one does not swear on an uncertain claim. It is difficult to say that finding the document among their father's papers is equivalent to their saying, "Our father told us that you owe him one hundred," and even such a statement does not generate an oath according to the Rambam and most authorities, as the Tur cites below in siman 75, seif 21. The Rambam's wording in chapter 15 is precise in this respect: he first says that if the creditor stipulated that the debtor shall be believed whenever he says, "I paid," the document cannot be collected from heirs or purchasers, and then says that when the debtor claims payment and the creditor says he did not pay, the debtor takes a heset oath. This sounds like a case where the creditor is alive. On the other hand, the Rambam in chapter 17, law 7, writes that when an heir seeks to collect with a document containing debtor credibility, the debtor takes a heset oath that he paid and is exempt, even if the clause did not expressly extend to heirs. Perhaps that case is one where the heirs themselves make a definite claim that their father was not paid. The Tur's phrase, "even during the creditor's lifetime," may likewise mean that all the more so after his death the debtor swears when the heirs make a definite claim. The matter requires study.
The creditor cannot collect with this document from the heirs, etc. This is the Rambam's wording in chapter 15 of the laws of loans, and it implies that even if the heirs themselves say nothing, beit din advances for them the defense that the debt may have been paid. Baal HaTerumos, at the end of Shaar 57, and the Tur explicitly understand the Rambam this way. The Maggid Mishneh likewise explains that the creditor cannot collect from the heirs because we plead for them every defense that the debtor himself could have raised. This creates a difficulty with the Mechaber, who here copies the Rambam but in seif 22 cites an opinion that the heirs must pay unless they bring witnesses that their father said the debt was paid. That later opinion is the Rosh and plainly disagrees with the Rambam, as explained below in seif katan 50 and as is explicit in the Tur. The Mechaber must therefore have relied on what he writes later.
Nor from purchasers, because we are concerned for collusion. See the responsa of Maharashdam, siman 228. This does not contradict what I write below in siman 106, that an unverified document may perhaps collect from encumbered property on the strength of the debtor's admission even though it is otherwise treated only as an oral claim. Here the debtor was expressly granted credibility, which weakens the document. Alternatively, there an ordinary document does not require authentication on the Torah level, and the Torah level status can therefore remain operative.
He takes a heset oath. The credibility clause exempts him from a Torah oath, as stated by the Maggid Mishneh and Sema. The Bach and Gidulei Terumah explain more fully that the Torah oath of partial admission ordinarily rests on the assumption that a debtor would like to deny the entire claim but lacks the brazenness to do so. Here, because he was granted credibility, he is willing to deny and therefore the ordinary rationale for the Torah oath is absent. The Sema adds that the exemption is not based on the rule that one does not take oaths concerning documents, because once the debtor has credibility this is no longer treated as a document claim in that sense. I do not understand the relevance of the Sema's reference to the rule concerning oaths on documents above in siman 66 and below in simanim 88 and 95, where the claim concerns the documents themselves and their bodies are not monetary property. Perhaps there is a textual error.
