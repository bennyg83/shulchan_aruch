It is effective even against heirs. See Taz, who asks that perhaps the borrower trusted him only in his own presence, since a person will not be so brazen before him, as Sema writes in seif katan 20. One cannot answer merely that since the borrower was willing to leave such a note in the lender's possession because he relied upon the ne'emanut, the instrument no longer has the status of a note at all. Although many authorities hold that even a note that cannot be collected from purchasers lacks full status as a note, here the note likewise cannot be collected from purchasers. Yet this remains difficult according to the opinion cited in seif 22, which holds that if the borrower dies the lender does collect from the heirs, proving that it does retain the status of a note. Taz's own answer is very strained. Rather, the authorities compare ne'emanut to a litigant's statement, "My father is trusted by me," as the Tur explains in siman 22. It is therefore like qualifying a relative to serve as a witness. Where one accepts his father as a witness, that witness is certainly believed even after the litigant dies and against his heirs, for he has the full legal force of testimony. We do not say that he was trusted only in the litigant's presence. The same applies here. For this reason it also appears that even within the due date the borrower cannot collect on the strength of the ordinary presumption that a person does not repay before maturity. One cannot say that the ne'emanut was granted only to nullify the note, and that the claim should at least be no weaker than an oral loan within its term. Ne'emanut instead gives the trusted person the force of a qualified witness, and just as one who accepted his father as a witness could be defeated even within the due date, so too here. This also explains why the Rosh obligates the borrower when the lender who had been trusted dies. The borrower had qualified only that lender as a witness. Once he dies, that testimony is absent and the instrument reverts to the law of an ordinary note. The reason ordinary ne'emanut of the lender does not operate against the borrower's heirs is different. Many authorities follow Abba Shaul, who says, "What can I do, for they said that one who comes to collect from orphans may collect only with an oath," even if the deceased expressly exempted him, because the exemption is not strong enough to uproot the enactment of Chazal. Even authorities who disagree and hold that an express exemption works nevertheless agree that a general clause of ne'emanut was not intended to uproot that enactment. It merely nullified the borrower's own defenses in the face of the lender's credibility, as though before witnesses, but it did not abolish an oath instituted by Chazal. The Mishnah indeed lists together an oath imposed outside the debtor's presence and an oath required against heirs. For the same reason, one who admits part of the claim is exempt from a d'oraisa oath, despite Shach's difficulty in seif katan 19. Under the explanation above, ne'emanut gives the trusted party the actual force of witnesses, as in one who says, "My father is trusted by me," and witnesses certainly exempt from a d'oraisa oath. The reason a shevuat heset may still be imposed is that there is a migo concerning the very scope of the ne'emanut, as explained in seif katan 1. A d'oraisa oath, however, cannot be imposed through such a migo, as Shach explains in siman 296 regarding a claim of "I returned it" with a migo of "it was taken by unavoidable accident." If a lender trusted the borrower in an oral loan, the borrower is certainly exempt even from heset, for otherwise what purpose did the ne'emanut serve? Tumim asks why, where one accepted his father as a witness, he cannot later make him swear after payment, whereas with ne'emanut he may make the trusted party swear after payment. The answer is that the former case concerns acceptance of the father after a dispute has already arisen, which is like accepting that person's ruling for the entire dispute, as Tosafot explain in Bava Metzia 74. It is as though he was granted the force of witnesses for that entire case. Here, however, the borrower granted ne'emanut whenever the lender says, "I was not repaid." Unless he added the word "forever," the clause is naturally limited to claims of repayment. If, after payment, the lender makes a new demand, he is then effectively asserting a new loan, and the original ne'emanut does not cover that claim.
