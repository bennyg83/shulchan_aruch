At the time of the loan. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 9, who questions the law of a credibility clause in a deposit. According to the Nimukei Yosef and Mordechai, cited by the Shach, credibility given at the time money is drawn takes effect because the recipient then decisively binds himself, similar to a kinyan. The Ran writes similarly in his novellae to Zeh Borer. Perhaps this applies specifically to a loan, where the lien begins immediately when the money is received and the debtor simultaneously binds himself to the credibility clause without a separate kinyan. With a deposit, however, there is initially no monetary lien, which arises only upon an accident creating liability. Even according to the view that liability relates back to the time of receipt, the Ritva explains that this means only that once the accident occurs, the liability is deemed retroactive. Regarding a later claim that the deposit was returned, there is no retroactive lien at all. Since the only original undertaking was to guard the item, granting the depositor credibility to deny a claim of return may therefore be mere words and require a kinyan. On the other hand, according to Baal HaTerumot, who explains that no kinyan is required for credibility at the time of a loan because of the benefit the debtor receives when the creditor trusts him, that rationale may apply equally to a deposit, just as it applies to a gratuitous custodian who undertakes added liability. This requires study.
But after the loan. See Baer Heitev through the statement that this is not the view of the Taz, who holds that in every case, even an ordinary credibility clause, does not bind after the loan without a kinyan. This is straightforward. Atzei Almogim, Maamar Kaddishin, and Misgeret HaShulchan did not reproduce the point accurately.
