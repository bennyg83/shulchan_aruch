Seif 5. He may return and make a claim. Suppose a note contains broad creditor credibility regarding all matters of the note, and the note arose from a sale of merchandise. The debtor later produces one witness or two witnesses that the merchandise had a latent defect that he could not have discovered earlier. Because the claim on the note is procedurally prior, and because one who comes first is heard first, the creditor's credibility can require the debtor first to pay, or at least deposit, the amount stated in the note. Afterward, however, the debtor may swear on the basis of one witness or recover on the basis of two witnesses regarding the defective merchandise. This is particularly so where the note states that the debtor received cash in good currency. Even if both parties admit, or witnesses establish, that the note actually arose from merchandise, the wording operates as an acknowledgment requiring payment on the note first. Precisely because the writing treats the transaction as cash, the later dispute about the merchandise is not literally a matter "of the note," so the general credibility clause does not necessarily govern it afterward. If the note had not recited cash payment, the holder might actually have had stronger ground to invoke the credibility clause concerning the underlying merchandise. Merchants customarily write that cash was received because this strengthens the instrument in its ordinary form and prevents delaying defenses such as, "I never completed the sale of the merchandise." Even without an express credibility clause, such an acknowledgment has some benefit because it requires payment of the note first. With an express credibility clause there are competing advantages, as explained.
