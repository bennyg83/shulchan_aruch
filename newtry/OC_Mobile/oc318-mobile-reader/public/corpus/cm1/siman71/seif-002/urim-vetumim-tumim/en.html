Shach infers from Mechaber's wording that even ordinary credibility granted to the lender requires a kinyan if it was given only after the loan. Yet for practical halachah Shach distinguishes between ordinary credibility and credibility "like two witnesses," requiring a kinyan only for the latter. Before deciding that question, we must clarify Tur and Shulchan Aruch. Tur in siman 22 cites Ramah that where the borrower says to the lender, "You are believed by me," this works without further act if said at the time of the loan. If said afterward, no kinyan means no binding commitment, because the borrower is accepting a disqualified person, namely the opposing litigant who is personally related to himself, as equivalent to two witnesses. That sounds as though the problem is specifically the double defect: a relative-like litigant accepted with the force of two. Rif and Nimukei Yosef likewise imply that two defects together require a kinyan. If so, Mechaber above in siman 22 follows Rambam that even two defects do not necessarily require a kinyan once the procedural acceptance is complete. Why then does Mechaber require a kinyan here? The same question applies to Maggid Mishneh, who says Rambam appears to agree with Ramah, and to Rashi in Shevuot, who explains the credibility case as occurring at the time of the loan and thereby implies that later credibility would require a kinyan, even though Rashi elsewhere does not treat the double defect as independently decisive. The answer is that Ramah's reason cannot be the double defect alone. Even if the borrower accepted only one disqualified witness as one witness, before final judgment he can ordinarily retract. Here no money has yet been extracted and no final judgment has been completed. Why should the borrower be unable to retract without a kinyan? Ramah must be using the special rule that once a litigant accepts a disqualified witness and that witness actually testifies, the testimony itself can constitute completion of the procedural stage. The Talmudic credibility case appears to involve parties already before beit din. The lender has asserted nonpayment. Once the borrower there says, "I believe him," and the lender states he was not paid, the accepted testimony has already occurred. Ramah then says that because the acceptance was doubly defective, the borrower can still retract unless there was a kinyan. Rambam, however, defines final judgment more strictly: until money is actually extracted, the matter is not fully completed. If so, even an ordinary one-defect acceptance remains retractable before collection unless supported by a kinyan. That is why Maggid Mishneh can say Rambam agrees with the practical need for kinyan here even though Rambam does not adopt Ramah's entire theory of double defect. Rashi and Mechaber can be understood similarly. Thus Mechaber's rule naturally applies even to ordinary credibility granted after the loan: before final execution, the borrower can retract unless the undertaking was strengthened by kinyan. Now to Tur's apparent contradiction. In siman 22 Tur says Rosh disagrees and does not require kinyan for this credibility arrangement, while here Tur seems to follow Ramah. The answer is that Rosh's Talmudic case is best understood as a formal note, not an oral loan. Rosh says that when the borrower believes the lender like two witnesses, the effect is that he disqualifies all contrary witnesses. If the lender already holds a formal note, then once the contrary payment witnesses are neutralized, the lender wins on the strength of his document. His own word functions only like one witness assisting the note or removing the oath. That is not equivalent to allowing one litigant personally to extract money from an oral borrower by his own testimony. In an oral loan, once the ordinary debtor defense of payment is present, merely treating the lender as one witness would not extract money. The borrower would have to empower him at the level of two witnesses or otherwise create a binding monetary undertaking. That is a more severe departure and requires kinyan when undertaken after the loan. This explains why Tur can follow Rosh in the formal-note case of siman 22 and still require kinyan here in an oral-loan context. It also explains Rif's proof structure. Rif derives from the case of "believed like two" that a disqualified person can be accepted with the force of two witnesses. In an ordinary formal note, mere credibility can be understood as only one-witness force because the note does the rest. Ramah, who does not accept the rule that one assisting witness removes the lender's oath, may instead need to understand even ordinary credibility as stronger. For practical halachah, however, the simple rule remains: A credibility clause imposed after the loan is materially stronger than an ordinary evidentiary direction such as "repay only before witnesses." If the borrower is to lose his normal payment defense merely on the lender's own word, a kinyan is generally required unless the agreement was made as part of the original loan. One might still ask why a later instruction "do not repay except before witnesses" can sometimes work without kinyan. The answer is that such an instruction does not make the lender unconditionally credible. If the borrower produces proper payment witnesses, he still wins; and even if the named witnesses later die, some formulations allow him to explain what happened. The lender's post-loan credibility is therefore not absolute. A true credibility clause is different: unless the borrower affirmatively produces disqualifying proof, the lender's word itself controls. That stronger legal commitment requires kinyan when added later. Accordingly, Shach's broad statement that ordinary credibility never needs kinyan after the loan is not clear. Mechaber's stricter reading has substantial foundation.
