After the loan. The Tumim asks why this differs from a stipulation, “Do not repay me except before witnesses,” which does not require a kinyan. In my opinion the distinction is as follows. Ne'emanut requires the lender's own later statement, because if the lender dies, the ne'emanut does not automatically pass to his heirs. The borrower trusted only the lender's assertion, “I was not paid.” For that reason a kinyan is required, since the arrangement resembles an asmachta. The borrower may have relied on the assumption that the lender would not falsely say in his presence that he had not been paid. Every asmachta similarly involves a commitment contingent on a future event. By contrast, if the lender says, “Do not repay me except before witnesses,” then even if the lender dies, the borrower is not believed to say that he paid, because he violated the warning. The force of that rule does not depend on a future statement by the lender. It is merely a warning, and the borrower is disbelieved because of the chazakah that he would not have violated it. Therefore no kinyan is required. The Tumim further asks from the rule of “My father is accepted upon me as a judge,” where a litigant cannot retract after the father has already testified, whereas ordinary ne'emanut is ineffective without a kinyan even after the trusted party has already made his statement in beit din. The Tumim overlooked Tosafot in Bava Metzia 74a, who write that there the arrangement is effective because the parties already have an existing dispute and are deemed to have accepted that this is how their case will be adjudicated. Their words show that accepting one's father is effective without a kinyan only after a dispute has arisen between them. If one says, “My father is accepted upon me,” before any contradiction or dispute has arisen, it is ineffective without a kinyan. The same therefore applies to the ne'emanut here. The Tumim also asks, according to the view that ne'emanut is effective without a kinyan, why it differs from accepting one's father as a judge, where one may retract before final judgment. This too is no difficulty. There the acceptance is made after the relevant payment or event has already occurred, while here it is made before repayment. It is as though he says, “Do not repay me without a receipt or witnesses, and if you do, I will be believed against you,” as the Shach writes in the name of the Ran.
