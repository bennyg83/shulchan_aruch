A credibility clause does not help. Responsa Rosh writes that if the borrower says, "Why should my document lie here for nothing? Let the creditor swear that I did not pay him," the creditor must swear even though the document contains a credibility clause. Likewise, if the creditor says, "Let the borrower swear that it was paid, and if he does not I will collect without an oath," the borrower must swear. If each insists that the other swear, they should compromise. The Shach asks that this matters only according to the view that a document held by a third party does not create a concern of collusion. According to the earlier opinion in siman 65 that collusion is feared even in a third party's hands, the document may not be returned even if both parties agree, so what practical difference does the present rule make? There are several differences. First, a document containing no property warranty may be returned according to all opinions, because it is no worse than an ordinary fallen document of that type. Second, the rule against return for fear of collusion applies where the borrower does not actually pay the creditor now and merely asks that the document be returned. The concern is that they are conspiring to injure purchasers or others. But if the borrower offers immediate cash payment, or the creditor orders the third party to return or destroy the document without receiving payment, the concern falls away. The creditor could always issue a receipt, as explained above. Thus the borrower may say: "There is no reason my document should remain indefinitely in the third party's hands. Let the creditor swear. If he swears, I will immediately pay him in cash; if he does not, let the third party tear the document." There is then no possible collusion. The creditor responds that he should not have to swear because the document contains a credibility clause. The ruling teaches that because the document's status has been weakened by being held in this uncertain fashion, the credibility clause no longer exempts him from the oath. The Rosh's discussion of the dispute about collusion concerns only a different case in which the borrower does not offer immediate payment and instead merely asks that the document be returned.
