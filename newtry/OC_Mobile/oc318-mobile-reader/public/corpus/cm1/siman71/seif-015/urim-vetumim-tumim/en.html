Specifically where he said so, and so forth. Rama uses this qualification to reconcile the earlier rule that a post-loan credibility agreement requires a kinyan. In my opinion, "kinyan" there is not exclusive. A written undertaking can be equally effective because the force of a formal document strengthens and memorializes the obligation. There is a proof from Tosafot Ketubot 86. The Mishnah says that if the husband writes to his wife, "I have no vow or oath against you," she is exempt from certain oaths. The Gemara applies the clause to a wife who later became his guardian and asks: how could he have known at the time of the original writing that she would later become a guardian? Tosafot ask why the condition could not have been added afterward, once she became guardian. At first glance Tosafot's question is difficult. The Mishnah says only "he writes," not that he made a kinyan. At the time of marriage, writing can operate as part of the original marital undertaking, much like a condition made at the time of a loan. But after the marriage obligation already exists, a new waiver of oath should resemble a post-loan credibility term and require a kinyan. Why would Tosafot assume mere writing later is enough? The answer must be that a formal writing itself has the strength of kinyan for this purpose. By creating a document expressly binding himself to the waiver, the husband makes a serious enforceable undertaking. This fits Tur and Shulchan Aruch here and above, where they say a handwritten credibility clause can be effective without distinguishing whether it was written at the time of the loan or afterward. Rivash siman 494 also indicates that a written ketubah undertaking has kinyan-like force. Accordingly, the rule should be understood as follows: A post-loan oral credibility agreement requires kinyan. A properly written undertaking can itself supply the necessary formality and does not necessarily require a separate kinyan.
