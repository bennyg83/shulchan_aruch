Baal HaTerumot and Tur explain that they may say, "We signed concerning this, but not concerning that." The Shach asks: according to the opinion above in siman 43 that witnesses cannot make such a distinction where their handwriting is independently authenticated, why are they believed here against an explicit credibility clause? This is a strong question. One could answer, with difficulty, that since scribes often insert standard credibility clauses without express instruction from the lender, and those clauses can be treated as standard form, the possibility of witness error is stronger than in ordinary testimony. Yet this remains difficult and requires study.
Since there is no universal custom. This language explains why the standard-form doctrine does not automatically apply. Where every person in the city writes a particular clause, one who orders an ordinary deed can be presumed to intend all the standard provisions, whatever they are. Where only some people write them and others do not, no such presumption exists. This is correct as practical law. The Shach adds that even where everyone customarily writes the credibility clause, if the borrower did not know of that custom the clause should not bind him. See what I wrote above in siman 61 at length. This is especially persuasive regarding credibility. The theory behind such a clause is that because the borrower knows the lender is strict and will be believed, we infer that he would not repay without obtaining a receipt. If the borrower did not know the clause would be inserted, that reasoning does not apply. The Shach's view is therefore compelling.
