If the witnesses themselves, etc. Even where the note says, “We made a kinyan from him concerning everything written above,” it is doubtful that the witnesses are believed to say that the kinyan did not include the credibility clause. The Tur relies on the Yerushalmi that witnesses may say, “We signed regarding this but not regarding that.” Yet Ramban explains that passage only where their signatures are not independently authenticated; where they are authenticated, they may not retract. The Mechaber and Rama elsewhere cite this view. Moreover, even authorities who sometimes accept such testimony do so where scribes commonly err, not where the note expressly states that the kinyan covered everything above. If the signatures were not independently authenticated, the witnesses would plainly be believed by the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. Thus Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Mechaber can be understood only where local scribes insert credibility language merely as ornamental formula and not with intent that it take effect. Otherwise, once the note and final kinyan clause include it, the witnesses cannot exclude it.
Since it is not a universally established custom, etc. The Beit Yosef distinguishes between a place where every note routinely contains a credibility clause and a place where only many notes do. This is difficult, for Baal HaTerumot and the Tur expressly say that the scribes regularly write it in all notes. His second answer, that the Rosh spoke where the witnesses were unavailable to say that the debtor did not instruct them, is also unconvincing. If the custom itself shows that the debtor intended the customary terms, the witnesses' statement that he gave no express instruction should not matter. Gedulei Terumah and Bedek HaBayit distinguish instead between a custom to insert the clause merely as ornamental wording, without intent that it be enforceable unless the parties expressly approve it, and a custom to insert it as a binding term. This distinction is correct. Yet the primary resolution is that the Rosh's rule applies only where it is known that this debtor was aware of the custom. If he knew that all local notes included the clause, his undertaking is measured by that custom, and the clause may be treated as written even if omitted. If his awareness is not established, the customary wording does not bind him. This reconciles Rav Hai's statements and the rulings of Ritva, Nimukei Yosef, Maggid Mishneh, Maharshal, and Mahari ibn Lev.
