Here. The Tur writes that if the witnesses are not present, the borrower cannot invalidate the clause, since the document states "and we made a kinyan from him concerning all of the foregoing." If the witnesses are present, however, there is no problem of retracting testimony, because they can say that they signed concerning the other provisions of the document. See siman 29. The Shach writes that other authorities disagree and hold that where the witnesses' signatures can be authenticated independently, they are not believed to say that they did not sign concerning the ne'emanut clause.
Established custom. If the custom is universally established, it cannot be said that the clause spread merely as ornamental boilerplate. Rather, the custom itself demonstrates that parties truly grant one another such ne'emanut. As explained in siman 61, seif 5, one who accepts a kinyan generally does so subject to established custom, and scribes may include provisions ordinarily written even if the borrower did not expressly accept them. Where the custom is not universal, however, even if many people use the clause, that inference is unavailable. Sema. The Taz questions this from the Tur's wording that it is "customarily written in all documents," and explains that perhaps ne'emanut required explicit stipulation whereas other clauses were normally inserted merely to improve the document, and the witnesses mistakenly treated this clause in the same manner. See the Shach there.
Explicitly. The Sema explains that this means the document states in explicit language, "and we made a kinyan," and also states that the ne'emanut was expressly agreed between lender and borrower before the witnesses. All the more so where it expressly states that the kinyan itself included the ne'emanut.
