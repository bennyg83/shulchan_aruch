He acknowledged before us. See Shach, seif katan 8, and his reference to the siman called “two half moons.” His meaning requires explanation. The Tur first records two rulings. First, he cites Ri Migash that if the borrower trusted the lender as two witnesses, the lender may be sued after collection if witnesses later establish that payment was made. The Tur then records a second rule, namely that the witnesses are disbelieved only regarding payment, but are believed regarding an acknowledgment by the creditor. He cites the Ramban, who disagrees and holds that they are not believed even regarding acknowledgment. The Tur then writes, “My father the Rosh wrote like Ri Migash.” The Shach understands these words as I have explained. In my opinion, however, the Tur's statement that the Rosh wrote like Ri Migash refers only to the first rule, that the creditor can be sued after payment made in the presence of witnesses. It does not refer to the Tur's subsequent distinction between payment and acknowledgment. The phrase “specifically,” introducing that distinction, is itself part of Ri Migash's position, as the Bach explains. The Bach adds that the Rama likewise understood it this way. The Shach objects that in Darkei Moshe the Rama writes the opposite. The version of Darkei Moshe before us follows the Bach, while the Shach evidently had a different version, which reads in substance: “This requires study, for what relevance do the words of Ri Migash have here?” The Darkei Moshe means that the Tur's distinction between payment and acknowledgment need not be part of Ri Migash's statement. If so, Ri Migash may agree that witnesses are not believed regarding acknowledgment, in accordance with the Ramban. The Tur, however, after citing the Ramban's view that witnesses are not believed even regarding acknowledgment, writes that the Rosh follows Ri Migash, apparently treating the Rosh's adoption of Ri Migash's rule as relevant to the dispute about acknowledgment. The Darkei Moshe therefore questions how the Tur can infer from the Rosh's acceptance of Ri Migash's rule about suing after repayment that the Rosh also disagrees with the Ramban regarding acknowledgment. The Darkei Moshe then answers, “Therefore it appears,” meaning that the Tur's statement that the Rosh wrote like Ri Migash means that the Tur inferred the Rosh's position from the Rosh's unqualified citation of Ri Migash concerning the creditor's inability to retain a second payment. Since the Rosh did not distinguish between acknowledgment and payment, the Tur inferred that the Rosh likewise saw no such distinction. The Shach then questions this from the abridged rulings of the Rosh, which explicitly state that witnesses are believed regarding acknowledgment. There is a textual error in the Shach there, and the text should read approximately: “They are not believed except where he acknowledged before beit din,” and so forth.
For he disqualified them only regarding payment. See Tumim, who writes that he is greatly astonished by this ruling because it appears contrary to reason. Payment, acknowledgment, and waiver all concern the same underlying discharge. In my opinion the reason is as follows. The borrower granted the lender ne'emanut specifically regarding payment because the borrower had a clear means of protecting himself: he could refrain from paying without obtaining a receipt in the lender's handwriting. If he fails to do so, he is not believed even if he pays before witnesses. By contrast, regarding an acknowledgment or waiver by the lender, the borrower has no comparable practical means of ensuring that the event occurs differently. Therefore he did not disqualify witnesses as to those matters. Those poskim who treat acknowledgment as a form of waiver thus hold that witnesses remain valid regarding it. Those who disagree regarding acknowledgment, even though they agree regarding an express waiver, hold that because the borrower says truthfully that he paid, and at the time of payment he had the ability to obtain a handwritten receipt but failed to do so, the witnesses are disqualified as to what followed from that payment. This is especially so according to the Tumim's explanation that the core ne'emanut was not actually intended to contradict the witnesses, but to allow the creditor to appropriate the payment to another debt and claim that the money was for a different obligation, as the Shach writes in seif katan 17. See Shach, siman 98, seif katan 11, that where a borrower paid a creditor but did not take back the instrument, it is treated as known that another debt exists between them. This logic applies where witnesses saw the payment. But if the lender later acknowledged before witnesses, independently of the act of payment, the logic runs in the opposite direction. Why would the lender volunteer such an acknowledgment before witnesses, and what compelled him to do so, unless the debt was truly paid? According to this, testimony to an acknowledgment is effective only if the witnesses heard it after the payment had already been made. If they heard the acknowledgment at the time of payment, the lender may have been compelled to acknowledge in order to induce payment and can still claim that the money was allocated to another debt.
