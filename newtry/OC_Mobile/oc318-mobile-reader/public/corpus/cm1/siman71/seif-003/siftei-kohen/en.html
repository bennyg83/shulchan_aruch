But if they testified, etc. This ruling requires study. Rabbi Yeshayah, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Baal HaTerumot, Ran, and Maggid Mishneh maintain that witnesses whom the debtor disqualified concerning payment are also disqualified to testify that the creditor admitted payment, unless the admission was before beit din. The Tur's attribution of the opposing view to Rambam and Rosh is not conclusive. The language of Rambam, that the debtor’s remedy is to have the creditor cancel the note or acknowledge before others that he received the debt, can readily mean an admission before beit din, which all agree is effective. The Rashba expressly rules that even if witnesses testify that the creditor admitted payment before them, the creditor remains believed as two witnesses, unless he admitted before beit din. He states that this was the accepted ruling and practice. This also appears to be the view of Maharshal and Mahardam. Therefore, the principal ruling is that an out of court admission proved by witnesses does not overcome the stipulated credibility, whereas an admission before beit din does.
For he disqualified them only regarding payment, etc. If the wording of the credibility clause clearly shows that the creditor was also believed concerning admission or similar matters, he is believed in all of them. Every monetary condition is valid. But the condition must certainly include the matter in question. Where its scope is doubtful, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.
Likewise, if they testified that he waived, etc. Even Rabbi Yeshayah and Ramban agree that witnesses are believed to testify that the creditor waived the debt.
The credibility extends only to that for which he was believed, etc. The Rashba rules that where a deposit note states that the custodian granted the owner full credibility “regarding the principal funds,” this covers claims that the transaction was merely an accommodation or involved interest, but not a later claim of payment. Likewise, a clause making one party credible “in all matters of this note,” as scribes commonly write, concerns the validity and contents of the note, such as whether the stated money was delivered or whether it was an accommodation note. It does not make the creditor credible to deny payment unless that is stated expressly. The Rivash and Mahari ibn Lev agree, and the same principle applies to claims of waiver or extension of time. Mahari ibn Lev remains uncertain where the document contains broad language that it is made with every possible scribal strengthening, for such language may have been intended to encompass payment, waiver, and similar defenses.
