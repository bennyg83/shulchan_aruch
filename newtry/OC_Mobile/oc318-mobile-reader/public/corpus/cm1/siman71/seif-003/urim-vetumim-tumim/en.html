He is correct that the dispute is substantial. Shach also says Rambam is not decisive because when Rambam advises that the lender can protect the borrower by acknowledging before others that the debt was fully paid, perhaps he means an admission before beit din rather than before ordinary witnesses. That is difficult. If Rambam meant beit din, what is added by saying the admission may be made outside the borrower's presence? An admission before beit din is obviously effective whether or not the borrower is standing there. The natural reading is that the lender can acknowledge before ordinary witnesses. Rambam's novelty is that the lender cannot later say, "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy," because the borrower is already in possession of his money and the admission operates against the lender. The commentators, including Mishneh LaMelech, read Rambam this way. Shach also questions Tur's attribution to Rosh of the view that an admission can defeat the credibility arrangement. But Rosh's position is clear from the parallel case where the lender had said, "Do not repay me except before so-and-so and so-and-so." Rosh discusses an episode where a later statement before Rav Nachman was dismissed as mere joking speech. That discussion matters only because a genuine admission would have had legal effect. Otherwise, even a serious admission would be irrelevant. One might argue that the formal "repay before named witnesses" arrangement differs from a full credibility clause. But Rosh himself compares the structures. He says the reason ordinary credibility can work is that the borrower effectively neutralized contrary payment testimony just as one can require repayment before particular witnesses. This strongly suggests that if an express lender admission remains effective in the named-witness case, it should also remain effective in the ordinary credibility case. There is also an intuitive reason. Suppose witnesses saw the borrower hand money to the lender. What stronger admission could there be? If those witnesses merely say, "The lender admitted that the debt was paid," many authorities would accept the admission. Why should their testimony become weaker if they add the concrete fact that they saw the money pass from hand to hand? The only coherent distinction is this: A credibility clause is not based on suspecting witnesses of deliberate lying. Rather, payment transactions can be misunderstood. The borrower may hand over money on one debt while believing he is paying another. Witnesses can easily assume the payment relates to the disputed note when legally the lender applied it elsewhere. That is why the borrower grants the lender special control over payment evidence. A direct, specific admission or release by the lender is different. There is little room for that type of factual confusion. The lender knows exactly which debt he is acknowledging or forgiving. Therefore the credibility clause can neutralize ordinary payment testimony without neutralizing the lender's own unmistakable later admission. This makes good conceptual sense and supports Rosh and Tur.
