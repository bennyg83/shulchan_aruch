The law favors the borrower. See Shach, seif katan 25. The Ketzot HaChoshen writes that there is likewise room for doubt where there is a receipt signed by witnesses. His words are surprising. If the receipt does not identify the instrument and merely states that the witnesses saw a payment, it is certainly no stronger than if the witnesses testified orally to that fact, where the rule is that we neither tear up the instrument nor collect with it, as explained in siman 55. If, however, the receipt identifies the instrument and states that the witnesses saw payment made on that instrument, seizure by the creditor is certainly ineffective. This is precisely how a receipt is properly written when the witnesses do not know the amount stated in the instrument, as explained in siman 54. If seizure could nevertheless prevail, a borrower would never be required to make payment in reliance on such a receipt, because the creditor could seize from him again the next day. It must therefore be that once the receipt identifies the instrument, it is equivalent to the creditor's statement, “The instrument in my possession has been paid,” and the instrument is entirely nullified.
