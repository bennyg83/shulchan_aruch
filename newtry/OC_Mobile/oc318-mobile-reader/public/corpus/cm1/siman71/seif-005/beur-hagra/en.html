(Collected note) Behold... As stated there 88a, "If he is wise..." It follows that although initially the debtor could not impose an oath because of the document, he may impose it after payment. The same applies here. (End of note.)
(Collected note) Even if he paid him... By granting the creditor credibility against two witnesses, the debtor disqualified those witnesses concerning this debt. Otherwise, what benefit would there be in credibility against two? This differs from Kesubos there, where the witnesses themselves were not disqualified. Here, if the debtor expressly trusted the creditor even after payment, he could not impose an oath later, as explained subsequently. Therefore, with ordinary wording, we interpret the clause as applying only initially, so that he cannot impose a stringent oath upon the creditor. But where credibility was granted even against witnesses, the debtor necessarily meant it to continue afterward, because otherwise the clause would accomplish nothing. (End of note.)
Therefore, if. Without such a clause, the debtor could impose upon him an oath through gilgul, whether a lighter or more stringent oath, as stated there 48b. This is unlike the Rosh in HaKosev, siman 18, citing Rabbi Shimshon of Narbonne. See Even HaEzer 98:4 and the Shach.
(Collected note) Therefore... Without the clause, he could impose a lighter oath at the end, and through gilgul even initially a light or stringent oath. At the end too, light and stringent oaths are possible. This is the meaning of the phrase that light and stringent oaths and gilgul apply always, meaning that even afterward the creditor is not protected against them unless the clause expressly says so. The Mishnah there says, "a vow and an oath..." (End of note.)
