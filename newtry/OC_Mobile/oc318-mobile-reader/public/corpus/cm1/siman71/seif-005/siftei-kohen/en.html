Even if he first repaid before witnesses, etc. This is the view of Ri Migash, Ran, and the other authorities, against Rav Hai and Raavad. It applies only where the lender says that the first payment was for a different debt. If he denies receiving it altogether, he must return the money, as explained above.
He has against him only, etc. The Bach challenges this from the rule concerning a ketubah, where a woman who collected once before witnesses and then collected again may be sued for the first payment as a loan. He argues that here too, after the debtor pays a second time, the first payment should become a loan. His suggested answer, that empowering the creditor as two witnesses converts the first payment into a gift or waiver, is not persuasive. The correct distinction is that our case concerns a creditor who claims the first payment was applied to another debt. Since he was empowered as two witnesses regarding payment of this debt, the witnesses are disqualified from establishing that the money paid before them discharged this particular debt. They may still establish that money was delivered and that it was not a gift, but they cannot contradict the creditor’s claim that it was applied elsewhere. This follows the principle of dividing testimony. If he instead claims expressly that the first money was a gift, the witnesses are believed that it was paid toward a debt and he must return it, because his credibility extends only to identifying which debt was paid, not to turning a payment into a gift. The Bach's further objection concerning differing grades of land is also answered. If the first transfer is treated as payment of this very debt, there is no continuing reciprocal indebtedness and no practical difference in exchanging collections. The authorities' argument that such an exchange would be pointless therefore stands.
And an oath imposed by extension, etc. The wording here suggests that an ordinary exemption from an oath does not protect the creditor from an oath imposed by gilgul. Ramban and a Gaon are cited accordingly. The Rosh, however, agrees with Rash that one who was exempted from an oath cannot be required to swear even through gilgul. The Tur and Mechaber themselves rule this way in Even HaEzer 98, as do the French rabbis cited by the Beit Yosef. The matter therefore requires study.
Forever, etc. The Sema writes that even without the word “forever,” empowering the creditor as two witnesses necessarily exempts him from every oath both before and after payment. This is incorrect. Baal HaTerumot expressly distinguishes between a clause exempting the creditor from every oath and one adding the word “forever.” Without “forever,” the creditor is exempt before collection, but after the debtor pays again he may administer a hesset oath concerning the prior payment. Only the added word “forever” exempts the creditor both before and after collection. This applies even where the creditor was empowered as two witnesses. The Tur's text therefore appears deficient and should include “forever.” The word is needed because otherwise the broad exemption may be read merely as standard embellishment of the note and as applying only until collection. Mahari ibn Sasson likewise rules that the result depends upon the word “forever.”
It is effective, for it is as though he said: Do not repay me except before so and so, etc. Rama's comparison requires study. Rashbatz distinguishes two clauses. If the debtor says that unless repayment is recorded on the note he will not be believed, he has effectively made the notation itself the exclusive evidence of payment, like disqualifying all witnesses other than specified witnesses. But if the note merely states that the lender remains credible while the note is un-torn and bears no receipt, the debtor has not necessarily disqualified all witnesses. He has only made the lender more credible than himself. Thus, under ordinary credibility, witnesses to payment defeat the note, and the lender cannot claim that the money was applied to another debt. Baal HaTerumot and the Rosh say this expressly. Rama must therefore mean only that this clause is effective and that the debtor is discharged when he brings witnesses, though it is weaker than an instruction to repay only before specified witnesses.
