He first repaid before witnesses. Bach challenges this rule from Ketubot. There, where one witness says a ketubah was paid, Rav Papa says that if the husband is clever he can bring the matter to a Torah oath by paying her again before the first witness and a second witness, and then using the first payment as a loan against her. This shows that money originally given as payment can afterward be treated as a loan once the obligation was satisfied a second time. The Shach answers that this works where the creditor says the first payment was applied to another debt, not where he completely denies receiving it. But the sugya itself shows that even when the creditor does not deny the first payment, the witness can generate the Torah oath. Rav Ashi asks that she can say there were two ketubot. That answer would make no sense if the creditor had denied ever receiving the first payment. It follows that the first witness remains effective even where the creditor admits receipt but claims the money related to a different obligation. The law therefore cannot be explained merely through dividing the witness's statement. Rather, once the debtor has paid the document a second time, the first money is no longer needed for repayment of that document and can stand as a separate loan claim. The witness who saw the first payment can testify to that transfer even though he originally understood it as repayment. Thus Rav Hai Gaon's position, that the first payment may become an ordinary debt after the second payment, has strong support.
A hesset oath. This is difficult. Two qualified witnesses stand before us and testify that the borrower paid, yet because the borrower himself has disqualified their testimony through the credibility clause he is required to swear. How can beit din accept an oath that, from our perspective, contradicts two valid witnesses? A similar issue appears in siman 30, where one witness says one hundred and another says two hundred, and the claimant does not claim both amounts. The defendant swears although, from the court's perspective, both witnesses at least establish one hundred. There one can say that the claimant has effectively waived the amount he does not claim, so the oath concerns only the remaining disputed sum. But where the defendant swears that he owes nothing even though two witnesses establish at least part, the problem remains. The answer resembles the rules concerning a document claimed to be forged. Torah law presumes ordinary signed testimony valid, yet a defendant may still swear that the document is forged. Monetary presumptions do not necessarily define the subjective truth required for an oath. Likewise, siman 87 rules that one who swore he paid and afterward a document appeared does not automatically become a suspected false swearer, even though the document defeats him monetarily. The oath is evaluated according to his own asserted knowledge. Maggid Mishneh nevertheless proves that one established as a false denier does not take an oath against two witnesses, because beit din cannot administer an oath whose falsity is already established. The difference is that there the witnesses establish the very fact in direct contradiction. Here the borrower contractually granted the creditor superiority over the witnesses for purposes of collection, but did not make the witnesses themselves false. The later hesset concerns the borrower's personal claim after the contractual credibility has been applied. Because he swears to exempt himself rather than to extract money, the oath can still be administered despite the witnesses' ordinary validity. This distinction is also reflected in the authorities regarding one who holds a document but faces opposing evidence.
