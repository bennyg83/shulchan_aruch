But he swears only. Meaning, he swears that he has another debt against the borrower. He does not swear that the borrower failed to pay this debt, because the witnesses testify that this debt was in fact paid. Although the borrower disqualified those witnesses for purposes of the lender's credibility clause, beit din itself does not treat them as false witnesses and therefore will not allow the lender to swear directly against them. Forever. The Sema holds that the word "forever" is not essential. Since the document says that the lender is believed like two witnesses and also says "without any oath," the extra language necessarily means that the exemption continues even after payment. The Shach disagrees and holds that the word "forever" is specifically required. One should not derive such a broad result merely from redundant wording, because the phrase may be standard formulaic language. The Shach and Taz rule this way, and it is primary. See Beurim. If the document says that the lender is believed like two witnesses forever, then even if the lender later completely denies receiving the payment, the borrower cannot sue him after payment:
Unless he expressly stated. According to the Sema, the meaning is as follows. If the document did not expressly provide otherwise, then even where the lender had credibility equal to two witnesses, a handwritten receipt prevents him from later claiming that the payment was for a different debt. Since the receipt itself relates to the debt, the lender's enhanced credibility has ended. If the document expressly stated that no receipt would be effective unless written directly on the debt document, and the borrower instead produces a separate handwritten receipt, then even with ordinary credibility the lender may claim that the payment was for another debt. If the borrower brings witnesses to payment or a receipt signed by witnesses, however, even such an express clause does not help where the lender had only ordinary credibility, unless he had credibility equal to two witnesses. The Shach holds differently. In his view, a handwritten receipt always prevents a claim that the payment was for another debt, while an express clause can defeat even a receipt signed by witnesses. The words "if he brings witnesses" refer only to the earlier case where there was no express clause. He concludes that without an express clause the lender cannot claim another debt where he has only ordinary credibility. But if the document says that the borrower shall not be believed until a receipt is written directly on the document itself, that language functions as an express disqualification of witnesses and the lender may claim that the payment was for another debt.
