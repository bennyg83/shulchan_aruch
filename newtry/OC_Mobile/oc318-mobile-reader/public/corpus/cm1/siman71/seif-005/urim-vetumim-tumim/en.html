He has against him only a hesset oath. Bach asks from Ketubot, where the Gemara says a clever person can convert the matter into a Torah oath by first paying and then suing again with one witness. This shows that even after the first payment the money can still be claimed back. Bach answers that here the original payment is treated as forgiven. Shach objects: if the money is truly forgiven, why does Mechaber above permit a cherem against whoever wrongfully holds the money? There is no theft if the borrower waived it. I do not understand why Shach goes so far afield. The same question exists in the very words before us: why is there a hesset oath at all if the money was already forgiven? One might answer that the waiver is conditional: if the lender swears he was not paid, the original payment becomes waived retroactively, but if he admits the truth before swearing, there is no waiver. Then cherem and hesset would still have purpose. But that cannot be the full explanation. Once the lender swears, the legal waiver would make his statement retrospectively true in the monetary sense. He would never meaningfully swear falsely. That would turn the oath into an empty formality. The better answer is that Ran's reason is not a retroactive monetary waiver but evidentiary disqualification. The borrower empowered the lender and thereby disqualified the contradictory witnesses for this debt. Once those witnesses have been excluded, they do not automatically regain validity merely because the borrower paid under compulsion and later sues again. This differs entirely from the Gemara's one-witness case. There the witness was never disqualified. Initially, before payment, the lender may collect because the claim structure requires only a rabbinic oath or because the underlying lien remains. After payment, however, the borrower now has a fresh monetary claim, and the same one witness can generate a Torah oath against the former lender. The witness remained valid throughout. Here the borrower himself excluded the witnesses from this matter. There is therefore no contradiction. Bach further answers Ran's "why move money back and forth" question by suggesting a difference in the quality of property collected. The lender may initially collect superior property, while the borrower's later recovery would be from ordinary intermediate property. Shach objects that once the later proof establishes the original collection was mistaken, perhaps the borrower should recover the very property previously taken. That issue requires study. In any event, there are many clearer practical differences. If each side has two witnesses, without the special credibility clause the rule is ordinarily that the possessor retains the money. With the clause, the payment witnesses are treated as neutralized and money can initially be extracted. Afterward, if the borrower sues again and the evidentiary structure is treated anew, the original lender may now be the possessor. Or the repayment witnesses may die or become relatives between the first and second proceedings. Their first testimony was ineffective because of the credibility clause, and they may no longer be able to testify again later. Or the lender may owe earlier creditors. Before the borrower pays him, those creditors may have no access to the disputed money. Once payment is made and the borrower has only an ordinary restitution claim, the earlier creditors can take priority. Thus there are many situations where the initial collection is not merely pointless circular movement. Still, on further analysis Bach's waiver theory has one important strength. If the borrower has disqualified the payment witnesses, how can beit din allow the lender to swear that no payment occurred when valid witnesses are physically standing there saying that it did? We may treat their testimony as legally ineffective for collection, but they remain upright witnesses. Are we knowingly administering an oath that contradicts their factual testimony? Even if the lender says, "That money was for another debt," the witnesses may explicitly say it was for this very debt. There is also a difficulty from Shevuot 34 concerning a deliberate false deposit oath. The Gemara assumes that where witnesses prove the underlying obligation, the denial is not a meaningful monetary denial because the witnesses defeat it. But if a litigant could pre-disqualify those witnesses and still swear against them, we would have a case where their testimony does not extract money yet can establish that the oath was factually false, producing punishment. The sugya does not seem to entertain such a structure. This suggests that a special credibility clause may indeed contain a deeper monetary waiver: if the lender denies payment under the agreed framework, the borrower's earlier transfer is treated as ineffective toward that debt. Then the lender's oath is not directly false concerning the legal status of the debt. Yet the problem of the apparently pointless hesset remains. Perhaps the oath is formulated not as "you did not hand me money," but as "I presently hold none of your money wrongfully." If the earlier transfer was legally redirected or waived under the credibility condition, that can be true even if money physically passed. A claim of an older offsetting debt can likewise matter. This remains conceptually difficult. There is another difficulty according to Rif and Ramah, who compare believing the lender "like two witnesses" to accepting a disqualified relative as two witnesses through kinyan. If the accepted relative's testimony has the legal force of two witnesses, how can a later one-witness oath be generated against it at all? It is strained to say the relative has two-witness force only for the first collection but not for subsequent proceedings. The full mechanics therefore still require study. For practical purposes, however, Ran's core rule remains: once the borrower gave the lender special credibility, the borrower cannot simply reverse the first collection through the same evidence he had disqualified.
Unless he stated it explicitly. Rama appears to write this in order to reconcile two responsa of Tashbetz. In one responsum, Tashbetz says that if the agreement was, "If the repayment is not written on the face of the note, you will not be believed," the case resembles one who says, "Do not repay me except before so-and-so and so-and-so." If the debtor pays before other witnesses, the special condition still matters. In another responsum, Tashbetz deals with the ordinary formula, "So long as this note is not torn and no receipt is written upon it, the lender shall be believed." There he says that even according to Rif, who is strict where specific payment witnesses were designated, the phrase "written upon it" is not necessarily literal. Shach struggles to distinguish whether the borrower or lender initiated the condition. As Avodat HaGershuni notes, that distinction is weak. Rama's distinction is better. The standard formula "so long as the note is not torn and no receipt is written upon it" is conventional document language. Responsa Ramban and Maharik explain that "upon it" in such formulas need not mean physically written on the very parchment. A valid separate receipt can satisfy the practical intent. But where the parties expressly stipulate, "If you do not write the repayment on this very note, you will not be believed," that is no longer merely standard drafting. It is a specific condition. A true explicit condition must be fulfilled according to its terms. That is what Rama means by "unless he expressly specified it." Rama then says that if the debtor produces other witnesses, he is believed. Shach seems to understand this as referring to the ordinary nonexplicit formula and objects that if "on the note" is not literal, even the lender's claim that the payment applied to another debt may fail. In my opinion Rama's sentence refers back to the explicit condition. Even where the debtor specifically agreed to document payment on the note, if he nevertheless produces actual witnesses to payment, Tashbetz allows their testimony under the view Mechaber follows. The explicit condition still matters in several ways. If the lender says the witnessed transfer was for another debt and the witnesses do not clearly identify the payment, the condition strengthens the lender. Also, if the original note is no longer in the lender's possession, an ordinary note may lose the "your document is in my hand" presumption and resemble an oral debt. But where the borrower expressly agreed that payment must be documented in a particular way, he still must establish compliance even if the physical note is elsewhere. Maharik likewise treats a specific agreed proof condition more strongly than routine formula language. Sema adds that if the lender was believed like two witnesses, actual contrary witnesses may not help at all. Maharik's own language supports this where the instrument says not merely that the borrower is not believed, but that "neither the borrower nor any other person" is believed to say payment occurred unless the note is judicially torn or a receipt is written. That is stronger than ordinary credibility. It expressly excludes all outside proof. Accordingly, the practical distinctions are coherent.
