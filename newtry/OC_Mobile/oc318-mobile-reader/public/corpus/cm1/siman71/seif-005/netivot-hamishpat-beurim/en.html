And he has against him only an ordinary debt instrument. See Shach, seif katan 13, through his quotation of the Tur and his question why the creditor should merely pronounce a general cherem. The Shach's words are very difficult, because the point is already established from the ruling itself that the creditor must take a hesset oath. If the excess money is treated as a gift, why must he swear at all? The Tumim raises the same objection. There is no difficulty. The ne'emanut means that the legal reality will be as the lender states and acts, even if in truth he lies, so long as the matter is not established otherwise by witnesses or by his own admission. Thus, if he lies and takes money improperly, after the act the money is his as a gift, even though at the moment of taking it he transgressed the prohibition against theft. He is therefore subject to a hesset oath that he was not paid twice. If that oath is false, he is indeed swearing falsely. The fact that after the wrongful taking the money becomes his as a gift does not erase the fact that the original act was an act of theft. He swears according to the intent of beit din regarding the beginning of the transaction, namely that he did not initially take the money wrongfully and that his original assertion was true. Perhaps the prospect of the oath will cause him to withdraw and admit the truth, in which event the gift will not operate retroactively, because once the matter is clarified through his own admission it is comparable to clarification by witnesses. The Shach's objection from a general cherem, however, is sound. As explained in seif 7, one who pronounces a general cherem should not say, “whoever robbed me,” but rather, “whoever presently holds property wrongfully and does not return it.” According to the Bach's approach there is presently no wrongful property in the lender's hands and no obligation of restitution, because after the taking the borrower has given it to him as a gift. The Tumim nevertheless asks how beit din can permit the lender to swear at all. Even if the borrower disqualified the witnesses for his own monetary claim, we did not disqualify them for purposes of determining whether the lender would swear falsely. The witnesses testify that the oath is false. The Tumim adds that even according to the Shach, who limits the lender's ne'emanut to a claim that the payment was for another debt, how can he swear when the witnesses testify that he accepted the money specifically as payment of this debt? I do not understand the question. If it is known that the borrower owes two debts, one evidenced by an instrument and one oral, even if the borrower gives money expressly on account of the written debt and the lender remains silent, the lender may allocate it to the other debt, as explained in siman 83. The reason a creditor ordinarily cannot claim that a payment witnessed by others was for a different debt is only that the alleged second debt is otherwise unknown and he is not believed to assert an additional debt in the face of witnessed possession. But where the borrower trusted the lender as two witnesses, the Shach writes in siman 58, seif katan 11, that it is as though witnesses testify that another debt exists. It is therefore treated as a known debt, and the lender may allocate the payment to that debt if he remained silent. Our case must concern one where the witnesses did not hear the lender acknowledge the purpose of the payment, because if they did, that is an acknowledgment and the witnesses are believed under seif 3. Accordingly, after collection the lender must swear that another debt was owed to him. It appears that even according to the opinion that he is believed without expressly claiming that the payment was for another debt, the rationale is that since he was trusted as two witnesses, his position is treated like two witnesses against two witnesses. Whenever possible we reconcile conflicting testimony even by a strained interpretation, as explained above in siman 30, seif 8 in the gloss. Similarly, in Keritot 13a a person's statement “I did not eat” is reconciled by interpreting it to mean “I did not eat inadvertently, but rather deliberately.” So too here, when the lender simply says, “I was not paid on this debt,” since he was trusted as two witnesses we reconcile his statement by construing it to mean that he accepted the money on another debt and therefore this debt remains unpaid. His oath may likewise be phrased that he was not paid on this debt. But if he expressly claims that he received no money at all in the presence of the witnesses, everyone agrees that the borrower may sue him after paying again. This also removes the question raised from Shevuot 34a. Nevertheless, the passage in Shevuot still requires explanation. The Gemara discusses one who intentionally violated an oath concerning a deposit and was warned, and asks what follows from the formulation. It infers that without warning he would incur the relevant obligation, establishing that denial of a loan supported by witnesses can generate liability. One could ask why the Gemara does not establish the case where the custodian claimed that he returned the deposit and the owner had accepted a relative as equivalent to two witnesses, or where the owner trusted the custodian himself under oath as two witnesses. In those cases the custodian would be exempt from monetary payment because the owner had disqualified the witnesses for monetary purposes, yet the witnesses should remain valid for purposes of a korban or lashes. It appears that the Gemara's wording is precise: it says that he acted intentionally and was warned, not that he later admitted. This implies that the same witnesses alone are sufficient to impose the korban or lashes. If the owner had disqualified those witnesses, however, beit din could not impose monetary liability on their testimony. Where no monetary liability exists, there is no additional fifth or korban, as explained in Pesachim 29a regarding me'ilah. The Ketzot HaChoshen further writes that according to the Shach the same should apply to the case of “if he is clever” in Ketubot 84a, so that a claim of a different debt would not help. I do not understand this. There the creditor was not granted ne'emanut and the witnesses were not disqualified. A claim that the money was for another debt is ordinarily believed only through a migo that the creditor could have denied receiving it at all. On the contrary, siman 58 explicitly states that where the creditor claims one payment was for another debt, he becomes one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear. See Shach there. The Shach's comment on the Bach's answer, that there is a practical difference where one party has intermediate-quality land and the other does not, should be understood as follows. According to the Shach, ne'emanut as two witnesses helps only if the lender claims that the payment was for another debt. The Ran's proof is therefore that the lender can still make that claim after the borrower pays again. If we were to say that after the second payment even the claim of a different debt is unavailable, because the witnesses are then believed that the first payment was specifically for this debt, the lender would certainly have to return the second payment he received, like for like. The Bach's distinction concerning reciprocal collection from intermediate-quality land makes sense only if the Ran's proof concerns an express denial of receipt, where after the second payment the witnesses are believed and we divide the creditor's statement by treating the first funds as a separate loan in his hands. It does not fit if the witnesses are believed after the second payment to establish that the first payment was made on this very debt, in which case the second payment must simply be returned.
Forever. It appears that the same rule applies if the borrower stipulated that the lender would be believed as two witnesses forever. In that case the borrower cannot sue him even after repayment, even where the lender does not claim that the money was allocated to another debt. The borrower expressly trusted him as two witnesses even against a later post-payment claim. For the same reason he also cannot compel the lender to swear after payment.
And if he brought witnesses that he paid him. See Sema, seif katan 13, through his discussion of a separate handwritten receipt. At first glance this is difficult. The Rama concludes that unless the parties expressly stipulated otherwise, the condition must be fulfilled in its precise terms. This would seem to imply that even a handwritten receipt is ineffective, which is difficult, for why should a receipt in the lender's own handwriting not be effective? We must say that the Sema follows his own view in seif katan 21, that where the lender was trusted as two witnesses he can claim that the payment was for another debt even against a receipt in his own handwriting. With ordinary ne'emanut, however, he cannot make that claim. Accordingly, the Rama's words should be understood according to the Sema as follows. If the instrument contains either ne'emanut as two witnesses or ordinary ne'emanut and says merely that it remains effective “so long as no receipt is written upon it,” the intent concerns the debt. Once a handwritten receipt is written for the debt, the ne'emanut ends. Even where the lender had been trusted as two witnesses, he can no longer claim that the payment was for another debt, because the parties granted that heightened credibility only until a receipt was written for this debt. But if they expressly stipulated that the receipt must be written on the instrument itself, the ne'emanut has not ended merely because another receipt exists, and the lender can still claim that the payment was for another debt. The Sema did not explain the case as involving a receipt signed by witnesses, because then the Rama's concluding stipulation would be difficult. If an ordinary receipt signed by witnesses is ineffective, why should oral testimony by those same witnesses be effective, as the Rama states in the gloss? It would be strained to say that the parties disqualified only testimony from writing. The Sema therefore necessarily understood the discussion as involving a handwritten receipt. The meaning is that if the receipt is not written by the lender on the instrument itself, the lender can claim that the money was for another debt. Accordingly, even with ordinary ne'emanut he may make that claim because of the special condition, while with ne'emanut as two witnesses he may certainly do so. But if the borrower brings witnesses that he paid, the lender is not believed to allocate it elsewhere under ordinary ne'emanut. The Sema therefore writes that this last rule applies only to ordinary ne'emanut. Where the lender was trusted as two witnesses, he can certainly claim that the payment was for another debt.
It is effective. See Shach, seif katan 16, who writes that according to the Rama the lender may claim that the payment was for a different debt. If so, the Shach cannot have understood the Rama as the Sema did, according to what I wrote in the preceding seif katan, because according to the Sema the lender necessarily cannot make that claim once the specified receipt exists. The Shach must therefore understand the Rama to mean that even a receipt signed by witnesses is ineffective until a receipt is written on the instrument itself. This creates a difficulty. Why should witnesses nevertheless be effective when the lender does not claim another debt, if the stipulation expressly says that no receipt is effective unless written on the instrument itself? We must say that the Shach understands the Rama's statement, “and if he brings witnesses,” as referring back to the first case, where the parties did not expressly specify this. The Tumim explains it the same way. What forced the Shach to read the gloss that way is that if the clause expressly states that the lender's ne'emanut continues until a receipt is written on the instrument itself, then it effectively disqualifies all ordinary receipts. An ordinary receipt is usually witnessed. If so, the parties have effectively disqualified all witnesses as to payment, and there would be no basis for the Rama to say that witnesses nevertheless suffice. Hence the Rama's statement that witnesses are effective must refer back to the opening case, where the parties did not expressly specify that form. The Shach then properly asks why the lender should not be able to claim that the payment was for another debt. The Shach's answer, given so that the ruling not contradict a responsum of the Tashbetz, shows his understanding in the second explanation. The phrase “until a receipt is written upon it” can mean upon the debt, not literally upon the physical instrument. Therefore, where the borrower granted ordinary ne'emanut to the lender, a receipt signed by witnesses is effective. But where the clause says that “the borrower shall not be believed” until a receipt is written upon the instrument, the language necessarily intends to disqualify witnesses, because without witnesses a borrower is never believed against an outstanding instrument. Since the clause says that the borrower shall not be believed until the receipt is written “upon it,” the intent must be literally upon the instrument, thereby excluding ordinary receipts. If only an ordinary separate receipt exists, the law is as in a stipulation, “Do not repay me except before witnesses.” There is a textual error in the Shach there, and the text should read approximately: “He cannot claim that it was for another debt, and the instrument is entirely discharged; see there,” and so forth.
