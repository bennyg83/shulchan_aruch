Credibility does not help against purchasers. If the borrower granted the lender credibility only after purchasers had already acquired the borrower's property, then certainly the clause cannot prejudice them. The borrower cannot enlarge the lender's rights over property no longer in his control. There is some language in Tosafot Ketubot 94 that might suggest a broader power, but the straightforward rule is as above. The real dispute concerns credibility granted before the later sale. Shach lists many major authorities who hold that even prior credibility does not bind purchasers, while others disagree. No single authority can simply dismiss the opposing side. A textual difficulty is often raised. The Mishnah speaks of "her and those who come through her," which the Tosefta explains as purchasers of her ketubah. But in the parallel phrase "him and those who come through him," the commentators sometimes interpret the latter as agents acting by authorization rather than purchasers. Why should the same phrase shift meaning within one Mishnah? The answer is linguistic. "Those who come in his authority" implies that the original party's legal power remains active and the later person acts through it. This differs from a phrase meaning merely "successors," where the original right may have fully passed away. When a woman sells her ketubah, her original legal relationship has not disappeared entirely because a sold debt can still be forgiven by the original creditor. The personal lien remains with her in an important sense. The purchaser therefore truly "comes through her authority." On the debtor side, an ordinary purchaser of the debtor's field does not act through the debtor's continuing authority. The debtor's ownership has been transferred away. The phrase therefore more naturally refers there to an authorized representative, or, as Ran says, a deathbed recipient whose status remains closely tied to the transferor. This resolves the wording. For practical halachah, another distinction is important. If the borrower believed the lender "like two witnesses," that should not bind purchasers against actual payment witnesses. The borrower can disqualify witnesses for his own dispute, but who gave him authority to disqualify them against later third parties? Ordinary credibility is different. If the clause merely exempts the lender from a rabbinic oath, one can argue that purchasers, who acquired with notice of the public document containing the clause, accepted that risk. Since the Torah baseline may not have required the oath at all, Chazal's protection can be waived in advance. That is why ordinary credibility remains genuinely disputed with respect to purchasers. But where two valid witnesses testify that payment occurred, the debt is substantively discharged. A private agreement between borrower and lender cannot ordinarily erase that testimony to the detriment of strangers. Thus strong "like two witnesses" credibility should not be read as binding purchasers against actual witnesses. There is one further case where even ordinary credibility should, in my opinion, operate against purchasers. Suppose the borrower granted the credibility in Nisan and sold the field in Iyar. There is no possibility of payment after the sale because witnesses establish continuous circumstances from Iyar onward. The only question is whether payment occurred in Nisan, before the purchaser existed. At the time of the original loan and credibility agreement, the borrower still fully controlled his property and rights. He could have forgiven an earlier payment, redirected it to an older debt, or otherwise agreed that unless the lender acknowledged payment, such payment would not discharge the note. If the effect of the clause is that a disputed Nisan payment is treated as legally ineffective, that legal consequence arose before the Iyar purchaser acquired anything. The purchaser cannot complain that the borrower later harmed him. The underlying debt status was already fixed. This seems materially different from a post-sale payment that the borrower and lender attempt to disregard afterward. It also explains why inclusion of a gift recipient among successors can matter despite the general rule that credibility does not bind later transferees. I have not found this exact distinction stated by the earlier commentators, so it requires caution, but conceptually it is very strong.
