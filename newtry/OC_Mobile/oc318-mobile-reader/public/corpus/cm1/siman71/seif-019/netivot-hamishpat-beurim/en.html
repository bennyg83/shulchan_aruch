Against purchasers. Tumim writes that if it is known that the borrower did not repay after they purchased, and the only uncertainty is whether perhaps he repaid before they purchased, the ne'emanut is effective because any repayment was already nullified by the prior stipulation before their purchase. I do not understand this. There remains a concern for collusion: perhaps he did repay, and after the purchase the borrower transferred the money back and the lender returned the note to him as part of the collusion. Tumim further writes that where the lender was granted ne'emanut equal to two witnesses, all agree that purchasers may say, "We relied upon witnesses." I do not understand this either. There was publicity at the time of the loan that the borrower obligated himself from that moment that whenever the lender would say he had not been repaid, the debt would stand even if witnesses contradicted him. Even if the lender would be speaking falsely, the borrower had already obligated himself for the stated amount. Purchasers therefore certainly should have protected themselves. This resembles siman 61, seif 7, where one obligates himself up to one thousand gold coins according to what Shimon will later acknowledge under the note.
