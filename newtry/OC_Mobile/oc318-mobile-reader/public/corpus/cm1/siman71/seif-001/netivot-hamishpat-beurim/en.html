He collects without an oath. See Sema and Shach, seif katan 1, who labor to distinguish between a lender who granted ne'emanut to the borrower and a borrower who granted ne'emanut to the lender. In my opinion the distinction is simple. Language of ne'emanut is in principle comparable to saying, “My father is accepted upon me as a judge,” which can exempt the trusted party without an oath. The reason a lender who granted ne'emanut to the borrower may nevertheless have to swear is different. As explained in Sema, seif katan 5, and Shach, seif katan 4, a migo concerning the very matter of the ne'emanut is effective. A case where the lender grants ne'emanut to the borrower must concern a loan evidenced by an instrument. If it were an oral loan, the borrower would certainly be exempt without an oath, for otherwise what practical effect would the lender's grant of ne'emanut have? Since it is a written loan and the ne'emanut clause appears in the instrument held by the lender, the lender has a migo. Had he wished, he could have concealed the instrument, sued orally, and said, “I never granted you ne'emanut.” Even if witnesses had once seen the instrument, he could say that the instrument they saw had already been paid and that he now holds a different oral loan against the borrower. By virtue of this migo, he can require the borrower to swear. Accordingly, if the lender gave the borrower a separate instrument containing the ne'emanut so that it remained in the borrower's possession, the borrower is exempt without an oath. This is also evident from what the Shach writes later in seif katan 45.
Without an oath. See Sema, seif katan 3, who holds that ordinary ne'emanut is effective even against one witness. Shach, seif katan 2, disagrees. There is a textual error in the Shach, and the text should read: “But with ordinary ne'emanut he swears and collects; see what I write below in seif 5,” and so forth. This proves that where the lender said, “Do not repay me except before witnesses,” although he collects without an oath as explained in siman 70, he must nevertheless swear if one witness testifies to payment. Ne'emanut is comparable to a stipulation not to pay except before witnesses, as the Shach writes in seif katan 7, and despite that, one witness obligates an oath. According to the Sema, by contrast, ne'emanut and a stipulation not to pay except before witnesses function like a chazakah, and one witness cannot overcome a chazakah, just as with a debt still within its due date in siman 84. One might question the Sema's view, that ne'emanut is effective against one witness, because this seems to include even where the creditor says that the event never occurred. The Shach writes in seif katan 3 that if two witnesses testify, the creditor must return the money after collection because we divide his statement. If so, with one witness he should at least be obligated in an oath after collection, since whenever two witnesses would obligate payment, one witness obligates an oath. This is not so according to what I wrote above. Since ne'emanut operates as a chazakah, analogous to a debt still within its term in siman 84, the creditor cannot be obligated after collection. The situation is treated as one where there is a chazakah that the single witness is lying.
Who brought witnesses that he had paid him. See Shach, seif katan 3, who explains that this applies specifically where the creditor claims that the payment was for a different debt. In that case, even if the witnesses testify that the money was given as payment of this debt, the creditor is believed, because with respect to this payment the borrower granted him credibility greater than that of the witnesses. There is a textual error later in seif katan 7 of the Shach. It should read approximately: “It is possible that even the Sema said only that he may contradict the witnesses by claiming that the payment was for another debt, unlike an ordinary claim of a different debt, where he is believed only when he does not contradict them in a contrived manner. Here he is believed to claim another debt even against witnesses who expressly testify that payment was made on this debt, because,” and so forth. If, however, the creditor admits the witnesses' account that the borrower gave the money in payment of this debt and the creditor remained silent, but he now claims that he had another oral debt against the borrower and that he appropriated the money to that other debt, as discussed in siman 83, it appears that he is not believed. So long as he does not contradict the witnesses, they remain fully valid witnesses, and he cannot assert an additional debt in the face of their testimony. It is as though he seized the funds in the presence of witnesses.
Even if the borrower had a migo. See Sema, seif katan 5. His intent is that ordinary ne'emanut given by a custodian to the owner of the deposit is not effective merely because the custodian always has a migo that the property was lost through accident. The word “borrower” in the Sema's phrase “the borrower to the lender” is a textual error. The Sema means to ask why the Rashba had to explain the ruling on the ground that the custodian trusted the other party as two witnesses. Even ordinary ne'emanut must necessarily have been intended to prevail over the custodian's migo, because otherwise the ne'emanut would have no practical content at all, since the custodian always has the migo that the property was lost through accident. The Sema's following words, “and why should we not say,” introduce a separate question. The Tumim explains the Sema in this way, removing the Shach's objection. The Tumim wishes to derive from this that if there were no witnesses at all when the ne'emanut was granted, then even if the trusted party later has a migo concerning the very matter of the ne'emanut, the migo should not be effective. His argument is that the grantor must have trusted him even against that migo, because otherwise what would the ne'emanut accomplish, since such a migo would always be available. According to the Tumim, the Rama's statement above that a migo concerning the ne'emanut itself is effective applies only where witnesses were present when the ne'emanut was granted but later died. In my opinion this is incorrect. If so, the poskim would not have omitted such a basic distinction. Moreover, even where the creditor trusted the other party as two witnesses, a migo concerning the ne'emanut itself is effective although that migo stands against witnesses, and witnesses are stronger than a migo. Thus, by trusting him against witnesses, the grantor effectively trusted him expressly even against a migo, yet the migo concerning the ne'emanut itself still works, as explained by the Tur. The reason is that a migo concerning the ne'emanut itself is based on ha'peh she'asar hu ha'peh she'hitir, which is stronger than an ordinary migo, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 72a, s.v. ein lecha bo. It can operate even in the face of witnesses. The speaker's words creating his own disadvantage do not weaken the claim, and we treat the matter as though he had never made the damaging statement at all. This appears clear. The Tumim also seeks to prove that a migo is ineffective from Even HaEzer, siman 141, regarding a shalish, who is believed even though the opposing party has a migo that he could have denied the deposit arrangement. This too is not comparable. A shalish is different. The parties entrusted the matter to him with authority that the legal reality would be as he states, even if he lies, as Tosafot write in Gittin 64a. Therefore, even if there is evidence that the husband is speaking truthfully, the shalish remains believed. Even if he is lying, the law follows his statement. A migo can add only evidentiary force showing that the opposing party is telling the truth. That adds nothing where, in the absence of witnesses, the shalish's statement controls regardless. Our case is different.
As three. See Shach, seif katan 5. According to what the Sema writes in seif katan 4 regarding one who trusted the creditor as two witnesses, we do not say that this creates a situation of two witnesses against two witnesses, because if so the same result would follow from ordinary ne'emanut and there would have been no purpose in expressly trusting him as two. At first glance this creates a difficulty here. If he trusted him as three witnesses, why does the ruling specify that repayment before four witnesses defeats the ne'emanut? Even repayment before three should create three against three, leaving the money with its possessor under the rule that the burden of proof lies on the claimant. We must therefore say that the case of four witnesses was chosen to teach that even seizure by the creditor is then ineffective. According to the Shach in seif katan 2, however, it is possible to say that when one says “as three,” he means more than three, because the person whom he trusted is not himself contradicted, while the witnesses who say that the debt was paid are the ones whose testimony is contradicted.
