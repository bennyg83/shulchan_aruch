But if he brought witnesses, and so forth. Sema infers from Mechaber's use of "witnesses" that Mechaber follows Rosh: an ordinary credibility clause in favor of the lender is effective even against one witness who says the debt was paid. Shach argues that the inference is weak because many major authorities, including Raavad and Ramban, hold that ordinary credibility does not defeat one witness. The dispute is ancient. Its root is the Mishnah in Ketubot: if the husband writes, "I have no vow or oath against you," the woman is exempt from oath. The Gemara asks which oath is meant. Rav says the guardian's oath; R. Nachman says the oath of partial collection. Raavad infers: why does the Gemara not say the oath imposed by one witness who says payment occurred? Evidently an ordinary exemption from oath or credibility clause does not neutralize a one-witness oath. Rosh disagrees and says the Gemara merely chose common examples. If we seek Mechaber's own position, Even HaEzer siman 98 is highly relevant. There he rules that if the husband writes, "I have no vow or oath against you," no oath can be imposed upon her. Beit Shmuel understands this as including the oath generated by one witness. That strongly supports Rosh. One could try to distinguish two formulations. A clause saying, "No oath shall be imposed on you," expressly exempts from every oath and therefore includes a one-witness oath. A clause saying merely, "You shall be believed by me when you say you were not paid," might mean only "believed against me," not "believed against a contradictory witness." This could reconcile Rosh's responsum, where he appears stricter, with his ruling in Perek HaKotev regarding explicit exemption from all oaths. But Rabbenu Chananel's interpretation of the sugya makes the distinction difficult. He explains one of the cases of credibility as ordinary lender credibility and treats it as effective against one witness. If the distinction were valid, why assume the Gemara's case used general credibility rather than an explicit oath exemption? In the parallel episode before Rava, it is more natural to say the document contained explicit exemption from oath, because Rava himself holds elsewhere that broad credibility language can be even stronger. Thus Rabbenu Chananel appears not to recognize the distinction.
And now, for practical halachah, there are still many major authorities on the stricter side, including Raavad, Ramban, Raah, Ritva, and others. I therefore suggest a distinction that preserves Rabbenu Chananel's ruling while respecting the dispute. In a formal written loan, one witness who says "paid" does not by Torah law destroy the note. Chazal imposed an oath on the creditor to satisfy the debtor and respond to the witness. If the borrower himself granted credibility to the lender, there is little reason to retain that rabbinic oath. The debtor voluntarily said he trusted the lender. Therefore ordinary credibility should work against one witness in a formal note. An oral loan is different. There the creditor has no intrinsic document-based right. His entire ability to collect despite contrary evidence may depend on the language of the credibility agreement itself. When that language is ambiguous, the burden of proof rests on the claimant. We can read it narrowly: "I believed you against myself, not against an independent witness." Rabbenu Chananel and Rosh need not have disagreed with this oral-loan limitation. Thus in a formal note, ordinary credibility can defeat the one-witness oath; in an oral claim, where credibility is the very source of the extraction, one should interpret the clause narrowly. This seems the best practical synthesis.
And even if he brought one hundred witnesses that he repaid, and so forth. Shach limits this to a case where the lender says, "Those payments were for other debts." If the lender simply denies receiving the money at all, Shach says he cannot use the credibility clause to contradict outright testimony. His reason is that even if the clause forces the borrower to pay now, the borrower can immediately sue the lender afterward using the same witnesses: "You took money from me without right." Why move money back and forth pointlessly? Shach elaborates similarly in siman 70. I do not find the limitation convincing. Ran discusses whether, after payment under such a credibility clause, the borrower can sue a second time on the strength of the disqualified witnesses. Ran's language does not suggest that his entire analysis is limited to a claim of "other debts." Conceptually, either the borrower disqualified those witnesses for this matter or he did not. If he did, why should their testimony become effective immediately after the compelled payment? If he did not, then even a claim of "that was payment on another debt" should not help when the witnesses explicitly say the money was paid on this very debt. Once we say the witnesses are lying about the allocation, they are already false witnesses in substance. The distinction between total denial and "other debt" becomes artificial. There is also a proof from the one-witness cases. Suppose one witness says the debt was paid and the lender denies it outright, without saying "other debt." According to Shach, after the lender collects he could be sued again on the basis of that one witness, generating a Torah oath. Why then do Raavad and Ramban prove from the Mishnah's omission of the one-witness case that ordinary credibility does not defeat one witness? Perhaps the Mishnah omitted it simply because even if the creditor initially collects, he would immediately face a second one-witness claim. The same circularity Shach invokes for two witnesses would apply. Yet the early authorities do not frame the issue that way. This suggests that a valid credibility clause can indeed make the contradictory evidence legally ineffective for the entire debt dispute, not merely for the first procedural stage. There is also the case cited by Knesset HaGedolah: if the borrower credited the lender against three witnesses and four witnesses later testify, one can require the lender to swear against the fourth witness. If Shach were correct, the structure becomes very difficult. If the lender totally denies the payment, even the original three should create the later circular claim. If he says "other debt," then after payment the fourth witness should again create the same problem. The more natural reading is that the original three were legally neutralized by the agreed credibility, while the fourth was not included and therefore still creates an oath. That framework does not depend on "other debt" at all. One might answer that a migo could help against one witness, but most authorities do not allow a migo merely to escape an oath, and in any event the cited case specifically involves a witness outside the agreed number. Thus the primary rule should be read broadly: when the borrower credibly empowered the lender against a specified number of witnesses, those witnesses are treated as legally ineffective regarding this debt even if the lender simply denies their testimony. The separate question of whether the borrower can later sue again is governed by the same disqualification and does not automatically revive their evidence.
And even if the borrower had a migo, and so forth. Sema asks from Rama's earlier ruling: where no witnesses prove the credibility agreement, the borrower is believed through a migo that there was no such agreement. Sema gives two answers. First, perhaps a migo fails only where the lender was believed "like two witnesses," while ordinary credibility remains vulnerable to migo. Second, the earlier migo attacks the existence of the credibility clause itself, whereas the present migo is collateral to the underlying dispute. Shach rejects the first distinction. Baal HaTerumot expressly says that even where the lender claims the borrower granted him credibility like two witnesses, the borrower can still use a migo to deny the condition entirely. That resembles the rule that even a note containing strong credibility language still requires authentication if its execution is denied. This is straightforward. Still, there may be a genuine distinction where the borrower cannot deny the credibility clause itself and has only some independent migo, such as a custodian's migo of unavoidable accident. Rashba explains the failure of such a migo by saying: if actual witnesses testified against the borrower, the migo would be useless because it would be a migo against witnesses. Once the borrower granted the lender credibility comparable to witnesses, the lender's word occupies that position. This reasoning is strongest where the lender was expressly believed like two witnesses. If the clause grants only ordinary credibility, actual witnesses would still defeat the lender. Why then should the borrower's migo not have comparable force? Shach says even ordinary credibility defeats migo. I am not fully persuaded. There is support for Shach, however, from Rosh's view that ordinary credibility can neutralize one witness. A migo can sometimes be stronger than one witness, but if the borrower intentionally empowered the lender to override evidence against him, perhaps the same logic reaches a migo. The law of a trusted third party also points that way. Where the owner admits appointing a third party but disputes the terms, the third party's credibility can defeat the owner's migo even though two witnesses would defeat the third party. Beit Shmuel says expressly that the owner's migo is treated as against the entrusted person's special credibility. That suggests special entrusted credibility need not equal "two witnesses" in every respect to defeat a migo. On the other hand, if ordinary credibility does not defeat one witness according to Raavad and Ramban, it is difficult to say it defeats a migo that may be stronger than one witness. The matter therefore depends substantially on the underlying dispute about ordinary credibility against a single witness. Mechaber appears inclined to the stronger view that even ordinary credibility can defeat a migo. There is also a practical inference from the rules of an iska document against heirs. If the owner was trusted to say the business assets were not returned, the heirs are not given the claim of unavoidable accident. The wording does not necessarily require "like two witnesses." This suggests ordinary credibility can be enough. Sema makes another important observation. Why would a person create a credibility clause that can always be neutralized by the other side's routine migo? A contract should not be interpreted as self-defeating. This principle must be applied carefully. If, at the time of the agreement, witnesses were present and later disappeared, the condition originally had real force. The migo arises only afterward because the proving witnesses are gone. In that situation there is no reason to reinterpret the original condition as eliminating all future migo arguments. But if the condition was made privately from the outset, with no one able to prove it, then a reading that always permits the borrower to deny the condition through migo would make the clause nearly pointless. There is stronger reason to understand the parties as intending the credibility clause itself to defeat that migo. This helps clarify a ruling of Taz concerning an iska. The recipient agreed that he would not be believed to claim business loss unless he proved it. He later claimed a loss and asserted a migo that he could deny the entire transaction. Taz says the migo can work where the condition itself is not independently proven. That is correct where witnesses originally proved the condition but are now unavailable. The migo is a later evidentiary development. But where the condition was private from the beginning, one can argue the opposite: the very purpose of the stipulation was to deny the recipient reliance on unproved loss claims, and the parties cannot have intended a condition that his own denial could always destroy. Accordingly, the practical result depends on when and how the migo arises. If the credibility term itself is genuinely unproven and the migo attacks its existence, the borrower can often rely on the migo, especially where the term originally had independent proof. If the borrower admits the credibility term and only invokes a collateral migo, the stronger view is that the credibility clause defeats it. And where the condition was privately made from the outset, the contract's evident purpose may itself show that ordinary migo was meant to be excluded.
