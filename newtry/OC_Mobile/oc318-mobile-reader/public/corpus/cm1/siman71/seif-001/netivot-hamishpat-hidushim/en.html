Without an oath. Even with an oral loan, an ordinary credibility clause exempts the lender from an oath. See Beurim for the reason. Nevertheless, after payment the borrower may require a hesset oath, as stated in seif 5:
There are no witnesses. Even if the borrower admits that he granted the lender credibility equal to two witnesses, that does not defeat his migo, because the migo concerns the very scope of the credibility agreement. The principle is that the mouth that created the restriction also created the qualification. This is unlike the Sema, who holds that credibility equal to two witnesses defeats even this kind of migo. The rule applies even if no witnesses were present when the money was originally delivered. We do not say that the borrower necessarily granted credibility specifically to overcome this migo, because otherwise the clause would always be meaningless. See Beurim:
He brought witnesses. Against one witness, the Sema holds that the credibility clause is effective to exempt the lender from an oath. The Shach disagrees, and therefore the current possessor may assert kim li in accordance with the Shach. If the document expressly states "without any oath," the lender is exempt even from an oath generated by one witness. See Beurim. Even after payment, the borrower cannot impose an oath upon him through that witness. See Beurim. Like two witnesses. The Shach holds that this applies specifically where the lender claims that the payment was applied to a different debt. In that case, even if witnesses testify that the borrower explicitly paid on this very debt, the lender's enhanced credibility permits him to contradict them. But if the lender completely denies the witnesses and says, "The payment never happened," the borrower may sue him after making the payment and recover it from him. Accordingly, the lender gains nothing by collecting first because beit din does not require a pointless circular transfer of money. See Beurim. If the lender admits the witnesses' statement that the borrower explicitly designated the payment for this debt, but says that he silently accepted it intending to seize the money for another debt, he is not believed through a migo:
The borrower had a migo. Specifically a migo concerning the scope of the credibility clause itself is effective, as above in the gloss. See the Shach, unlike the Sema, who should have distinguished that with credibility equal to two witnesses even such a migo is ineffective, while with ordinary credibility even an unrelated migo can be effective:
You are believed by me like three. This means that if four qualified witnesses contradict the lender, the four constitute complete testimony and prevail. This is unlike Rav Hai, who holds that three become treated as disqualified by the credibility clause, leaving only one witness, which in a document would create merely an oath obligation. Shach, seif katan 6. See Beurim. Even if there are only three witnesses, the lender remains more credible than they are. If the borrower writes, "You are believed like a beit din," this is not treated as a reference to a numerical panel.
