He collects without an oath. This applies even to an ordinary credibility clause that does not expressly state, 'you shall be believed without an oath,' as I explained in Perishah. With a documented loan the reason is straightforward: if the lender still had to swear, what was gained by granting him credibility? Without the clause he would already swear on his document and collect, as the Tur and Mechaber write at the beginning of siman 82. With an oral loan, however, there is some difficulty. Without such credibility the borrower would take a hesset oath and be exempt. Perhaps the clause should merely mean that the lender may collect after swearing, analogous to the law later in seif 21 where the lender granted credibility to the borrower and the borrower is not exempt except with an oath. In Derishah I wrote that here the lender performed a benefit for the borrower by advancing him money, and did not wish to trust him without a special credibility clause. We therefore regard it as obvious that his intent was that he himself be believed even without an oath. Why would he place his money in the borrower's hand only to expose himself to an oath? Even if the transaction was also beneficial to the lender, Chazal did not distinguish among credibility clauses; one who accepts ordinary credibility does so with the ordinary meaning of credibility in a loan. This differs from where the lender grants credibility to the borrower. There, because it is contrary to the normal logic for a lender to trust the borrower, the holder of the document has the weaker hand. See what I write further on this.
See above siman 69, seif 2, and seif katan 13. In Perishah to this siman I wrote that even Baal HaItur, who disagrees in siman 69 and at the beginning of siman 46, agrees here where credibility was granted orally. He says only that where there is a document or handwriting containing a credibility clause but it has not been authenticated, the borrower cannot say it was paid, because if so he should not have left such a document in the lender's hand, since the lender might find witnesses to authenticate it. That reasoning does not apply to an oral arrangement. See there.
But if he brought witnesses that he paid, etc. The Tur likewise writes this. The wording implies specifically two witnesses. One witness who says payment was made does not defeat an ordinary credibility clause, following the Rosh and unlike Raavad. See Derishah. In my responsum to Lechem Mishneh, siman 8, I wrote that one should not think there is a contradiction between the Rosh's rulings in chapter HaKotev, where he holds that ordinary credibility overcomes one witness, and Responsa Rosh, klal 68, siman 17, where he concludes concerning a document with an ordinary credibility clause that the lender must swear against one witness. Independently, the responsum is difficult in saying that a person does not transmit to his children a claim requiring an oath against one witness, whereas the Rosh's rulings and the Tur do not decide that way. I discussed this at length in siman 108 and at the end of siman 84.
Even if he brought witnesses that he paid, etc. In Derishah I wrote that this too gives some proof that ordinary credibility overcomes one witness who says payment was made. When he expressly says that the lender is to be believed like two witnesses, the phrase must therefore mean even more than two witnesses. If ordinary credibility applied only against the borrower's own denial but not against one witness, then even an express statement, 'you are believed like two witnesses,' could be interpreted merely to elevate the lender above one witness to the level of two, but not above two actual witnesses. With two witnesses to payment there would then be two against two, and the money should remain with its possessor, the borrower. One cannot answer that the document itself assists the lender, for this law also concerns an oral loan. See Derishah for more.
We do not believe him through a migo. At first glance one might distinguish this from Rama's immediately preceding gloss, where a migo was accepted, by saying that there the migo concerned the credibility arrangement itself: he could have said, 'I never granted him credibility.' Here, however, witnesses establish that credibility was granted, and the borrower merely has another possible claim with which to exempt himself. But I later saw the actual Responsa Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, which says: Reuven is not believed to say, 'I returned it,' through a migo that he could have said, 'It was lost through an unavoidable accident.' Since the lender granted the borrower credibility like two witnesses, if two witnesses had testified that he did not return it, he would not be believed to say that he did return it through a migo that it was lost by accident, because that would be a migo against witnesses. The same applies here where the lender was trusted to say that the borrower did not return it. From this wording it follows that the rule applies specifically where he was granted credibility like two witnesses. Ordinary credibility would not defeat such a migo. That is why Rama states the case here, where the lender was trusted against two witnesses, unlike a mere ordinary credibility clause. The reason is that if the borrower claimed unavoidable accident, he would be believed by Torah law upon taking an oath, as though two witnesses testified that the accident occurred. He is therefore likewise believed to say he returned it, as though two witnesses testified to the return. The lender was trusted only against the borrower himself, not against two witnesses. Admittedly this is somewhat strained, for if so ordinary credibility granted by the borrower to the lender would often be ineffective, since the borrower could always invoke a migo of unavoidable accident. Why not say instead that once the borrower trusted the lender against himself, the lender's statement that the item was not returned is itself like two witnesses and defeats the migo, just as a migo does not overcome an established presumption? We must distinguish: an established presumption is equivalent to testimony before us, whereas ordinary contractual credibility is not. If actual witnesses stood before us, the lender could not contradict them.
