Oath. This applies even where the lender was granted ordinary ne'emanut without an express statement that he would be believed without an oath. In a documented loan the reason is straightforward, since without the clause of ne'emanut the lender would in any event collect upon an oath, so the clause must add that no oath is required. In an oral loan the matter is more difficult, because without ne'emanut the borrower could take a shevuat heset and be exempt; perhaps the intent was merely that the lender should collect upon an oath. The Sema answers that because the lender did the borrower a benefit by lending him money, we presume that he intended to be believed even without an oath, since otherwise he would be placing his own money at risk and exposing himself to an oath. Even where the transaction also benefits the lender, the law does not distinguish. This differs from a lender who grants ne'emanut to the borrower, where even if written in a document it runs against the usual presumption, and the holder of the document bears the disadvantage of ambiguity. The Taz and Chacham Tzvi offer other explanations. The Shach adds that after payment a shevuat heset may nevertheless be required, as stated above in seif 5.
Above. The Sema writes that even authorities who disagree there would agree here. Their dispute there concerns a document or signed writing containing a ne'emanut clause that has not been authenticated; they maintain that the debtor is not believed to say it was paid because he should not have left such a document outstanding when witnesses might later authenticate it. That concern does not exist in an oral case.
Witnesses. The Sema understands this as specifically two witnesses, so that one witness who says the debt was paid does not defeat ordinary ne'emanut. The Shach rejects that inference. With two witnesses the lender collects nothing; with one witness he may collect, but only after an oath. Baal HaTerumot likewise indicates that ordinary ne'emanut does not override one witness. Responsa Maharam Alshich 28 states this explicitly. Only if the document provides that the lender shall be believed against the debtor as against one witness may he collect without an oath when one witness contradicts him, just as a clause granting credibility like two witnesses allows collection without an oath even when two witnesses contradict him.
Collects. The Shach writes that ne'emanut against two witnesses helps only where the lender claims that the money received was applied to another debt. In that case, even if the witnesses testify that he explicitly received it toward this debt, he may contradict them. But if he claims that no payment occurred at all, he is not believed, since he could still sue the borrower later for the alleged other debt.
Migo. The Sema initially distinguishes the Rama's earlier ruling, where the party is believed through a migo relating to the ne'emanut itself, since he could have said that no ne'emanut was granted, from this case, where witnesses establish the clause and the alternative claim would merely exempt him on another ground. The Rashba's responsum, however, says that Reuven is not believed to say "I returned it" through a migo that he could have said "it was lost by unavoidable accident," because the other party had granted him ne'emanut. The Sema therefore suggests that the Rashba speaks specifically of ne'emanut equivalent to two witnesses, whereas ordinary ne'emanut may not overcome a migo. The Rama therefore wrote here that the case involves ne'emanut against two witnesses. The Shach rejects this analysis, noting that the Rashba is expressly discussing a deposit, for otherwise a claim of unavoidable loss would not apply. He concludes that there is no distinction between ordinary ne'emanut and ne'emanut against witnesses, and accepts the Sema's first distinction, namely whether the migo goes to the existence of the ne'emanut itself. He cites Responsa Maharit 60.
Paid. The Shach notes that the Tur and other authorities state the rule without distinguishing between a documented and oral loan, and the Rambam and Semag appear to do likewise. Rav Hai Gaon, however, does not appear to agree, so the Shach concludes that one should be cautious and the matter requires study. If the borrower says, "You shall be believed against me like a beit din," then even an admission or payment before one beit din does not help if he is later sued before another beit din. A handwritten receipt, however, is effective even then.
