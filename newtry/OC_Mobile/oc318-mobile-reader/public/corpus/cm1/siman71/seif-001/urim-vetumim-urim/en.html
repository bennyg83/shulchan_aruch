He collects without an oath. Certainly this is so for a documented loan, because the entire purpose of the credibility clause must be to dispense with an oath, since without such a clause the lender already collects by virtue of the document. Even for an oral loan, where one might have said that the borrower merely believed him to collect upon taking an oath, we say that an unrestricted grant of credibility disqualifies all of the borrower's contrary claims. By contrast, when a lender grants credibility to the borrower, his ordinary language means only that the document will be defeated by the borrower's claim. So writes Ramban. The Sema explains why a lender's general grant of credibility is construed broadly, whereas a borrower's is not: when a lender agrees to lend only after demanding credibility, he presumably intends not to need an oath at all, for why should he lend his money only to be required to swear later? But when the borrower asks to be trusted, why should the lender be assumed to trust him excessively, even to the point of exempting him from an oath? That is contrary to ordinary reason. This explanation is correct in my view, although other later authorities questioned it and offered different reasons.
But if there are no witnesses, etc. Even if the borrower admits that he granted the lender credibility equivalent to two witnesses, his migo is still effective because he could have denied the entire grant of credibility. So writes the Shach, and this is the principal ruling, as I wrote in Tumim, seif katan 3, against the Sema and Bach, who hold that once he admits granting credibility like two witnesses, a migo does not help.
He is believed by virtue of a migo. See Tumim, seif katan 3. It appears that this applies only where there originally were witnesses to the grant of credibility but they are now unavailable, so the borrower has a migo to deny that he ever granted it. If there were never witnesses at all when the condition was made, then he always possessed that migo, and the condition would have been pointless unless its very purpose was that the migo not help. On this basis I discussed a person who received money for an iska and stipulated that he would not be believed to claim a loss unless he proved it. He later claims a loss by virtue of a migo that he could deny the condition entirely. The Taz holds that he is believed by the migo, while Chacham Tzvi disagrees. I distinguished: if the condition was originally made before witnesses who are now unavailable, the law is as the Taz says; if there were never witnesses, Chacham Tzvi is correct, and he must fulfill the condition and prove the loss.
But if he brings witnesses, etc. The Sema holds that the credibility clause is effective against one witness, so two witnesses are specifically required to overcome it. The Shach disagrees and holds that even one witness defeats the clause. This is in fact an early dispute. The Rosh holds that the credibility clause remains effective, whereas Raavad, Ramban, and those who follow them hold that it does not. See Tumim. I clarified that the Shulchan Aruch intends to rule like the Rosh, both where the borrower writes, "I have no oath against you," and where he writes, "You shall be believed." For practical law I wrote that in a documented loan the Rosh and Rabbeinu Chananel are correct, whereas in an oral loan the principal view is that of Raavad and Ramban. It is possible that even Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rosh agree in the latter case and never disputed it.
Even if he brought witnesses, etc. The Shach holds that this applies only where the lender does not wholly contradict the witnesses, but says that the payment they saw was for another debt. He is then believed even if the witnesses clearly state that the payment was for this debt. But if he completely denies their testimony and says that no payment occurred, credibility does not help him against witnesses. In Tumim I wrote that this is incorrect. The borrower disqualified the witnesses by his condition, and therefore the lender is believed even when he says that the event never occurred. See there.
Even if the borrower had a migo, etc. The principal view is as the Shach: unless the borrower has a migo concerning the very existence of the credibility clause, as at the beginning of the siman, an ordinary migo does not prevail against the clause. Whether this is true for every credibility clause or only where the lender was made equivalent to two witnesses depends upon the dispute just mentioned. If a general credibility clause is effective against one witness, then it is likewise effective against a migo. If it is not effective against one witness, then certainly it should not defeat a migo, which is stronger than one witness. See Tumim.
But if he said, etc. Meaning that if there are four witnesses, all four remain valid, and there is complete testimony. This is unlike Rav Hai Gaon, who holds that three of them are disqualified so that only one witness remains, and in a documented debt one witness merely imposes an oath. The silence of the authorities rejects that view. So writes the Shach, and this is correct.
Like three. But if he said, "like a beit din," he did not descend to a particular number. Rather, he intended to strengthen the lender's credibility with the expression "like a beit din," and thereby disqualified all contrary witnesses. This is correct. Shach.
