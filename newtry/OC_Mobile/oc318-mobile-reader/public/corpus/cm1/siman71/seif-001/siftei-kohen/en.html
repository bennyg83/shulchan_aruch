He collects without an oath. This applies even to an ordinary credibility clause that does not expressly state that the creditor shall be believed without an oath, and it applies even to an oral loan. The Sema and Bach write this, and it is explicit in Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 26, with which the Ramban agrees, as cited by the Beit Yosef in seif 12. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes this in chapter 15 of the laws of loans, law 3. The Sema labors to explain the reason, but Baal HaTerumos states it clearly in the name of the Ramban: the ordinary meaning of granting credibility is credibility without an oath. Once the debtor accepted the creditor as credible, he invalidated his own contrary claim, and the creditor is believed on his statement alone. The Bach writes similarly, as does the Maggid Mishneh, that an unqualified credibility clause means without an oath. By contrast, when a creditor grants credibility to the debtor, he ordinarily intended only to nullify the force of the document. It further appears to me that here we are effectively witnesses that the debtor did not repay, because he expressly accepted this condition. How could he repay and then rely on the creditor's honesty? This is no weaker than a condition, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," where the creditor collects without an oath for this reason, as explained above in siman 70, seif katan 11. Nevertheless, after collection the creditor must take a heset oath if required, as explained below in seif 5.
Without an oath. If one witness contradicts the creditor, the Rosh and Rabbeinu Chananel in the chapter HaKotev hold that he still collects without an oath. The Sema questions this from a responsum of the Rosh, Klal 68, siman 17, but there the creditor had acknowledged the matter before the witness, and the Rosh may distinguish that case just as he distinguishes regarding two witnesses between witnesses who saw repayment and witnesses before whom the creditor acknowledged repayment, as discussed below in seif 3, seif katan 8. The Raavad and Ramban, however, hold that the creditor must swear. The Sema infers from the Mechaber's phrase, "But if he brings witnesses that he paid," that only two witnesses defeat ordinary credibility and therefore one witness does not impose an oath. This inference is not compelling. Two witnesses prevent collection altogether, whereas one witness may still allow collection but only after an oath. The Sema further argues that because the Mechaber later states that where the creditor was made credible like two witnesses he collects without an oath even against witnesses of repayment, ordinary credibility must already suffice against one witness; otherwise the purpose of the enhanced clause could have been merely to eliminate the oath against one witness. He also reasons that where two witnesses oppose a creditor who was granted credibility like two witnesses one could otherwise say that there are two against two and leave the money with the debtor, and this cannot be answered simply by saying that the document supports the creditor, since the rule also applies to an oral loan. This too is not decisive. Baal HaTerumos, at the beginning of Shaar 26, records several explanations. One answer is that we rely on the creditor because the debtor himself accepted him as equivalent to two witnesses, thereby treating the opposing witnesses as disqualified for this issue. Another explains that the phrase "like two witnesses" means that he is to be believed with the force that two witnesses ordinarily have throughout the Torah even when witnesses come against him. If it meant only exactly two, the addition would accomplish nothing, since an ordinary credibility clause would already make him believed against the debtor himself. The Raavad, because of this difficulty, instituted language stating that the creditor is believed "like two valid witnesses." Some questioned him from the Gemara, which does not say "valid," and distinguished seizure from initial collection, but Baal HaTerumos rejects the distinction and notes that ordinary witnesses are presumed valid. This discussion itself indicates that ordinary credibility does not defeat one witness without an oath. Baal HaTerumos likewise writes in Shaar 21, part 5, that when one witness testifies that the document was paid and the document does not contain credibility "like two witnesses," the creditor must swear and collect. The responsum of Maharam Alshich, siman 28, is explicit in the same direction. If, however, the document expressly says that the creditor shall be believed against one witness, he collects without an oath when one witness says that he was paid, just as credibility against two witnesses dispenses with an oath when two witnesses contradict him. Although Maharam Alshich speaks of credibility like two witnesses, that is because such wording was customary and because his formulation addressed the broader case. In the particular case of one opposing witness, credibility expressly granted against one witness would suffice. It should also be noted that where two witnesses say a document was paid and two say it was not, one does not simply preserve the document's force and collect. A document of doubtful payment is not collected upon, though it is not torn. This is explicit in the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 29. The statements in the Sema and Baal HaTerumos about relying on the document must therefore be understood only as proposed answers within the discussion, not as the final practical ruling.
He brought witnesses that he paid, yet the creditor collects from him, etc. I wrote above, siman 70, seif 4, seif katan 15, that credibility against two witnesses is effective only where the creditor claims that the money they saw was applied to another debt. In that case, whether the witnesses testify merely that money was paid or expressly say that it was paid toward this debt, the creditor may contradict their characterization because the debtor granted him credibility concerning repayment of this obligation, as explained in siman 58, seif katan 9. If, however, the creditor says, "I never received the money," he is not believed to deny the witnesses altogether. The debtor could later sue him for the very money that the witnesses saw him receive, and the creditor could not deny receipt. The witnesses may be ineffective to prove application to this debt, but they remain valid to prove that money was received. There is no purpose in requiring a circular payment. I proved in siman 70 that this is also the Raavad's view. Although the Sema in siman 58 writes that even without a claim that the payment was applied elsewhere the creditor may contradict the witnesses and retain the money, that does not appear correct. Possibly the Sema means only that the creditor need not accept the witnesses' description of the payment and may instead say that it was applied elsewhere; he did not mean that a bare denial of receipt is believed.
Even if the debtor had a migo, etc. We do not believe him through a migo. The Sema initially suggests distinguishing the earlier case, where the migo concerned the credibility clause itself, because the debtor could have denied granting credibility, from this case, where witnesses establish the credibility clause and the debtor merely has some other possible defense. The Rashba's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe says that one who was trusted like two witnesses cannot say, "I returned the deposit," through a migo that he could have said it was lost through unavoidable accident, because the creditor's accepted credibility makes the return claim like a migo against witnesses. The Sema therefore concludes that this applies specifically where the creditor was trusted like two witnesses, while ordinary credibility would not defeat a migo. His reasoning is difficult. The Rashba was discussing a deposit, where a defense of unavoidable accident exists; such a defense has no place in an ordinary loan. More fundamentally, once witnesses establish even an ordinary credibility clause and the law is that the creditor collects without an oath, the debtor's contrary claim should be treated like a claim against established testimony. The Rashba's phrase "like two witnesses" may simply reflect the facts of his case. There is an additional reason: once the debtor stipulated the matter, the circumstances themselves attest that he would not repay contrary to the condition. This is no weaker than "Do not repay me except before witnesses," where even a migo does not help, as explained above in siman 70, seif katan 11. The Bach also follows the Sema's path but interprets Baal HaTerumos as speaking where there were no witnesses to the loan and therefore the debtor had a migo to deny the loan. That is difficult, for Baal HaTerumos and the Tur expressly speak of a condition that the creditor be believed like two witnesses. It further appears from Baal HaTerumos, the Itur, and the Tur above in siman 69 that a migo does not defeat even ordinary credibility unless the debtor's migo allows him to deny the credibility clause itself. Accordingly, if a person granted another ordinary credibility concerning a future loan, and later admits that the loan occurred but claims that he paid, he must pay even though he could have said, "I trusted you if you would lend to me, but you never lent to me," provided witnesses establish the credibility clause. The simple distinction is therefore whether the debtor has a migo directed against the very existence of the credibility agreement. The Ir Shushan and Maharit likewise discuss this distinction. This appears clear.
But if he said to him, etc. If he was made credible like three witnesses and the debtor repaid before four, the debt is paid. In my view this means that the four are four witnesses establishing repayment with no equal testimony opposing them. It therefore appears that even if the loan was documented, the document is defeated. The Tur, Mechaber, and other authorities state simply that it is paid, without distinguishing between an oral loan and a written one. The Rambam and Semag also imply this, for immediately afterward they discuss the practical remedy for a creditor who was trusted like two witnesses when the debtor repays, namely tearing the document or writing a receipt, indicating that the prior discussion also involved a document. I later found in Mishpetei Shevuot of Rav Hai Gaon a different formulation: if the creditor was trusted like three or four witnesses, he is believed against exactly that number, and if the debtor repays before more witnesses than the number specified, the consequences depend on the additional number, one additional witness imposing an oath and two additional witnesses imposing monetary liability. I am not worthy to dispute the Gaon, but since the later authorities did not make this distinction and the Rambam and Semag do not appear to adopt it, the practical ruling remains difficult and requires study.
Like three, etc. This applies specifically where he says simply, "like three witnesses." If he says, "You shall be believed against me like a beit din," or, "like a beit din of three," that is plainly stronger than ordinary credibility like two witnesses. Even an acknowledgment or repayment before another beit din would then not defeat the clause once the creditor sues before a different beit din, even according to Rabbi Yeshayah and the Rashba discussed below in seif katan 8. A receipt written in the creditor's own hand, however, certainly remains effective.
