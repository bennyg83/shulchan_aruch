(Shulchan Aruch, seif 1) “He collects without an oath.” The Sema’s reason is insufficient. In my opinion, where a creditor has trusted the debtor, the creditor has two potential claims: the written claim, under which the debtor must pay, and an oral claim, under which the creditor would require an oath. The creditor may relinquish the written claim and preserve the oral one. The reverse arrangement, in which the debtor trusts the creditor, cannot be construed as though the debtor imposed some unmentioned condition that the creditor perform a particular act. One might argue that the debtor likewise has two rights, to swear and be discharged or to shift the oath to the creditor, and that he relinquished only the latter. That cannot be correct, because the credibility clause applies equally whether the debtor claims full or partial payment. In a partial-payment claim he is subject to a Torah oath and cannot shift it; therefore the same grant of credibility must operate consistently in the full-payment case. The glossator suggests another basis: an ordinary credibility clause naturally means that the creditor is believed without an oath unless the wording or circumstances indicate otherwise. When a creditor accepts the debtor’s word while still taking a written instrument, however, complete credibility cannot have been intended, for otherwise there was no reason to write the document at all. The writing shows that the creditor intended at least to preserve an oath.
