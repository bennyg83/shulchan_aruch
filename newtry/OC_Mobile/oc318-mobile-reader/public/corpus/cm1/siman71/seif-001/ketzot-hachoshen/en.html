Even if the borrower had a migo. Rama previously ruled that a borrower can sometimes be believed through a migo despite a credibility clause. The Sema and Shach distinguish between a migo by which he could deny the very existence of the credibility clause, which remains effective, and a different migo after the credibility clause itself is established, which does not. Maharit disagrees with Maharam Halevi and argues that the essential distinction is between ordinary credibility and credibility equal to two witnesses. He proves from Tosafot at the beginning of Gittin that even where a document contains a credibility clause, heirs and purchasers are still advanced the claim that it was paid through a migo that the document was forged. Maharit concludes that a migo has the force of witnesses, and ordinary credibility cannot prevail against it. This proof is not decisive, because even if one writes credibility equal to two witnesses, the heirs can still say through a migo of forgery that the entire credibility clause itself never existed. This actually supports Maharam's distinction. Maharit further proves from Tosafot and the Rosh concerning a deposit made before witnesses, or where the depositor said, "Do not return it except before witnesses," that the custodian is nevertheless believed through a migo of unavoidable accident. But those cases do not involve a true contractual credibility clause. They merely remove ordinary credibility from the custodian and require stronger proof. A migo can replace that missing proof. Even the warning, "Do not return except before witnesses," is only a procedural warning, as Ran explains, not a complete grant of credibility to the depositor. Therefore the primary distinction is Maharam's: when the defendant could deny the very credibility clause, the migo helps; once the credibility clause is independently established, an ordinary migo does not defeat it, and all the more so where the creditor was granted credibility equal to two witnesses.
