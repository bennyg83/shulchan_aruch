The Maggid Mishneh writes that no Torah oath applies because the debtor granted credibility, and that only a heset oath applies whether he denies everything or admits part. Many have wondered why this credibility should be stronger than an oral claim. Its effect is only to neutralize the document; it should not exempt a partial admitter from the Torah oath. The Bach answers that the Torah oath for partial admission rests on the presumption that a person does not brazenly deny his creditor. Here, because the creditor trusted him, he is willing to deny; therefore even on a partial admission he is believed with a heset oath through the migo that he could have denied all. This is difficult, for by that reasoning even a heset oath should not be required, as the Gemara says concerning a claim against one’s son: since a person is bold toward the son, a partial admitter is like one returning a lost object and is exempt even from heset. The glossator answers that the cases are not comparable. A finder is exempt for public policy, and a defendant against the son is exempt from the newly generated partial-admission oath, whether because of the lack of brazenness or because of a migo. But no one suggested that his partial admission should exempt him from the oath he would have owed had he denied everything. In my opinion, the ordinary credibility granted to the debtor includes both a claim that he paid all and a claim that he paid part, as explained in siman 84, seif 5. Just as one claiming full payment swears only heset, so too one claiming partial payment, for the clause treats both alike. Although the Tur says the matter becomes like an oral claim, he means only that the credibility clause does not exempt the debtor from an oath altogether. The glossator offers another sound reason. If the debtor were required to take a Torah oath, he could not reverse it onto the creditor. If he declined to swear, he would have to pay without any oath by the creditor. The credibility clause would thus worsen his position, because without it the creditor could not collect without swearing. It is untenable to make the debtor choose between a Torah oath and requiring the creditor to swear, since the two consequences are inseparable.
