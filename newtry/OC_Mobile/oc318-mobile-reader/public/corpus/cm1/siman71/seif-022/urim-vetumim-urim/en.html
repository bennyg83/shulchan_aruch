By a general ban. But not by an oath, because heirs do not swear on an uncertain claim merely to exempt themselves. If, however, the lender claims with certainty that their father instructed them that he had not paid, they must swear that their father did not so instruct them.
That they are obligated to pay. Even if they claim, "Our father instructed us that he paid," and even if payment is alleged after the due date, the lender can say, "I trusted your father, but I do not trust you." The document therefore returns to its full force and he collects. It appears that according to this approach he may even collect from purchasers, because just as we do not raise the claim of payment for heirs, we likewise do not raise it for purchasers. This is certainly so where the debtor himself admitted that he did not pay, for there is no concern of collusion: had he remained silent, the document would retain its force because we would not plead payment, and it is only by his statement that we know anything at all. The same rule therefore applies whether the defensive claim would be raised for a purchaser or for an heir.
