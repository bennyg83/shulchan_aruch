The Sema notes that the Rosh, whose view this is, indicates that even if the father died after the due date and the heirs say that he paid after the due date, or that they themselves saw him pay, they are not believed. The Shach cites Responsa Maharshdam 53, which questions this as contrary to the fundamental rule that beit din advances claims for heirs. The Shach answers that such claims are advanced only where the father himself would have been believed without relying on a ne'emanut clause. Here the father was believed only because the lender granted him ne'emanut, and the lender can say, "I trusted your father, not you." He need not deny that the father ever said he paid. Responsa Maharanach 21 makes this clear.
Witnesses. The Sema asks why, even if the heirs bring witnesses, the lender cannot say that he granted their father ne'emanut because the father would not dare lie to his face, whereas he may lie before witnesses. He answers that a father ordinarily does not lie to his children, and further, that the concern about greater audacity applies where the ne'emanut is used to extract money, not where it is used merely to exempt the estate. The Taz offers another reason: the lender must have trusted the borrower even against the lender's own heirs, otherwise the arrangement would itself harm the borrower.
