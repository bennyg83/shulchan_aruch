Even before the due date, etc. This ruling requires study. Its source is Baal HaTerumot, gate 24. He first questions whether a borrower believed by a special credibility clause may say that he paid before the due date, and then concludes that he may because "since he stipulated, he stipulated." If the clause merely says in ordinary terms that the borrower is believed to say he paid, why should that include payment before maturity? The entire purpose of the clause is to remove the ordinary evidentiary strength of the document and make the debt like an oral loan. Yet even with an oral loan, one cannot ordinarily claim payment before maturity. Baal HaTerumot's case must therefore be one where the clause expressly says that the borrower is believed "at any time" to say he paid. The words "at any time" clearly include the period before maturity. Only then does the conclusion, "since he stipulated, he stipulated," follow. With a generic credibility clause there is no reason to make it stronger than an oral loan, and the present unqualified ruling requires study.
Because we claim that their father paid. This is difficult in light of the rule below that we do not make for orphans an uncommon claim such as unavoidable accident merely because their father could have made it. Payment before maturity is also uncommon. Why, then, should we claim for the heirs that their father paid during the term? Gidulei Terumah and Knesset HaGedolah already connect the two questions. One could distinguish that in the deposit case the father's claim of unavoidable accident would have required a Torah oath, and if he could not swear he would pay under the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. The Torah therefore gave significant weight to the oath, and we do not create such an uncommon claim for heirs who cannot make the father's oath. Here, if the father had claimed payment, there would be no Torah oath, only heset, and the monetary liability would end with his claim. Perhaps for that reason we are more willing to make an uncommon claim for the heirs. This distinction is nevertheless strained. Tosafos at the beginning of Gittin appears not to accept such a division, as indicated by its comparison to the claim of forgery for heirs. See below, siman 108, where I discuss this further.
