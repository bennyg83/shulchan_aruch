Nevertheless, with a general ban, etc. The Mechaber follows his ruling in siman 69, seif 5, that heirs need not take a hesset oath to exempt themselves; a general ban suffices. This is the view of the Rosh and Tur there. The Tur here, however, copies Baal HaTerumot's view that they take a hesset oath, as he cites that view in siman 69. See Perishah.
That they are obligated to pay. The Tur, after citing Baal HaTerumot, quotes the Rosh: if the borrower died and the orphans say, 'Our father told us it was paid,' their words are of no consequence. The lender says, 'I trusted your father if he himself would say he paid, but I did not trust you, and I say your father never told you anything.' If, however, they bring witnesses, etc. From this it is clear that according to the Rosh, even if their father died after the due date and the heirs say that he paid after the due date, or that they themselves saw him pay, they are not believed. This is the Mechaber's intent in the 'some say they must pay' opinion. The Rosh says that heirs are believed to exempt themselves even without an oath only where the lender produces the deceased borrower's authenticated handwriting, as the Tur writes at the end of siman 69, and all the more so with an oral loan. But where the lender produces a properly witnessed and authenticated promissory note of their father that contains no credibility clause for either party, the lender swears on the document and collects even from the borrower's heirs, as the Tur writes below in siman 108, seif 5, and the Mechaber likewise. Here, because the borrower has a credibility clause, Baal HaTerumot holds that this credibility benefits the heirs too. The Rosh holds it does not benefit them, and the case therefore returns to the ordinary law as though no one had been granted credibility: the lender swears on the witnessed document and collects from the borrower's heirs.
Unless they bring witnesses that their father said before them that it was paid. The Tur likewise writes this in the Rosh's name. One might ask: even if they bring witnesses, why should it help? The lender can say that he trusted the father because he knew the father would not dare lie in his presence, unlike statements made before his children, where he might brazenly lie, as I wrote above in seif katan 32 regarding credibility granted by a borrower to a lender and in seif katan 38 regarding collection outside the debtor's presence. The answer is that we do not apply such reasoning to a father's statement to his children, because it is not his way to lie to them. Moreover, above the principle was used to enable extraction of money; here it is being used merely to exempt the heirs.
