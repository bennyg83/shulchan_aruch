For he believed that he had not given him his name, and that he had not given him his part, and that which was what I wrote:
He has no power to do so. See Shach, seif katan 54, citing Raavad that if the borrower proves that he paid the other named creditor, that payment is effective. At first sight it is difficult to understand why proof should differ from the other creditor's own admission. It appears that before any dispute arose between the two named creditors, the borrower was entitled to pay each one his share, because the note was written to "so and so and so and so," even though ne'emanut was granted to the bearer. Since the bearer had not informed him that the entire debt belonged to him alone, the wording of the note effectively authorized the borrower to pay the other named creditor as well. Once the dispute arose, however, and the bearer asserted that the entire note was his, the borrower could no longer pay anyone but the bearer. Since the borrower had granted ne'emanut to the bearer, and the other creditor knew that the note was written in that form and delivered into the bearer's possession, that circumstance is treated as an admission that the entire note belongs to the bearer, except that the borrower retained authority to pay half to the other creditor before being informed otherwise. If the borrower now claims that he paid before the dispute arose, he is not believed unless he proves it with witnesses. Shach disagrees and holds that because the note says that the bearer is trusted, the borrower has no authority to pay until he knows who the bearer is. Consequently, even if he proves that he paid the other creditor, he must still pay the bearer the full amount, because payment to the other person does not count as payment. When the Mechaber concludes, "But if they were partners in the debt," this means that it is independently known that the debt belonged to both of them. Then the dispute concerns only one half, whereas in the opening case the controversy concerns the entire debt, as Shach explains in seif katan 55. Yet what novelty is taught by saying that if the parties are partners he is liable only for his own half? The entire section, including the precise nature of their claims and Shach's discussion of the oath in siman 53, is difficult to understand. It therefore appears that Shach understands the clause granting ne'emanut to the bearer as though the borrower obligated himself only to the bearer. The bearer is the legal holder of the note and is believed to say that the money represented by the note belongs to him, and that the other person's name was inserted merely for convenience. It further appears that even if the bearer admits that the original loan money belonged to both creditors but claims that he later acquired the note from the other, he is believed. A debt instrument can be acquired by delivery without a written assignment according to Shach in siman 66, seif katan 18. Even according to the side that requires both writing and delivery, as discussed there in my Biurim and in Tumim, see also the responsum of Rama that the other creditor cannot waive the debt. Although partners are generally not believed to say they took partnership property from one another where they could simply claim, "I purchased it," here the bearer is believed because he could have claimed that all the money was his from the outset. His admission that it began as partnership property, coupled with a claim that he later purchased the other's share, is therefore comparable to property not known to remain partnership property, where a claim of purchase is believed, as explained in siman 179. Once he is believed against the other creditor that the whole note belongs to him, he is likewise believed against the borrower that payment made to the other creditor was not payment. This differs from siman 50, concerning a note payable to any bearer, where a receipt from any bearer may be effective. As I explained there, one must establish that the note was actually in the receipt writer's possession when the receipt was written. That is absent here. It is possible that this is itself why the clause "and he trusted the bearer" is treated as though the borrower obligated himself only to the bearer. Ne'emanut ordinarily means only that the holder is believed to say, "I was not repaid," but this too can be included, because when he says, "The entire note is mine, and payment to the other person does not count," he is in substance saying that this note has not been repaid. By contrast, where the note merely names two persons and does not grant ne'emanut to the bearer, its wording is equivalent to an express statement that the money belongs to both of them. The holder is then not believed to say either that the money was originally his alone or that he later purchased the other's share, just as one cannot claim purchase of a note written in another person's name, as explained in siman 66. Payment to the other named creditor therefore counts for that half. As to the remaining half, the bearer must swear, because the other creditor admits having received the entire payment and perhaps he received it with the bearer's authority or had acquired the debt from him. Where the note includes ne'emanut, however, the bearer is believed that payment to the other does not discharge his half, just as where one said, "Do not repay me except in the prescribed manner." The Mechaber's concluding case, "if they were partners," then means that witnesses independently establish that the money originally belonged to both and that they were partners in the debt. In that situation the bearer cannot claim a later purchase, since one is not believed to claim purchase of property known to belong to a partnership, as explained in Bava Kamma 79. Accordingly, the half paid to the other creditor is valid payment, and the litigation concerns only the remaining half.
