Since he granted credibility to whoever produces the note, he cannot say that he paid, etc. The Bach writes that the Rashba's ruling, cited below in siman 77, that whoever holds the note may collect with it even when the note contains no credibility clause, concerns a case where the other creditor does not claim that payment was made, such as when he is not present. Here, however, the other creditor says that he was paid, and the holder could not collect without the clause granting credibility to whoever produces the note. This distinction is unnecessary. The present ruling teaches that because the debtor granted such credibility, the holder collects without an oath. Accordingly, even without that clause, if the other creditor says that he was paid, the holder of the note may still collect his half upon taking an oath.
He cannot say, etc. Mahara ibn Chaim writes that this implies that if it were clearly known that the debtor paid the other creditor, the debtor would thereby be discharged, notwithstanding the provision that whoever produces the note may collect the entire debt. That provision means only that the debtor may not insist on paying each creditor his own share. Once he actually paid the other creditor's share, however, the payment is effective. The wording, "since he granted credibility to whoever produces the note, he cannot say that he paid," indicates that the problem is merely his lack of credibility, and that known payment would suffice. This is not compelling. The meaning may instead be that by granting credibility to the producer of the note, the debtor subjected himself to that producer. He therefore cannot discharge himself by paying the other creditor, because payment to the other creditor is not legally considered payment while the note remains in this creditor's possession.
If they were partners in the debt, etc. The Sema limits the ruling to partners. When they are partners, payment made to one of them has at least some legal basis; when they are not partners, the holder collects his share from the debtor, and the debtor then recovers from the person who received the excess. The practical difference is where the debtor owns intermediate-quality land and the recipient only inferior land, or where one is easy and the other difficult to collect from. In my view, this distinction is strained. Even when they are partners, the admitting partner cannot prejudice his colleague if he has no assets, yet when he has assets beit din descends upon his property. If the rule were truly that one first collects from the admitting party whenever he has assets, the same should apply even where they were not partners. Rather, the Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch did not mean that one collects first from inferior property of the admitting creditor while the debtor has intermediate property. That would reward wrongdoing, for by receiving payment improperly he would gain the advantage of leaving the holder with inferior land. Moreover, the holder's lien and contractual credibility run against the debtor. He may say, "You owe me; pay me from your property," without regard to his colleague's admission. The Tur, citing the Rosh below in siman 108, rules even more strongly that in an ordinary partnership note the holder cannot prejudice his partner by an admission, since one business partner has no power to cause his fellow any loss by his own admission. How, then, could the admission here be effective so as to reduce the holder to collecting only inferior property? Since the admitting creditor is not believed regarding the alleged payment, there is no reason to believe him regarding the order of collection. Credibility cannot be divided in half. It therefore appears that he is not believed at all. Indeed, even if witnesses testify that the debtor paid the other creditor, and even if the creditor to whom credibility was granted admits this, the holder can say: "You should have paid only the person producing the note, whom you accepted as credible." The phrase "if they were partners in the debt" comes only to distinguish the amount collected. If they were not partners, the creditor whom the debtor accepted as credible collects the entire debt. The statement that, when the admitting creditor has assets, beit din descends upon his property is merely the ordinary practical course, in order not to make the debtor pay and then require a second recovery from the admitting creditor. It concerns a case where no legal difference results, such as where their properties are of equal quality. The same practical shortcut applies even where they were not partners and the entire debt belongs to the holder, provided their assets are equivalent. Partnership is mentioned only to teach that if the admitting partner has no assets, the holder receives only his own share. If they were not partners, he would receive the whole debt. This is also the plain meaning of the responsum of the Rosh, the source of this ruling: where it is known that they were partners and one admits receiving his share, the holder does not collect that same share twice from the debtor, even though the note says that whoever produces it may collect the entire debt.
