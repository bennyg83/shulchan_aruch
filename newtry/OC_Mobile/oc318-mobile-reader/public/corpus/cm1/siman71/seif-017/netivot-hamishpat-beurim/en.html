See Shach, who distinguishes between a ketubah, where an exemption is ineffective against the heirs without a kinyan, and an ordinary loan, where an exemption made at the time of the loan is effective without a kinyan. Ketzot HaChoshen wrote that with marriage one can never have a "time of the loan." This is imprecise, because the time of marriage is equivalent to the time of the loan, since she marries him only on that understanding, as Tumim writes. As for the Ritva's statement that this is included among matters acquired by verbal declaration, he wrote that only as an added novelty: even though such matters are acquired by verbal declaration, that declaration is nevertheless not sufficiently strong to uproot the oath instituted by Chazal for collection from orphans. I was uncertain about one who says, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," according to the view that the borrower is not believed to say, "I repaid before so and so, who have since died," or "they went overseas," or where the lender says, "Do not repay me except before these specified witnesses." Does such a stipulation operate against heirs? It appears that the limitation concerning heirs applies specifically to ne'emanut, which depends upon the lender's own declaration. We say that since his declaration was not directed toward the heirs, it is ineffective against them. By contrast, "Do not repay me except before witnesses" means that the borrower is not to be believed unless he establishes payment by proof, and absent such proof he is not believed. Whatever their father himself could not have claimed, we likewise do not claim on behalf of the heirs. The same is true if the lender dies: according to the view that the borrower is not believed to say that he repaid before named witnesses who have died, the lender's heirs collect from the borrower without an oath.
Recipients of a gift are also included among his agents. See the Tur, who states this rule regarding ne'emanut granted to the lender and to the lender's agents. Recipients of a gift from the lender are included among those agents. Thus, if the lender gives the note to another as a gift, the recipient need not take the oath otherwise imposed upon him, as explained in siman 66, seif 16. Certainly, however, with respect to persons who received a gift from the borrower, the ne'emanut does not operate, as explained below, because ne'emanut is ineffective against purchasers.
