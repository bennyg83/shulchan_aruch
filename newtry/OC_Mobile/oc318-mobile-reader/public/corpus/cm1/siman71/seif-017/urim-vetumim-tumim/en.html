Unless he explicitly stated that he exempts him from it. Shach asks from Even HaEzer, where Rama cites authorities holding that even if the husband granted the wife credibility against his heirs, she must still take the special heir-facing oath when collecting after his death. The difficulty is not only on Rama. Tur here cites Rabbenu Chananel that an exemption can be effective against heirs even though it is not effective against purchasers. Yet in Even HaEzer Rosh cites Rabbenu Chananel as ruling like Abba Shaul that even an express exemption referring to heirs does not remove the heir-facing ketubah oath. To resolve this, Beit Yosef cites Ritva's responsum in Even HaEzer siman 98. Ritva explains Rabbenu Chananel as follows: when the husband says the wife is exempt from oath against him and his heirs, we do not necessarily interpret the language as waiving the special oath required when collecting from orphans. It may have been intended only for the guardian's oath and the oath of partial collection. At first glance this is difficult. Rabbenu Chananel follows R. Shimon and Abba Shaul, according to whom the wife can still be required to swear when she claims her ketubah from heirs despite ordinary credibility. Yet R. Shimon disagrees with the earlier Tannaim regarding the husband's ability to impose a guardian's oath. If the husband himself cannot impose that oath, how can we say his exemption language was directed to it? And the partial-payment oath normally arises precisely when she collects the ketubah, when in any event she faces the heir-facing oath. The same interpretation appears in the Shaarei Rif tradition, which says an ordinary exemption from the husband and heirs was intended for the guardian's oath, not the heir-facing collection oath. The answer is that this tradition distinguishes between the husband and the heirs. R. Shimon can hold that during the husband's lifetime he may not demand the guardian's oath under those circumstances, yet after his death the heirs can demand an accounting oath regarding management of their or inherited property. Then the clause "from me and from my heirs" can operate distributively: "From me" exempts her from claims such as partial payment. "From my heirs" can exempt her from the guardian-accounting oath they might otherwise demand. It need not waive the special oath required to collect from an estate. There is still a textual difficulty because the Mishnah says that when she is not claiming the ketubah, the heirs do not make her swear. That seems to imply R. Shimon himself limits the heirs' guardian oath. The Shaarei Rif approach likely follows Rav Hai Gaon, cited by Rosh: if the husband had the right to impose the guardian's oath but did not do so during his life, his silence is treated as waiver and the heirs cannot later revive it. R. Shimon can disagree with the earlier Tannaim regarding the husband's present ability to administer the oath while still allowing heirs some later accounting power when there was no prior waiver. The Gemara's discussion can then be read as asking why the Mishnah needed ordinary credibility language in the noncollection case if the heirs could not administer the oath anyway. The answer depends on the precise Tannaitic framework.
Therefore, I think the better interpretation is that the dispute between the first Tanna and Abba Shaul concerns how broadly we read the original waiver language itself. The first Tanna does not assume a husband writes an exemption merely for some hypothetical future guardianship unless the language clearly points there. Thus an exemption "from my heirs" is naturally read as removing the estate-collection oath. Abba Shaul is more restrictive. He permits us to say the husband anticipated future guardianship and intended the waiver for that administrative oath, while not giving up the stronger protection Chazal imposed when his estate later pays the ketubah. Because the claimant controls the document, ambiguous exemption language is construed narrowly against her. This also explains why the Gemara moved away from one earlier interpretation: what seems an unlikely future contingency under the first Tanna can still be the very ambiguity Abba Shaul invokes. Rosh's citation of Rabbenu Chananel should likewise be understood through this narrow-reading approach, not as saying an express and unmistakable waiver of the heir-facing oath can never work. Beit Shmuel seems to understand Rosh more absolutely, but that creates needless conflict among the sources. Rosh himself says "and so wrote Rav Alfasi." That cannot refer straightforwardly to Rif's Hilchot, because Rif there follows the view that an exemption from the husband and heirs can be effective. It more naturally refers to the Shaarei Rif explanation that ordinary language is presumed aimed at the guardian's oath. If Rosh meant Rabbenu Chananel held that even the most explicit possible waiver of the heir-facing oath is invalid, his equation with that tradition would be very difficult. There is further support from Rosh and Tur's treatment of whether heirs can impose a guardian's oath at all. Their rulings fit the Shaarei Rif framework only if we distinguish between general exemption language and an express waiver directed to a specific oath. Accordingly, the apparent contradiction disappears. In the ketubah context, ordinary words "you are exempt from me and my heirs" can be interpreted narrowly as referring to administrative or other oaths, so the special heir-facing collection oath remains unless it was expressly waived. In an ordinary lender-borrower relationship, however, there is no guardian's oath naturally lurking in the background. If the debtor says the lender is exempt from oath "against me and my heirs," the obvious subject is precisely the oath that would arise when collecting from the heirs. There is no alternative meaningful oath to which the language can readily refer. Therefore the exemption should work against heirs when stated clearly. That is why Rama need not object here even though he cites Rabbenu Chananel's ketubah rule in Even HaEzer. Rivash contains language that may sound stricter even for an ordinary loan, but he also recognizes the power of a formal written undertaking. In light of the earlier authorities, the practical rule stated here is the more compelling one.
Against him and against his heirs. Sema writes elsewhere that if the heirs themselves say, "We repaid you after our father's death," their father's credibility clause should not protect the lender against that claim. I do not understand this. It is true that in Perek HaKotev the Mishnah distinguishes between past and future conduct. Rashi explains that even if the husband exempted the wife from oath against him and his heirs, if she later becomes guardian over the heirs' own property, they can require an accounting oath because the father could not waive claims concerning property that became theirs. But Rosh expressly distinguishes that from a partially paid ketubah. If the husband granted the wife credibility against the heirs and she later admits receiving partial payment from them, Rosh says she remains exempt from the ordinary partial-payment oath. Why? The husband exempted her from any partial-payment issue that might arise on that ketubah. He could reasonably anticipate that the heirs might be unable to pay the ketubah all at once and would make installments. By contrast, he did not necessarily anticipate that she would later become guardian of the heirs' independent property. The partial-payment case necessarily concerns payments made by the heirs themselves. The whole reason a partially paid claimant must swear is that partial payment creates suspicion that the balance too may have been paid. If the only partial payment was made by the heirs after the father's death, it would make no sense to infer that the father had paid the rest before his death, especially where the obligation was not then due. The heirs' claim is that they themselves paid the balance. Nevertheless, Rosh says the father's exemption against the heirs covers that situation. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 98 codify the same rule. This directly contradicts Sema's limitation. Accordingly, where the original debtor explicitly granted the creditor credibility against himself and his heirs, the clause can apply even to the heirs' own later claim that they personally repaid, unless the wording clearly limits the waiver to acts of the father alone.
And a recipient of a gift. Mechaber includes a gift recipient among those covered by the relevant successor language. At first glance, why is this necessary? Below Mechaber rules that ordinary credibility does not help against purchasers, and Ran explicitly says the same is true of recipients of gifts. If so, whether a gift recipient falls within the term "successors" seems irrelevant. One possibility is that the case concerns a deathbed gift. Ran says expressly that ordinary credibility can operate differently there, because a deathbed recipient stands more like an heir than an ordinary purchaser. A second explanation is more useful. The reason ordinary credibility sometimes fails against purchasers is concern for collusion. The borrower and lender might cooperate after the lender transferred some right to a third party, thereby harming that third party. Suppose, however, the lender had a debt note against the borrower and then sold or gifted that note to someone else with proper transfer and warranty. The borrower had earlier granted the lender credibility. If the lender later seeks to collect some offsetting debt or lien through the transferred note, the ordinary collusion concern can be weaker because the lender himself could have forgiven the transferred note while alive. If he did so wrongfully, he might owe damages, but where he gave warranty he remains financially exposed anyway. In that setting, whether the transferee by gift is included among the lender's "successors" can matter. Mechaber therefore teaches that a gift recipient can stand within the chain of delegated credibility and collect without an oath where the structure otherwise allows it. This may not accord with Ran's narrower theory that a person does not readily place himself in the position of a tortfeasor by destroying another's right. But most authorities do not follow Ran so broadly. Thus the inclusion of a gift recipient has practical significance.
