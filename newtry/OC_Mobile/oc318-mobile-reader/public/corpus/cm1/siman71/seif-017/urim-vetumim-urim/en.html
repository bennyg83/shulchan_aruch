And he comes to collect from his heirs. The credibility clause does not help. We plead for the heirs that perhaps in the borrower's presence the lender would not have dared to lie, whereas against the heirs, who cannot personally expose the falsehood, he may lie. The borrower therefore trusted him only against himself, not against his children. Sema, and this is clear.
Unless, etc. In Even HaEzer, siman 98, the Rama cites Rabbeinu Chananel and those who follow him, who hold that even so money may not be extracted from orphans without an oath. If a kinyan was made, however, all agree that the credibility clause is effective against heirs. See Tumim, where I clarified at length that between lender and borrower all agree that it is effective, and certainly so where it is written in the document, which has the force of a kinyan for this purpose.
For then he collects, etc. This refers to heirs who say, "Perhaps our father paid you." If they claim, "We ourselves paid you," the Sema writes that the lender must swear and the father's credibility clause does not help. According to his reasoning, even if they say with certainty, "We know that our father paid you," they should likewise be entitled to an oath by virtue of a migo that they could have said, "We paid you ourselves." See Tumim. I wrote there that this ruling is incorrect, and that even when the heirs claim that they themselves paid, the credibility clause remains effective. See there.
And against those who derive authority from him. If the document says "those who come with his permission," there is a dispute between the Ramah and the Tur. The Bach agrees, and Rivash, siman 494, likewise implies that heirs are not included; the phrase may refer only to agents acting under a power of attorney. This also appears to be the Shach's view.
And recipients of gifts, etc. One may ask what practical difference this makes, since a credibility clause is ordinarily ineffective against purchasers or gift recipients. See Tumim. I wrote that it matters for recipients of a deathbed gift, or where the borrower gives to another person a debt instrument that he holds against his lender, in a case governed by the rule that each party may collect his respective debt. In that situation the credibility clause certainly has effect, and the ruling teaches that such a recipient is included among those deriving authority from him. See there.
