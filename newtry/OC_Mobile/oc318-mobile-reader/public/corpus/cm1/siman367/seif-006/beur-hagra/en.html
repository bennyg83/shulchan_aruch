And he swore. Specifically where he swore. Without an oath it is as though he said, “Let it remain in your possession for me,” so it becomes a deposit and he acquires what is in his possession. Likewise, regarding one who robbed his father, the Mishnah says “and swore”; without the oath the rule is different.
Even if he admitted to the convert and converted it into, etc. See Beer HaGolah and the Maggid Mishneh. This is the Rambam's view: where he admitted before the convert died, he must remove the stolen property from his possession; without such admission he does not acquire it and gives it to the kohanim. The Raavad, however, holds that this applies specifically where he converted the obligation into a loan, because the relevant Gemara's statement applies only according to Rabbi Yose HaGelili. According to Rabbi Akiva, if he did not convert it into a debt, it belongs to the kohanim. This is also the Tur's view. In our time the Tur directs it to charity because the Beis HaMikdash is not standing, whereas the Shulchan Aruch uses the Rambam's formulation “even if he admitted and converted it,” to emphasize the strength of Rabbi Akiva's ruling. The Gemara appears to support the Raavad's reading unless Rambam had a different text. Rashi and Tosafos likewise appear to hold that according to Rabbi Akiva it goes to the kohanim even without conversion into a debt; see Mishneh LaMelech.
(Collected note) He is obligated, etc. Rambam writes that this applies where he admitted before the convert died, but if he did not admit he must give it to the kohanim. He interprets the rule that he must remove the stolen property from his possession like the Mishnah's case, where he need not specifically give it to the kohanim. “Converted it into a loan” is not essential, and is stated only to demonstrate the breadth of Rabbi Akiva's position. The Raavad understands it specifically, while Rashi and Tosafos understand the required removal as payment to the kohanim even without converting it into a debt. Rambam's reason is that the Gemara compares the case to the Mishnah.
He is obligated to remove it, etc. This is Rambam's language. It means, as stated in the preceding siman, that he may even transfer it to a creditor, according to Rambam's approach. The Gemara compares the cases, and Rambam states the same structure concerning one who robbed his father and one who robbed a convert. The Tur, however, writes that he should give it to charity, following his different approach that payment to a creditor does not suffice.
