One who robbed his father and swore to him. The Beit Yosef was uncertain whether the law specifically requires that he swore to his father. Darkei Moshe concludes that it does, and I discussed this in the Derishah. See there.
He makes an accounting with his brothers. Meaning, since the stolen object itself is no longer intact in his possession, he merely owes a debt to pay his brothers their shares. It is therefore sufficient that he account with them and say, "Such-and-such amount of our father's money is in my possession. Take your portions from me," or, "Each of you take an equivalent amount from another part of the inheritance, and we will divide the remainder equally." If the stolen object itself still exists in his possession, by contrast, he must physically remove it from his possession in order to fulfill the Torah's law of restitution, and afterward he may take an equivalent amount for his own inheritance share from elsewhere. This is what the Mechaber means by "and he makes an accounting with them."
Regarding the principal: the Mishnah and Rambam, chapter 8, say "the principal and the additional fifth." Since the additional fifth is not practiced nowadays, the Tur and Mechaber did not mention it. See the Perishah.
Therefore he gives the stolen property to his brothers, etc., until "and if he has no brothers, etc." The Mishnah states: one who robbed his father, swore falsely to him, and the father died, must pay the principal and the additional fifth to his sons or brothers. If he does not wish to do so, or has none, he borrows and creditors come and collect. Rabbi Yose says in the Gemara: even to a charity purse. Rambam and the Mechaber understand "his sons or brothers" to refer to the robber's own sons and brothers. In their view, the Mishnah's placing sons before brothers is not exact. The robber's brothers, who are direct heirs of the victim, should receive the property before the robber's own sons. Tosafos also interpret "sons and brothers" as the robber's, but hold that the Mishnah's order is deliberate. Regarding the robber's own inheritance share in the stolen property, his sons stand closer to his share than his father does, because the sons inherit the robber's portion through their father. Rashi, Rosh, and Tur understand "sons and brothers" to mean the sons and brothers of the victim. It is as though the Mishnah said that the robber must return the property to his own brothers, who are sons of the victim, and to the victim's brothers, who are the robber's paternal uncles. This is the dissenting view Rama cites below in seif 5. See what I write there.
Some say that he himself takes nothing. Meaning, the robber himself does not take back a share directly from them. If he is poor, however, his creditor may collect that share, as the Mechaber concludes. In the Perishah and Derishah I explained the basis of the dispute.
If he is poor and has nothing, etc. This is how the Tur interprets the Mishnah's phrase "if he has none." It means that the robber has no resources with which to forgo his own share. He therefore borrows an amount corresponding to his own share of the stolen property and keeps the loan proceeds. The lender then collects that share from the brothers out of the stolen property they received. The other two shares remain with the brothers, because they are their inheritance shares and the robber transferred the property to them on that understanding. Rambam and the Mechaber, however, explain "if he has none" to mean that he has neither brothers nor sons. He then borrows from others an amount equal to the entire stolen property and retains the loan money, while the creditor later comes and collects the stolen property from him. In this way the stolen property at least leaves his own possession, satisfying restitution. All the more so, if during his father's lifetime the robber already incurred debts to others, he can fulfill restitution by paying those creditors with the stolen property when he has no sons or brothers. From Rambam's words it follows that he need not return the stolen object itself to more distant heirs other than brothers and sons. The Tur's interpretation differs: he must always return the stolen property, or its value, to his father's heirs in order of proximity. When he returns it to his paternal uncles or more distant relatives, he borrows an amount equal to the entire stolen property and the lender then collects it from those relatives, since the robber himself is his father's sole heir and those more distant relatives are not. When the robber has brothers who inherit together with him, however, he borrows and the creditor collects only the robber's proportional share, as explained above. In the Perishah and Derishah I further clarified the basis of the dispute. It also follows that Tosafos do not follow Rambam's law, but rather the legal approach of Rashi and Rosh. Tosafos agree with Rambam only in interpreting "his sons or brothers" as the robber's own sons or brothers, but not in Rambam's reason or ultimate legal structure. Tosafos hold that the robber's sons are closer with respect to receiving his inheritance share in the stolen property, as explained above. I set all this out in the Derishah with full reasoning, unlike the Beit Yosef, Kesef Mishneh, and Darkei Moshe, who wrote that Tosafos follow Rambam. I was later told that Bedek HaBayit likewise states that Tosafos do not follow Rambam. See there.
Or to charity. Even according to the dissenting view of Rashi and Rosh, where the robber returns the property to charity in order to fulfill restitution, namely where no known heir of the victim can be found except distant relatives, as the Tur explains, he may first borrow from another person and then give the property to charity. The lender may afterward collect from the charity fund in the manner explained above at the end of siman 117. I clarified all this in the Derishah and Perishah. See there and examine carefully.
And some disagree. Their position is that if the robber has no sons, he gives the property to his father's brothers. This is the printed wording, but it does not appear correct. The proper reading is: "if the robber has no brothers, he gives it to his father's brothers." This is the view of Rashi, Rosh, and Tur, as explained above in interpreting the Mishnah's phrase "sons and brothers." "Sons" means the victim's sons, who are the robber's brothers, and "brothers" means the victim's brothers, who are the robber's paternal uncles. One cannot say that Rama wrote the printed version according to Tosafos, who hold that the robber's sons precede others, because then he should have written, "if the robber has no sons, he gives it to the robber's brothers." Why instead would he say to give it to his father's brothers? The correct text is therefore as I have written. Examine carefully.
