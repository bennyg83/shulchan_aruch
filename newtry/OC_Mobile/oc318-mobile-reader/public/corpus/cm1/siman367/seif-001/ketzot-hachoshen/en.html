Until they come and he informs them. Bach reads Tur and Rosh as requiring the robber to send notice to the owner wherever he is. Sema says Rambam does not require this and interprets Rama's wording as meaning that when the owner later comes to the robber's city, the robber must then inform him. The Shach argues that Rambam too requires sending notice, though the robber need not personally pursue the owner. I do not think notice is required where no oath was taken. The Gemara says that once the robber knows whom he robbed and is ready to return the property, it is treated like a deposit in his hands, as though the owner had said, "Let it remain with you." Rashba explains that because the money is available for return, there is no benefit in transferring it elsewhere. Accordingly, even if the robber has not formally admitted before the owner, once he himself wishes to restore the property and is prepared to do so, it has the law of a deposit and he need not chase the owner or even send notice.
But if he swore falsely. Sema says the reason he must pursue the owner is the verse requiring him to give the property to its owner on the day of his guilt offering. The Shach objects that the Gemara explains the reason as the need for atonement. These are not contradictory. The Gemara's requirement of actual delivery for the sworn robber is itself derived from the verse. Because he requires atonement for the false oath, the Torah demands that restitution actually reach the owner's hand before the atonement is complete. Sema's verse and the Gemara's atonement rationale are two sides of the same rule.
Because the owners already despaired. Rambam explains the sworn robber's stricter duty partly by saying that the owners have despaired after the oath. The Shach asks why Rambam introduced this reason when the Gemara already says that one who needs atonement is not discharged until the property reaches the owner. Rambam is answering Tosafot's question: if the robber later admits in the owner's presence and the owner knowingly leaves the money with him, why is that not simply a new deposit? The answer is that the prior false oath caused the owner to despair and cease relying on repayment. Even after the robber says he now wishes to return, the owner's confidence is not restored merely by words. Therefore actual delivery is required. This also supports the conclusion that without a false oath, no notice is necessary. If ordinary return always required notice, the Gemara would not need the special atonement rationale in the sworn case. The special problem is precisely that the false oath destroyed the owner's reliance and therefore words alone no longer recreate a deposit. Tosafot Yom Tov understood Rambam similarly: for an unsworn robber, even notification is unnecessary. In the sworn case, actual pursuit and delivery are required.
