He may choose to collect from average-quality land. See Sema seif katan 5 through his reference to the Perishah, who writes that according to the second opinion the case concerns a seller who died. The meaning is that the Sema's first explanation too was offered only according to that second opinion, because according to the first opinion there is no difficulty at all. He therefore gives two resolutions within the second opinion. This is also the reading in the Perishah.
He cannot collect. See Sema seif katan 7, who writes, "For this reason I was precise..." This reason is drawn from Bava Kamma 8 and Tosafot there. Tosafot ask why we need the reason that the purchaser deliberately chose land unsuitable for this creditor. Even without that reason, the purchaser should be able to say that he left the creditor an available place from which to collect, just as with other average-quality land. Tosafot answer that this additional reason is needed only where the purchaser bought inferior-quality land, because the creditor can otherwise force Shimon to give him that inferior land. But where the purchaser bought superior-quality land, the defense that he left average-quality land available is sufficient. This is somewhat difficult, because the Gemara itself also states the reason of deliberate selection in the case where he bought superior-quality land. It is further difficult according to the opinion cited by the Rama in siman 102 seif 1 that a creditor cannot compel the debtor to give him inferior land. Tosafot's question would then return. In my view the principal reason is as follows. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 4, explains that when one purchaser buys average-quality land and leaves comparable average-quality land with the first purchaser, the second purchaser may say that he left the creditor a place from which to collect because, just as Chazal protected a first purchaser where unencumbered property remains with the debtor, they protected a second purchaser against the first purchaser where comparable property remains with the first. According to that rationale, if at the time of collection the comparable average-quality land remaining with the first purchaser has deteriorated and become inferior, the creditor may collect from the second purchaser, because at the actual time of collection comparable property no longer remains. The defense that "I left you a place from which to collect" works only if such property still exists when collection is sought, just as a first purchaser cannot rely on property once left with the debtor if that unencumbered property was later destroyed. The same is true of the second purchaser against the first. But where the second purchaser bought superior or inferior land, the additional reason is that he deliberately exerted himself to buy land that was not appropriate for this creditor. That reason depends on the circumstances at the time of purchase. Therefore, if he bought superior land and left average-quality land with the first purchaser, and later the superior land in his hands declined to average quality while the first purchaser's average land declined to inferior quality, so that at the time of collection the creditor's ordinary lien would now attach to the second purchaser's land, the creditor still cannot collect from him. The second purchaser was not negligent at the time of purchase and deliberately chose land then unsuitable for the creditor. Likewise, if he bought inferior land and it later improved to average quality while the first purchaser's average land deteriorated to inferior quality, the creditor still cannot collect from him. Thus, when the second purchaser buys superior or inferior land, we look to the time of acquisition; when he buys average-quality land and leaves comparable average-quality land, we look to the time of collection. This is a significant distinction, and it explains why the Gemara needed both reasons.
And he bought inferior-quality land last. See Sema seif katan 10 and Shach seif katan 20. There are textual errors there. It should read that since Shimon can say, "I am content with this enactment," and the word "not" should be deleted. Later, the text should say that he may push the creditor toward superior-quality land, not inferior-quality land. Their position at first appears difficult. Bava Kamma 8 states that if one sold average and inferior land and retained superior land, all claimants collect from the superior land because it was the last land retained and the average and inferior parcels are no longer with the first purchaser, who can say that he does not wish to benefit from the enactment. According to the Sema and Shach there would seem to be no distinction at all between whether the superior land was acquired first or last, either as against the first purchaser or the second. I later found that the Perishah raises this question and answers that the Gemara teaches that even Abaye agrees in this case, though he disagrees later. I do not understand that answer, because Abaye disputes only the application of the principle "what did the first sell to the second" and has no disagreement concerning removal of a lien from the first purchaser. In my view the matter can be explained as follows. It is stated later in this siman that if land was sold sequentially and all liens have equal priority, all creditors first collect from the parcel sold last, even if it is superior land, and only if that parcel is insufficient do they turn elsewhere. But if all parcels were sold simultaneously, then even to three creditors the injured party can push the ordinary creditor toward average-quality land, even if the purchaser of that average land is a powerful person. Accordingly, suppose Reuven purchased all the parcels simultaneously and then sold the average and inferior land while retaining the superior land, and the remaining superior land is sufficient only for the injured party. The ordinary creditor cannot push the injured party off the superior land by claiming that he too wants to collect from the superior parcel in the first purchaser's hands merely because the first purchaser displaced the creditor's lien. Why should the injured party lose on account of that act? The injured party did not displace the creditor's lien. When all three parcels were originally in the first purchaser's hands, the ordinary creditor's proper source of collection was not the superior parcel. Indeed, if the second purchaser had originally been the direct purchaser from the debtor, the ordinary creditor would have had to pursue the average-quality land even if its purchaser were powerful. Therefore the same should remain true now. The first purchaser's later displacement of the ordinary creditor's lien cannot worsen the injured party's position. This explains the Gemara's emphasis that the superior parcel was last: because it was last, all of them may come and collect from the superior land. When all parcels were acquired together, however, the injured party can push the ordinary creditor toward the average-quality land, as explained.
