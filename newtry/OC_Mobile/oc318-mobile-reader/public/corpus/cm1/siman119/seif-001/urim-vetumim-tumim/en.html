Even superior land. The Rosh in Bava Kamma infers this from Rashi's language and ultimately decides by reasoning that the creditor may collect even superior-quality land from the first purchaser. Yam Shel Shlomo disagrees, as noted in Urim. The Shach wonders how he could overlook Rashi in Ketubot, who states explicitly that even if the first purchaser retained only superior land, the creditor may collect from him. Perishah already asks the same question against the Rosh: why did the Rosh struggle to infer Rashi's position and then decide it independently if Rashi states it explicitly in Ketubot? Perishah concludes that the Rosh must have had a different text of Rashi there. Perhaps Yam Shel Shlomo likewise debated according to the Rosh's version. Still, it is difficult to assume a different text unnecessarily. A distinction may explain the Rosh. Rashi does not appear to adopt the Rosh's separate rule that if a creditor asks for inferior land in a somewhat larger quantity, the debtor need not give it unless he has shown willingness to sell it at the anticipated higher future price. Rashi's unqualified language appears to follow the other commentators that the debtor must give the inferior land at its present lower valuation. On that view, if Reuven sold all his fields to Shimon and Shimon sold one to Levi, and only inferior land remains with Shimon, it is obvious the creditor may collect from Shimon. Even if all fields had remained with Shimon, a creditor asking for the inferior land would be entitled to it. Selling away some property cannot worsen the creditor's position. The baraita's novelty must therefore be that even if Shimon retained only superior land, the creditor may collect it because Shimon himself distanced the creditor's intermediate security. The Rosh, however, holds that a creditor cannot compel the debtor to give inferior land merely because he prefers it. On his approach, the baraita could be teaching only that even inferior land remaining with the first purchaser cannot be shielded by directing the creditor to the second purchaser's intermediate land. Thus the Rosh still had room to ask whether superior land may also be seized and had to decide that question independently. There is an even more plausible distinction. The Rosh explains the creditor's claim as, "Why should I trouble myself to collect from Levi?" Rashi in Ketubot says, "Why should I trouble Levi by collecting from him?" This difference may depend on whether Shimon's sale to Levi included warranty. If it did, then the creditor can say, "Why trouble Levi? If I seize from him, he will return against you anyway." In that situation everyone agrees that the creditor may take even superior land from Shimon, because otherwise the second purchaser would ultimately recover from Shimon's superior land after the creditor seizes him. The Rosh may deal with a sale to Levi without warranty. Then Shimon says, "Go collect from the second purchaser; he will have no recourse against me." The creditor's only response is that Shimon should not be able to distance his security and force him to pursue another person. Where Shimon retained only superior land, the Rosh could therefore reasonably be uncertain whether that inconvenience alone permits taking a grade better than the creditor's ordinary right. This distinction reconciles the sources cleanly.
