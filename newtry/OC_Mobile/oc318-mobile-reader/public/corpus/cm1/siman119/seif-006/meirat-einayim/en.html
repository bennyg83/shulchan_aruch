All of them collect from the superior land. The Tur explains that because the purchaser does not possess intermediate or inferior land, he cannot say, 'I do not wish to benefit from the rabbinic enactment.' In the Perishah I proved that the Gemara's statement that all collect from superior land reflects the ordinary assumption that a creditor prefers a slightly smaller amount of superior land to a slightly larger amount of intermediate land. But if the creditor prefers collection from the intermediate land, he may collect it from the second purchaser, because that intermediate land was the primary subject of his lien. Had it remained with Shimon, the first purchaser, Shimon ordinarily would have paid the creditor from that intermediate land rather than from superior land. Thus the creditor's lien remains attached to it.
The injured parties collect from the superior land. If the superior land had remained with the first purchaser, he could not have pushed the injured party away from it, because superior land is the injured party's lawful grade and it was also acquired last. Even if the purchaser said he did not wish to rely on the rabbinic enactment, the injured party's ordinary law would still be superior land. We do not go so far as to say that the purchaser could have returned the deed of the intermediate or inferior land to the original seller. According to the Rosh and Tur there is no difficulty in any event, because these rulings are explained where the seller died, so returning property to him is impossible.
