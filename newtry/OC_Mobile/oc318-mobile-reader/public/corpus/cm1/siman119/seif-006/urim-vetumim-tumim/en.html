They all collect from the superior land. The Sema and Perishah write that if they wish, the claimants may instead collect from Levi, the second purchaser, who holds intermediate land. This raises a question. The Gemara says the reason all may collect from the superior land is that the superior field was the last one acquired. Why is that reason needed? An ordinary creditor may in any event collect superior land from the first purchaser when that purchaser distanced the intermediate security by selling it, as stated above in seif 1. It appears that the ordinary creditor's right to collect superior land from the first purchaser is based on the complaint, "You distanced my security." Forcing the creditor to chase purchaser after purchaser creates a concern analogous to closing the door before lenders. Just as the Sages established intermediate land for ordinary creditors rather than inferior land to avoid discouraging loans, they treated security made remote through successive transfers as effectively inferior and allowed collection from the first purchaser's superior field. A ketubah is different. The Sages left the woman with the Torah default of inferior land. The special concern of closing the door before lenders does not apply to her claim. Mere distance of collection therefore would not by itself entitle her to superior land. But where the superior field itself was the last property acquired, and the purchaser cannot invoke the rule, "I do not want the rabbinic enactment," even the ketubah claimant may collect from it. This is why the Gemara emphasizes that all of them come and collect from the superior field. The principal novelty is the wife with a ketubah. The sugya therefore properly connects this ruling to the earlier baraita listing the injured party, creditor, and ketubah claimant.
