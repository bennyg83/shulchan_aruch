[Shulchan Aruch] And then all of them take effect simultaneously. Note: Perhaps this applies specifically where the three purchasers acquired three different grades of land, superior, intermediate, and inferior, which is the case discussed by the Gemara. But if all the parcels are of the same grade, the creditor cannot collect except from the last purchaser. Although with respect to the rule that one does not collect from encumbered property where unencumbered property remains, we say that because all the purchases occurred on the same day the first purchaser cannot say, “I left property available for you,” nevertheless the first purchaser acquired the defense that a seller transfers to the purchaser every right that may later come into his hand. The seller could have discharged the creditor with the other intermediate-quality parcels. With respect to the last purchaser, however, that transferred defense does not apply, because at the time of his purchase the seller could no longer discharge the creditor with those superior parcels. Even if all three acquisitions are deemed to take effect at the end of the day, purchasers still possess the transferred rights of their sellers. The Gemara’s law therefore applies only where the properties are superior, intermediate, and inferior, for the transferred-rights defense does not resolve the matter. If the inferior land was purchased last, the debtor himself could not have discharged the creditor with inferior land. If superior land was purchased last, even according to the Rosh, who holds that the debtor may choose to pay with superior land, the transferred-rights defense still does not apply, because there is no reason the debtor would have given the creditor superior land, as Tosafot write in Ketubot 92a. One must therefore resort to the rule that one does not collect from encumbered property where unencumbered property remains, and on that point we say that all the purchases took effect at the end of the day. Examine this carefully.
