Superior land last. See Sema, seif katan 27. Were this not so, once the law has already been established, even the seller's subsequent death would not alter it. Since during the seller's lifetime the purchaser could defer the creditor by means of the claim, "If I remain silent," that legal position has already been established, and it remains so even after the seller dies. This resembles the case in Yevamot 37b of one person deriving title from four persons.
Then all of them will be required. See Shach, seif katan 10, who proves his position from the question why the purchaser cannot say, "If I remain silent, so be it, but if not, I will return one field to its owner," so that both purchasers could defer the creditor. The distinction is that in the case of one person deriving title from four, where the purchaser has not impaired any right that the creditor previously possessed, he may say, "When you return it." By contrast, where one derives title from one person and the purchaser has impaired the creditor's right, we tell him that this argument is effective only when he actually returns the property. The Shach rejects this proof because in the case of the path, the purchaser actually owned the path itself and the other party could not acquire it, whereas here there is only a lien and the purchase itself was legally possible.
The same applies if he purchased inferior land last. See Sema, seif katan 29, in the name of the Tur. This cannot mean a case where the purchaser bought only intermediate land from the debtor while independently owning inferior land of his own. Heirs and purchasers may repay only with money and cannot compel a creditor to accept some other property. The argument, "If I remain silent," is effective only where the claimant's present right arose through the purchaser. For example, the purchaser's acquisition of the inferior land caused the creditor's right of collection to attach there. Since that right came about through the purchaser, he can undo its basis by returning the deed or the field. But if the purchaser bought superior or intermediate land and merely happens to own separate inferior land of his own, he cannot undo a right of the creditor that did not arise through him. Otherwise, the same argument could defeat even an express apotiki. Rather, the Tur means what is explained in Perishah: where the purchaser did acquire the debtor's inferior field, he can say that had he not purchased it, the creditor's lien would have rested on that field, and therefore the creditor should take the inferior land or the purchaser will restore the legal situation by returning property of equivalent effect. The novelty is that if the purchaser actually gives the debtor inferior land of his own, even when it is evident that he does so in order to force the creditor to collect from that land, the creditor can be deferred, because the debtor now owns inferior land from which collection may be made. The Tur's mention of movable property is also explained in this manner by Perishah.
