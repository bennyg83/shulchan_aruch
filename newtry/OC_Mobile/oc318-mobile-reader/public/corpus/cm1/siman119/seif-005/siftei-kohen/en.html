Maharshal rules that this reasoning helps a tort claimant collect intermediate property, but not inferior property; similarly, it helps an ordinary creditor collect intermediate property but not inferior property. He says the purchaser may answer, “Return first, and then pursue me,” and cites the law of a person who acquired from one owner and must provide an access path. In my view, that is no proof. There the claimant presently owns a path through the fields, so the purchaser is immediately obligated to provide it. Here the purchaser may return the inferior land to its owner, who can then satisfy the creditor with that inferior land. Even if all parcels had remained with the first owner, he could have sold the superior and intermediate parcels and paid from the inferior parcel. A creditor owns no specific part of the land; he has only a monetary debt and lien. Tosafot and Baal HaMilchamot likewise show that the access-path case is not a proof.
