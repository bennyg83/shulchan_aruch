Specifically when the seller died, etc. The Rif and Rambam, followed by the Mechaber, omit this qualification because they understand that the Gemara's proposed reasoning based on "If you remain silent" does not survive the conclusion. The commentators are surprised because the reasoning is expressly stated in the Gemara. One cannot challenge the Rama, who follows the Tur and Rosh, from Yevamot 37b. There Rabbi Abba explains the doubtful-son and yavam case by saying that once an earlier judgment became final it remains final, and connects this to the rule that one who lost a path to his field may have to buy a path at an exorbitant price. The Gemara suggests instead that the path rule may rest on "If you remain silent." The Tur and Rosh do not decide absolutely like Rabbi Abba but treat the matter as doubtful, which implies that "If you remain silent" may indeed be valid. Here, where the purchaser is in possession, that doubt suffices to prevent extraction. The Rama does not gloss Even HaEzer siman 165, where the Mechaber rules definitively that the prior judgment stands, which may seem to indicate rejection of "If you remain silent." But this can be reconciled. One answer is that where the alternative creates a genuine loss, we do not use "If you remain silent," so the prior-judgment doctrine is still needed. Here the difference is merely between property grades such as superior and intermediate, not loss of the principal claim. A second answer appears in my discussion at the end of Kuntres HaTefisot: even when the law follows the doctrine that the earlier judgment stands, the logic of "If you remain silent" may independently be true in its proper setting. The harder question is on the Rif and Rambam themselves. Why conclude that the Gemara abandoned the doctrine when the anonymous Gemara initially states it, especially since the Rambam himself uses "If you remain silent" in the lost-path case? Yam Shel Shlomo notes this. A clue appears in Rashi. Rashi says the case is where the seller died without leaving assets and adds that movable property of heirs is not subject to the father's creditors. Why is that relevant? If the heirs possessed sufficient movable property, the purchaser could simply say, "I left you property from which to collect." If they possessed none, there is no need to discuss whether heir movables are subject. The remark seems extraneous. The Rashba raises a powerful problem. When the purchaser says he can return the inferior land to the seller, he can return it as payment. If the seller is dead but the heirs are adults, why can he not pay the land to them? They would then be heirs who collected land on their father's claim, and the father's creditor could collect that land from them. The Rashba, finding this difficult, suggests that the case concerns minor heirs, an interpretation almost unique to him and contrary to the usual commentators. I discussed this above in siman 111. The Gemara in Pesachim provides the key. It asks why a creditor can collect land that heirs received in satisfaction of their father's claim, since the debt due to the deceased is merely movable property in the heirs' hands and heir movables are not subject to the father's creditor. It answers by invoking Rabbi Natan's rule of direct subrogation. Kiddushin 16b, however, implies according to several authorities that Rabbi Sheshet does not accept Rabbi Natan's rule. If Rabbi Natan is unavailable, Rabbi Nachman's rule that a creditor may collect land received by heirs likewise does not operate, because the underlying receivable remains an unencumbered movable claim. This explains Rashi's emphasis. He wishes to answer the Rashba's anticipated difficulty: although the purchaser could in form give the land to the heirs as payment, under the view operative in the sugya that does not transform the deceased's unencumbered receivable into land subject to the creditor, because movable claims of heirs are not liable and Rabbi Natan's bridge is unavailable. Thus the hypothetical return does not work after the seller's death. But for us, who rule like Rabbi Natan and Rabbi Nachman that heirs who collect land on their father's debt hold land subject to his creditor, the Rashba's question returns. Even after the seller's death, an adult heir can receive the inferior land in payment and the creditor can then collect from it. The special "seller died" limitation can no longer sustain the Gemara's initial "If you remain silent" reasoning. The Rif and Rambam therefore understand that at the conclusion the Gemara abandons that reasoning altogether. The Rashba's minor-heirs construction is not adopted as the general halachah. However, if the Gemara abandoned "If you remain silent," why did it need to answer the original baraita by saying the purchaser can declare, "I do not want the rabbinic enactment"? It could have retained the Gemara's initial construction that all fields were sold together and taught the major novelty that we do not accept the hypothetical-return claim. That would be a significant teaching. There are two ways to answer, both of which also explain why the Gemara initially entertained the doctrine. First, the Rashba asks how beit din can itself raise a hypothetical return that would nullify a Torah or rabbinic rule concerning proper collection grades. Beit din should not invent a device to take away an injured party's superior land or a creditor's intermediate land. The answer is that the logic differs depending on the claimant. For an ordinary creditor or ketubah claimant who seeks more favorable property than the grade fixed by the Sages, a return can be recognized in order to preserve the established rabbinic order. If the purchaser acquired superior land last and the creditor tries to exploit the purchaser's acquisition pattern to improve his grade, beit din can say the inferior document is treated as though returned to the seller, keeping the claimant within the legislated grade. But beit din should not use that device affirmatively to reduce an injured party from his established Torah right to superior land. Thus the initial "If you remain silent" theory can work for creditor and ketubah, while failing for tort damages. The Gemara then asks: if we treat the inferior deed as returned for the creditor and ketubah claimants, the return cannot be half effective. Once the inferior field is conceptually back with the seller, the injured party too should find it there and be forced away from the superior field. One cannot both suppress and unsuppress the same return depending on claimant. This is the force of the Gemara's challenge. The conclusion instead uses the purchaser's direct declaration, "I do not want the rabbinic enactment," which protects him without requiring beit din to fictionalize a return that would undermine the injured party's right. Once that answer is available, there is no reason to retain the initial hypothetical-return mechanism. The Rif and Rambam therefore omit it. A second approach follows the Rosh's view that "I will return the deed" means an actual new transfer, or at least depends on the purchaser's real power to restore the field. Even Yam Shel Shlomo, who understands it as suppressing the deed, agrees that its legitimacy derives from the purchaser's ability actually to return it. Why should we assume a purchaser will reconvey property merely to change a claimant from one grade to another when doing so may expose him to substantial risk? The seller might sell it again to others; if the creditor's after-acquired clause is absent, the purchaser may thereby lose his recourse. Even if the seller does not sell, another later creditor of the seller may share in the returned property, reducing what the original claimants receive and causing the purchaser to be pursued elsewhere. The hypothetical return is therefore plausible only where the claimants' liens include after-acquired property sufficiently to protect the purchaser from such loss. One can say that at the initial stage the Gemara assumed the ordinary creditor and ketubah claimant had explicit after-acquired clauses, or treated such clauses as implied by scribal practice, and therefore a return posed little risk. A tort claimant, however, ordinarily has no written after-acquired clause. The Gemara can thus distinguish why the purchaser would return for creditor and ketubah purposes but not for the tort claimant. The Gemara then asks that even this is not fully satisfactory, because an after-acquired clause itself is not always automatically implied, especially according to the authorities involved in the same sugyot. If such a clause must be express, the baraita would need to state a special case where even the tort claimant received one. Once that is possible, the supposed distinction collapses, and the more natural final answer is the purchaser's ability to reject the enactment. Moreover, according to the later accepted version concerning two creditors and property acquired afterward, both after-acquired liens attach simultaneously and the claimants divide. A reconveyance to the seller can therefore expose the purchaser to later creditors and cause genuine loss even where the earlier creditors have after-acquired clauses. There is no reason to assume he will make such a transfer merely to improve someone else's property grade. The initial "If you remain silent" theory consequently falls away under the final halachah. This gives a substantial reason for the Rif and Rambam's omission. The practical conclusion is that the Tur and Rosh preserve the doctrine where its factual assumptions are present, especially where the seller's death blocks a real return under their reading. The Rif, Rambam, and Mechaber understand the final sugya as relying instead on rejection of the purchaser-protection enactment and therefore do not make the law depend on the seller's death.
When the seller died. See what I wrote in the preceding seif katan. The Rashba asks that where the heirs are adults, the purchaser can return the inferior land to them as payment of a debt, making them heirs who collected land on their father's claim, from whom the father's creditor can then collect. I explained that one cannot simply answer that the purchaser owes the seller nothing, because he can make a binding admission that he does owe, like the admission in the case of Issur the convert. Yet there is another point. Even if he now gives the land as payment, the Gemara's difficulty from "movable claims of heirs are not subject to creditors" may remain. The Gemara elsewhere resolves that difficulty through Rabbi Natan's rule of direct subrogation: where A owes B and B owes C, C may collect directly from A. Here, however, the purchaser was not truly indebted to the deceased seller during the seller's lifetime. His supposed obligation arises only now through his own admission. Even if he says, "I owed the seller from long ago," that factual statement is false; his legal liability is created only now by the admission. The seller is already dead, so there was never a moment when the seller's right became subject to the three claimants. The heirs themselves owe those claimants nothing. Rabbi Natan's rule therefore cannot create the chain retroactively. We nevertheless learn that if the purchaser genuinely still owes the deceased seller purchase money or another real preexisting debt, he can return the inferior land to the heirs as payment, and then the father's creditor may collect that land as property collected by heirs. This concern, however, mainly helps the tort claimant. As to an ordinary creditor, if the purchaser genuinely owes money to the deceased seller, the creditor can invoke Rabbi Natan directly and collect that debt from the purchaser. The purchaser cannot manipulate the grade by paying the heirs inferior land, because the creditor stands in the seller's right and is entitled to collect his own debt according to the ordinary rule of intermediate land. If the heirs themselves wished to accept inferior land, they cannot defeat the creditor's direct subrogated right. This is simple.

Yam Shel Shlomo agrees with Rashi and Tosafot in Bava Kamma that the special argument allows the injured party to reach intermediate land, but not an ordinary creditor, because as against the creditor the purchaser can answer, "When you actually return it, then come." See what I wrote above in siman 141. There is some support from the baraita's elaborate formulation listing an injured party, creditor, and ketubah claimant, together with superior, intermediate, and inferior land. Why list all three? It could seemingly have taught only injured party and creditor with superior and intermediate land, or creditor and ketubah claimant with intermediate and inferior land. If it had listed only two, however, we could have explained the result without invoking the rule that the purchaser may say, "I do not want the rabbinic enactment." For example, if it listed injured party and creditor and the purchaser acquired the superior land last, one might say the seller is still alive. As against the creditor, whose proper grade is intermediate, we treat the intermediate document as though returned, because doing so does not destroy an established right and Tosafot allow such a return. As against the injured party, however, the purchaser cannot force him to take intermediate land by threatening to return it, because the injured party can answer, "Return it first," and his Torah right to superior land should not be defeated by a hypothetical return. Likewise, if the baraita listed only creditor and ketubah claimant, with intermediate land acquired last, we could say that the creditor cannot be forced down to inferior land by a hypothetical return, while the ketubah holder's right is not impaired because she ordinarily takes inferior land anyway. By listing all three claimants together, the baraita makes clear that the injured party actually receives superior land rather than merely intermediate. That result requires the seller to be dead and the purchaser's ability to reject the rabbinic protection for his own benefit, rather than an ordinary hypothetical-return argument. The wording therefore supports Yam Shel Shlomo.
From the seller. The Tur writes that the purchaser need not literally return this particular field to the seller. He may return another field, give him property as a gift, or even give movable property, so long as the seller then has property from which the creditor can collect. The Sema cites this in seif katan 29. Perishah asks: if the purchaser has movable property available, why is there any need to conceptualize it as though returned to the seller? If the purchaser himself has money or movables, he can ordinarily remove a creditor by paying them, so the same should apply even when the seller is dead. Perishah gives a strained answer. A similar difficulty arises with an explicit apotiki. We rule that the debtor may redeem it with money but a purchaser cannot. Why can the purchaser not say, "If you remain silent, fine; otherwise I will return the money to the debtor, thereby leaving you property from which to collect, and it is as though already returned"? It would be strained to say that the "If you remain silent" doctrine is only a defensive argument preserving possession and cannot be used to extract property, while an apotiki is treated as already in the creditor's possession. The problem would also remain under Rami bar Chama, who holds generally that a purchaser cannot remove a creditor with money. Why should he not use the same hypothetical return of cash? We should not invent a new dispute between Rami bar Chama and Rava over the "If you remain silent" doctrine, especially because Rava allows payment even against heirs, where the hypothetical-return reasoning cannot be his basis. The correct answer is that the Tur did not mean that every kind of property can always be used for this argument. He meant only that circumstances can exist where a return may take forms other than giving back the identical field. The doctrine operates where the purchaser bought without warranty, so that after seizure he has no recourse against the seller in any event. In such a case he can plausibly say he will give property back to the seller as a gift or otherwise without sacrificing a valuable warranty claim. Where the purchaser bought with warranty and retains a lien against a living seller, why should we assume he will gratuitously give property back and thereby risk or lose his own warranty rights? If he gives movable property on credit, another creditor may later seize it. If he gives a gift conditioned against creditors, why should we assume he will voluntarily do so? The Baal HaTerumot may use "gift" loosely for a reconveyance where the original purchaser is no longer owed payment, rather than a literal gratuitous transfer. Thus the hypothetical-return argument has force where the purchaser suffers no additional loss from returning property, most naturally a no-warranty purchaser. This also resolves the dispute of Rami bar Chama, whose case expressly involves a purchaser with warranty and a writ of seizure. The Tur's broader examples therefore do not apply there. Perishah's question can likewise be answered without treating an apotiki as already possessed by the creditor. Suppose the three fields, superior, intermediate, and inferior, were explicit apotiki fields and the purchaser bought without warranty. Were it not for the purchaser's ability to return money or movables to the seller, he could not remove the apotiki creditor by cash payment. But because he may say, "I will give this property back to the debtor, leaving you another source of collection," the hypothetical return can operate. Thus there is at least a possible case where the Tur's language matters. If so, a no-warranty purchaser might even be able to remove an explicit apotiki creditor with money or movables through this argument. I have not found such a ruling in the authorities, so the point requires study.
