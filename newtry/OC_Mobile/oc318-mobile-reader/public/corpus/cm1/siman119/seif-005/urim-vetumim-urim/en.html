Who purchased the superior quality land last. A creditor and a ketubah claimant do not collect from superior quality land, for the purchaser can say: I do not wish to benefit from the enactment of the Sages that creditors collect from the fields purchased last; rather, take according to the basic law. Likewise, if he purchased intermediate quality land last, an ordinary creditor and a claimant for damages collect from the intermediate land. Regarding damages, he cannot say, 'I do not wish to benefit from the enactment,' and therefore the claimant must collect from the last purchased field. By contrast, with respect to a ketubah, the purchaser may say, 'I do not wish to benefit from the enactment,' and she therefore receives only inferior quality land. The Mechaber holds that even if the seller is alive, the purchaser cannot say to the claimant for damages, 'Take intermediate land, and if not I will return the deed for the inferior land to its owner, making that field unencumbered, and you will then have to collect from it.' The Mechaber follows the Rif and Rambam, who do not accept this reasoning. For the basis of this position and what they saw in the Gemara to support it, see Tumim, where I discussed it at length.
Specifically when the seller died. For then, how can he return the deed for the inferior land to the orphans? What would this accomplish? It would be as though the orphans themselves purchased land. Would the creditor of their father then collect from it? Property belonging to orphans that they did not inherit is not subject to their father's debts. According to the Rashba's view cited above in siman 111, seif 13 in the gloss, that one may collect from encumbered property where the debtor leaves minor orphans, the law here would apply even where the seller died if he left adult orphans. It would be like a case in which the seller is still alive. Rather, the case must be where he left only minor orphans. See Tumim.
For he can return, etc. The Rosh's view is that the purchaser should make a new acquisition and transfer the inferior land back to the seller. The Yam Shel Shlomo holds that he can simply suppress the original deed of sale. This is not falsehood or injustice, because he could transfer the field back by a new sale, and thus the legal result is the same. Therefore, regarding orphans, where he cannot effect a new transfer back to them, suppressing the deed does not help, because we do not advance a false claim on his behalf. Know that according to this approach, even where all three fields were sold at one time in a single deed, as long as the seller is alive, the claimants receive only from inferior land, because the purchaser can return the deed for the inferior land to the seller. Tosafot wrote this in the cited sugya. See Tumim, where I clarified that this applies only when it is not known that the seller later became indebted to others. If it is known that he later became indebted to others, this rule does not apply, because he cannot return the field to the seller, since in a case of one who borrowed, then borrowed again, and then acquired property, the acquired property becomes encumbered to the later creditor as well. Returning it would therefore cause him an additional loss. This requires study, for it is strained to establish the law as a gift to the seller conditioned that no debt attach to it except the three debts discussed here. I also concluded that even where there are orphans, if the purchaser himself owes money to the seller, he can say, 'I will return the inferior land as payment.' This helps against the claimant for damages, who will then collect only intermediate land, but not against an ordinary creditor, whose proper collection is intermediate land in any event. See there.
And then all of them will have to, etc. Meaning, the claimant for damages will collect from inferior land, and so will the ordinary creditor. They cannot say, 'If so, since we gain nothing, first actually return it,' because whatever remains in his hands is adjudicated as though it had already been returned and stands returned. Even if he does not make this claim, beit din advances it for him. This is the view of the Rosh and Tur. The Yam Shel Shlomo agrees with Rashi and Tosafot in Bava Kamma that the claimant for damages collects intermediate land, because he gains by the return, since if the purchaser returns the field he would collect inferior land. But to force the claimant for damages to collect inferior land, or an ordinary creditor to collect inferior land, they can say, 'Return it first; what benefit have you given us by not returning it?' See Tumim, where I brought support for the Maharshal's position from the Gemara.
From the seller. The Tur writes that this applies even if the purchaser gives the seller property of his own, or gives the seller money. See Tumim, where I wrote that this appears to apply specifically to a purchaser without warranty. Where the purchase was with warranty, it is not reasonable that the purchaser should simply give the seller a gift. I also wrote there that the Tur's rule that he may give the seller money or movable property appears to refer to a case where all three fields, superior, intermediate, and inferior, had been designated as an apotiki. Without an apotiki, the purchaser could in any event discharge the lien with money. See there.
He purchased intermediate quality land last. Regarding a ketubah he says, 'I do not wish to benefit from the enactment,' whereas regarding the other claimants he says, 'I do wish to benefit from the enactment of the Sages.'
