The injured party collects from superior land. The Sema, following most authorities, concludes that the injured party may collect from purchasers even if the tort claim had not yet been adjudicated, because torts have publicity. The Shach asks from siman 66, seif 35, where the Mechaber cites a responsum of the Rashba that one who forgives a debt instrument and thereby damages the purchaser of that instrument is liable as a tortfeasor, yet the purchaser cannot collect that damages claim from encumbered property unless a written instrument was made, even from purchasers who bought after the forgiveness. According to what I wrote in siman 386, even authorities who treat dina d'garmei as a legal obligation rather than a penalty can still hold that it is rabbinic. The Sages may have established it as ordinary law rather than a fine, with consequences such as liability of heirs and other distinctions between law and penalty. But if the obligation is rabbinic, the principle of a Torah lien does not automatically apply. Where no Torah lien exists, publicity by itself does not necessarily create a lien. This is implied by the end of Bava Batra and by Tosafot in Bava Kamma 14b. A written instrument is therefore required in the forgiven-note case. There is also a more direct answer within the Rashba. In his novellae to Bava Kamma he asks why tort damages collect from purchasers and answers that torts have publicity. He cites the rule that once an ox has gored it becomes known as a goring ox, and says that through the publicity attached to the animal the damage it caused also becomes public. His language shows that the publicity is specifically collateral to property damage caused by an animal. An individual's personal act of damaging another, such as forgiving the note, does not necessarily have that publicity. Thus there is no contradiction: the tort rule here concerns damage by one's property, whereas the forgiven-note case is personal damage without comparable publicity. This also helps explain why, once tort damages have publicity, even an authority who generally holds lien liability is not d'oraisa may allow collection from purchasers. Rav himself holds that a public sale before witnesses creates collection rights despite holding lien liability is not d'oraisa. The Gemara's challenge at the end of Bava Batra from heirs of the owner of a pit can then be answered because that damage lacks the same publicity. The Rashba, however, cites Tosafot as saying that publicity helps tort damages collect from purchasers only according to the view that lien liability is d'oraisa. He distinguishes between strong publicity, such as a public sale, which the Sages recognized even under the other view, and weaker publicity, such as tort damage, which helps only where a Torah lien already exists. Hagahot HaRosh and many authorities cited by the Beit Yosef and Shach require the tort claimant to have first stood in judgment or obtained a kinyan before collecting from purchasers. Their position is difficult from Bava Kamma 14b. The Gemara expounds "before beit din" to exclude a case where the tortfeasor sold his property and only afterward went to court, and asks whether this proves that a borrower who sells property before appearing in court cannot be collected from. The Gemara rejects that reading and interprets the phrase differently. The conclusion therefore indicates that a tort claimant can collect from purchasers even if the sale preceded the judicial hearing. It is strained to say that the sugya follows only the view that lien liability is d'oraisa while those authorities rule otherwise. Most poskim who say lien liability is not generally d'oraisa nevertheless agree that an obligation explicitly written in the Torah itself has Torah lien force, as the Shach writes above in siman 39. Tort liability is of that type. Thus, according to the overwhelming majority, tort damages should indeed collect from purchasers without prior adjudication where the tort has the requisite publicity. Perhaps the authorities requiring adjudication speak only of damage without such publicity, while the standard case of an ox is different. That would reconcile the sources.
There is an authority who says that we listen to him, etc. The Sema and Bach are surprised that the Tur and Mechaber present a dispute concerning three purchasers whose deeds all bear the same date, whether each purchaser can deflect the creditor by claiming that he was first and left property in the seller's hands. A careful reading of the Baal HaTerumot shows that even the Ramban, who permits such deflection in a doubtful sequence of sales, does not say so where all the deeds were written on one day. The Ramban writes in Shaar 4, part 1, law 3, that the statement in the first chapter of Bava Kamma, "If he sold all of them together, they entered in place of the owner," means literally together, such as where all the deeds bear the very same date. We are not dealing with Jerusalem, where hours were written into deeds. We do not suspect that one deed was written and delivered earlier than another. We rule regarding two deeds issued on one day either that the parties divide or that beit din applies shuda. Accordingly, the claims are placed on their ordinary grades, and the purchasers enter in place of the owner. But if the properties were sold successively and it cannot be determined who bought first, each purchaser may deflect the creditor until the creditor proves the order. These are the Ramban's words. Thus the Ramban does not dispute the same-day case at all. Perishah answers by distinguishing between whether the purchaser himself makes a definite claim. On one day, he says, beit din does not make the claim for him, but if the deeds have no date beit din does. The Sema accordingly explains the Shulchan Aruch as a case where the purchasers themselves make the claim. This is strained. There is no conceptual difference between one undifferentiated day and several undifferentiated days. If the absence of a precise time leaves open the possibility that one purchaser received his deed first, the same uncertainty exists where all deeds bear one date. According to Rabbi Elazar, delivery witnesses create the transaction; even according to Rabbi Meir, a deed bearing one day can be comparable to a deed lacking a precise day, as the Ran and Ritva explain in Ketubot concerning two deeds issued on one day. There is therefore no basis for the proposed distinction between a deed with no date and deeds with the same date. The Ramban's true distinction is whether it is known that the sales occurred one after another but their order cannot now be established, or whether even that is unknown. The factual question submitted to the Ramban, as Baal HaTerumot states at the beginning of the passage, involved sales definitely made on successive days, but no one knew which came first because the relevant writings did not establish the order. The Ramban ruled that each purchaser may say he was first and left property available. By contrast, where all deeds bear one day, there is not merely uncertainty which came first. There is a real possibility that legally they all took effect together, because hours were not recorded. The Ramban says we do not go so far as to suspect a hidden sequence of writing and delivery. This is the "rabbinic shuda" by which each purchaser is placed into the seller's position for the corresponding property grade. The distinction therefore turns on whether we know there was a sequence, not on whether the writing says one day or no day. This is clear and undermines the Sema's entire distinction between a definite personal claim and a claim beit din would raise. The Bach likewise sees the difficulty, though his suggestion that the Tur means a case where one deed was actually known to have been delivered first is also strained. The Tur and Mechaber should then have said so explicitly, as the Ramban does. It appears that the Tur and Mechaber understood the same-day issue differently because they do not accept the Ramban's explanation of shuda. The Ramban's statement that the Sages simply established a shuda under which all purchasers enter the seller's position is itself difficult. We say division or shuda primarily where neither claimant is in possession, such as two deeds for the same field. Where one claimant is already in possession, the ordinary rule is that the one seeking to extract must bring proof. The Maharsha writes explicitly in Ketubot that if a loan document and sale document bear the same date, the creditor does not collect from the purchaser because the purchaser is in possession and the creditor must prove priority. One can distinguish that case by saying there it is possible that the creditor's lien never attached before the sale, whereas here one field is certainly subject to each grade of claim. Still, the distinction is not entirely comfortable. The Ramban was driven to his interpretation because the Gemara explicitly says that same-day purchasers enter in place of the owner even though one might imagine that one came first. Nor can the Ramban simply say that the baraita follows Rabbi Meir and therefore the deeds all take effect together at the end of the day. According to Rabbi Meir, where signature witnesses effect the deed, there can still have been a real sequence in execution, but because the date specifies only the day, the legal effect may be treated as simultaneous for certain purposes. The Ran discusses this at length. The Rashba also notes that the phrase, "One might say one preceded the other," is less natural according to Rabbi Meir. The Ramban follows his own deeper position, cited by the Ran, that even according to Rabbi Meir the earlier sale can acquire fully where appropriate and that the same-day baraita therefore requires a special rabbinic shuda. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch, however, define shuda differently. Below in siman 240 they follow the view that shuda means estimating the parties' intent and determining whom the transferor probably intended to favor, rather than giving the judge unrestricted discretion. On that understanding, the Ramban's answer cannot work here. There is no question of estimating which purchaser the seller preferred so as to decide a priority among different property grades, and the Gemara's rule that all enter under the seller cannot itself be called shuda. At the same time, the Tur and Mechaber do not follow the Ramban's position concerning signature witnesses. They rule that delivery witnesses are decisive, in accordance with Rabbi Elazar. The same-day baraita can therefore be understood as following Rabbi Meir, under whom the common date creates the simultaneous legal effect described by the Gemara. It is no proof for our accepted law under Rabbi Elazar. Accordingly, the Tur and Mechaber could properly extend the Ramban's practical rule of uncertainty even to same-day deeds under our halachah, because under Rabbi Elazar actual delivery priority matters and cannot be extracted from the common date. Their citation of a dispute is therefore understandable. This also explains a point in Bava Kamma. The Gemara eventually establishes the baraita as concerning heirs, which at first seems an unnecessary complication. According to Rabbi Meir, tort claimants ordinarily collect intermediate land, whereas from heirs everyone agrees claims are reduced to inferior land. If the property is in a purchaser's hands, why does an ordinary creditor still receive intermediate land even though had the debtor died and left the property to heirs he would receive only inferior land? One can compare a purchaser to a guarantor: the Gemara says a guarantor for an ordinary creditor pays intermediate land even though heirs pay inferior land. Property is a person's guarantor. A ketubah is different, because even a guarantor pays only inferior land since the ketubah's principal design is for collection after death. Thus a purchaser likewise need give the ketubah claimant only inferior land. If the same-day baraita follows Rabbi Meir because signature witnesses create the simultaneous effect, one might ask that Rabbi Meir gives a ketubah claimant intermediate land. The Gemara's establishment of the case as heirs answers this as well: from heirs, even Rabbi Meir agrees the ketubah is collected from inferior land, and a purchaser standing in their place has the same rule. The apparently forced reference to heirs therefore fits the larger structure. However, there is a difficulty in the opposite direction concerning Rabbenu Yonah, who disagrees with the Ramban and explains that when all deeds bear one day, none of their distinct acquisitions is recognizable from the instruments until the end of the day, so they take effect together. That reasoning belongs specifically to Rabbi Meir, who holds signature witnesses create the deed. According to Rabbi Elazar, who holds delivery witnesses create the transaction, the first delivery should acquire first. Tosafot say this expressly in Ketubot 94, s.v. "shall we say": the simultaneous-day rule follows Rabbi Meir, not Rabbi Elazar. We rule like Rabbi Elazar. Even according to Rabbi Meir, Tosafot limit the same-day simultaneous rule to instruments of conveyance. If the document is merely evidence of a transaction already completed by another kinyan, the legal acquisition occurred when that kinyan took place, and the common date does not postpone it until the end of the day. How then can the Tur state the rule without distinguishing deeds of conveyance from evidentiary deeds? It is strained to say that Rabbenu Yonah follows Rabbenu Tam's approach that the Sages, out of concern for the closing of the door, treated two loans or two ketubah obligations of the same day as though both liens arise only at the end of the day, and that the same policy was extended here so debtors cannot evade creditors by selling all their property on one date. This is not the apparent basis of Rabbenu Yonah's explanation. Still, as a practical matter his result may be correct for the policy reason stated by the Rashba and Ran: otherwise a debtor could manipulate sale timing and defeat the creditor's established grades of collection. The precise theoretical basis remains difficult, but the halachic concern is substantial.
