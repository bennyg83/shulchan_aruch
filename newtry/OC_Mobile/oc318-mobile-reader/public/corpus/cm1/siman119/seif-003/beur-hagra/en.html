(Supplement) One who, etc. Rambam rules generally that damages may be collected from encumbered property, because he holds that a property lien is biblical, following Rif. Yet Yoreh Deah 305:16 follows Tosafot that it is not collected. See citation 100.
(Supplement) And he collects, etc. Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri write that damages are collected only after judgment, following their reading of Kiddushin. Rashba holds that collection is available in every case, following Rif, because the lien is biblical and the case there also concerns an ordinary creditor. See citation 305.
(Supplement) And he collects, etc. This follows Rif that liens are biblical. It nevertheless requires study, because slaves are treated as land in some respects but as movable property for collection, as the Gemara rules. The authorities therefore explain that even if the lien is biblical, the Sages enacted that collection not be made from orphans through property that is movable and easily removed.
(Supplement) And he collects, etc. Rabbenu Yerucham likewise rules that because liens are biblical, no prior judgment is required, since damages generate publicity. Rashba agrees.
There is one who says, etc. This resembles the laws of a lost access road in Ketubot 109b, a firstborn in Bava Batra 127a, redemption of a firstborn, and two persons bearing the same name. Even the earlier claimant may be repelled by the argument, 'I left property available for you,' and the same applies here. The passage in Bava Kamma concerns documents issued on the same day, where we do not suspect that one was written and delivered earlier, because that is uncommon. The judges therefore place the purchasers in the seller's position, whether by judicial discretion or by division. If the sales occurred on different days but the documents are undated, each purchaser may repel the creditor. The wording of Tur and Shulchan Aruch is difficult, because Ramban agrees that documents of one day are treated alike. The second view is attributed to Ri, but Ramban himself cites and accepts Tosafot's explanation. The later commentators strained to reconcile the language. See citations 6, 40, 43, 82, and 205.
