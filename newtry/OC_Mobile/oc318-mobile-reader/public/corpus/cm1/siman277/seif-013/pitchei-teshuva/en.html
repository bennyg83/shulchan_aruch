Witnesses heard, etc. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 148, discusses a man who died leaving a wife and two sons. One son claimed to be the firstborn, the mother agreed, and the other demanded proof. The claimant produced two witnesses that he was the first child to open this mother's womb, and two other witnesses that she had been the father's first wife. From the combination of both testimonies he would be a firstborn for inheritance. The ordinary son argued that he could plead kim li like Rif, cited by Tosafot in Bava Kamma, according to whom each pair's testimony is merely "half a matter" and therefore invalid. Chatam Sofer notes that Sema in siman 30 cites only Tosafot's contrary approach, under which this is complete testimony. Yet Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer 142:12, rules like Ramah that delivery of a get must occur before the same witnesses, reflecting the Rif and Rambam's view that separate testimony of components may be "half a matter." Rema, Ramban, Rashba, Tur, and others likewise support this approach. Thus, not only may the ordinary son plead kim li like Rif and those authorities, but even if the firstborn were in possession he could not plead kim li like Tosafot against the ruling of Shulchan Aruch and Darkei Moshe. Chatam Sofer nevertheless finds support for the claimant from Tosafot's observation that an established status is treated as an existing fact rather than as one part of testimony. Here, the first pair of witnesses may have been unnecessary to establish that he was the mother's firstborn, because he had long behaved publicly as one, such as fasting on Erev Pesach. Moreover, the father was presumed upright and had never been suspected of relations with an unmarried woman, so it was already established that the claimant opened his mother's womb and that the father had not previously fathered a child with an unmarried woman. The testimony that his mother was the father's first wife could therefore constitute a complete matter. Still, there is room to question how those witnesses could know that the father had never married elsewhere and fathered a child there. The principle in Yevamot that one does not suspect an unknown additional co-wife may depend upon an existing presumption permitting yibbum, whereas in a monetary dispute the estate remains in the heirs' possession and perhaps even compounded possibilities must be considered. Chatam Sofer concludes that because Tosafot does not regard this as half a matter, and Sema, Taz, and Beit Shmuel cite that approach, while even under Rif the established fact that he opened the womb may render the remaining testimony complete, but there remains some weakness in the witnesses' assertion that there was no prior wife, the best course is a compromise.
