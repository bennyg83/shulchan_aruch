(Seif 6, although its head emerged alive.) The Mishna teaches: “There is a firstborn for inheritance who is not a firstborn for redemption by a kohen,” meaning the five sela redemption. “One born after miscarriages, although the head of the miscarriage emerged alive, or after a nine-month fetus born dead, is firstborn for inheritance but not for redemption by a kohen.” Rashi explains that where twins were present, one a miscarriage and one full-term, and the miscarriage first put forth its head alive but was drawn back, after which the other child emerged first, the latter is firstborn for inheritance. The miscarriage did not deprive him of that status. Although emergence of the head is considered a full birth, inheritance depends on “the first of his strength,” meaning one whose death pains the father. A nonviable fetus does not cause such grief. Redemption from the kohen, however, depends on opening the womb, and the first fetus opened it. Mahari Katz of Krakow infers that because the Mishna did not teach the greater novelty that even if the entire miscarriage was born before the second, the second remains firstborn for inheritance, the rule applies specifically where only the head emerged. One cannot say that the Mishna chose this case only to teach the novelty concerning redemption, that the second is not firstborn even though only the first fetus’s head emerged. Rambam, in chapter 2 of Hilchot Nachalot, discusses only firstborn inheritance and nevertheless writes that only the first fetus’s head emerged. The Tur likewise says that most of the first fetus emerged, not all of it. This indicates that if the entire fetus emerged, the second is not firstborn for inheritance. On this basis Mahari Katz ruled concerning a woman who gave birth to an infant that lived three or four hours and was born nine months after her immersion, but whose husband had not abstained from her during the pregnancy. The infant died, and he ruled that her later child is not firstborn for inheritance. The Sema writes that “the first of his strength” excludes a miscarriage, and therefore even if the entire miscarriage was born, the later child is firstborn for inheritance. Rashi’s statement that the first fetus was drawn back was included only for the novelty of the latter clause, that the second is not firstborn for redemption by a kohen. The Sema rejects Mahari Katz’s conclusion that if the miscarriage was born entirely the later child is not firstborn for inheritance. The Sema’s explanation is explicit in Rashi at the beginning of Behemah HaMekashah: even if the entire miscarriage was born, the second remains firstborn for inheritance, and the Mishna mentions only the head for the novelty concerning redemption. Nevertheless, Mahari Katz ruled correctly in his actual case that the second child is not firstborn for inheritance. The Mishna’s case of one born after miscarriages concerns a definite nonviable fetus. Rashi explains in Bekhorot and Behemah HaMekashah that the first fetus had not completed its months while the second had. This cannot ordinarily be known unless the form of the fetus shows that its development was incomplete, as Beit Yosef cites in siman 276. We require physical signs or survival for thirty days, after which the infant leaves the status of a possible miscarriage. Rashi therefore says in Chullin that even if the entire fetus emerged, it remains a miscarriage. But where we see no definite defect, even if only its head emerged alive, the second child cannot receive firstborn status. Even if the first was merely a doubtful miscarriage, the second cannot acquire firstborn rights through doubt. Moreover, reason requires following the majority, that the first was viable, as Tosafot write in Niddah 44, except that for mourning the Sages were lenient in doubt and allowed us to consider that perhaps it was a miscarriage. In Mahari Katz’s case, therefore, the later child certainly has no firstborn right, since no defect was seen in the first infant and it lived for some time. Even if only its head had emerged alive, I would rule the same, because no defect was observed in the first child. This appears correct.
