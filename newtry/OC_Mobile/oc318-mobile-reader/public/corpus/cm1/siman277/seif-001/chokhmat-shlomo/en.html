Seif 8. One born after miscarriages is a firstborn even though the head of the miscarriage emerged while alive. Note: I propose a novel rule. The Torah's section of the beloved and hated wives was interpreted by the Sages as referring to a relationship involving a prohibition where kiddushin nevertheless takes effect. Why did the Torah choose that case if even a mamzer born from a relationship punishable by karet or a court-imposed death can be a firstborn for inheritance? The Sema's statement that the "hated" wife is one with whom kiddushin does not take effect is difficult, for the Gemara expressly establishes the verse where kiddushin does take effect. I suggest the following. Our seif teaches that a child born after a nonviable fetus can still be the firstborn because the father's heart does not grieve over the nonviable child. The verse says that the beloved wife gives birth first, yet the firstborn son belongs to the hated wife. This can describe a first child of the beloved wife who was nonviable, so that the later child of the hated wife is still legally firstborn, as indicated by the phrase "the first of his strength," from which the Sages derive the requirement that the father's heart grieve over the prior child. If the later child is a mamzer, however, the father may not grieve over his death, since his continued life perpetuates mamzerut in Israel and is itself the result of an irreparable transgression. Still, a mamzer ordinarily remains a firstborn because children of that type would ordinarily cause paternal grief; the Torah excludes only a child whose very physical condition makes him nonviable and therefore generally not mourned. Yet if the lawful wife first bore a living miscarriage and the later son from the prohibited union is a mamzer, both children are ones over whom this father does not grieve. In that special case the verse may reveal that we assess this particular father, so the later mamzer may not displace the first. This explains why the Torah formulated the case specifically with a prohibited relationship where kiddushin takes effect. The textual implication supports this novel rule. The Mechaber also omits the Mishna's cases of an amorphous fetus or a dismembered fetus, as does Rambam, while the Tur records them. See Beit Yosef and my Even HaEzer, sixth edition, siman 94, where I explained Rambam differently and questioned the Mechaber's change of wording.
