The head of a stillborn infant. See Baer Heitev, which states that even if the entire stillborn infant was born the same rule applies, contrary to Responsa She'erit Yosef 25. Chatam Sofer, Even HaEzer II:161, writes that elsewhere he produced strong support for She'erit Yosef, and that this issue arose twice in his own community, where they persuaded the fathers during their lifetimes to reach a settlement with the son whose firstborn status was doubtful. Shaar Mishpat writes that the dispute concerns only an infant known not to have completed its months, a definite nonviable birth. If it is not known that the months were incomplete, for example where it was born nine months after the mother's immersion, then even if it died immediately, and certainly if it died within thirty days, the majority rule is that women give birth to viable children. For this reason, if a man dies leaving a pregnant wife and she gives birth to a child who emerges alive, even if it dies immediately the child exempts its mother from chalitzah and yibbum by Torah law. The next son is therefore not certainly a firstborn for inheritance. At the least his status is doubtful and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Moreover, he may not even qualify as a doubtful firstborn because he was not recognized as such at birth, as Rama rules in seif 11 regarding one born after a child whose paternity was uncertain between a nine-month child of the first husband and a seven-month child of the second. Accordingly, She'erit Yosef correctly ruled in his actual case, where the earlier child was born after nine months and lived four or five hours, that the next child was not a firstborn for inheritance. Sema and Shach objected only to his broader statement that even a definite nonviable infant ceases to count as a stillbirth if it was fully born. On that broader point their objection is correct, but the practical ruling in his case was sound.
