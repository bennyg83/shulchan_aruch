Even if the father said of one who was not previously established as his son at all: He is my son and my firstborn. Rambam holds that a father is believed by the Torah's law of recognition to say, "This is my son and my firstborn," even where the person had never previously been established as his son. Rashbam and Tosafot disagree. In their view the special law of recognition applies only where the person is already established as the father's son and the father identifies him as the firstborn. If he was not established as his son, the father is believed only through a migo, because he could have given him all his property as a gift. Rosh appears to follow Rambam and grants recognition even without such a migo. There is a difficulty from Ketubot. A man says, "This is my son and he is a kohen." Rabbi says he is believed to feed him terumah but not to marry him into priestly lineage. Rabbi Chiya asks why. Rabbi answers: I believe him for terumah because it is within his power to feed him terumah, but not for marriage because it is not within his power. If the father is believed by recognition even where the person was not previously established as his son, why is a migo needed even for terumah? Just as the Torah believes a father to say that his son is the child of a divorced or chalutzah woman and therefore disqualified, all the more he should be believed to establish him as a valid kohen. Nimukei Yosef cites an opinion that if a husband says with certainty that the child is legitimate, he can be believed to validate him because the Torah believed him to invalidate and the mother has a presumption of fitness. This difficulty points toward Rashbam and Tosafot. The father's Torah credibility of recognition exists only regarding someone already known to be his son. Therefore, when he brings an otherwise unknown person and says, "This is my son and a kohen," he is not believed through recognition. His limited credibility for terumah comes only from the fact that he could feed him terumah himself. The same may explain the opinion in Nimukei Yosef that where paternity itself is disputed, the child remains of doubtful legitimacy. The father cannot invoke recognition to validate someone not established as his child. Likewise, the Torah's credibility for saying, "My son is a mamzer," applies only when he says this person is his son but was born through a prohibited union. Shiltei Gibborim writes that if he says his wife committed adultery and the child is not his at all, he is not believed. The Torah believed a father regarding his son, not regarding one whom he says is not his. Accordingly, both for disqualification and validation, recognition presupposes that the person is known to be the son. The passage concerning customs duties, where the father first called someone his son and later his slave, can likewise be understood as a case where the person was known to be his child but the doubt concerned whether his mother was a Jewish woman or a maidservant. The Jerusalem Talmud adds a further complication. A man came before Rabbi and said, "This is my son and he is a kohen." Rabbi accepted that he was his son but not that he was a kohen. Rabbi Chiya objected that if you believe him he is his son, believe him he is a kohen; if not, do not believe even the paternity and then he is a remote witness who can testify about him. One can answer that Rabbi says, "I believe he is your son, but perhaps he is the son of a divorced or chalutzah woman." But if the father has Torah credibility to disqualify a known son as the child of a divorced or chalutzah woman, why should he not be believed all the more to validate him? The Jerusalem Talmud appears to imply that paternal recognition gives credibility to disqualify but not necessarily to confer priestly status. This point remains difficult.
