(Seif 1: "and he shall send forth his animal.") The Sema emends the verse to "and it shall consume," and then struggles to explain the phrase here regarding an animal that eats, etc. All of this resulted from overlooking the opening sugya of Bava Kamma, where the Gemara explicitly says that "and he shall send forth" implies both tooth and foot: foot from the verse, "those who send forth the foot of the ox," and tooth from the verse, "and I will send the tooth of beasts against them." The phrase "and it shall consume" is needed only to teach liability for tooth and foot even where the animal went on its own without active sending. Although the baraita says, "and it shall consume, this is tooth," according to the conclusion the principal word "consume" is naturally associated with tooth because the verse needed that word in any event to teach the broader liability. The Gemara further says that "and he shall send forth" implies damage even where the object is not completely destroyed, whereas "and it shall consume" suggests complete consumption. We do not say that because tooth is also alluded to by the word "consume," one might have thought liability applies only where the item is totally destroyed. Rather, "and he shall send forth" teaches otherwise. This is exactly the intention of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. They did not begin the law of tooth with the verse "and it shall consume," because they wished to conclude that liability applies even when the food is not completely consumed, a rule derived from "and he shall send forth." They therefore first explain that "and he shall send forth" also refers to an animal eating. This appears correct and true, unlike the Sema's claim that the rule is inferred merely because trampling by foot does not totally destroy an item. That has no source. The rule is learned expressly from Scripture.
