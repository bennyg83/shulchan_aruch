The Rosh writes that payment for benefit applies when one’s person benefits through another, or when one’s property benefits from another through an act of one’s own property, such as an animal eating from the side of the public way or falling into a garden. But where one’s property benefits through another person’s act, the owner is exempt. This follows Tosafot’s first answer and implies that if another person pushes food into an animal’s mouth, the owner is exempt even for the benefit. Maharshal rules similarly. It nevertheless appears that this concerns food belonging to a third party, from whose owner the animal’s owner is exempt. If the feeder used his own food, the animal’s owner must reimburse him, no less than one who improves another’s property without authorization, because he intended to confer benefit. All this follows Tosafot’s first answer. Tosafot ultimately offer another answer, however, namely that the benefit in their case was merely decorative and therefore not legally significant. That answer appears stronger, because the Gemara does not state the distinctions introduced by the first answer, and the Gemara’s attempted proof from orlah and shemittah also fits it better. Consequently, whether one is exempt when his property benefits from another’s property solely through a third person’s act remains an unresolved question of law.
