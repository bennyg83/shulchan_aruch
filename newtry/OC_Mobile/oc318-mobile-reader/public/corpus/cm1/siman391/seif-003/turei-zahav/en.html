[Seif 7] Some obligate payment. This is the Ramah's opinion cited by the Tur. Maharshal rejects it. He argues that although below, concerning the mouth of the cow, we conclude that the law follows the place of eating rather than the place of taking, even to impose liability, that is only where the taking itself involved a possible basis for liability, but payment could not be imposed because the actor who handed over the food was a deaf-mute, minor, or mentally incompetent person. If the animal took the food in the public domain, where there was no liability at all because it had permission to walk and eat, exemption is proper. Therefore, when the matter is doubtful, liability cannot be imposed. Maharshal also says that Tosafot and the Rosh evidently disagree with the Ramah, because in the discussion whether the cow's mouth is considered the injured party's courtyard, they strained to find a practical difference in a case where Reuven hands Shimon's produce to the animal through a legally incompetent person. Why did they not give the simple case of food taken in the public domain and carried into the private domain, which would be liable only if the cow's mouth is treated as the injured party's domain? These words are difficult. How can Maharshal say that the Rosh disputes the Ramah when in siman 390, seif 5, we cited the Rosh's view that the verse concerning foot makes liability depend on the place where the destruction occurred? That is the very principle here, for liability is determined at the place of consumption, and the two cases share one rationale. Maharshal's question as to why Tosafot and the Rosh resorted to the case of a legally incompetent person is also not difficult. Everyone agrees that if the animal took the food in the public domain and then brought it into the private domain, it is liable, because upon entering the private domain it is considered to have taken the food anew at that moment. This resembles the rule in siman 390, seif 11, concerning an animal that continues following a bucket: the rooster's force is not considered interrupted from the cord. Although the initial uprooting was direct bodily force and the later movement might appear to be pebbles, because it continues following and moving the object, the act remains its direct force. The same applies here to the continuing taking. Therefore the Gemara's question whether the cow's mouth is treated as the injured party's courtyard could not concern that simple case, and Tosafot had to find the practical difference involving a legally incompetent person. This is clear in my opinion.
