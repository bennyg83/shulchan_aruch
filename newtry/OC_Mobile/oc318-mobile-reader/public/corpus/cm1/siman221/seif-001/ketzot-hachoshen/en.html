There was an original price agreement. Nimukei Yosef, citing Ramban, writes that when an employer and workers first agreed upon a wage and the employer later retracted, even if he expressly said, "I am willing to continue only for a different amount," and the workers then persuaded him to continue, the renewed agreement is ordinarily presumed to rest on the original terms. The reason is that the initial agreement created a valid grievance if one side withdrew. When they later reconciled, they are presumed to have reconciled on the basis of the original agreement. This differs from the Tosefta concerning a seller and buyer who had no earlier binding price. There, there is no prior agreement to which the later reconciliation can be referred. Nimukei Yosef adds an important limitation: even where there was an earlier agreement, if the withdrawing workers can immediately find other employment and therefore have no grievance, we do not automatically return to the original price. The presumption depends on the existence of a valid grievance. Sema, when quoting Nimukei Yosef, emphasized the distinction between an earlier price and no earlier price, but the original source makes clear that the grievance is the key. This requires study regarding Rama's application of the rule here to sale. In labor law the Gemara explicitly recognizes a grievance when one party backs out. What is the corresponding grievance in an ordinary sale? There is a parallel concept. Bava Metzia says that one who promises a small gift and then gives it to another causes a grievance because the first recipient relied upon the promise. Tosafot further says that even when the market price changes, retracting from an agreed transaction can constitute bad faith. Rama rules accordingly in siman 204. Therefore, in a sale where the parties had fixed a price and one side retracted in circumstances that generate the recognized grievance of bad faith, a later reconciliation can be presumed to revive the original price. But according to the Shach, who holds that when market prices change there may be no bad faith, there would likewise be no such grievance and no reason to presume the old price after reconciliation. This also bears on a case in Even HaEzer where a man and woman had discussed one sum for kiddushin, disagreed, and later resumed the arrangement. Beit Shmuel applies our rule and says the original sum governs. In my view that is not automatic. A mere verbal negotiation between a man and woman does not create the same recognized grievance as an employment agreement or a small promised gift. Without such a grievance, there is no basis to presume that the later kiddushin revived the earlier price terms. The decisive criterion is the legal grievance, not merely the fact that some amount was previously discussed.
