(In the gloss.) There was initially a fixed price. To explain this ruling, we must cite the Nimukei Yosef in the chapter HaOmanin on the Gemara's ruling that if labor worth six dinars was performed, the employer gives them only a sela. The later authorities wrote in the Ramban's name that this does not refer only to an employer who withdrew without specification, but even where he expressly said that he wished to pay only the lesser amount. Nevertheless, if the workers later persuaded him, they are presumed to have persuaded him on the basis of their original condition. This is because they expressly protested and appeased him after his withdrawal. Where they say nothing, however, they certainly accept the position he expressly stated upon withdrawing. Any subsequent pulling or act of acquisition is then presumed made at the revised price, since he was legally entitled to withdraw and expressly did so. The Nimukei Yosef's statement in the Ramban's name that the case is different because there was no initial fixed price does not mean that there was no later appeasement. Rather, it teaches a stronger point: even appeasement is ineffective where no price had ever been fixed. According to this, the Rama's words here, “if one withdrew and they afterward pulled without specification,” require substantial study. We have no indication from the buyer that he refused to purchase at the revised price. How, then, was the seller's express withdrawal from the first price nullified? The Rama himself says that there was initially a fixed price and one party withdrew, which implies an express withdrawal. Nor can one say that the Rama speaks where the buyer repeatedly urged and appeased the seller, as with the workers, for he should not have omitted a fact that is fundamental to the law. I can uphold the Rama's words only as follows. One party withdrew from the fixed price, thereby nullifying the sale, and afterward that same party came and pulled the item without specification. He is certainly presumed to have pulled on the basis of the original fixed price, because the opposing party never revealed an intention to nullify that price. The law is then like the Mechaber's ruling where the seller was the party who demanded completion of the sale. This matter, however, is unrelated to the Nimukei Yosef cited above. Although the rule is sound in itself, I find no source for it and it requires study. As to the principal law, the correct view appears to be that whenever the seller expressly withdrew from the first fixed price and the buyer afterward pulled without specification, he pulled on the basis of the second price.
