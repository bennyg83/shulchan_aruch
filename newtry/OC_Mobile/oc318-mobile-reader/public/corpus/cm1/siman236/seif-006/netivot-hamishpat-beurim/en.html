The owners cannot remove it. This appears to concern a sale at fair value, where the amount of the debt equaled the value of the land. If the non Jew sold it for less than its value, the case is no better than a field that was merely collateral in his hands and he sold it, where Rama in seif 9 rules that the sale can be undone even for a small deficiency and even produce consumed must be returned. If the non Jew collected under his own legal courts, the Jewish owner may be unable to recover, as Rivash writes. Tumim in siman 158 likewise says that one who obligated himself to a non Jew effectively subjected himself to the governing legal system. At first glance Rambam and Mechaber are difficult, as Sema notes. Shach also asks regarding seif 9 why, where there is a king or authority, the owner cannot recover because of despair, when siman 371 rules that despair does not acquire land. In my view Rambam follows the principle that where, under the non-Jewish law, a creditor who lawfully collected land after the debt matured and at fair value has an indefeasible right, the Jewish debtor cannot later recover that land from him under their law. Mishneh LaMelech cites Maharit and Radbaz that whenever a Jew could not recover from the non Jew under that legal system, and the non Jew later sold to another Jew, the first Jew cannot recover from the second under Jewish law either. The second can say, "I come with the right of one against whom you could not litigate successfully." This also explains seif 9. If a legal tribunal was available and the owner never brought a claim, his conduct indicates that he lacked a provable right under that law. If he cannot recover from the non Jew under that law, he cannot recover from the Jew who purchased from him, because the Jew purchased the non Jew's legal position. If the original owner still has a viable claim against the non Jew in that legal system, he may bring it. If he succeeds and would have been able to recover the land, he can likewise proceed against the Jewish purchaser, because the purchaser acquired only whatever right the non Jew actually had. The case here is therefore where the owner does not wish to sue the non Jew but instead sues the Jewish purchaser. The purchaser may answer: "Since you do not sue the non Jew, you evidently know that you could not prevail there, perhaps because he has legally recognized witnesses or other proof. If you have no right against him under that law, you have no right against me who comes through him." This reasoning applies specifically where the first wrongdoer is a non Jew whose legally recognized right can differ from ours. With a Jewish robber, Jewish law applies equally to robber and purchaser, and one who buys stolen land must return it. This is why siman 371 rules that despair does not acquire land. Even with a non-Jewish robber, if the owner actually complained in their courts and the non Jew merely refused to obey because of violence, the Jewish purchaser cannot invoke the non Jew's position. The non Jew prevailed only through force, not through a legally recognized right.
