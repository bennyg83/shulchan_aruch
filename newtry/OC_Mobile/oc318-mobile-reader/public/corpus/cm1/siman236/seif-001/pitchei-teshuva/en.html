See Baer Heitev, who writes that Rosh in the name of Rabbeinu Gershom and Tur disagree, and see Responsa Shav Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 16, to which Baer Heitev refers, who likewise agrees with those authorities against Rambam. He further discusses a case where Reuven purchased Levi's house from a non Jewish creditor of Levi's father Yaakov. The creditor had collected his debt from the house because, in the governmental registry, it still stood in Yaakov's name even though according to Jewish law Yaakov had already sold it to his son Levi. At the time, Levi was absent because he had been detained in a distant land. Levi's relatives warned Reuven not to buy the house because the non Jew had wrongfully taken it, and that when Levi returned he would litigate against the creditor. Reuven ignored the warning and bought it. When Levi returned, he strongly protested and considered suing Reuven, but first chose to proceed against the creditor himself, reasoning that perhaps he could recover the house without paying Reuven. The authorities told him the matter would have to be brought directly before the king. Because this required great expense and loss of time, Levi abandoned that course and instead offered to reimburse Reuven all his expenses if Reuven would return the house. Reuven refused. Shav Yaakov ruled that Reuven must return the house. In this case even Rambam would agree, because it is considered a case where the owners do not acknowledge the creditor's right. Although Levi admits that his father Yaakov had owed the debt, Levi himself, to whom the house now belongs, owes the creditor nothing. The creditor's argument is that the house belonged to Yaakov, the debtor, and Levi disputes that factual premise. Nor can one say that because a governmental court existed in that city and the house was presumably collected through that court, dina d'malchuta dina validates the seizure, as discussed at the end of siman 236. The governmental collection itself was based on a mistake, because the authorities thought the house still belonged to Yaakov. This also differs from the Gemara's rule that where there is an available court and the owner fails to complain, his silence is treated as waiver. Levi did in fact go to pursue legal relief and did not waive his claim. Any expenditures Reuven made improving the house are treated as expenditures made without permission, as stated in Shulchan Aruch, seif 8, all the more so after the relatives had warned him not to build. Shav Yaakov adds that even if the house truly had still belonged to Yaakov and was lawfully collected for Yaakov's debt, it should eventually be returned to the owner under the rule that judicial appraisal returns forever, as stated in the Gemara and Shulchan Aruch, siman 103. Although where the creditor sells the appraised land to another it ordinarily does not return because the purchaser acquired with intent to keep the land, this case is different because Reuven was warned beforehand not to buy. He concludes that for all these reasons Reuven must return the house to Levi.
