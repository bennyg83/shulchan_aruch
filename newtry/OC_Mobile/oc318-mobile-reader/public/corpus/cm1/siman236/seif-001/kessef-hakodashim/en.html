The law of the kingdom is law. Regarding what is stated at the end of siman 236 about a transaction performed through a governmental judge, the distinctions in siman 68 concerning gentile courts must also be considered. When a gentile sells property on account of a debt owed to him by a Jew, the considerations discussed in simanim 45, 66, and 154 apply as to whether a Jew who derives his claim through the gentile receives the same rights that the gentile would have under their law. See the Sema, Shach, Taz, and Urim VeTumim. The Urim VeTumim questions why siman 66 states the matter without qualification, since it depends on the dispute discussed in simanim 45 and 154. At first glance, after the Rama ruled in siman 154 there is no further difficulty, especially since the Urim VeTumim himself writes in siman 25 that in these lands one who rules like the Mechaber against the Rama's accepted ruling is considered to have erred in an explicit rule. It appears, however, that his intent was to prevent error in places whose custom is to follow the Mechaber against the Rama. This also explains the purpose of the Sema and Shach in siman 45. There is room to distinguish a promissory note from a neighboring property right. Concerning a window or similar servitude one can say, "sufficient is the difficulty in its own time," while the neighbor was a gentile. A promissory note is different, because from the outset the debtor obligated himself according to the mode of enforcement in gentile courts, knowing that the gentile would pursue the matter only there. We similarly rule at the end of siman 68 that even one who did not understand the writing or language of a gentile instrument nevertheless accepted it. Thus, even when the gentile transfers his right to a Jew, the Jewish transferee may receive that right as well. This requires study. It is also not fully clear from the Sema in siman 45 whether, when dina demalchuta is added to the other considerations, all agree that the Jew receives the gentile's original rights. The Sema first presents it as an additional consideration and then offers another resolution. At minimum, where it is established that the Jew receives the rights of the gentile and it is known that under gentile law the rights contained in the instrument would not be altered, then in our present circumstances, when governmental authority is the practical means of enforcing judgments, one cannot remove land from the purchaser merely because of halachic doubts, so long as there is sufficient basis to recognize that he possesses the gentile's right, even in a case where ordinary acceptance cannot be invoked. The distinctions of the Sema and Taz in siman 154 appear to apply even according to the Mechaber. In such a case I arranged a compromise after explaining that one cannot simply declare that the purchaser acquired unlawfully, because there are substantial grounds recognizing his acquisition.
