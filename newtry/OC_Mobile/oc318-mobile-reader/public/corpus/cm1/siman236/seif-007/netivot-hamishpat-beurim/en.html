At first glance this implies that the owner must reimburse even up to the full value of the house and cannot say, "Keep the land yourself and give me one fourth in money." Why is this different from a purchaser from sikarikon, where the purchaser must give the owners one fourth even if he paid the full market value to the sikarikon? The distinction is that in sikarikon the Sages made a fixed enactment that the owners receive one fourth. Even if the purchaser paid only half value, he still gives only one fourth. Conversely, even if he paid full value he must still give one fourth, because the Sages did not distinguish. Rashba adds that the enactment was known to everyone, so the purchaser is presumed to have bought on that understanding. The remaining question is from siman 356. One who buys from a notorious thief and claims, "I intended to help and rescue the owner's property," can recover what he expended. This implies that if he admits he intended to buy for himself and not rescue the owner, he may not recover. Why then does our ruling appear to require reimbursement even where the purchaser intended to acquire for himself? Ketzot HaChoshen writes at length that even there, one who did not intend rescue can recover according to the benefit received. In my view, here too the purchaser recovers only when he claims, "I acted for your benefit," meaning he descended to improve another's property. Then he is like one who improves another's field without permission and is entitled to reimbursement. This is not merely one who drives away a lion or pays another's debt, because the property was already effectively in the lion's hands, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 58. But if he bought for himself, even inadvertently not knowing it was stolen, he need not necessarily be reimbursed. That resembles the rule cited by Shach in siman 391 that when another person's property is benefited through one's act but the actor did not intend to improve the other's property, liability can be absent. The special liability of one who descends into another's field arises because he intended to improve it. At minimum, according to Shach that question is a safek din, so the original owner cannot extract money from one who bought inadvertently. Perhaps an inadvertent buyer can argue that had he known the true facts he would have acted for the owner's benefit. But one who knew it was stolen and nevertheless intended only to acquire it for himself unlawfully should certainly be exempt from reimbursement according to all opinions, as in siman 356. Rama did not need to repeat that qualification here because he already stated in siman 356 that the purchaser must say, "I intended for your benefit." Mechaber's phrase, "if the purchaser expended in order to remove it from the non Jew," also implies that he intended to rescue it from the non Jew for the owner's benefit rather than unlawfully acquire it for himself.
