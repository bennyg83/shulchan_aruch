To remove it. Baer Heitev cites the dispute between Rambam and Rosh in the name of Rabbeinu Gershom. Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 16, supports the authorities who disagree with Rambam. He discusses Reuven, who purchased Levi's house from a non-Jewish creditor of Levi's father Yaakov. The house remained registered in the secular records under Yaakov's name even after it had been transferred to Levi according to Torah law. While Levi was detained in a distant country, the creditor seized the house for Yaakov's debt. Levi's relatives warned Reuven not to buy it, because the creditor's seizure was unlawful and Levi intended to challenge it on his return. Reuven ignored the warning and purchased the house. When Levi returned, he initially pursued the creditor in secular court, hoping to recover the house without paying Reuven, but the court told him the case required an expensive appeal before the sovereign. He therefore abandoned that route and offered to reimburse Reuven's expenditure in exchange for restoration of the house. Reuven refused. Shev Yaakov rules that Reuven must return the house. Even Rambam would agree because this is effectively a case in which the owner does not concede the creditor's right. Levi admits that his father owed the debt, but Levi, to whom the house belongs, personally owes the creditor nothing. The creditor's claim depends on treating the house as still belonging to Yaakov, which Levi expressly denies. Nor can one invoke dina de-malchuta merely because the local court authorized the seizure, since the seizure rested on the mistaken assumption that the house still belonged to Yaakov. The rule that one who had access to a government court and failed to complain is deemed to have waived does not apply, because Levi did in fact pursue his legal remedy and never waived his claim. Reuven's building expenses are treated as the expenses of one who improves another's property without authorization, all the more so after he was explicitly warned not to build. Shev Yaakov adds that even if the house had still belonged to Yaakov and had been lawfully seized for Yaakov's debt, there would be an argument that it should return under the doctrine that judicial appraisal of land to a creditor is reversible. Although land sold by the creditor to a third party ordinarily does not return, Reuven purchased after explicit warning and cannot rely on the ordinary purchaser's assumption. For all these reasons Reuven must return the house to Levi. Shev Yaakov notes that one could even argue for return without reimbursement, although in the actual case Levi offered to reimburse all proper expenditures.
