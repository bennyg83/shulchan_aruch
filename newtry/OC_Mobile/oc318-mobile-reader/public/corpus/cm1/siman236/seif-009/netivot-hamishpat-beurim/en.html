For he sold it lawfully. See Sema, seif katan 24. He writes that although their law may differ, one cannot say here, "What the first sold to the second..." in the manner discussed above. The distinction is that in the earlier case the non Jew had an actual legal right even while the property remained in his own hands. The Jewish purchaser could therefore say, "I bought the first holder's existing right." Here, by contrast, while the land remained in the non Jew's hands, if it was worth more than the debt he had no right even under their law to keep the excess. Their law only protects a later purchaser after the non Jew sold below value. Since that special protection exists only in favor of the purchaser, and the purchaser here is a Jew, he cannot rely on their purchaser-protection rule against the requirements of Jewish law.
