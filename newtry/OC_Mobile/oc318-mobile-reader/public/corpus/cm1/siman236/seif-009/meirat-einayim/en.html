And he need not give the owners anything. Even though this non-Jew presumably sold the field cheaply because it came into his hands through coercion and without payment, once the Jewish owner despaired of recovering it, no right remains. This differs from the coercer and sikarikon discussed in seif 1. There, silence does not prove despair, because fear of death forced the Jew to surrender the field and remain silent. Therefore the Sages enacted that the purchaser must give the original owner one third, as explained in seif 1. This is clear. It is unlike the Levush, who writes that here we do not say the coercer sold cheaply because the land did not come to him gratuitously; rather, he took it in payment of a debt or claim. The Levush therefore assumes this case concerns only a non-Jew who took the land for a debt. That is incorrect. The Tur and Mechaber apply the rule also to a non-Jew who took the field from a Jew through coercion without any claim, and nevertheless the purchaser need not give the owner one third. Even on the Levush's reasoning, it is difficult why the matter depends on despair. Without despair, he could say that since the owner did not protest, surely the non-Jew's claim was valid and the owner effectively surrendered the land for the debt. Moreover, according to his approach this law is entirely redundant, because the same matter was already stated in seif 7.
For he sold it lawfully, etc. Beit Yosef writes at the end of siman 150: once the time fixed for repayment passed, the creditor had authority to sell the property. But if he sold it for even slightly less than its value, the sale is void, like an agent who made an error. Darkei Moshe cites this language at the end of the present siman. The meaning is that the Jew did not transfer the land absolutely to the non-Jew if payment was not made by the fixed time; rather, he authorized him to sell it. In our law and similar cases the non-Jew therefore has only the legal status of an agent, even if the wording used avoided asmachta as explained in siman 73. Even if their secular law would treat the matter differently, Beit Yosef there cites Rashbatz: "Since this sale is void under our law, even if it would be valid under theirs, we void it." See the proof cited there. This is unlike the Levush, who explains that if the non-Jew sold it below value, it returns to the owners because we see the non-Jew intended to acquire only the amount he actually sold, meaning that even though the Jew said, "If I do not redeem it by the deadline it is yours," the non-Jew did not intend to acquire the full field, only the amount corresponding to the price he ultimately received, leaving the owner with a right in the excess. The Levush did not examine the source of the law and supplied a reason of his own that is not satisfactory.
