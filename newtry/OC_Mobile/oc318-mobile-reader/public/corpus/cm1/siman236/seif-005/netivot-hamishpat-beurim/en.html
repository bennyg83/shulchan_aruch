We plead for him that the first purchaser acquired it from them. All the more so, if the purchaser himself claims that he later bought it directly from the original owner, he is believed through a three-year chazakah. Rashba questions this. Even if we actually saw him buy from the owners after first buying from the sikarikon, we say the owner may have transferred only from fear. All the more so, perhaps he was afraid to protest and remove the purchaser from possession. Rashba answers that the law of sikarikon is different from ordinary robbery. The Sages expressly enacted that one who buys from a sikarikon acquires upon giving one fourth to the owners, and this enactment is publicly known. Therefore, if the purchaser has consumed the property for three years, there are grounds to presume that the one-fourth was paid. By contrast, a purchaser from an ordinary robber acquires nothing from the dispossessed owner merely by paying the robber, and people do not ordinarily buy from a robber and then pay the full value again to the true owner. Sema in siman 149 appears to hold that even a purchaser from an ordinary robber can establish chazakah and that we do not say the owner feared the purchaser. Yet with sikarikon he holds that a later sale by the owner can be void because of fear. On that approach one must distinguish that sikarikon involves life-threatening coercion, so the owner may fear even the person who bought from him, while an ordinary robber does not produce the same continuing fear. In my view the primary ruling follows Rashba. If Sema were correct, the Gemara in Bava Batra 47, which challenges Rav Nachman from the law of sikarikon, could simply answer that sikarikon is different. No posek is known to disagree with Rashba, and it appears Sema simply did not see these words. Therefore, where it is known that the property came to the holder from an ordinary robber or non Jew, even a deed or evidence of a later purchase from the original owner does not necessarily help, because the owner may have acted from fear. The rule in siman 149 that a possessor can say, "I bought it from you," applies where it is not known that his possession came through the robber. Then his own direct-purchase claim is credible. The reason chazakah helps here with sikarikon is that the purchaser acted within a lawful rabbinic enactment intended to prevent land from becoming permanently lost to the coercive holder. Even Moshe and Aharon could properly buy on that basis and pay the owners one fourth. The purchaser is therefore not treated as a robber, and we do not presume a kosher Jew to be a robber merely because the former owner says, "I was afraid to protest." Since the owner had an immediate right to one fourth of his land without paying money, he should have protested. It is possible this too is part of Rashba's intent.
