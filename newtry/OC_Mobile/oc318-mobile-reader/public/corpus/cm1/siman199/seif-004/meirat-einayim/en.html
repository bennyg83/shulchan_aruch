He has the status of a paid bailee. Meaning, he is liable for theft and loss because of the benefit that, before the orphans retracted, the purchaser had permission to eat the produce or do whatever he wished with it. The Sages were not concerned that if we leave the matter under Torah law, the purchaser might tell the guardians or orphans, "Your wheat burned or was lost in the attic." Since with respect to theft, loss, and guarding the produce the purchaser is a paid bailee and is exempt only for fire and other unavoidable accidents, which are uncommon, the Sages were not concerned about that possibility. They enacted the rule out of concern for "your wheat burned" only where there are additional common risks or losses that could result from the acquisition.
But in the other cases, the law of orphans, etc. The Tur writes this. The reason is that if the produce decreased in value after the purchaser performed meshichah, the purchaser cannot retract by arguing, "Just as the orphans could have retracted if the produce rose in value because with orphans only money acquires, so we should be able to retract if it decreases in value." The rule that their property is acquired specifically through money was established only for their benefit. If the orphans received the money, the purchaser did not pull the produce, and the produce then increased in value, the orphans may retract just like anyone else. If the produce decreased in value and the purchaser wishes to retract, he may retract only by accepting the curse of mi shepara. Otherwise, when the orphans need money, no one would give it to them until they first handed over the produce. If the orphans purchased produce and pulled it but did not yet pay, and the produce increased in value, they acquired it like anyone else. If the produce decreased in value, we do not restore Torah law and say they did not acquire until they paid, allowing them to retract. If so, when orphans need produce no one would sell it to them until they paid first. If the orphans paid money and did not pull the produce, and it then decreased in value, they may retract like anyone else. If it increased in value, the seller may retract upon accepting mi shepara. Otherwise, the seller might say, "Your wheat burned or was lost in your attic, because it entered your domain from the moment you gave the money." One cannot say that if the produce later burns the orphans can then retract just as they can when it decreases in value, because it is unreasonable that while the produce is valuable it should be considered in their domain, but after it burns it should retroactively return to the seller's domain. See the Perishah for further discussion.
Specifically orphans. Meaning, specifically with orphans do the ordinary rules of money not acquiring apply in the other cases. Hekdesh is different. This is clear.
If someone gave money to hekdesh and the property increased in value, etc. This clause is stated as an even greater novelty. Although hekdesh suffers a loss under this rule, it nevertheless cannot retract.
