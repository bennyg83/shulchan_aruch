Consecrated property and charity acquire through money. Kiddushin teaches that if one redeemed consecrated property for one hundred and, before pulling it, its value rose to two hundred, the redemption stands at one hundred. The Gemara says the rule prevents an ordinary person's power from being stronger than consecrated property. Tosafot asks from the law that orphans are treated like consecrated property: if a buyer paid orphans for produce and the price later rose before meshichah, the orphans can retract so they not be worse than an ordinary person, even though an ordinary seller who retracts after money is subject to the "Who exacted payment" curse. Tosafot answers that orphans are ordinary persons by Torah law. The Sages strengthened them by giving them some advantages of consecrated property. They did not intend their enactment to cause loss. Therefore, where applying the special rule would harm the orphans, the law reverts to ordinary private law. True consecrated property is different because money acquires it by Torah law. From this distinction it follows that ordinary charitable funds and our forms of consecrated communal property should be treated like orphans, not like biblical Temple consecration. Charity for the poor is private monetary property in many areas: oaths apply, ordinary interest law applies, and mere verbal dedication does not have every law of Temple consecration. Our communal consecrated property likewise has private monetary status in siman 95. Therefore Rama's broad ruling that charity or communal consecrated property cannot retract after receiving money appears incorrect when the price moves against them. Since their private law status is primary and the Sages only strengthened them for their benefit, they should be able to retract where otherwise they would lose, just as orphans can. Mordechai's discussion is not a decisive proof for Rama because it speaks primarily of true Temple treasury consecration. Even if his concern for disrespect could sometimes extend to communal sacred property, Tosafot, Rashba, and Ran ground the distinction in the more fundamental principle that rabbinic strengthening of ordinary property should not operate to its detriment. On that basis, charity and contemporary communal consecrated assets receive the stronger rule only when it benefits them.
Ran's own language supports this understanding. He says that charity can be compared to the property of orphans and can also have certain consecration like features, such as the special force of speech and perhaps the laws of an incomplete declaration. His comparison does not transform charity into full Temple consecration in every respect. If charity were completely equivalent to consecrated property, ordinary laws of oaths, interest, and overreaching would not apply as they do. Rather, the comparison is selective and works to strengthen the charitable fund. Ran's reference to the discussion in Nedarim whether charity has the law of an incomplete vow formula shows only that charity has some sacred characteristics. Accordingly, the conclusion is the same as for orphans: when either charity or our communal consecrated assets buy or sell through money, the special rules should work for their benefit, but they should not be trapped by a rabbinic strengthening when a price change would cause them loss. They may retract in the unfavorable direction. This is also implied by Ran's closing statement that one can learn the relevant charity rules both from the present sugya and from the chapter concerning orphans. If he meant to give charity every strict law of Temple consecration even to its detriment, the orphan passage would supply no useful analogy. His intent is therefore a protective analogy, not a complete identity.
