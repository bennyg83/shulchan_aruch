He may retract. See Shach, seif katan 2, who writes that if one performed meshichah on chametz belonging to hekdesh, he cannot retract, because the power of hekdesh should not be weaker than that of an ordinary person. If the object rises in value, the consequence follows accordingly. At first glance this is difficult from Tosafot in Gittin 52, who ask regarding "draw produce from orphans" that this rabbinic rule could harm them because buyers might say, "Your wheat burned." Tosafot answer that the buyers become paid custodians because they are permitted to eat the produce. Hekdesh is different. The ordinary laws of custodians do not apply to hekdesh, and the buyer cannot eat the item before paying, because it remains sanctified until redeemed onto the money. How then can meshichah be made effective rabbinically without recreating the concern that the buyer may say, "Your wheat burned"? It appears that the concern underlying the enactment applies only in ordinary dealings between private parties. A person exerts himself more to save his own property than another's. Hekdesh, however, is under the management of a treasurer, and after another person draws the item that person becomes, in effect, the one responsible for property of Heaven. Hekdesh is not privately owned by either the treasurer or the recipient. There is therefore no reason to assume that the treasurer will exert himself more than the recipient merely because of private ownership. Wherever it is, it remains in the treasury of Heaven. When we say that if one drew property worth two hundred the power of hekdesh should not be weaker than that of an ordinary person, this does not mean that the original reason of "your wheat burned" applies to hekdesh. Rather, once the Sages established meshichah as a valid mode of acquisition for ordinary property, they did not distinguish and say that hekdesh should be weaker. We find similarly that even where the original concern is absent, as with artisans discussed in siman 198, meshichah nevertheless acquires. There is also another answer. Where one drew hekdesh and had not yet paid, the Sages could not institute that meshichah itself remove the object from hekdesh merely because of concern for fire. D'oraisa, hekdesh does not leave its sanctity without money. The Sages cannot uproot a Torah law through an affirmative act, except in forms allowed by the general principles discussed in Yevamot 90. Therefore they could not make meshichah alone transfer it to ordinary ownership.
