Other movable property to sell. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov III, siman 170, who writes that the same applies, and all the more so, to those very movable objects themselves. It is exceedingly uncommon that one should sell them and then repurchase those identical utensils. The poskim spoke only of the more usual case. Moreover, the Gemara's expression, “there are monies that are like barter,” naturally refers to receiving another object in exchange. Our case therefore follows by an a fortiori inference, and one who disputes this is astonishing. Nevertheless, the wording of the Maggid Mishneh cited by the Sema, who explains the rule because “we view it as though...” does not appear to support this conclusion. See the next seif katan and Netivot HaMishpat, s.v. “but,” and the matter requires study.
If both parties agreed, he did not acquire. See Sema, seif katan 5, who refers to siman 204, seif 10, where the Mechaber holds that a debt arising from a prior sale is treated as though the money itself were presently in the purchaser's hand. This is the Rambam's view. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 170, writes that at first glance this could imply that according to the Rambam a loan is equivalent to money and effects a full Torah acquisition like cash, with only a rabbinic decree preventing completion lest the seller say, “Your wheat burned in the attic.” If so, in cases where money effects a complete acquisition, such as the four special times discussed in the next seif, a debt should likewise effect a complete acquisition. Yet the Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 5 of Laws of Sale, cited by the Sema in the preceding seif katan, does not imply this. He explains that when the debt arose from a sale, the transaction is fully effective because we view it as though the cow had been exchanged for the ox, not because the price money is deemed present. Ordinarily one might still have decreed lest the seller say that the goods burned, but the Gemara says that such a case is uncommon and the Sages did not decree. This indicates that without the character of an exchange of movable property, a loan would not effect a complete acquisition, because it is not treated as actual money. The fact that payment by loan nevertheless subjects one to Mi Shepara even when the debt did not arise from a sale, as appears from chapter 7 of Laws of Sale, means only that acquisition by debt is stronger than mere words but weaker than cash, and its legal consequence is Mi Shepara. Accordingly, even at the four special times, purchase by an existing debt would create only the obligation of Mi Shepara, not a full acquisition. So writes Chatam Sofer. In my humble opinion, his words require study; see Netivot Tzion for what I wrote on this.
