But if, etc. Meaning, it does not complete acquisition, but it does create the obligation of Mi Shepara, as Rambam rules in chapter 7 and Shulchan Aruch in siman 204, seif 10, according to Rambam, like other money.
Nevertheless, some say, etc. They challenge Rambam from Kiddushin, which states concerning a sale that one party acquires, and explains that a debt cannot serve as consideration. This shows that an existing loan does not acquire even land, just as it does not effect betrothal, and certainly not movable property. Raavad explains that the first clause imposes Mi Shepara, while the second does not. Ramban and Rashba hold that even the first clause entails no Mi Shepara because the consideration is a loan. The Yerushalmi likewise states that betrothal through a loan is treated stringently, land is not acquired, and regarding movable property no Mi Shepara is administered. Maggid Mishneh cites this. The cited Babylonian passage, however, concerns the benefit of extending a loan, through which a woman may be betrothed. Rambam rejects the other passage in favor of the rule that some payments function like exchange, which he reads literally, and Bava Metzia 48 supports him. The opposing authorities explain that the debtor designated a specific object at the time of the loan. See siman 195, seif 10, where Shulchan Aruch follows Rama's view here. Maggid Mishneh writes that all later authorities agree with it.
