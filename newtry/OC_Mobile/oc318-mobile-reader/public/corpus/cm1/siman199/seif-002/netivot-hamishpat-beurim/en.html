But if there was a debt owed to him. See Sema, seif katan 3, who explains that when the debt arose from an earlier sale, the Sages treated the first merchandise as though it were still in existence. The reason appears to follow what I wrote in siman 191 from Shitah Mekubetzet and Machaneh Efrayim: if one sells for immediate cash and the buyer refuses to pay, the seller may cancel the sale entirely. Since he can undo the first sale, using the debt from that sale as consideration for the second resembles barter. According to this, the original object should still need to be in existence. Some authorities, however, follow Rambam and hold that even a debt not arising from a sale can acquire. They question this from Kiddushin 6, where the Gemara says plainly that a loan does not acquire in a sale. Rambam's view can perhaps be explained as follows. The reason a loan does not acquire is that a loan is given to be spent and therefore no money remains with which to make the acquisition. Yet according to Abaye, who holds that a creditor collects retroactively, if the creditor consecrates or sells the collateralized property, the transfer can take effect because the property is viewed as having been acquired by the creditor from the time the money was originally advanced, once he ultimately collects it. It is as though he had said, "Acquire from now, when I later collect from you," and the later collection is only fulfillment of the condition, which requires no new kinyan. Although we rule like Rava that a creditor collects only from the time of collection onward, siman 103, seif 6, cites an opinion that once the repayment date has passed the creditor may sell the debtor's property. Baal HaTerumot explains that those authorities hold that even Rava agrees that after the due date collection works retroactively. Rambam can therefore rule that, for sale, when the debtor transfers land or movable property in payment of the entire debt, the creditor acquires. Rambam speaks after the due date, as Sema explains in siman 204 regarding extension of time. Perhaps Rambam would even say this before the due date where the debtor is present and waives the remaining time, for that is as though the due date has arrived. Kiddushin is different because the debt there is not being collected by receiving the particular property. Similarly, when the Gemara says generally that a loan does not acquire in a sale, it can be speaking where the debt is used merely as the token money for a much larger field, such as where the field is worth one hundred maneh and the buyer has a debt of one perutah against the seller and seeks to use that perutah as the acquisition money. There is no collection of the loan itself in that transaction. Because the original money is gone, it cannot serve as acquisition money. This can reconcile Rambam.
