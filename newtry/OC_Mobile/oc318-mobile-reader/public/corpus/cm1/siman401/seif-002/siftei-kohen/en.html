The first injured party is liable, etc. Likewise, if the second injured party seizes the animal, he becomes a paid custodian for the first injured party and for the owner, and the same pattern applies to later injured parties. Tosafos notes that sometimes the fourth injured party receives nothing while the third still has a share, and even the fifth may fail to recover his entire damage if his loss exceeds the fourth party's share. Maharsha asks why the later injured party should not collect from the owner and the earlier injured parties out of the body of the damaging animal. There is no difficulty. In the case where the animal was entrusted to a custodian, the custodian voluntarily assumed responsibility. Here, by contrast, the injured party seized it against the owner's will, and the owner may say that he was prevented from guarding it, like an animal forcibly removed by bandits. Nor can an earlier injured party be required to pay more than the share he has in an innocuous animal, because an innocuous animal pays only from its body. Even if his share were entirely his own, he could not be liable beyond that share. This distinction also matters for a forewarned animal, where seizure can create paid custodial responsibility for subsequent damage.
And some say, etc. The Tur cites Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh, and this is also the view of Rabbeinu Ephraim, Baal HaMaor, Ramah, Tosafos, Bartenura, and Agudah. This appears to be the primary interpretation of the Gemara, and Maharshal rules likewise. With a forewarned animal, however, everyone agrees that the one who seizes it becomes a custodian, because payment comes from the tortfeasor's general assets rather than only from the animal's body. At first I thought that seizure of a forewarned animal should even create liability for unavoidable accidents, like one who seizes collateral outside beit din. Baal HaMaor and Nimukei Yosef call him only a paid custodian, which seemed difficult. The matter is resolved, however, because liability for unavoidable accidents in collateral applies where the debtor has no other assets and the lien rests specifically upon the collateral. Here the tortfeasor can pay from elsewhere, so the lien does not rest specifically on this animal. Seizing the very animal that caused the damage is also not wrongful seizure. Thus the one who seizes it is a paid custodian, but does not acquire full liability for unavoidable accidents merely by seizure. Another possibility is that Baal HaMaor and Nimukei Yosef agree that Rabbi Yitzchak's rule creates liability for unavoidable accidents up to the debt amount, while the present case teaches paid custodial liability even for the excess value. In practice, full liability for unavoidable accidents applies only where there are no other assets from which payment can be made.
That there is no distinction, etc. The Sema distinguishes between responsibility for loss of the owner's share and responsibility for damage caused to third parties, relying on the law of partners as paid custodians. His distinction is incorrect and has significant practical consequences. The true distinction is different: here the injured party had no preexisting duty to guard the animal, so by seizing it he becomes a custodian only with respect to the share he acquired, and not even an unpaid custodian for the other party's share. Baal HaMaor and Mordechai explicitly make this distinction. Therefore, if the animal is lost while in his possession, he is exempt regarding his fellow's share. By contrast, with an ordinary jointly owned animal where each partner is obligated to guard, the partner in possession is liable even for the co-owner's share if the animal causes damage.
