A stipulation in a rental. If a person rents a small room opening into the landlord's house on condition that during cold weather he may place his beds in the landlord's house, the meaning of 'cold weather' or 'severe cold' follows ordinary local usage. If a woman then becomes a childbirth patient, that circumstance may not be included unless stipulated. Since both sides could have anticipated such a possibility and did not address it, the loss falls on the tenant, particularly because the tenant had greater reason to consider it. One who has only a right of passage through another's house cannot multiply customers and other persons entering and leaving for business in a manner that the parties did not contemplate. If the landlord merely grants 'permission' to place movables in a particular room, that does not necessarily prevent the landlord from placing his own objects there as well, even if the mixture reduces security. If he says, however, 'This room shall belong to you for your movables,' it appears that the landlord may not put his own items there. The fact that many people entered for some period does not easily establish a chazakah of waiver, especially where there is a reasonable explanation. If the rental provides one room for residence and one for storage, and during severe cold the tenant is to live together with the landlord, the phrase 'a room for residence' suggests that the residential room is not separately his during the period he lives in the landlord's house. If instead the wording is, 'I rent you two rooms, and during the cold you will live with me,' both rooms remain the tenant's even then. If the rent is stipulated to be paid monthly and the tenant fails to pay and has no other assets, the landlord may collect from the leasehold itself and rent the premises to another. Even if nonpayment was not expressly made a condition terminating the lease, collection may proceed through beit din, and any surplus received must be returned to the original tenant.
