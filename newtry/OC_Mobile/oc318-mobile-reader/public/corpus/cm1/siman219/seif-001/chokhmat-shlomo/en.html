Seif 2. The matter is entrusted to beit din, and they shall act according to whichever interpretation they believe his intent favored. Note: Baer HaGolah cites Kesef Mishneh as saying that the Rambam explains this like Rashi. According to that, those who disagree there, namely Tosafot and their followers as cited by the Sema in siman 240, would also disagree here. In my opinion, however, all agree here that the result cannot depend simply upon the judge's arbitrary preference. With two documents dated the same day, the underlying doubt is between two recipients. The donor himself has unquestionably divested himself of the property; the only uncertainty is which recipient acquired it. Neither recipient has any prior possession, so it is understandable that the law might entrust the choice to the judge. Indeed, one can suggest that the donor intended this very result. He issued equally dated documents to both because he loved them both and could not decide whom to favor, so he left the decision to beit din and removed it from his own judgment. Here, by contrast, the doubt is between seller and purchaser. The seller has the presumption of prior ownership. It is unreasonable that a judge's bare preference should allow him to remove property from that possession. Regarding the seller himself, there is no way to say he was uncertain whether he wished to retain the property or give it away. If he intended to give it to the other party he could have stated so, and if he intended to retain it he could likewise have clarified. He certainly had one intent; the uncertainty is ours in determining what he meant. In such a case the law turns not on the judge's will but on the judge's assessment of the transferor's intent. We find many cases where property is removed on the strength of a compelling assessment of intent. That is the rule here as well. One might ask why Tosafot, when challenging Rashi from the use of the term shuda in Gittin concerning an agent, did not instead cite this case, where the Gemara also uses the term shuda, apparently supporting Rashi. The answer is that the word shuda can bear both meanings. It derives from the idea of casting or directing something. It can mean that the matter is cast upon the judge to decide as he wishes, or that the judge casts the property to the person whom his assessment identifies as the proper recipient. Thus the same word can describe either judicial discretion or judicial assessment, depending upon context. Tosafot's difficulty from Gittin is specifically against Rashi's assertion that everywhere shuda means assessment of the parties' intent. An ordinary agent is not a judge upon whom adjudication is imposed. The term there can only be borrowed from shuda de-dayanei. If nowhere else does shuda refer to discretionary choice, it would be strange to use the term for the agent's choice. Therefore Tosafot's proof is sound. Afterward Hashem enlightened me and I found an explicit statement in the Rosh, Bava Batra, chapter 4, ruling 2 on 35b, that the shuda de-dayanei there is not like the ordinary meaning of shuda, which according to Rabbeinu Tam is whatever the judge chooses. The same is true in our case. This is a clear proof for my position.
