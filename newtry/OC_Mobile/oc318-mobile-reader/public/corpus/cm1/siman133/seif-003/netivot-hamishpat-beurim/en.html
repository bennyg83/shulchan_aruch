There were both deposit witnesses and witnesses who saw it. See Shach, seif katan 8, who questions Sema and concludes that even without witnesses to the original deposit the holder is not believed. In light of Sema's statement in siman 64, seif katan 4, and Shach's own statement there in seif katan 5, that a person who says from the outset, 'It is collateral in my possession,' is believed even without a migo, Sema's position can be understood. As I explained there, the basis is Ramban in Sefer HaZechut on Ketubot, chapter HaKotev, who writes that even without a migo a person should be believed to say that an item is collateral, for why should a claim of purchase be stronger than a claim of collateral? A sale is itself comparable to a pledge. Just as one is believed to claim purchase because of the chazakah that whatever is in a person's possession is his, based on the question how it came into his hands, the same chazakah supports a claim that it is collateral. Ramban answers that the limitation arises where the claimant seeks affirmative collection. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes explicitly that since a creditor acquires a pledge, possession of collateral is itself a kind of acquisition and resembles claiming partial ownership or ownership of the fruits, which is certainly supported by this chazakah. Thus, where siman 72 requires a migo, it concerns one who seeks to collect money. But if he does not seek payment and wishes only to retain the pledge until he is paid, he is believed even without a migo. Ran, cited by Shach in siman 72, seif katan 71, similarly rules that where the lender does not sue the borrower but the borrower sues for return of the pledge, the lender takes only a heset oath, because the dispute concerns the pledge itself. For this reason, a claim of pledge is a substantial claim even without a migo. Sema therefore requires witnesses to the original deposit in order to prevent the holder from being believed even to retain the pledge until payment. Ketzot HaChoshen asks on the Rosh, who explains that one who told witnesses that an item was collateral is no longer believed because a claim of collateral requires an oath. Ketzot argues that then, where there is a written record of a deposit and the custodian told witnesses that he returned it, a claim of return should likewise cease to be believed before beit din through a migo of ones, since the alternative claim requires an oath. There is no difficulty. In the pledge case, the statement before witnesses contains an admission of an existing obligation concerning the object itself. But when the custodian says, 'I returned the deposit,' he is asserting a ground of exemption. Even before beit din a person may change from one exempting claim to another exempting claim, and all the more so outside beit din, because a person does not wish to reveal his full claims except in beit din. He may therefore later change and claim ones. By contrast, after he told witnesses that the item is collateral, he cannot later claim before beit din that he returned it, because he first admitted an obligation regarding the object and would now be moving from liability to exemption. Concerning the words of Mabit cited by Shach in seif katan 5, and mistakenly printed as belonging to seif katan 6, Shach is certainly correct that there is no distinction between witnesses actually seeing the item in his possession and his admitting before them that it is there. Proof is found in Nimukei Yosef on Bava Batra 45 concerning the deceivers of Pumbedita. Nimukei Yosef explains that the phrase means that the owners had already appraised the garment, but does not say that they had appraised 'your garment,' because such wording would itself admit that the garment belonged to the claimant and was presently in the holder's possession. This explicitly shows that an admission before witnesses is equivalent to witnesses seeing the item.
And he warned him. At first glance it is difficult, because the holder should still be believed through a migo that he could have claimed ones. The same difficulty arises from Sema, seif katan 5, cited by Rama in siman 72, that if the holder claimed purchase before witnesses, he cannot later claim before beit din that he purchased it through a migo of return, because an item claimed as purchased is not intended to be returned. Why should he not be believed through a migo of ones? Ketzot HaChoshen answers that once the purchase claim is not believed, the holder is treated as one who denied a deposit and is liable even for ones. In my view, this may be correct where he claims purchase, because the chazakah of the original owner causes us to regard his claim as false, and he is consequently treated as one who denied a deposit. But if from the outset he says only that he is withholding the item because of a debt owed to him, there is no denial of the deposit at all. He does not deny ownership of the object and is merely withholding the deposit, a case in which Ketzot himself writes in siman 292 that he does not become liable for ones. Yet Mechaber's wording implies that the warning is effective even where the holder said that he was retaining the object for a debt. In my view, where the owner stipulates, 'Do not return it except before so and so and so and so,' the holder is certainly not believed through a migo. This is obvious according to the poskim who hold that even if he brings other witnesses who saw him return it, their testimony is ineffective, all the more so a migo. Even according to the poskim who accept other witnesses, the migo should still fail. The Gemara explains that specifying particular witnesses is designed to prevent the debtor from evading payment by saying, 'I paid before witnesses who have since died.' If we allowed a migo of ones in a deposit case, he could always evade the condition, since he would always possess that migo. The stipulation was therefore necessarily intended to defeat the migo. Although Sema rules that the owner did not intend to invalidate all other witnesses, he certainly intended to invalidate the migo, because otherwise his warning would accomplish nothing. Where he merely said generally, 'Do not return without witnesses,' one might explain, as above in seif katan 2, that he did not intend to eliminate a legal claim but simply relied on the condition to prevent an actual return without witnesses. But when he specified particular witnesses, the Gemara expressly states that the purpose is to prevent evasion. The migo is therefore ineffective. As for the case in siman 72, which concerns an earlier claim of purchase, Ketzot's explanation can be accepted.

It appears that if the parties' dispute concerns only the ownership of the object and not the money, for example where the holder says, 'I purchased it, but I have not yet paid the price and I still owe you the money,' he is believed through a migo that he could have said, 'I returned it,' even though he was warned not to return without witnesses. If he had claimed return, he would still have been obligated to pay only the price. This can resolve Tosafot's question in Bava Batra 45, as I wrote in my novellae to halachah and Tosafot.
