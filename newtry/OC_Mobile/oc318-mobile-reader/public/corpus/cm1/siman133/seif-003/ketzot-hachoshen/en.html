The statement can properly be treated as though there were witnesses and present observation. Taz rejects Sema's explanation that we accept what the possessor said outside beit din only to his detriment. If a person says outside beit din, "I borrowed and repaid," we do not split his statement and accept only the borrowing. Rather, Taz says the key is that the defendant must still possess a migo when he comes before beit din, at the moment the court would impose the relevant oath. Shach similarly limits the rule chiefly to the severe oath on a pledge. In my view this distinction between hesset and a severe oath is not convincing, for Shach himself rules in siman 87 that even hesset can require a sacred object in doubtful law, and the two forms of oath are not conceptually different enough for this purpose. Moreover, under that distinction, one who had a written deposit and said outside beit din, "I returned it," could lose the migo of unavoidable accident merely because the later oath would require a sacred object. That is difficult. The principal explanation is Mabit's. When witnesses actually saw the object in his hand before the litigation, the factual circumstance of present observation exists independently of his own words. Then, even if he had previously said it was held as collateral, the later migo that depended on concealing the object is gone. But if the only reason we know he held it is his own admission, even if the admission was made before beit din, we do not split his words and use the disadvantageous portion while rejecting the favorable explanation. He is therefore believed through the migo that he could have denied possession or said he had already returned it. This also resolves the supposed proof from one who said outside beit din, "I borrowed and repaid." There too we do not accept half the statement. The Rosh's case is one of actual observation by others. Once his own outside statement is disregarded for litigation purposes, the fact that others saw the object remains. In that sense it is treated as "witnesses and present observation." Mabit's distinction is therefore correct, contrary to the Shach's criticism.
