Beit Yosef explains Rabbi Yonah as speaking where the depositary is present and says, “I returned it before so-and-so and so-and-so, who have died,” in which case he is not believed. I am astonished, because even then the depositary might have been able to produce witnesses who saw the return. The Tur merely selected one possible defense. It is nevertheless possible to explain Rabbi Yonah where the depositary is present, the depositor demands payment, and the depositary says that he returned it without witnesses despite having been warned not to do so. The depositary is then liable. The depositor declines to litigate with him because he is not financially secure and instead seeks recovery from the present possessor. The circumstances show that the possessor received it from the depositary, because we are witnesses that the depositary did not return it: had he done so, he would have returned it before witnesses, and he himself admits that he did not. One cannot answer that the depositary has a migo of accidental loss and is therefore believed to claim return. Rabbi Yonah may follow Rambam and Ran that this is a migo against witnesses, and he rejects the migo of accidental loss as above. The Tur's objection is then as follows. If the depositary is absent, no migo is needed, because perhaps he would have claimed explicit repayment before named witnesses or produced those witnesses. If he is present and admits return without witnesses, he should be believed through the migo of accidental loss. The Tur did not need to restate this, having just ruled it explicitly.
