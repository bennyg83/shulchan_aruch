And some say that the claimant must swear and collect. And this appears to me the principal view. The Shach in seif katan 11 asks that here the Mechaber rules without qualification that no oath is required, whereas below in siman 150 seif 6 he also cites the opposing view that we plead for heirs every claim their father could have made. He similarly asks why the Rama rules here that the oath view is primary but below gives no such ruling. The Shach calls the Sema's answer in siman 149 seif katan 39 strained and proposes instead that below the claimant resembles one who claims against a pledge and therefore must swear with a Torah type oath according to the Geonim. This answer is incorrect. If so, the oath in siman 150 should be a Torah type oath. Yet that opinion is the Rosh's, and the Rosh explicitly requires only a heset oath, not an oath while holding a sacred object. Baal HaTerumot, gate 49, part 2, already explains that the case in siman 150 is unlike one who swears and collects against a pledge. There the claimant has already consumed produce, and the heirs demand money from him while he says, Your father owed me. He is therefore not taking anything from them at all. Rather, it appears that regarding objects ordinarily lent or rented, even the Rosh may agree that we do not plead for heirs that their father purchased them. The entire reason the owner is believed regarding objects commonly lent or rented is an enactment for social order, so that people will not cease lending to one another and be forced always to use witnesses, as Tosafot in Bava Batra 45 explains. If a lender were required to take an oath whenever the possessor dies, people would still refrain from lending, fearing that an unexpected death would impose a needless oath upon them. That policy concern does not exist in the case of siman 150. As for the Rama, the Sema's distinction is proper and indeed necessary. The Raavad himself holds in our case that the claimant swears a Torah type oath, while in Baal HaTerumot's discussion of Rabbah bar Rav Huna concerning one who seized produce through a migo of purchase, the Raavad says no oath is required because the claimant has already consumed the produce. Necessarily we distinguish that here the claimant is considered one who extracts property, while below he is the possessor. This is simple.
