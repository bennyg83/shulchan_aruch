(Seif 7) And the other claims that they were stolen. Rambam does not include these words. The Mechaber added them to answer the Tur's question: even if the intermediary were present and said that the claimant sold the articles to him, he would not be believed when the claimant says that they had merely been lent, so how does it help the possessor to say that the intermediary sold them to him? Beit Yosef answers that Rambam treats the possessor's claim that the intermediary stole and sold them as a claim of theft, which is not legally effective. In my view this is not correct, as I will show in siman 134. The Mechaber therefore states here that the claimant expressly alleges theft. This would imply that if he says, “I lent them to the intermediary,” he is not believed. It appears that Rambam's first ruling applies even where the claimant expressly says that he lent them to that intermediary. The possessor may answer that had his seller been present he might have proved by witnesses that he bought them. This is an ordinary claim advanced for a purchaser. In the later clause, however, the claimant says, “I lent them directly to you and knew of no other possessor until you asserted that you bought from someone else,” and he is then believed. The Tur disagrees: since these are articles normally lent, a sale is not presumed, and in the absence of witnesses they remain in the original owner's possession. The present possessor is no better than the intermediary himself, whether the claimant says, “I lent them to you” or “I lent them to him.” Yet Rambam is difficult in light of siman 134 and the rule below that normally lent or rented articles may be recovered from heirs. Why do we not say that if their father were alive he might prove purchase? Rambam's reason appears instead to be that the present possessor did not receive them from the claimant but from the intermediary. Once the claimant sued that intermediary and no witnesses of current possession existed, the intermediary acquired a defensible position. A purchaser from him succeeds to that position, even if witnesses now see the articles in the purchaser's hands. In siman 134, by contrast, the person from whom the possessor derives title had no such defensible position because the article had been seen in the artisan's hands. Nevertheless, one who says, “You sold it in my presence,” is believed through a migo that he bought it directly. See my discussion in siman 134.
