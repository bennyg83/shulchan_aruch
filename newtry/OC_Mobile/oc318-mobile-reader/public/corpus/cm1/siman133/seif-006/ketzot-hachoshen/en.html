And I told him, 'Do not return it.' The Sema writes that even if there are witnesses to the warning, since the present possessor holds the object we say that if the original custodian were before us he might clarify his position. The Bach holds that where the owner said, 'Do not return it except in the presence of these witnesses,' and those very witnesses testify that the custodian never returned it before them, the property is removed from the purchaser, because return in accordance with the condition was impossible. The Shach disagrees, reasoning that perhaps the owner later canceled the warning before others, or perhaps the custodian gave him a written acknowledgment of return. He cites Bava Batra 45a, where even according to the view that one who deposited before witnesses must return before witnesses, if the possessor claims, 'You sold it to me,' he may be believed. This does not refute the Bach. The Bach speaks specifically where the instruction was, 'Do not return it except before these particular people,' and they testify that no such return occurred. In Bava Batra the rule requires return before witnesses generally, leaving open the possibility that return occurred before other witnesses.
More fundamentally, however, the Sema and Shach's reasons, that perhaps the custodian could prove return through witnesses or a written acknowledgment, are difficult. If that were enough, how could we ever remove property ordinarily lent or rented from heirs? We should always fear that if their father were alive he could produce witnesses or a writing proving purchase. It therefore appears that Tur and Shulchan Aruch rely on a different point. The condition, 'Do not return it,' was imposed by the depositor upon the custodian, not by the purchaser. The custodian is bound by the condition, but the purchaser did not assume it and may claim that the owner sold him the object. Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit writes similarly: even if witnesses establish that the depositor said, 'Do not return it except before witnesses,' the present possessor may answer, 'Perhaps it was returned to you before witnesses. I do not believe your claim that it was not returned. Indeed I know you are lying, because you sold it to me, and therefore it must have been returned to you.' Even if the instruction had been 'Do not return it except before so-and-so and so-and-so' and those persons testify that no return occurred, the custodian may owe the depositor for violating the condition, but the purchaser did not accept that condition and remains believed in his purchase claim.
The Tur's objection to Rabbeinu Yonah, that if the custodian himself were present he could say, 'I returned it before so-and-so and so-and-so and they went overseas,' merely strengthens the question. The deeper point is that the purchaser never became bound by the depositor's private condition. Moreover, even if the purchaser claims he bought the object while it was still with the custodian, such a sale can be valid. Thus, even if we do not believe that the custodian returned the object, the purchaser may still have a valid purchase claim.
Rabbeinu Yonah's position is difficult from Bava Batra 45a, where the possessor is believed to say, 'You sold it to me,' even according to the view that a deposit made before witnesses must be returned before witnesses. Perhaps that passage speaks where the object was not actually seen in the purchaser's possession by prior witnesses. If so, one could ask that even without the purchase claim we should advance for the purchaser the artisan's claim through a migo of unavoidable loss, as Sema writes in siman 134, seif katan 12. Yet the purchaser himself stands in place of a witness who sees the object, creating the structure of the case known as the metal ingot of Rabbi Abba. Even according to the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 93, who does not treat the purchaser as equivalent to a seeing witness, that is because the purchaser must take a hesset oath and thus is a witness who requires an oath, and hesset itself was instituted only in the days of Rav Nachman.
