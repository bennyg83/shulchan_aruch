["And the other claims that they were stolen, etc. The Sema questions the Rama: the Rama concludes by citing a dissenting view and says that because the person from whom the present holder acquired the objects is not believed, etc., yet regarding a claim that the objects were stolen all agree that the original owner is believed. My teacher explained that the Mechaber's phrase, he claims they were stolen, does not mean that the person who sold them actually stole them. Rather, the owner had lent the objects to that person, who then sold them, and the Mechaber calls that conduct theft. The Beit Yosef explains it this way, and this is the point disputed by the Raavad and Tur. The Mechaber's wording supports this, because he concludes, if this one claims, I rented them to you. If the Sema were correct, the Mechaber should have taught the greater novelty that even if the owner says, I rented them to that other person, he can recover them. My teacher's explanation is correct. The Maggid Mishneh's statement that the Rambam speaks of a theft claim must likewise mean this type of case. Otherwise the Rambam's continuation, if he says, I rented or lent them to you, would be unnecessary, since he could have ruled even where the owner says that he lent them to the seller. Moreover, the Rambam himself does not actually say here that they were stolen, and earlier he already separately discusses the claim, they were stolen from me. The Maggid Mishneh explicitly explains the Rambam's underlying principle: we do not presume people to be thieves, so if the owner admits that he did not lend or rent the objects to the present holder, he is not believed to say that they were stolen from him or that he lent them to another who then sold them. Thus when the Maggid Mishneh says that this case involves a theft claim, he means that if the borrower or renter sold what had been entrusted to him, that act would amount to theft. The Ir Shushan also explains the Mechaber this way. But the Ir Shushan records only the Mechaber's view that the object is not removed and omits the Rama's dissent. That omission is not correct. The primary law follows the Raavad and Tur, as the Rama rules. There is no concern here of presuming a person to be a thief, because the owner does not say that the other person stole the object; he says only that he lent it to him and that he improperly sold it, which is not the type of theft presumption at issue here. In fact, as a matter of law, even if the owner says that the objects were literally stolen from his house, he is believed through a migo, as Tosafos at the end of HaMekabel write: even if no public report of theft arose in the city, he can say, they were stolen from me, because he could have said, I lent or rented them to you. Hagahot Ashri likewise records this. The Bach argues that Tosafos permits this only according to the view that a migo may be used to extract property, whereas we rule that a migo does not extract. He therefore says that the Tur must hold that the owner is believed directly when he says, I lent them to that other person. This is not correct. Tosafos first state without qualification that he is believed through a migo. Only afterward they cite another explanation that views this as a migo to extract. It follows that Tosafos themselves do not regard this case as a migo to extract. Even according to the general rule that a migo does not extract, this case may be different for two reasons. First, because the original owner must reimburse the purchaser under the market protection enactment, as explained below, recovery of the object is not treated as extracting value without payment. Tosafos in Bava Batra 92b indicate this distinction, whereas the alternate view still considers it a migo to extract even though the purchaser receives his money. The Rosh expressly rules in Shevuot that when one recognizes his own utensils or books in another's hands and they are objects normally lent or rented, he may say, they were stolen from me, through a migo that he could have said, I lent or rented them to you. We do not say that this presumes someone a thief, because he does not accuse the present holder of theft, only says that the objects were stolen somewhere in the world. Yet the Rosh elsewhere holds that a migo does not extract. Mordechai rules the same way. This proves that where the owner recovers only the object while reimbursing the purchaser, the migo is not treated as extracting. A second distinction is that when the owner identifies his own objects and witnesses testify that they were his, he is treated as having a possessory basis. The first distinction appears primary. The Semag likewise follows this approach. Although Mordechai, Agudah, and Hagahot Maimoniyot understood the Semag to require a public report of theft, the Semag must be read consistently with the Sema and Bach's reading of the Rambam on this point: where the objects are of a type normally lent or rented, the owner is believed through his migo even without any public report of theft. This is also shown by the Semag's later discussion of books normally lent or rented. The result is that Tosafos, Rosh, Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Semag, and Raavad all hold that where the objects are ordinarily lent or rented, the owner may say they were stolen through a migo, even without a public report of theft and even if the owner commonly sells his property. This is the law. This applies only where he says generally, they were stolen from me. If he specifically accuses the present holder, or identifies the seller as the person who stole them from his house, and that person is not already known as a thief, he is not believed through migo because we do not presume ordinary people to be thieves. Accordingly, in objects normally lent or rented, if witnesses presently saw the object in the holder's hands, the owner may recover it where he says generally that it was stolen, or that he lent it to a particular person who then sold it, but he must reimburse the purchaser under the market protection enactment. The Beit Yosef, citing Rashba, and the Maggid Mishneh hold that market protection applies even to objects normally lent or rented. The Raavad's objection to the Rambam does not mean that the purchaser never receives reimbursement. The Raavad merely means that the object itself is removed from the present holder. If the holder received it as a gift, there is no market protection and he returns it without payment. If he purchased it, he receives reimbursement. The same applies to an heir, who returns it without payment because he gave nothing for it. This is also the Tur's and Rama's position. The practical rules are therefore as follows. For objects not normally lent or rented, if the owner merely says they were stolen, the object remains with the purchaser unless there was a public report of theft in the city and the owner is not one who normally sells his property. In that case the owner recovers the object and reimburses the purchaser, who swears how much he paid, under the market protection enactment. If the holder claims that he received it as a gift, he returns it without reimbursement. For objects normally lent or rented, if the object was not presently seen in the holder's possession in a manner that would allow him to claim, I returned it, the rule is the same as for ordinary objects. But if witnesses presently saw it in his possession, then even without any public report of theft and even where the owner normally sells his property, the owner is believed to say it was stolen from him through a migo that he could have said, I lent or rented it to you, and he recovers it. If the holder bought it, the owner reimburses him under the market protection enactment. If there was no public report of theft, the owner must swear that he did not sell the object and that it was stolen; where there was a public report, he is exempt even from that oath. If the owner says, you stole it, or the person from whom you bought it stole it from my house, he is not believed through migo unless those persons are already established thieves. Similarly, where the object is normally lent or rented and witnesses presently saw it, if the holder says, I bought it from so and so, and the owner says, I lent it to that person and he sold it, the owner recovers it, reimbursing the purchaser if it was a sale and without reimbursement if it was a gift. He likewise recovers without reimbursement from an heir if he swears that he lent it to that person's father, even though the purchaser or heir himself does not make a definite counterclaim."]
