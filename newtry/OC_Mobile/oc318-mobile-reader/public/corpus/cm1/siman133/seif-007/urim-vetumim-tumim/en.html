And the other claims that they were stolen, etc. See Shach seif katan 13, who explains in the name of his father that Rambam does not mean the claimant says that his present opponent stole the objects. Rather, the claimant says he lent them to another person and that borrower improperly sold them to the present possessor. Since we do not presume people to be thieves, Rambam treats the unauthorized sale of a borrowed item as analogous to theft. The Maggid Mishneh supports this by writing that because we do not presume people to be thieves, the claimant is not believed to say, They were stolen from me, or, I lent them to another and he sold them to you. This interpretation itself requires explanation. If selling a borrowed object is deemed theft, what difference is there between saying, This object is stolen in your hands, which is not believed, and saying, It is merely borrowed in your hands while the possessor denies it? Denial of a deposit is itself theft in the broad sense. We must distinguish that when property originally came into a person's hands permissibly through a loan or deposit, we may suspect later denial because perhaps he believes he has some old claim against the owner or another justification. But we do not readily suspect a person of an initial outright theft, which is uncommon and dangerous because discovery would bring public disgrace. If so, however, when the claimant says another person originally borrowed the object and later sold it, why should that be treated like outright theft? It began lawfully and only afterward involved misuse, precisely the sort of wrongdoing we do sometimes suspect. Another difficulty is an explicit Gemara in the sugya of a craftsman. The Gemara distinguishes the first clause from the latter by establishing the latter where the object emerges from a third person's hands and that person claims, In my presence you told the craftsman to sell it, giving him a migo that he could have said, I bought it from you. Why is this migo needed? The law of a craftsman and the law of objects ordinarily lent or rented are parallel. Since the claimant admits that the craftsman possessed the item and transferred it to this third person, under the Shach's understanding of Rambam the owner's assertion would itself amount to accusing the craftsman of theft through unauthorized sale, and should fail even without the third person's migo. This seems difficult. To sustain the Maggid Mishneh, we must say his language is not literal in the sense that the claimant loses solely because of the presumption against theft. Rather, since the original seller is not before us, and the claimant's account would make that person guilty of wrongdoing by selling entrusted property, we do not remove the present possessor from his possession and thereby brand the absent seller dishonest. We say that if the seller were present, perhaps he could prove with witnesses that he lawfully purchased the object from the claimant. The present possessor therefore remains protected. The Bach writes something similar, though more broadly. His broad version creates a difficulty from heirs: if we always assume that an absent possessor might prove lawful purchase, then even heirs should be allowed that plea, whereas the Gemara states that objects ordinarily lent or rented may be recovered from heirs. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. The Maggid Mishneh's additional factor is that accepting the claimant's story would affirmatively brand the absent intermediary as one who misused entrusted property. We do not lightly strip a person of his presumption of uprightness when he is not present to defend himself, and therefore we say perhaps he could prove purchase. With heirs, no such wrongdoing is attributed to their father merely from the fact that the object is now with them. He could have returned the loan properly while alive, and there is no special presumption of righteousness strengthening their position. Thus we do not invent the uncommon plea that he purchased it. The Gemara concerning the craftsman is likewise consistent because there the craftsman is present and cannot prove a purchase. Once the person who allegedly made the unauthorized sale is before us and cannot establish lawful ownership, his general presumption of uprightness does not by itself defeat the owner's claim. The third possessor needs the migo based on the claim that the owner himself instructed the sale. Rambam appears to have learned his rule from the Mishnah concerning one who recognizes his books or utensils in another's possession, which requires that a report of theft have arisen in the city. Tosafot ask that books are ordinarily lent, so why is a report of theft required? Rambam answers that although the owner might otherwise have a migo that he lent the books, a migo used to extract property is weak, and here there is an additional reason not to believe the story because it would require attributing serious wrongdoing to an absent intermediary. If the claimant says directly, I lent them to the present possessor, however, the special presumption against the absent seller is irrelevant. The Shach argues that the plea, I lent them, is not really a migo to extract because objects ordinarily lent remain presumptively in the original owner's possession. Yet even if that is generally correct, the combination of factors here can still explain Rambam. One might still ask that the claimant has a different migo: I lent the objects to another, who sold them to you. That claim too avoids confronting the present possessor directly. Necessarily, Rambam holds that such a claim is not accepted at all for the reasons just given, so it supplies no migo. Were I not hesitant to depart from the Maggid Mishneh's exact reading, I would suggest an additional limitation in Rambam. Suppose the object is of a type ordinarily lent to the present possessor, but not of a type that would ordinarily be lent to the particular person from whom the possessor says he bought it. For example, books of aggadah in the hands of a Torah scholar might ordinarily be lent, while the alleged seller is entirely unlearned and has no use for such books. If the possessor says, I bought them from that person, and the claimant admits the books reached that person's hands but says they were only lent to him, the claimant cannot extract them from the present possessor. Had the books still been in the seller's hands, the owner himself would not have been believed to say he lent them to that seller, because relative to that person these are not objects ordinarily lent. The purchaser who derives title from him therefore stands in his shoes. Rambam teaches that although the owner has a migo that he could have said directly to the present possessor, I lent them to you, a claim that would ordinarily be strong because relative to this possessor the objects are commonly lent, he cannot use that migo to assert that the prior seller had only borrowed them. Such a migo is brazen and is also a migo to extract because the object had already left the claimant's presumptive ownership while in the first possessor's hands. The purchaser who derives from that first possessor is therefore treated as the current possessor. On this reading Rambam's ruling is clear and practically sound. It also explains why two seemingly repetitive laws in Rambam, Hilchot To'en chapter 8, are actually distinct. One concerns a direct claim of theft, while the other concerns a purchaser deriving title from a prior possessor to whom the object was not ordinarily lent. Examine this carefully.
