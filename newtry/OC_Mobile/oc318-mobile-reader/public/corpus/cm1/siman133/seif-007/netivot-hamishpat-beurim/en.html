See Sema, seif katan 16, who questions Rama for citing an opinion that disagrees, since where the claimant says the objects were stolen, all agree that he is believed, as stated in seif 6. Shach, seif katan 13, cites his father, who answers with difficulty that the case is where the claimant says that he lent the item to a certain person and that this borrower then sold it. According to Rambam, that is treated like a claim of theft. Taz in siman 134, seif 4, objects that if so, where an artisan's work leaves his possession and another person says that the artisan sold it to him, why is the owner not believed? The owner says that he gave it to the artisan for repair and the artisan sold it, which should likewise be equivalent to a theft claim. This is a strong objection. Tumim here overlooked Taz's words. Shach himself also questions the basic ruling in seif 6 that the claimant is not believed when he says the items were stolen. Tosafot and Rosh write only that where he says, 'You stole them,' a migo is ineffective because a migo cannot be used to brand another person a thief. If he merely says, 'They were stolen,' he should be believed, since there are many thieves in the world. In my view, Sema and Shach can be reconciled by reading Rama as distinguishing between two cases. If the possessor says, 'You sold it to me,' and the claimant says, 'It was stolen from me,' the claimant is not believed. But if the possessor says, 'So and so sold it to me,' the claimant is believed even on a theft claim. The distinction is based on Bava Metzia 5b, where Rashi explains that one who denies a deposit is disqualified as a witness because there is no plausible temporary excuse. If it was lost, he could have said that it was lost and been exempt. Shitah Mekubetzet explains that since he could have avoided brazenness by claiming loss, his outright denial is incriminating. Therefore, when the possessor says, 'You sold it to me,' and the claimant says it was stolen, believing the claimant through a migo that he could have said it was lent would require us to say that the possessor himself stole it. If some other thief had stolen and sold it through intermediaries, the possessor should have said that he purchased it from another rather than brazenly denying the claimant's ownership. The same reasoning underlies the disqualification of one who denies a deposit. We cannot remove the object merely because perhaps the owner lent it, because the owner himself says it was stolen. Thus, if we remove it from the possessor based on the owner's migo, we necessarily brand the possessor himself a thief. A migo cannot remove another person from the chazakah of honesty and establish him as a thief, for that is a migo against a chazakah. By contrast, in seif 7, where the possessor says that a third person sold it to him and that seller is not before us, the owner can be believed that it was stolen without declaring the seller a thief. The seller may have bought it from another person who in turn acquired it from the thief. The migo can therefore operate. Many poskim, however, hold that the claimant is not believed for another reason, namely that this is a migo used to extract property. Shach concludes that it is not a migo to extract because the claimant reimburses the possessor for the price. One should not object from Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Metzia, who hold that a person cannot claim half of an object through a migo that he could have claimed all of it because that is a migo to extract, even though the case involves one who admits part and pays money. Ketzot HaChoshen asks this in siman 82. The distinction is that there the claimant's demand concerns the object itself without obligation to pay for it, while the other party can seek his money from the seller. If that money is then lost from the seller, the claimant bears no liability. It is therefore truly a migo to extract. Here, however, the claimant cannot take the object without reimbursing its price, and Shach can therefore say that it is not a migo to extract. Even so, the rule remains difficult. If the original transfer was a gift, or if the object is worth much more than the price the possessor paid, the migo would again be one to extract, yet no posek states such distinctions. Therefore, the possessor can say kim li in accordance with the opinions that this is a migo to extract. Sema in siman 134, seif katan 2, likewise rules in such a case that it is a migo to extract. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that we allow the migo here because the purchaser has only a doubtful claim while the original owner has a definite claim, and with a definite claim against a doubtful one a migo to extract may be used, as in the claim 'I was violated after betrothal.' These are Tumim's words in his rules of migo. I explained in my novellae that this is incorrect. The claim of heirs and purchasers is itself treated as a definite claim because beit din advances claims on their behalf. This differs from the case of 'I was violated after betrothal,' where we do not advance an alternative claim for the husband. The Rosh at the beginning of the chapter HaIshah Shenitarmelah explicitly treats the claim advanced for heirs as a definite claim. If the owner asserts a deposit claim concerning objects ordinarily lent out, he is believed according to all opinions, as Rambam states in chapter 8 of Hilchot To'en, cited by Shach in siman 357, seif katan 1. See Shach, seif katan 13, who writes that the distinction whether the owner had a public reputation for theft concerns objects normally sold. His meaning follows the Semag cited earlier. Semag writes 'even regarding objects normally sold,' implying that the same basic law applies whether the object is ordinarily sold or not. Regarding an object not ordinarily sold, the owner is certainly believed on a deposit claim for objects ordinarily lent out even where there was no public report of theft, as Shach writes in siman 357.
