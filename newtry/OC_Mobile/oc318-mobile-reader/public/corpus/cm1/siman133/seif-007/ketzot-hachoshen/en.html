Shach concludes that even if the possessor says the items were stolen, he can be believed through a migo that he could have said they were lent or had been returned. Bach had objected that we do not use a migo to extract. Shach answers that this is not truly extraction because the claimant receives only the value while the possessor retains the item. Accordingly, if the possessor claims that the item was given to him as a gift and therefore the claimant receives no value in exchange, the migo would indeed be a migo to extract and should fail. The result also depends on the theory of "I do not know." Tosafot holds that where the party could not have known, we do not apply the rule that one obligated to swear but unable to swear must pay. The heirs' exemption is based on exactly that lack of knowledge. Under that view, an heir's or purchaser's uncertain plea is no stronger than ordinary uncertainty, and against such uncertainty we can use a migo to extract when the claimant is certain. The classic example is the ketubah case, "I was raped after betrothal." Rambam, by contrast, applies the rule of inability to swear even where the defendant could not have known. He exempts heirs because their plea is stronger: beit din advances for them the definite plea their father could have made. A purchaser's plea is likewise treated as a definite plea because we advance the seller's defenses for him. Against such a plea, a migo to extract does not work. This is why Rambam does not believe one who says, "These are stolen," against a purchaser through the same migo, while Tosafot, Rosh, Semag, and Mordechai do.
