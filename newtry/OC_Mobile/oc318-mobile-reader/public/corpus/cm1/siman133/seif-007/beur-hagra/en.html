One who had in his possession, etc.; and if this one claimed. Bava Batra 45b shows that if the owner says, 'I gave it to the artisan for repair,' while the possessor says he bought it from someone else, the possessor is believed. An artisan therefore has the law of objects ordinarily lent or rented. See citation 81.
And if they were objects, etc. Rabbah's distinction there applies specifically where the possessor says he bought them from an artisan. If he bought them from someone who is not an artisan, or where the objects are not ordinarily lent or rented, the ordinary rule applies. Maggid Mishneh explains the phrase 'but so-and-so sold them' where the former owner claims they were stolen. Shach, citing his father, explains that the claim is that the seller sold them by theft. Rambam nevertheless holds that we do not readily presume people to be thieves, while the usual presumption concerning such objects has been weakened. Rama records a dissenting view based on the Gemara's objection, and requires the stronger claim that the owner made the admission in the possessor's presence. Even according to Rama, such a claim is believed through a migo, since he could have said, 'I bought it from you,' where the owner does not claim he lent it. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in siman 357:1 remain difficult. Even if their ruling here is read as a claim of actual theft, Sema's explanation does not fully resolve the contradiction. Rama cannot mean that every theft claim equals a loan claim, because he would also have disputed the earlier seif, and siman 357 rules otherwise. Shach's interpretation is therefore necessary, though Rambam and Shulchan Aruch still require study. The phrase 'although he admitted' highlights the latter clause, where the possessor is believed even without witnesses that the object belonged to the claimant. Where the object is not ordinarily lent or rented, possession itself supports the possessor, even if the claimant brings witnesses of prior ownership.
