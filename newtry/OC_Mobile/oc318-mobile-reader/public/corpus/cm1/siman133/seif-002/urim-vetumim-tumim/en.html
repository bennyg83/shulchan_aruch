And if he said to him, Do not return it, etc. He is believed through a migo that he could have claimed the item was lost through accident. The Shach in seif katan 2 questions this, although the rule is stated by the Rosh and Tur, because according to the Nimukei Yosef there is no migo. Once the depositor told him not to return the item except before witnesses, there is an evidentiary presumption that he did not repay without witnesses, and a migo cannot overcome what is treated like witnesses. The Shach supports this from Tosafot in Chezkas HaBatim. In truth, it is difficult to say that the mere instruction, Do not return except before witnesses, gives the depositor greater power than an actual written deposit document. A document is certainly stronger than a verbal instruction, for it shows that the depositor did not trust the bailee and was unwilling to rely even upon witnesses unless he took the trouble and expense to obtain a formal document. Yet nowhere do we find that the verbal instruction is stronger than the document. Since with a deposit evidenced by a document we rule that the bailee is believed through a migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable loss, how could a bare instruction to return only before witnesses destroy that migo? It therefore appears that when Tosafot entertained the view that Rabbah considered a deposit before witnesses equivalent to saying, Do not return except before witnesses, and thus rejected the migo, they assumed that Rabbah did not accept Rav Chisda's rule that one who deposited with a document may say he returned it through a migo of unavoidable loss. Ramban writes explicitly that Rabbah does not accept Rav Chisda. Tosafot's question was only that even those who disagree with Rabbah there appear to disagree specifically where there is a document, because the claimant can say, What is your document doing in my hand? With mere witnesses they concede that repayment need not occur before witnesses. Tosafot answer that the dissenters hold that a deposit made before witnesses need not be returned before witnesses and therefore does not resemble an express instruction to return only before witnesses. Rabbah, who holds that return must be before witnesses, treats it like such an instruction and denies the migo. All of this follows those who disagree with Rav Chisda and hold that a migo cannot overcome the strong presumption created by an outstanding document. According to our ruling like Rav Chisda, however, even an actual document can be overcome by the migo of unavoidable loss, despite the powerful presumption, What is your document doing in my hand? All the more so the same migo overcomes the weaker verbal instruction to return only before witnesses. This is clear. As for the Nimukei Yosef's reason, the migo there to say that he paid before named witnesses is not a strong migo in any event, as the Rosh and others write, so a relatively weak presumption can defeat it. By contrast, the migo of unavoidable loss is strong and is not defeated by such a presumption. Moreover, the Nimukei Yosef's presumption applies naturally to a loan, where the debtor sees that the creditor does not trust him and fears denial, and therefore specifically instructs him to repay only before witnesses. The debtor then would not ordinarily repay privately because he knows the creditor is distrustful. With a deposit, however, one cannot say that the depositor truly distrusts the bailee, for why would he entrust property to him when the bailee could always claim unavoidable loss with no one able to contradict him? Thus an instruction to return only before witnesses can reasonably be understood as prudent advice rather than evidence that the depositor utterly distrusts him. The bailee may therefore still return privately. The words of the Rosh and Tur are simple, correct, and free of difficulty.
