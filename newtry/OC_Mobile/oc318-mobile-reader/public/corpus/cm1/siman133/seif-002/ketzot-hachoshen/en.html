If the owner told the custodian, "Do not return it except before witnesses," the Shach argues that the custodian should not be believed even through a migo of unavoidable accident, because the owner's warning creates an evidentiary presumption that the property was not returned privately. He compares it to the rule in siman 70 and to Rabbeinu Tam's explanation that one who initially deposits before witnesses is treated as though he required return before witnesses. In my view, even under the Shach and Taz, a custodian who says, "I purchased the object," can still be believed through a migo of unavoidable accident. He need not first say "I returned it" through a migo and then derive purchase from that. The purchase plea itself is supported directly by the migo that the item was lost through unavoidable accident. This purchase plea does not contradict the owner's condition. The condition was only, "Do not return it except before witnesses." A purchase is a different transaction. Just as a credibility provision concerning repayment does not automatically extend to a later waiver, so a condition about return does not automatically say, "Do not purchase it except before witnesses." The Tosafot passage that seems to deny even a purchase plea under Rabbeinu Tam's view is different. There, the very reason return before witnesses is required is that the owner demonstrated distrust by depositing before witnesses. If the owner's concern was that the custodian might falsely claim the object became his, a later purchase plea is within the very suspicion. The custodian should have purchased it before witnesses. Our practical law does not infer such a broad suspicion merely from the original deposit. We require witnesses only because of the explicit condition regarding return. The wording of that condition does not include purchase. Therefore a direct purchase plea supported by the migo of unavoidable accident remains credible.
Even a custodian who says, "I returned it," should be believed through a migo of unavoidable accident according to Maggid Mishneh's explanation of the rule requiring repayment before witnesses. He writes that one cannot use the migo, "I repaid before witnesses," because the defendant himself admits he did not fulfill the condition. So long as payment has not been established, the defendant remains personally obligated by his own admitted condition. That logic applies specifically to a loan or payment obligation. Until payment is established, the debtor's obligation and the agreed condition remain outstanding. A deposit is different. Once the actual deposited object has returned to the owner's hands, even privately, there is no remaining property or debt to which the return condition can attach. The custodian is no worse than one whose deposited object was lost through unavoidable accident. The condition did not create an independent monetary debt after the object itself ceased to be under custodial responsibility. Accordingly, under Maggid Mishneh's rationale, a custodian can say he returned the object privately and be believed through the migo that it was lost through unavoidable accident. Rabbi Tzvi Ashkenazi in his gloss to Taz accepted Maggid Mishneh's explanation as primary. The distinction between repayment of a loan and return of an intact deposit is therefore significant.
