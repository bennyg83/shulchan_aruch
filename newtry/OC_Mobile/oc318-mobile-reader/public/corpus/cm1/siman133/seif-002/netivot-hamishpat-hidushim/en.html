That it is presently in his possession. Meaning, it was seen in his possession close to the time of the beit din claim, in circumstances where he cannot say that he returned it afterward. But if it was seen in his possession in beit din itself, he is believed through the migo that he could have refrained from showing it and claimed, "It never happened," or, "I returned it." If it was seen in his possession shortly before the claim, then even if he said at that time, "I purchased it," or, "It is collateral," he is not believed where the challenger has witnesses that it belongs to him. The reason is that statements made outside beit din ordinarily have no legal force, and the object was seen in his possession near the time of the claim. If he claimed purchase before witnesses even a long time before the beit din claim, the Sema holds that he is no longer believed in beit din to claim purchase through the migo of return, because once he claimed purchase he no longer stood ready to return the item. The Shach disagrees. If he claimed purchase before witnesses at a time when he had a migo before those witnesses, he is later believed in beit din to maintain the purchase claim, unless the witnesses had already seen the item in his possession before he made that claim, so that he lacked a migo before them. But if he claimed that it was collateral before witnesses, then even if he had a migo before them, he is not believed, because a collateral claim cannot succeed without an oath and the migo before witnesses does not substitute for the judicial oath. The primary law follows the Shach.
Through a migo that it was lost through unavoidable accident. The Shach disagrees. Since the owner warned him, we are witnesses that he did not repay him, and this is a migo against witnesses. See Beurim: the primary law follows the Shach. The owner's intention in warning him was certainly to destroy the migo. Otherwise the warning would serve no purpose, because he would always retain some migo.
And he swears a Torah oath. The reason is that had he claimed the item was lost through unavoidable accident, he too would have been obligated to take a Torah oath. Sema. This is not literally a Torah oath, but rather a rabbinic oath taken while holding a sacred object, patterned after a Torah oath. Shach.
