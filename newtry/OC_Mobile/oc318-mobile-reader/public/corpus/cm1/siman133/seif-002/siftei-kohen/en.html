He is likewise believed to say, 'I purchased it,' through a migo that he returned it, etc. In siman 72, seif 18, the Rama rules that if he stated before witnesses, even long before litigation, that he had purchased it, he may no longer later claim before beit din that he purchased it through a migo that he returned it. According to what I wrote there, that he may still subsequently claim that he purchased it, the case here must be one in which he was not believed when he made the statement before the witnesses, such as where the witnesses saw the object in his possession before he told them that he had purchased it.
He is believed through a migo that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances, etc. So write the Tur and Rosh in the chapter Chazakat HaBatim. According to the second reason stated by the Sema above, siman 70, seif katan 13, based on the Beit Yosef, namely that an established presumption supported by our certainty that he did not repay after being warned defeats the migo, here too he should not be believed through a migo that the item was lost through unavoidable circumstances. Tosafot in Bava Batra 45b also imply this. They cite Rabbenu Chananel that according to the view requiring one who received a deposit before witnesses to return it before witnesses, the deposit is treated as though the owner had expressly said, 'Return it only before witnesses.' Therefore the custodian is not believed through a migo, since he brought the loss upon himself. Although Tosafot later reject that explanation and say that even according to the view requiring return before witnesses, a migo is effective, that concerns an ordinary deposit. Where the owner expressly said, 'Return it only before witnesses,' it appears that even according to Tosafot's conclusion he is not believed through a migo. The Mechaber's ruling therefore requires study. I later found my view stated in the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri: Avi HaEzri writes that this applies where the owner warned him before witnesses and said, 'Return it only before so-and-so and so-and-so.' Otherwise the custodian may plead through a migo that he returned it. Avi Asaf rules likewise.
A Torah oath, etc. This is not literally a Torah oath, but a rabbinic oath administered while holding a sacred object, patterned after a Torah oath, as I write below in siman 296, seif katan 3. When the Rosh writes, as cited by the Bach, that he is not believed by a rabbinic oath, he means a heset oath, as explained there immediately beforehand.
