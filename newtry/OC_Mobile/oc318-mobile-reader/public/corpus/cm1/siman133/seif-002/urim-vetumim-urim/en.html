That it is now in his possession, etc. Meaning, witnesses saw it in his possession close to the time of the claim in beit din, so that he cannot say that he returned it afterward. If they saw it in his possession in beit din itself, that alone does not harm him, because his proof and answer can be presented together there. But if witnesses saw it in his possession before the litigation, then even if he told those witnesses that it was purchased property or a pledge in his hands, he is not believed where the claimant has witnesses that it belongs to him. The reason is that statements made outside beit din have no legal force, and the object was seen in his possession immediately before the claim. See Sema.
It is purchased property, through a migo. The Sema refers to siman 64 and to the Rama in siman 72, seif 18, who rules that if one told witnesses, even long before coming to beit din, 'I purchased it from him,' he can no longer come to beit din and claim ownership through a migo that he could have said, 'I returned it,' because once he claims purchase he is not expected to return it. The Shach responds that according to what he wrote there, that one can later claim purchase, the present case must be where at the time he told the witnesses, 'I purchased it,' he was already not believed, such as where witnesses had first seen the object in his possession and only afterward he made that statement. See the cited simanim and what I wrote there at length.
He is believed through a migo that it was lost by unavoidable accident. The Shach, seif katan 2, questions the Mechaber, although this ruling is written by the Tur and Rosh in chapter Chezkas HaBatim. Based on the Sema above in siman 70 in the name of Nimukei Yosef, that where the owner said, 'Do not return it except before witnesses,' we regard it as certain that it was not returned without witnesses, the Shach argues that this presumption should likewise defeat a migo of unavoidable accident. He supports himself from Tosafot and leaves the Mechaber requiring study. The Taz writes similarly. In Tumim I concluded that the Shach's objection has no place. How can the instruction, 'Do not return it except before witnesses,' be stronger than an actual written instrument attested by witnesses? Yet with such a document we still allow a migo of unavoidable accident. To say that no migo exists merely because of the instruction is unreasonable. I further explained that Nimukei Yosef's presumption applies there because the Rosh and Tur already regard the migo of claiming payment in the creditor's presence as a weak migo. A slight presumption therefore suffices to reject it. A migo of unavoidable accident is different. Moreover, the presumption that he did not repay without witnesses applies primarily to a borrower, who sees that the lender distrusts him and therefore would have no reason to repay privately while relying on the lender's honesty. With a deposit, however, the depositor still trusted the custodian to the extent that the custodian could claim unavoidable accident. Even if some authorities apply the presumption to deposits as well, that is because the particular migo there was otherwise weak. Where there is a strong migo, the Mechaber's words are correct and obvious, and one should not depart from them.
A Torah oath. The Shach writes that this is not literally a Torah oath, but a rabbinic oath taken while holding a sacred object, modeled on a Torah oath, as explained below in siman 296. When the Rosh, cited by the Bach, says that he is not believed with a rabbinic oath, he means hesset, as explained above.
