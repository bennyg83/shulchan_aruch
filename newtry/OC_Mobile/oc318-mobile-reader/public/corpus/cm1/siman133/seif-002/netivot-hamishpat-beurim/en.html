He is believed to say that he purchased it. See Shach, seif katan 1, and what I wrote in siman 72, seif katan 38, resolving the apparent contradiction from Bava Batra 45, where one sees his servant in the hands of a craftsman and it appears that even if the craftsman claimed before witnesses that he had purchased him, he is no longer believed. I also explained there why, according to Shach, if he claimed before witnesses that he had purchased the item and later claimed before beit din that it was collateral, he is not believed through a migo that he could have maintained before beit din as well that he purchased it. See there for the resolution of both difficulties.
Do not return it except before witnesses. See Shach, seif katan 2, who proves from Tosafot in Bava Batra 45 that he is not believed through a migo that he could have claimed that the deposit was lost through ones. Tumim questions this: even where there is a written instrument he is believed through such a migo, all the more so where the depositor merely warned him not to return without witnesses. Tosafot there spoke only according to the opinion that a written instrument defeats the migo, whereas according to our ruling that he is believed even against a document, certainly a verbal warning should not be stronger. In my view there is no difficulty. Tumim himself writes in siman 71, seif katan 3, that where the owner granted another special credibility concerning a deposit, the holder is not believed even through a migo of ones. Otherwise the special credibility would accomplish nothing, because he would always possess that migo. This resolves the present case. A written instrument may have been taken because the owner feared that the witnesses might die or travel overseas and the holder would then deny the transaction entirely. It also has practical effect concerning a claim of purchase where there are witnesses who saw the item but no witnesses to the original deposit. By contrast, when the owner expressly warns, 'Do not return it except before witnesses,' his intent is necessarily to defeat the migo of ones. Otherwise the condition accomplishes nothing. Before witnesses to the deposit, the holder could still say that he returned it without witnesses through a migo of ones whenever there are no witnesses who presently saw the item. Where there are witnesses who saw the item, even without the condition the original deposit witnesses alone prevent a claim of return. Therefore, the condition must have been intended to eliminate the migo of ones. See what I wrote above in the commentary to siman 71, seif katan 4. Those who disagree here and allow the migo can be explained only with difficulty. They may hold that the condition was not designed to make a claim of return legally unavailable. After all, the holder could still say that he returned it before witnesses who later died or traveled away. Rather, the owner relied on the condition to ensure that the holder would in fact not return it without witnesses and would be reluctant to make such a claim because it would appear false to the public. This is strained, and Shach's ruling is primary. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that even according to Shach, the holder may claim purchase through a migo of ones, just as a special grant of credibility is effective concerning payment but not concerning waiver, and a claim of purchase resembles waiver under siman 71. This is certainly incorrect. There is no meaningful distinction between saying that he returned the object itself and saying that he returned its value. Ketzot's further distinction, that where a deposit was made before witnesses the requirement to return before witnesses is based on suspicion of the custodian and therefore even a purchase claim fails, whereas an express warning is merely contractual, is also incorrect. If the owner stipulated such a condition, he did so precisely because he suspected him. Ketzot also distinguishes according to Maggid Mishneh, who explains that a debtor cannot claim payment through a migo because he obligated himself to fulfill the condition, whereas a custodian who in fact returned a deposit privately would be discharged and the owner's denial would be like an unavoidable loss for which a custodian is exempt. This too is incorrect. A debtor who actually pays privately is likewise discharged as a matter of fact, yet he remains legally liable because he undertook the condition. The custodian likewise obligated himself not to return without witnesses, so if he did so he assumed liability for the resulting inability to prove the return.
