Sema says the Tur and Rosh require both witnesses and present observation, and he understands the source case as one where witnesses had originally seen the object delivered as a deposit. Shach objects that once witnesses presently see the object and the possessor says it is held as collateral, his migo has already disappeared even if no witnesses saw the original deposit. Shach adds that in the Rosh's responsum there was only one witness to the original delivery. According to what I wrote above, however, Sema's requirement is understandable. Mere present observation is not enough where the type of object is one that a possessor could ordinarily claim to have purchased. Unless there are also witnesses establishing that it came to him as a deposit, he retains an independent ownership plea. Thus the combination of original deposit evidence and present observation removes the relevant defenses.
As explained above, where the possessor merely made an admission outside beit din, we cannot accept only the disadvantageous portion of what he said and reject the favorable explanation. We do not split the statement. Only where actual witnesses saw the object can the later litigation be treated as one of present observation while disregarding what he claimed outside beit din. It follows that witnesses who merely see the object now, without witnesses to the original deposit, do not necessarily help. With property not ordinarily lent or rented, the possessor can still say it was purchased. Even if he once admitted it was not purchased but was held as collateral, that outside admission cannot be split so that the court accepts the damaging concession and rejects the beneficial collateral claim. Therefore, for such property, both evidence of the deposit and present observation are needed. For property ordinarily lent or rented, it is enough that witnesses establish the claimant's prior ownership and now see the object, because the ordinary purchase plea is itself weak. The Shach's point that the Rosh's case involved one witness is not decisive, because one witness can function like two for the relevant purpose under the law analogous to Rabbi Abba's piece of metal. The structure is therefore coherent.
