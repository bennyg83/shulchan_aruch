Tumim explains the ruling of Rabbeinu Yonah cited by the Tur, who holds that the challenger is believed, by establishing the case where the possessor claims that the challenger sold the item to him and instructed the custodian to give it to him, and he then received it from the custodian. The custodian also knows that the challenger sold it to the possessor. Since the custodian remains liable to compensate the depositor for the object, Tumim says that the possessor must return it because the custodian can say, "What dealings do I have with you?" as in siman 58. The possessor would then owe the depositor under Rabbi Natan's lien. This is incorrect. I have already written in siman 58 that the claim, "What dealings do I have with you?" applies only when a person seizes property on account of a debt. Where he holds it because he claims absolute ownership, the custodian cannot make that argument. Ketzot HaChoshen asks against the Mechaber: if so, in the case of articles that are normally lent or rented, which may be removed from heirs, why do we not plead for the heirs that perhaps their father, had he been alive, would have clarified his title through witnesses? There is no difficulty. Here the possessor initially has the presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him. We seek to undermine that presumption only because the object had previously been deposited with another person and the challenger warned that custodian not to return it. We therefore plead that perhaps the possessor could have produced evidence in order to preserve his existing presumption. With articles that are normally lent or rented, however, the heirs have no such presumption, and by Torah law the property remains in the status of its original owner. There is therefore no basis to invent such a claim in order to remove property from the original owner's chazakah.
