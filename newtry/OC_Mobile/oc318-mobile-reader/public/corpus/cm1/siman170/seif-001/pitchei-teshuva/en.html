All repair together with the upper owner. See Noda BiYehudah, Second Edition, siman 24. The rule applies where all owners presently need the water, so the lower owner must assist the upper owner because he too requires the water to pass through the upper area. If, however, the upper owner already dug the channel for his own field at a time when the lower owner did not cultivate his field, and only later the lower owner decides to extend the channel to his field, he need not reimburse the upper owner for the earlier digging. His benefit caused the upper owner no additional expense. If the upper owner says, however, that unless the lower owner contributes he will undo his work and block the channel so that neither field receives water, Noda BiYehudah inclines to permit the upper owner to do so. This is not necessarily coercion by the rule against middat Sedom, because the upper owner knows the lower owner now needs the channel and would otherwise have to compensate him. According to the Rosh, whose view Rama cites in siman 174:1, coercion based on middat Sedom does not apply where the owner could obtain money for the benefit. Even according to the Rambam, the rule of coercion applies most readily to undivided joint property where each side already has an equal right and neither has incurred expense. Here the upper owner spent his own money to create the channel adjacent to his field, and the lower owner now seeks to benefit from his fellow's actual work without payment. This resembles one who seeks to compel another to allow free residence in his courtyard. The familiar exemption where the courtyard was not intended for rent concerns payment after one has already lived there; it does not necessarily create an initial right to compel free use. Although the Mordechai cited by Rama in siman 363:6 may imply a broader rule where the owner could not otherwise earn money, Noda BiYehudah holds that at least according to the Rosh such coercion does not apply.
