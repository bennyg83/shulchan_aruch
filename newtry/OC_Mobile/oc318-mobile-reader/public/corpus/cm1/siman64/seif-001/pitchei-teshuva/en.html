Even though he has no migo. See Shach, who writes that the Sema already challenged the Rama on this point, for certainly no authority holds that where he has no migo and the debt is not established he is nevertheless believed, and he concludes that this appears so even nowadays. See Responsa Panim Meirot, part II, siman 181, who writes that the Sema and Shach challenged the Rama because they understood him to be speaking even where there are witnesses to the deposit. In truth, however, the Rama never intended to say that where witnesses testify that the property was deposited with him he is believed to say, ‘I seized it as collateral,’ when the debt is not established. The Rama’s intention is explained explicitly in Responsa Panim Meirot, siman 86, namely that there are no witnesses that the items were deposited with him. The Rama comments on the Mechaber, who states that the case is where there are witnesses that the items were deposited with him and witnesses who now saw them in his possession, for otherwise he would be believed through a migo that they were lost through unavoidable accident or that the deposit never occurred. This implies that witnesses to the deposit are specifically required, and witnesses who merely saw the items are insufficient. Without witnesses to the deposit, even though he lacks a migo of return or denial because there are witnesses who saw the items, and even though he also lacks a claim of purchase because acquisition would require a written instrument and delivery, the Rama rules that if he seized them during the owner’s lifetime he may say, ‘I seized them as collateral.’ This is the view of Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur. The Rashba alone holds that since he cannot claim purchase, he likewise cannot claim that he seized them as collateral, and the Rama disagrees. On this basis the Panim Meirot ruled in a case involving Reuven and Shimon, partners in a shop, who were compelled to borrow from others and asked Yehudah to act as guarantor. They delivered the keys of the shop to him so that all merchandise and movables in the shop would stand as collateral in Yehudah’s hands, and they were not permitted to remove anything without his knowledge. Later disputes arose between them and the guarantor, and the beit din sent officers to inventory everything in the shop. They found merchandise and also debt instruments owed to the partners, some being bearer notes and some naming the partners. The guarantor sought to retain even the notes naming the partners, although he had received no written assignment and delivery. He ruled that if there are witnesses that these notes were deposited with the guarantor, he is not believed to say, ‘I seized them as collateral,’ where the debt is not established. But if there are no witnesses to the deposit, he may say that he holds them as collateral even though he cannot claim that they were purchased by him without written assignment and delivery. He concludes that this is clear and beyond doubt: where there are no witnesses to the deposit, even though the debt is not established, he may claim that he seized them as collateral, in accordance with the Rama’s ruling and unlike the Rashba. See there, and see Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat on this matter.
