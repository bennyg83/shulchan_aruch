For if his purpose were to collect them. See Shach, seif katan 3. His words imply that with our bearer notes, which are written payable to whoever produces them, since the possessor can claim a purchase without a written assignment, he is believed to say, "I took them during the creditor's lifetime," by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed purchase or gift, even for the purpose of collecting the debt. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that although such a note does not require a written assignment, it still requires actual delivery from hand to hand, whereas here there was only unilateral seizure. I am surprised by the objection. As against the debtor named in a bearer note, no transfer act is required. The debtor cannot say, "You are not my proper litigant," because he obligated himself to whoever produces the instrument, even one who did not receive it through the original creditor, as explained in simanim 50 and 60. Delivery is needed only to acquire the creditor's rights from the creditor. Where the creditor himself owes money to the possessor, no formal acquisition is required as against the creditor. The possessor may seize from him whatever rights the creditor has in the note, and nothing further is required vis-a-vis the underlying debtor. See Tosafot to Bava Batra 172, s.v. De'i, which indicates that where a document says "from you," meaning payable to whoever produces it, even a claim that the document was lost by another is no claim at all. This is straightforward. This differs from what I wrote above in siman 104, that seizure of a bearer note by a later creditor does not help against an earlier creditor. There, seizure can give him only the rights comprised in the lien. In the bearer note itself, however, the possessor acquires even a direct personal right against the debtor, because the debtor expressly obligated himself to whoever produces the note. That is why such acquisition helps against heirs when the possessor acquired it during the creditor's lifetime. Against an earlier creditor, by contrast, what is required is true acquisition of the underlying money. Acquisition of a lien alone adds nothing, since the later creditor already enjoys Rabbi Natan's lien where his debtor is alive, has no assets, and is owed money by the third party. Moreover, seizure of the note cannot displace the earlier creditor's rights, because all creditors had already acquired their respective rights in the debtor's claim under Rabbi Natan's rule before the later creditor seized the note. Only if the bearer note or ordinary note is transferred by a complete proprietary acquisition does it help even against an earlier creditor, because that is analogous to selling movable property subject to a lien, and the Sages abolished recovery of movable property from purchasers even where the underlying lien is biblical. Without such a true acquisition, however, Rabbi Natan's biblical lien, shared by all creditors according to their rights, remains unaffected.
Without a written assignment. See Shach, seif katan 1, who writes that even if the creditor gave the note as collateral in the manner described in the siman, it does not thereby enable the pledgee to collect directly on it. Certainly, while the original debtor is alive, a pledged promissory note is like any other pledged movable property. Without beit din, even ordinary movable collateral cannot simply be appropriated in payment; through beit din, however, even notes can be collected and appraised for the creditor, as explained in siman 101. It appears that to this extent the pledgee does acquire a special right in the note. If the creditor who pledged it wishes to force him to collect from other movable property instead, the pledgee may insist that beit din appraise and assign this particular note to him. Any right the original creditor has to sell the note to another can likewise be subjected to a pledge. By physically taking the note as collateral, the pledgee acquires the right to have beit din collect for him from this note in a manner that will vest in him the rights that the original creditor could have sold. A pledge has the status of a designated source of payment, as explained in siman 117. When the Shach says that the pledge does not help "to collect," he means that merely receiving the note as collateral does not convert it into the pledgee's movable property as against the original creditor's heirs. Until beit din actually assigns it to him, it does not become his property. A creditor acquires the property collected only from the time of collection onward. If, at the time collection is sought, the debtor whose name appears in the pledged note pays with movable property instead, then had he paid land the first creditor could recover that land through Rabbi Natan's lien, as explained in Pesachim 31. But if he pays movable property, that property immediately becomes movable property of the deceased creditor's heirs. Since beit din did not assign the note to the pledgee during the original creditor's lifetime, the note never became his. At the time of collection, the property is already orphan-owned movable property. It is in this sense that the Shach says the pledge does not enable collection. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that according to the opinion that notes can be subjected to a lien by means of acquisition together with land, even a pledged note can be collected upon, and only unilateral seizure fails because there was no delivery from hand to hand. This is difficult. The Rosh's ruling that notes may be subjected to a lien together with land means that the creditor's lien against the underlying debtor, even if ultimately realized in movable property, can receive the legal status of land just as other movable property may be encumbered together with land. Thus, if the original debtor sells the note, the creditor may recover the document itself just as he would recover land. But for the pledgee to sue the debtor named in the note directly, no ordinary form of lien suffices. Until beit din assigns the claim to him, the underlying debtor can still say, "You are not my proper litigant." The Sages established written assignment and delivery as the means of transferring that personal claim, and without it the debtor's personal obligation was not transferred even rabbinically. The original creditor likewise cannot create such a direct right merely by pledging the note. Because a creditor acquires collected property only prospectively, if the heirs have already received movable property before assignment, the pledgee cannot collect from that movable property after the original creditor's death. This differs from a lien on movable property created together with land. There, if the debtor paid land, the creditor may recover it, as explained in Ketubot and Pesachim, and if movable property was validly encumbered together with land, recovery of that movable property follows the same rule as recovery of land, whether from heirs or purchasers. A mere pledge of a note does not have that effect.
It is considered seizure. Ketzot HaChoshen questions what sort of coercive leverage this provides. Once the underlying debtor pays, the possessor will have to return the paper document, just as where a note was delivered without the required written assignment. The objection itself is difficult. First, the central leverage is that the heirs cannot compel the possessor to return the note. Consequently, the debtor named in the note need not pay the heirs until they return the instrument. Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 51, part 4, states this regarding delivery without written assignment: where the recipient is not obligated to return the note, the debtor need not pay the original creditor. There can be no greater leverage than preventing the heirs from collecting at all. Second, the possessor may need the instrument because it contains rights against purchasers. Third, even where a note was delivered without written assignment, Ran in the chapter HaKotev writes that if the paper itself was sold to the recipient for its material value, he need not return it. Ordinarily we do not assume such a sale because people do not normally sell debt instruments merely as scrap paper. If the parties attempted to sell the debt for collection and that transfer failed, the attempted sale of the paper itself is likewise considered part of the failed transaction. Here, however, the possessor was not given the note as a formal pledge of the debt but simply took possession of the physical instrument. With respect to the paper itself, his possession is therefore like a sale of the document for its paper value, and he need not return it. Ketzot also explains that he need not return it because the paper is analogous to movable property belonging to the debtor's debtor, and one may seize such movable property. This too is difficult. The physical paper of the note is not movable property of the debtor's debtor. It is the movable property of the creditor himself, for the creditor may sell the paper for its material value, as Ran writes in HaKotev. Moreover, this is not yet a seizure from the debtor's debtor at all, because that third party never demanded the paper from the possessor. "Seizure" in this context means that the person entitled to the object demanded it and the possessor refused to return it, as the Mechaber states based on the Gemara. As to the underlying question whether seizure of movable property from one's debtor's debtor prevents that property from becoming orphan-owned movable property upon death, according to talmudic law, and similarly today in cases governed by the rule that one does not transmit an oath to one's sons, where seizure can help as explained in Even HaEzer siman 96 and below siman 108, the matter depends upon the dispute cited by the Shach in siman 128, seif katan 18. If Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply when the intermediate debtor has other assets and the seizing creditor must return the property, then his possession is not effective seizure. Ketzot further questions the Mordechai in the chapter HaMekabel. The Mordechai asks against those who hold that a creditor cannot retain collateral for more than its market value and say, "It is worth more to me," from our case where the possessor can retain the paper. The Mordechai answers that the possessor's seizure counts only up to the note's value as measured by what the debt instrument could be sold for. Ketzot objects that if the possessor himself cannot collect the debt, the note should be worth only the paper's scrap value. That objection is likewise difficult. Every right that the original creditor has in the note, including the ability to sell it to another, can be reached by the creditor who seized it. During the underlying debtor's lifetime, the possessor could go to beit din and have the note assigned to him through judicial collection. Once beit din assigned it, his status would be that of a purchaser and he could then sue and collect the debt. It is only because beit din did not make such an assignment before the debtor's death that he cannot now collect directly. At the time he seized it, while the debtor was alive, however, his seizure extended to the full sale value of the note. Therefore, when the heirs seek to recover the notes from him, they must return the full amount the notes were worth at the time of seizure. Moreover, even if the note has limited personal value to this particular possessor, it has market value to the heirs because they can sell it. An item's value is determined by what it is worth generally. Only where an object is worth very little to everyone do some authorities reject the possessor's claim that "to me it is worth more." Here the note has substantial market value to anyone able to purchase it, even if the particular possessor cannot himself turn it into cash. This is simple.
And the case is where there are witnesses. See Shach, seif katan 4, through his statement that if the possessor could have claimed complete denial or that the notes were lost through unavoidable accident when the borrowers later demanded them, he would have a migo. The difficulty is that he would still have the very migo that the Shach himself mentions in seif katan 5, namely, that the notes originally came into his hands as collateral. Since there are no witnesses that they were deposited with him, that claim is available to him, and it is surely stronger than saying that he himself later seized the notes as collateral. It must therefore be that the authorities cited there hold that even the claim, "It originally came to me as collateral," itself requires a supporting migo, in accordance with the dissenting view that the Rama cites in his gloss. This will be explained below in seif katan 6. One practical consequence for us is whether a possessor is believed concerning notes where the debt is not otherwise known by virtue of this migo. See below, seif katan 6.
He would be believed by virtue of a migo that they were lost through unavoidable accident. See Shach, seif katan 6, through his statement that otherwise he would have to swear by virtue of a secondary oath attached to another oath. At first sight this is difficult. He is not presently seeking to extract money from the heirs, but only to retain the paper instruments. This resembles the rule of Ran cited by the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 71. Just as on that ground he is exempt from the stringent oath ordinarily imposed upon one claiming on collateral, he should likewise be exempt from the oath imposed upon one collecting from orphan property. It must be speaking, for example, where he also has a note against the heirs for one thousand gold coins, the heirs own land worth only five hundred, and for the balance he retains these debt instruments. Since he must swear on his own note in order to collect the land, under the law governing one who collects from orphans, he can also be required through the principle of a rolled oath to swear concerning his possession of the other notes. This is straightforward.
Even though he has no migo. See Sema, seif katan 4, and the Shach, who are greatly troubled by this ruling because in the ordinary law of collateral a migo is required. Tumim likewise concludes that although a promissory note is comparable to an article not ordinarily lent out, according to Rashba a claimant to collateral still certainly requires a migo. Ketzot HaChoshen similarly objects that the accepted rule is that any creditor claiming through a pledge requires a migo. See Taz for his attempted resolution, which is difficult because siman 133, seif 3, expressly teaches that even if the possessor first says, before the article is shown, "Know that it is pledged to me for its full value," the subsequent testimony of witnesses who saw the article still counts as witnesses and prior observation. In my opinion the words are to be understood literally. The Rama himself, in responsum 86, explains that he is discussing a case in which there are no witnesses that the notes were deposited with the possessor, but there are witnesses who saw them in his possession. The possessor therefore lacks all of the particular migo claims listed by the Mechaber, such as complete denial or return, because the notes were seen in his hand. Yet the Rama writes that he is believed because he still has the migo that he could have said, "They originally came into my hands as collateral," as the Rosh states and as cited by the Sema, seif katan 4, and the Shach, seif katan 5. That is not the same migo of complete denial mentioned by the Mechaber, despite the Sema's strained explanation. The Shach in seif katan 5 correctly indicates that the two are distinct. It necessarily follows that the substantive claim, "They originally came into my hands as collateral," is itself credible without an additional migo. Otherwise the Rosh's statement that he is believed by virtue of the possibility of making that claim would prove nothing, since that alternative claim itself would be unsupported. The question remains why this should be stronger than ordinary movable property, where even one who says, "It originally came to me as collateral," requires a migo, as explained in siman 72. The answer is found in Sefer HaZechut of Ramban, where Raavad records an objection. Some argued that without any migo the possessor should be believed to say that an object is pledged to him, asking why a claim of purchase is stronger than a claim of pledge. The objection is powerful. When we believe one who possesses an article and says, "I purchased it," even where the article was previously known to belong to another, we rely on the presumption that whatever is under a person's control is his. We ask: how did it come into his possession? Presumably through a sale. Why, then, should the same possession not support his claim that it came to him as collateral? Raavad answers that a pledge differs from a purchase because the possessor concedes that the body of the object belongs to the other person. We therefore leave the principal ownership in the conceded owner's presumptive possession. If the possessor says, "It is pledged to me," he must bring proof, unless he has a migo. A similar distinction appears in the second answer of the Rosh in chapter Kol HaNishba'in, siman 4, cited by the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 71. The Rosh explains why a creditor claiming on collateral must take a stringent oath: he is not claiming ownership of the collateral itself, but seeks to extract money from the other person through it. The Shach explains that he does not claim, "You sold it to me," or, "You gave it to me," with respect to the object itself. Ran there writes that if the creditor does not presently demand payment but remains silent and merely wishes to hold the pledge until he is paid, his position is equivalent to a claim to the object itself. His lien rests upon the object until payment, and he seeks only to retain the object so that the lien remains effective. In that setting he needs only a rabbinic oath of denial. The same distinction applies to migo. If the claimant is merely retaining an object and is not demanding payment, he is functionally asserting a present right in the object itself. There is no conceptual difference whether he says that he owns it entirely, acquired its produce, or acquired it as security for a debt. It is only when he sues the other person for money through the collateral that he is no longer treated as one claiming the object itself. Therefore, just as the nature of his oath changes, so too the need for a migo changes. When he merely retains the collateral, no migo should be required, since an ordinary claim to an object in one's possession does not require a migo. When he demands collection through it, the claim is no longer a claim to the object itself and a migo is required. That is precisely the present case concerning promissory notes. Possession of the notes does not by itself enable him to collect the underlying debts. It enables him only to retain the documents. His claim that they are collateral is therefore a claim to a present possessory right in the very instruments, and no migo is required. One may object that with promissory notes even a direct claim to ownership, such as "I purchased them," is not ordinarily believed merely by possession, and that this remains so even where the alleged mode of acquisition did not require a written assignment, such as certain deathbed gifts. Why, then, should a claim of pledge be believed merely because no writing is necessary? The answer is that Ran in the chapter HaKotev explains that where the claimed acquisition was not effected through delivery of the physical instrument, possession of the note does not create the ordinary evidentiary presumption of ownership. By contrast, a pledge of this kind is acquired, for the limited purpose at issue, through physical delivery alone. When the possessor says that the note came to him as collateral, the very physical possession is therefore the sort of possession that supports his claim. This explains clearly why the Rosh, Sema, and Shach indicate that one may say, "It originally came to me as collateral," without a separate migo. The same reasoning would at first suggest that with ordinary movable collateral too, if the creditor remains silent and merely retains the object without suing, no migo should be necessary, since siman 72 generally discusses one who seeks collection and therefore must take the stringent oath. However, Ran's discussion in Bava Batra concerning an artisan, at 45, indicates that even where the garment remains in the artisan's possession and the artisan is not affirmatively suing, the Gemara still requires a migo. We must therefore distinguish ordinary movable property. Such property is ordinarily delivered as collateral precisely so that it may eventually be collected upon, and a claim of that sort is not independently believed without a migo. A promissory note, by contrast, cannot be delivered as collateral in this manner for direct collection without further transfer formalities. The limited claim that one merely received the physical note to retain as security is therefore credible, because this limited possessory right can be acquired by Torah law through delivery alone. See Tosafot to Ketubot 85. There is, however, an additional practical point. If the original creditor is alive and makes a definite claim that the note fell from him and was merely found by the possessor, then although he cannot recover the paper from the possessor, he may demand payment of the underlying debt from the debtor and write a receipt. He may say that his note fell and that the possessor found it and now wrongfully withholds it. The debtor cannot answer, "Since the note still exists I need not pay," under siman 54, because the creditor claims that the person holding the note is a thief. This is no stronger than a case of ordinary collateral where the creditor says that a third party stole the pledge from him by force, the third party claims that he purchased it, and the original creditor insists it was stolen. The debtor who gave the pledge must still pay. He cannot say, "Perhaps the creditor already sold the pledge and received its value," because that is a case of uncertainty whether the debt was discharged. If the original creditor has died, however, the heirs cannot make a definite claim about how the note left his possession. They therefore cannot demand payment until they return the note. The same would be true of collateral. If heirs concede that the debtor gave their father a pledge securing the debt reflected in the note, the pledge is now missing, and they do not know whether their father sold it or whether it was lost through unavoidable accident, they cannot collect. As long as the instrument still exists, the law of a promissory note and the law of a pledge are parallel in this respect. See what Taz writes in siman 60, seif 8. As to the primary rule, when the possessor says, "The notes originally came into my hands as collateral," the Sema, seif katan 7, and Shach, seif katan 5, imply that he is believed even without a migo. According to what I have explained, the Rama also ruled this way. Their objections to the Rama arose only because they understood him as speaking even where there are witnesses that the notes were deposited with the possessor, in which case no migo of any sort exists. Where there are no witnesses of deposit, however, it appears that they too agree that the claim that the notes originally came as collateral is credible. One might therefore have ruled that way even where the underlying debt itself is not otherwise known. Nevertheless, I have explained that the result depends upon Ran's reasoning cited by the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 71, and the Shach concludes there that Ran's words require further study. The matter is therefore a genuine unresolved monetary doubt, and property is not removed from the party presently in possession.
