Even according to the Rambam, who holds that a person may say, "I had a written transfer document and lost it," here he is not believed because there are witnesses that he originally received the document only as a deposit, Sema. Where one is believed to claim purchase without a written transfer, as with negotiable instruments, he is also believed to say, "I seized it as a pledge," through a migo that he could have claimed outright purchase. With debt documents written in the lender's name, even if the lender explicitly handed the document over as a pledge for collection, that does not prevent it from being treated as ordinary movable property of the lender's heirs after the borrower dies. But during the borrower's lifetime, or according to our law that movable property of heirs is subject to creditors, a pledged debt document is like an ordinary apotiki. Beit din must award that particular document to the creditor if the debtor has no cash, and beit din writes that the document was awarded to him in payment of the debt. He may then sue the underlying borrower. See Beurim, seif katan 2:
Witnesses. The Shach concludes that with debt documents, a migo that he could have denied receiving them or claimed to have returned them does not help, because in order to collect he ultimately has to produce the documents. A practical consequence in our time is where the underlying debt itself is not known. With ordinary movable property, seizure can be effective because of a migo that he could have denied possession. If he seized debt documents, however, he is not believed through that migo, because he is afraid to claim nonreceipt or return when he will need to produce the documents. These rules therefore matter in cases where the debt is not independently known and there is no useful migo with respect to documents. See Beurim, seif katan 4, where I wrote that if there are no witnesses that the documents were deposited with him, so that he can claim, "They came into my possession originally as a pledge," then possession protects him. Likewise, if he expressly claims that they came into his possession originally as a pledge and he has no additional migo, the matter is one of unresolved law and we do not remove the documents from the possessor:
That they were lost through unavoidable accident. Meaning, during the deceased's lifetime we would not have had to demand return because he would have been believed through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable loss. Although he could not then have produced the documents for collection, the migo can be understood as follows: before beit din he would claim that they were lost, while privately he would disclose to the owners that the documents remained in his possession and would return them once they paid him. Therefore, where he claims outright ownership of the documents, such a migo is unavailable because he claims that the entire property belongs to him. The Sema writes that where he is believed through the migo of unavoidable loss, he must swear a hesset oath. The Shach rules that he need not swear at all, because the heirs make only an uncertain claim and no oath is imposed on an uncertain claim. Thus, when they say, "He seized them as a pledge," he does not swear for this reason. However, if his own debt is larger and he is also collecting from land inherited by the orphans through his own debt document, so that he must swear as one collecting from orphan property, he may also be required by extension to swear concerning these documents. According to the Shach's earlier position that a migo is ineffective with debt documents, most of these distinctions have practical application only where he claims that he returned the documents or never received them in a context where he needs a migo of unavoidable loss, such as a deposit of a document which ordinarily does not require an oath. Yet the Shach himself rules in siman 66 that even with documents a custodian must swear a rabbinic oath that the document is no longer in his possession. And although. The Sema and Shach ask that even with ordinary movable property one is not believed to claim that it was given as a pledge where he has no migo. See Beurim, seif katan 6, where I explain that the case is one where there were no witnesses to the original deposit, only witnesses who later saw the item. He therefore has a migo that it came into his hands originally as a pledge. As a practical matter, where he has such a migo, or where he claims that it came into his hands originally as a pledge but has no other migo, we do not remove the document from the possessor. His possession does not, however, allow him to collect the debt itself. He can hold only the physical paper. He need not return it even if the borrower pays the heirs. The borrower likewise need not pay the heirs while it is known that the document still exists, until he redeems the document and receives it back. But if the original lender is still alive and claims against the borrower that the possessor found the document after it fell and is now withholding it unlawfully, the borrower must pay the lender, who can give him a receipt.
