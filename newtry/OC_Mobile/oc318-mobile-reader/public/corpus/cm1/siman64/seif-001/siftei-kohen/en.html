As collateral, etc. Meaning, only to retain the physical paper. He cannot collect from the borrower unless he claims that the document was sold to him through writing and delivery. If he merely claims that it was given to him as collateral so that he may collect from the borrower, that is ineffective, because he did not acquire the debt embodied in the document. On what basis could he then collect from the borrower? This explains why the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber specify that there are witnesses to the deposit and that the documents were presently seen in his possession. Otherwise, even if he had claimed denial or unavoidable loss, he still could not collect from the borrowers. The Rosh clearly implies this. See Responsa Mahari ibn Sasson, siman 141, and Responsa Mahari ben Lev, volume 2, siman 55, folio 76, and volume 4, folio 9a.
For to collect them, it would not have been effective without writing, etc. Although the case concerns witnesses to the deposit and present sight of the documents, so that the custodian could not claim purchase, nevertheless, since the debt owed by the depositor to the custodian is known and witnesses testify that the custodian seized the documents during the depositor's lifetime, one might have thought that he could collect the debts. The ruling therefore teaches that rights represented by documents are acquired only through writing and delivery. Since the documents were not acquired by him, his seizure is effective only to retain the physical paper until he is paid, not to collect from the borrower. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, beginning of siman 4.
It would not have been effective without writing, etc. This implies that where he could have claimed, "I purchased it," without requiring writing, as with our mamram notes and similar instruments, he is believed to say, "I seized them as collateral," through a migo that he could have claimed purchase. This is clear from all the authorities. The Bach writes that Rashi implies that with documents, even if the custodian has a migo of purchase, he must nevertheless bring proof, because once the documents were deposited with him they are considered as resting in the depositor's domain. This is not correct. Rashi comes only to explain why proof is required that the depositor demanded them during his lifetime and the custodian refused to return them. Since they were originally deposited with him, they are treated as remaining in the depositor's domain, and the custodian's possession does not constitute seizure because he did not initially take them on account of his debt. But the case still concerns circumstances where he has no migo of purchase, as all the authorities explain. This is clear.
And the case concerns witnesses, etc. The Ran in Perek HaKosev writes that according to the Rif the rule applies even where there are no witnesses to the deposit and the documents were not presently seen, and even then the custodian is not believed even to retain the physical documents as collateral. This is indeed implied by the Rif, and likewise explicitly by Tosafos at the beginning of Kesubos 85a and by the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 11 of Hilchos Malveh, who ask why the custodian should not be believed through a migo of purchase. The same view appears in Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 1, in the name of the Raavad and Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir. I am surprised that the Mechaber did not cite their view, even in the Beit Yosef. Baal HaMaor and Ramban in Milchamos on Perek HaKosev also appear to agree. The Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber ask why the custodian should not be believed through a migo that he could claim unavoidable loss, denial, or return. One may answer that had he made such a claim he could not have displayed the documents and would thereby lose their entire value. He now believes that the documents are worth a substantial amount and that his possession may help him at least to the value of the paper. Alternatively, this migo applies to ordinary movable property because if he claimed denial or unavoidable loss he could sell the goods to others. With documents, however, no one would purchase them because they are written in another's name and the custodian lacks a written transfer. Moreover, any later sale would reveal that the documents had remained in his hands and were not lost. He therefore claims that he seized them as collateral, believing that this will allow him lawfully to collect them or sell them. Or he may believe the borrowers themselves will pay him, reasoning that they do not care whether payment is made to him or to the depositor, whereas if he had claimed denial or unavoidable loss the borrowers would know he was lying and would not pay him. We cannot assume he could arrange a collusive scheme with the borrowers, because perhaps they are honest and would refuse. This answer is alluded to by the Raavad as cited in Baal HaTerumos. It appears primary to me. A practical difference arises today where it is not known that the depositor owed the custodian anything and the custodian seized documents. With documents there is no such migo, for the reasons stated. This is also implied by the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef below at the beginning of siman 66, that with documents there is no migo that he could have said they never came into his possession, because if he said so he would be afraid later to produce them and collect from the debtors, like the case of the bundle of documents in Perek HaKosev. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 130, and Responsa Mabit, part 2, end of siman 129 and siman 289.
For otherwise he would be believed through a migo that they were lost through unavoidable circumstances or that he never received them, etc. The Sema writes that the omission of the migo that he returned them is because that would be a brazen claim, and that where there are no witnesses to the deposit, "I never received them" means he never received them as a deposit but rather originally as collateral. This follows his view below in siman 82. I have already challenged Nimukei Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and the Sema at length on this point. Their words are not correct. Even a migo involving brazenness is accepted in such cases, as I explain below in simanim 75, 81, 93, 361, and elsewhere. The wording is simply not exhaustive. The Bach also writes that one of several possible migo claims was chosen, and the same applies to "I returned them." In any event, this must be so. Otherwise, why would witnesses to the original deposit be required? If there were merely witnesses who presently saw the documents, there would already be no migo of unavoidable loss or denial. Necessarily the witnesses to the deposit are required for the Rosh's separate reason, that otherwise the custodian could say the documents were originally pledged to him. The Tur merely chose one of several possible migo claims. The Sema's interpretation of "I never received them" as "I never received them as a deposit" is strained, especially since the Rosh explicitly lists the migo that they were originally pledged to him, or that he returned them, or that they were lost, or that he never received them.
He would be believed through a migo that they were lost through unavoidable circumstances, etc. The Sema explains that he would be believed with a heset oath, just as one claiming unavoidable loss would take a heset oath. In my humble opinion, he would not be required to swear even heset, because heset is not imposed on an uncertain claim. This is explicit in a responsum of the Ritva cited by the Beit Yosef below at the end of siman 66, that one who deposits documents with another, even by written deposit, and the custodian claims unavoidable loss, is believed without any oath, even heset. The Ritva says this according to Rambam, who holds a negligent custodian liable for documents. According to the Rosh and those who follow him, the custodian likewise has a migo that the documents were lost even through negligence. Throughout this siman, where we speak of a migo of unavoidable loss, this is not exclusive. The same applies to a migo of loss through negligence according to the Rosh and most authorities. Below, in siman 66, seif 40, however, I rule that the Rambam's view is primary. Regarding one who says, "I seized them as collateral," the Sema holds that he takes a heset oath, while the Bach requires an oath holding a sacred object, analogous to one who holds collateral below in siman 72, seif 17. In my humble opinion the cases differ. Here he does not seek to collect through the documents, because possession without written transfer cannot give him the right to collect. This resembles the case I cite below in siman 72 in the name of the Ran. Therefore, if his alternative claim of unavoidable loss would require no oath, his present claim through that migo should likewise require no oath. The Ritva records such a view from some of his teachers. Even according to the other teachers of the Ritva, who require heset where he claims that he returned documents, as I rule below in siman 66, seif 39, that is because there the depositor directly contradicts him. Here the orphans do not claim certainty that he did not seize the documents during the deceased's lifetime, so no heset is required according to any view. This assumes that he could otherwise collect from the orphans without an oath, for example because the deceased granted him credibility. Otherwise, an oath may arise by gilgul. All this is according to the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber. As I concluded above in seif katan 4, however, when he claims, "I seized them as collateral," he is not believed through a migo of unavoidable loss, denial, or return even if there are no witnesses and no present sight of the documents. A practical difference remains in an ordinary case where he claims that he returned them to the orphans or never received them, where he may be believed through a migo of unavoidable loss without an oath according to some of the Ritva's teachers, or where he claims he returned them to their father, where according to all views he may be believed without heset through that migo. Certainly if he actually claims unavoidable loss itself, the Sema and Bach are incorrect. Still, according to what I write below in siman 66, seif 39, that there is a rabbinic oath regarding documents no longer in one's possession, an oath would be required where he claims return to them or to their father.
Gloss: Even though he has no migo, etc. The Sema has already challenged the Rama on this point, and see the Rama's own responsum, siman 86. There is no authority who says that where the custodian has no migo and the underlying debt is not known, he is believed to say that he seized the documents as collateral. Tosafos, the Rosh, and the Tur and Mechaber speak where the debt is known. The Bach likewise challenges the Rama, and the Levush properly omits this gloss. The Raavad and Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir in Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, end of part 8, also state that the case concerns a known debt. It appears that even today possession is ineffective where he has no migo and the debt is not known, according to all views.
