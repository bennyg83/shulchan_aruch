For if he seized them in order to collect them, etc. Meaning, even if during the borrower's lifetime he expressly declared before witnesses that he was seizing the promissory notes in payment of his debt, this does not help, because acquisition of documents requires writing and delivery. But if he says before witnesses that he is seizing them as collateral, meaning that the essence of his seizure is directed to the physical paper itself, that seizure is effective. We must be speaking where the debtors on those notes ultimately paid with movable property. Otherwise, if they paid with land, the present creditor would collect from that land by virtue of Rabbi Natan's rule, and no written assignment would be necessary. Where the debt was paid with movable property, however, it resembles the rule that if orphans collected movable property on their father's debt, their father's creditor does not collect it, and Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply. I state this because I saw a contemporary scholar err on this point.
And the case is where there are witnesses, etc. The Shach writes that most authorities disagree with the Rosh and hold that even without witnesses to the deposit and without witnesses who saw the documents, the possessor is not believed to say that he seized them during the creditor's lifetime. He therefore wonders why the Mechaber omitted their view. There is no difficulty with the Mechaber. The Raavad, Rabbi Moshe ben Nachman as cited by Baal HaTerumot, and apparently Ramban in Milchamot hold that even without witnesses who saw or witnessed the deposit the possessor is not believed by migo. But they follow their position that even against the creditor himself one is not believed to say that he had a written assignment and lost it. The documents therefore remain in the creditor's chazakah unless the transferee proves the acquisition, and a migo cannot remove that chazakah. This is also the position of the Rif if one reads him as not requiring witnesses who saw the documents. The essential reason is that documents differ from ordinary movable property because the possessor cannot establish ownership without the written assignment. In truth, however, the Rif seems to agree with Raah and Ritva that witnesses who saw the documents were present. Otherwise, why does the Rif ask only that she should be believed by a migo that they were purchased, instead of by the stronger migo that she never had them, returned them, or they were lost through an unavoidable accident? Necessarily there were witnesses who saw the documents, but there were no witnesses establishing that they had originally been entrusted to her. The Rif's question is therefore that she should be believed to say they were purchased just as with ordinary movable property even though witnesses saw them, and he answers that documents require writing and delivery. This also explains why the Gemara, in the case of the cattle seized by the creditor, asks whether there are witnesses, while in the case of the bundle of documents it does not ask whether there are witnesses to the deposit. The distinction is that with the documents, once witnesses saw them in her possession, no additional witnesses to the deposit were required under the Rif's approach. Tosafos likewise did not establish the case as one with witnesses to the deposit, for then Rabbi Nachman should have asked about those witnesses as he did in the cattle case. Tosafos instead explains differently. The woman first said only, "I seized them during his lifetime," and did not explain that the creditor demanded them and she refused to return them. Had she stated that immediately, she would have had a migo at that time and would have been believed, like the creditor who seized the cattle. But once she gave only the vague statement, she could not later reinterpret it to mean that she had refused a demand during the creditor's lifetime, because that would be a retroactive migo. The Rosh disagrees because he holds that a litigant may later explain an initially ambiguous statement, as in Bava Batra where one who says, "I bought it from so and so who bought it from you," may later explain, "he bought it from you in my presence." Tosafos holds there that such later explanation is a retroactive migo, while the Rosh permits it. The authorities therefore follow their respective positions. This is also why the Mechaber, who below in siman 146 follows the Rosh and allows explanation of an ambiguous statement, needed here to require witnesses to the deposit as the Rosh does. There is no support here for saying that the Rosh allows a retroactive migo. Rambam presents a different difficulty. In chapter 11 of Hilchot Malveh he rules that one who seized movable property during the debtor's lifetime may claim against it up to its value, plainly where there are no witnesses to the deposit and seeing, because otherwise there would be no migo. He then writes that if what was seized consisted of documents and the possessor says that they were held as collateral and were seized during the creditor's lifetime, the burden is on him to bring proof, because he does not claim ownership of their bodies but only the evidence they contain. Maggid Mishneh already notes that this language seems to imply that one cannot say, "I had a written assignment and lost it," which conflicts with Rambam elsewhere. In my view there is no contradiction. The migo of complete denial, return, or unavoidable accident is weak here. If he says the documents no longer exist, the heirs can still proceed against the debtors and, since the documents are not known to exist, compel payment with a receipt. Furthermore, several practical considerations weaken such a migo. The only meaningful migo is that the documents were purchased, but even that is weak because he would be expected to produce the written assignment, and claiming that such an important proof was lost is uncommon. More fundamentally, Rambam says precisely that the claimant does not assert ownership of the bodies of the documents, only a right in the evidence they embody. The physical papers remain in the chazakah of the creditor and borrower. This is therefore a migo used to extract and is ineffective. Rambam's wording is thus coherent. Accordingly, the Mechaber's ruling follows the Tur and Rosh and stands largely alone against the majority of authorities according to the Rama, who below in siman 66 rejects Rambam's rule that one may claim a lost written assignment. For practical law, the majority view is therefore the stronger one.
A migo that they were lost through unavoidable accident. The Sema asks why the source does not use the migo that he returned them. The Shach correctly answers that in monetary matters we do use a migo involving brazenness. But according to the Shach's own view that a claim of unavoidable accident here would not require an oath, it is preferable to formulate the migo through a claim carrying no oath rather than through return, which would require an oath. There is also a practical reason. If he falsely swore that he returned the documents and afterward reached a compromise with the debtors, his false oath could become exposed. If he claimed that they were lost or stolen, he could later say that they were subsequently recovered from the thief or otherwise returned to him. Thus the source naturally chooses the stronger and cleaner migo.
Only where it is known that the debtor owes him. The Rama implies that even if the debt is not independently known, he may nevertheless be believed, despite having no ordinary migo. The Sema and other later authorities object that without a migo or independent proof of the debt there is no basis for allowing seizure. The Rama can be explained from the Rosh. The Rosh required both witnesses to the deposit and witnesses who saw the documents. If the Rosh holds, as the Rama does, that one is not believed to say that he had a written assignment and lost it, then witnesses who merely saw the documents should already suffice. He cannot claim complete denial, return, accident, or purchase without the written assignment. Why, then, did the Rosh also require witnesses that the documents were originally deposited? The answer is that without such witnesses he could still say that they came into his hands as collateral. A claim of collateral concerning documents is believed from the strength of possession itself, like a claim of purchase concerning an object not ordinarily lent, even though there is no independent migo. The witnesses to the deposit are therefore needed to defeat that collateral claim. If so, documents are treated like objects not ordinarily lent with respect to a claim of collateral. That remains true whether or not the underlying debt is independently known. Just as a person holding an ordinary object not usually lent can say, even without prior proof of the debt, "this is collateral for money owed to me," so too one holding documents can say they were pledged for his debt. This is the Rama's reasoning. His phrase that he has no migo means that there are witnesses who saw the documents, but there are no witnesses to the deposit. Responsa Rama, siman 86, indeed concerns that type of case. Nevertheless, although this explains the Rama, practical law appears to follow Rashba and the other authorities cited by the later decisors, that where there is no migo of complete denial or purchase, the possessor is not believed merely to say that the documents were pledged to him.
