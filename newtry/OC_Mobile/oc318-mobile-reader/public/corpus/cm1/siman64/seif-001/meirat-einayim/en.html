For otherwise he would be believed, etc. Meaning, we would not need witnesses that the depositor demanded the documents from him during his lifetime. Rather, the bailee would be believed on this by his own statement. Although the documents were deposited with him in the presence of witnesses, so long as witnesses did not see them presently in his possession, he is believed by virtue of a migo that, had he wished, he could have said that they were lost through unavoidable circumstances. Although in that event he could not have produced the documents, he could nevertheless have colluded with the borrowers, taken some payment from them, and delivered the torn debt instruments to them. Alternatively, the migo operates as follows: when the heirs demand that he return the documents their father deposited with him in the presence of witnesses, he could answer them before beit din that the documents were lost through unavoidable circumstances, while privately revealing to them that he still held the documents and would return them once they paid what their father owed him. Therefore, in siman 67 seif 11, where the document must be produced in beit din in order to collect with it, we do not invoke this migo of unavoidable loss. See also siman 133 seif 25 for what I wrote further on this. We do not say that he has a migo that he could have claimed, "I returned them to your father," because then there would have been no need for testimony that the documents were seen presently in his possession, only that they were seen in his possession after the father's death. Nor can we say that he has a migo that he could have claimed, "I returned them to the heirs," because that would be a brazen migo. See the Derishah for more. If the documents were seen presently in his possession but there are no witnesses that they came to him as a deposit, he is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have denied the matter entirely. Meaning, he could have denied that they came into his possession as a deposit and claimed instead that they were initially given to him as collateral. He would be believed, because holding them as collateral does not require a written assignment or proof.

Even though he has no migo and cannot, etc. This refers to the statement that if there are witnesses that the depositor demanded them from him, etc., his retention of them is considered seizure. The Rama explains that the case is one where there are witnesses both that the documents were deposited with him and that they are now seen in his possession. Even though he has no migo at all, neither a migo of unavoidable loss nor a migo that they originally came to him as collateral, and although he cannot say that he purchased them, nevertheless if the stated condition applies, his seizure is effective. The reason he is not believed to say that he purchased them is twofold. First, whenever there are witnesses that the item came into his possession as a deposit and there are also witnesses who now see it in his possession, he is not believed to say that he later purchased it from the owner, as the Tur and Mechaber write in simanim 72 and 133. Second, even without this, according to all authorities other than the Rif and Rambam, one is not believed regarding documents to say that he purchased them unless he possesses a written instrument stating, "Acquire this document, etc.," as the Tur writes at the end of simanim 62 and 66.
Not like the opinion that says, etc., but only where it is known that the other person owes him, etc. The Rama's words here are difficult, as I explained in the Perishah and Derishah. It appears clear to me that there is no dispute about this, and that Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur all agree that it must be known that the owner owes him money. We have an established rule that whenever there are witnesses who presently saw the item in his possession, he is not believed to claim against it up to its value unless he has a migo that he could have said, "I purchased it," as is explained in the Talmud in many places and in the Tur and Mechaber in simanim 72 and 133 and elsewhere. Why, then, should the fact that he has witnesses that he demanded the documents during the owner's lifetime and refused to return them benefit him, when if both parties had come before us during the owner's lifetime in such circumstances, the holder would have been required to return them to the person from whom he was withholding them? The rights of the latter's orphans should not be diminished. See the Derishah, where I discuss this at length.
