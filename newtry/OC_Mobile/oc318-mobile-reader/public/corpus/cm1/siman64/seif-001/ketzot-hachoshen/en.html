For as to collecting with them. It appears to me that even our bearer notes, which do not require writing for transfer, cannot be collected merely because a creditor seized them during the debtor's lifetime. Such notes may not require a written assignment, but they still require delivery. Delivery means a transfer from one hand to another through a person who intends to transfer ownership. The Shach likewise writes that even an instrument payable to whoever produces it requires actual delivery and cannot be acquired merely from ownerless status. Here the creditor originally held the note only as a deposit and later seized it on his own. There is no other person's intent transferring it. Therefore seizure does not make him owner of the debt instrument for collection. See siman 104.
Without writing. The Rosh writes that debt instruments can be subjected to a lien together with land even without the transfer formula needed for sale, because lien is stronger than acquisition. The Mechaber in siman 60 appears to follow this. If so, why does seizure of debt instruments as collateral not allow collection without writing? If lien can attach without writing, and movable property ordinarily becomes collateral through possession, why should holding the instrument not be at least as strong as acquisition together with land? The answer is that a lien still requires another person's act of transfer. In acquisition together with land, the debtor or owner knowingly encumbers the instrument. Here the creditor seized it on his own. There is no other person intentionally transferring the lien. Therefore the seizure itself cannot create the debt instrument lien. But if the debtor or owner actually handed the debt instrument to the creditor as collateral, it should in principle be effective for collection even without a written assignment, because collateral is a lien and the Rosh holds a lien on debt instruments does not require the formal writing needed for sale. This also clarifies the Rosh's responsum about heirs who find another's debt instrument among their father's property and claim their father held it as collateral. Their claim is effective only to retain the paper, because their credibility arises through the father's possible claims of accident, denial, or purchase. Those alternative claims would not have given him the right to collect from the underlying borrower. Therefore their present migo cannot create greater rights. Where witnesses establish that the owner actually handed the instrument to the creditor as collateral, however, collection may indeed be available. The Rashba's ruling about a debt instrument deposited with arbitrators as collateral in a compromise is not contrary, because there the very underlying monetary obligation had not yet arisen. "There is no maneh, therefore there is no collateral." If the parties seek instead to transfer the body of the instrument conditionally, that is an acquisition and requires writing. But where a valid debt already exists and the debtor hands over a debt instrument as collateral, the lien should attach without writing under the Rosh's approach. I have not found an earlier authority who states this explicitly, but the reasoning appears correct.
And the case is where there are witnesses. Ketubot relates that a woman held a bundle of debt instruments deposited with her. The heirs demanded them. She said, "I seized them during his lifetime." Rav Nachman asked whether she had witnesses that he demanded them during his lifetime and she refused to return them. When she said no, he treated the seizure as one made only after death. Rif explains that the requirement of witnesses is unique to debt instruments. With ordinary property, if the holder could claim purchase, she would be believed to say she seized it during the debtor's lifetime. Debt instruments are different because they cannot ordinarily be acquired by mere delivery without writing. Rif adds that even a lifetime seizure helps only as collateral to pressure payment, not to collect the underlying debts. His words are difficult. If the woman claims purchase, even if the assignment failed, why can she not at least retain the paper itself? Tosafot and Rosh therefore establish the case with witnesses and present observation, eliminating the migo of purchase. Rif appears to follow the Provençal authorities who hold that one who receives a debt instrument without a valid written assignment must return even the paper because the paper belongs to the borrower. Rashba indicates that Rif indeed may hold this. Yet if so, why should proof of lifetime seizure help even as collateral? She would eventually have to return the paper to the underlying borrowers when they pay the named creditor. It appears that a lifetime seizure can create a lien through Rabbi Natan's rule. Just as a creditor who seizes the debtor's movables during the debtor's lifetime can preserve a claim against the heirs, so too she can seize property belonging to the debtor's debtor when that property is subject to the chain of obligation. Although ordinary movables are not subject to Rabbi Natan's rule while freely transferable, once they are seized during the debtor's lifetime they become effectively fixed like land for this purpose. Therefore the paper can be held as collateral even though title to the debt itself was not transferred. If she instead claims outright purchase, the paper still belongs to the underlying borrower under Rif's approach and she must return it. Hence the purchase migo does not help, while witnesses proving lifetime seizure do. Rosh differs because where there are no witnesses and no present observation he allows claims such as accident, denial, or that it had already been pledged, and will be discussed below.
She would be believed through a migo. Rosh establishes the case with witnesses and present observation; otherwise she has a migo. Tosafot asks specifically why she is not believed with a migo of purchase. The migo of unavoidable accident is weaker because a person may not wish to make that claim. The purchase migo, however, seems strong. One answer is that where the paper is worth far less than the debt, a holder who knows the debt exists can say, "To me the pledge is worth my entire debt," and retain it for more than its market value. But where the very existence of the debt is known only through the holder's own migo, he cannot use "to me it is worth my entire debt" to enlarge the value beyond the pledge itself. Mordechai says that a person holding a pledge worth one sela and claiming a debt of two sela cannot collect the excess merely by saying the pledge is worth the entire debt to him, unless the debt itself is independently established. Therefore the migo of purchase would at most protect the value of the paper itself, not the whole claimed debt. This answers Tosafot's question. Where the debt is independently established, however, "to me it is worth my whole debt" can permit retention of the pledge even beyond market value. This also sheds light on the law of Shemittah. Rambam holds that a pledge worth less than the debt prevents cancellation only to its value. Yet if the creditor wishes to retain the pledge and says it is worth the entire debt to him, he should be able to hold it until full payment. The dispute at the end of Perek HaZahav, whether a pledge is acquired against the whole debt, concerns whether the borrower remains personally obligated for the whole amount when the creditor does not want the pledge and demands payment. If the creditor instead chooses to retain the pledge as subjectively worth the whole debt, he can do so. Rosh rules like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that the pledge is acquired against the whole debt for Shemittah, and therefore similarly allows full retention against heirs where the debt and lifetime seizure are independently known. His ruling concerning an ordinary encumbered field, where one cannot say, "To me it is worth the whole debt," is not contrary. Rabbi Yitzchak's special law of acquiring a pledge applies to movable collateral, not ordinary land security.
Or that it never happened. This is why witnesses and present observation are required. Even seeing the instrument now removes the migo of unavoidable accident, and because the creditor's name is written in it the holder cannot deny that it originated with that creditor. A purchase claim is also unavailable under the Mechaber's view that delivery without writing does not transfer even the paper. The Sema therefore suggests that if the document is now seen in the holder's possession but there were no witnesses when it was first deposited, she might claim it had already been given to her as collateral from the outset. This is difficult. Throughout the Talmud, the claim that an object is held as collateral ordinarily requires a migo of purchase or unavoidable accident. It is not an independent claim. Raavad explicitly says in the case of the bundle of documents that once the holder admits the body of the item belongs to another, a claim of pledge requires proof unless supported by migo. Even regarding orphans' cattle and similar cases, the pledge claim is believed only because the holder could have claimed purchase. The Mechaber and Rosh here appear to allow a pledge claim more broadly, which is difficult against those sugyot. See my discussion in siman 72, seif katan 20, where I explain this matter more fully.
