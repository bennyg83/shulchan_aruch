Writing. Meaning a written assignment stating, "Acquire it and all its liens," as explained in siman 66. Even Rambam, who holds that a person may sometimes say, "I had a written assignment and lost it," agrees here that he is not believed, because witnesses establish that the documents originally came into his possession merely for safekeeping. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that if the holder could have claimed outright ownership without needing a written assignment, as with the transferable promissory notes common in his time, he is believed to say, "I seized them as collateral," through a migo that he could have claimed purchase. This is clearly implied by all the poskim, contrary to the Bach. See Responsa Maharam Alshich at the beginning of siman 4.
Witnesses. The Ran writes that according to the Rif, even where there are no witnesses to the deposit but the original owner saw the documents in the holder's possession, the holder is not believed even merely to retain the physical documents as collateral. Tosafot and other poskim likewise clearly hold this way. The Shach is surprised that the Mechaber did not cite them. The Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber ask why the holder should not be believed through a migo that he could have claimed the documents were lost through unavoidable accident, that he never received them, or that he returned them. The Shach answers that with such claims he could not then display and use the documents, and would lose any benefit from them. Alternatively, perhaps such a migo does not operate with documents because without written assignment he cannot sell them: no purchaser would buy instruments written in other people's names when he cannot establish ownership. If he sold them, that very act might reveal that the documents remained in his possession and had not been lost. Therefore he claims to hold them as collateral, hoping either to prevail legally and collect them, or that the debtors will pay him because they do not care to whom payment is made. If he claimed he never received them or that they were lost, the debtors would not later pay him once they learned he had lied. Nor can we simply assume he could conspire with them, because there is no certainty they would cooperate. These distinctions matter where it is not otherwise known that the depositor truly owes him money and therefore no ordinary migo from possession of the documents exists. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 130, and Mabit, vol. 2, end of siman 129 and siman 289. Shach.
That they were lost by accident. The Sema explains that witnesses would not be needed to show that the depositor demanded the documents during his lifetime. The holder would have a migo. Even though on the hypothetical claim of accidental loss he could not display the documents publicly, the migo could work as follows: when the heirs later sue him he answers before beit din that the documents were lost by accident, while privately telling the heirs that he still holds them and will return them when they pay their father's debt. This is why in siman 66, seif 11, where the documents must be displayed in beit din in order to collect, this migo is unavailable. See also siman 133, seif 25. The Sema says the source does not mention a migo that he returned the documents because that would be a brazen claim. If there were no witnesses to the original deposit, he would be believed through a migo that he could have said they came into his possession as collateral. The Shach responds that the Sema's statements are not correct. Even a bold migo can be valid, as explained in siman 75, seif 6; siman 81, seif 23; siman 93, seif 2; siman 361, seif 7; and many other places. The text is therefore not precise, and the same rule applies to a migo that he returned the documents, as the Bach writes. The Sema further says that where the holder claims he seized them as collateral he takes a shevuat heset, just as he would have taken heset on the claim that they were lost by accident. The Shach disagrees because heset is not administered on a doubtful claim. The Bach says that one claiming collateral must take a severe oath while holding a sacred object, analogous to siman 72, seif 17. The Shach rejects that comparison too, because here the holder cannot actually collect on the debt documents without written assignment. His possession therefore does not function like ordinary possession of collateral. Accordingly, if he could have claimed accidental loss, he would not have had to swear even heset, and likewise on the collateral claim he need not swear. This assumes he could collect from the heirs without another oath, for example because the deceased expressly trusted him. Otherwise he may have to swear through gilgul. All of this follows the Tur and Mechaber. The Shach has already concluded, however, that even with no deposit witnesses and despite visible possession, the holder is not believed through the migo of accidental loss. A practical difference remains in other cases: if he claimed he returned the documents to the heirs or never received them, some poskim would believe him through a migo of accidental loss without an oath; if he claimed he returned them to their father, all agree he is believed without heset, and certainly if he directly claims accidental loss. This is contrary to the Sema and Bach. The Shach adds that according to his ruling in siman 66, seif 39, one must nevertheless take a rabbinic oath regarding documents no longer in his possession, so even here a person claiming return to the heirs or their father may have to swear.
