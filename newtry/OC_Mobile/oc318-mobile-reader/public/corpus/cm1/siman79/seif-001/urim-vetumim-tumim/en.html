And he said, "It never happened," etc. If he said, "I never borrowed," Tosafot in Shevuot 41b, s.v. lo laviti, give two answers. According to one answer, when he says, "I never borrowed," he can indeed explain his words to mean, "I did not remain indebted through a loan, because I repaid it." Only when he says, "It never happened," can his words not be explained in that manner. According to the other answer, even when he says, "I never borrowed," he cannot reinterpret his statement, because one who makes a statement in beit din is precise and states his position clearly. The unqualified language of the poskim implies the second answer. Tosafot in Keritot likewise appear to hold this way, for their entire difficulty is from the case in which a person says, "I did not become impure," while witnesses testify that he did become impure, and we interpret his statement to mean, "I did not remain in a state of impurity, because I immersed." There the Ri Baal HaTosafot answers that with respect to bringing an offering a person is believed concerning himself to explain his words, but not with respect to another person so as to exempt himself from payment. Furthermore, Tosafot's entire question is from the case of "I did not become impure," while the Rambam in Hilchot Shegagot, chapter 11, rules in that very case that the person is not believed to say, "I did not remain in a state of impurity, because I immersed." See what the Kesef Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh write there. I discussed this at length in my novellae.
For this person has been established as a denier. This is the Rambam's language. The Raavad already objects that this reason is unnecessary, since the borrower's position is contradicted by witnesses and by his own admission. The Shach in seif katan 3 writes that the Rambam teaches that even if the borrower later claims, "I repaid," he is no longer believed at any time. The Rambam's wording does not indicate this, since nothing of the sort is mentioned. It appears to me that if the borrower seeks to impose an oath upon the lender that he was not repaid, no additional reason is needed at all. How could the lender swear that he was not repaid when the witnesses contradict him, as the Maggid Mishneh writes? Moreover, beit din is compelling the borrower to pay. Are the witnesses suspected of lying? Rather, since the borrower himself admitted that he did not pay, that earlier payment is treated as having been waived or given as a gift, as in the case where one was sued for wheat and admitted barley, which is treated as a waiver. If so, how could the lender swear, when the prior payment has legally been waived? But it appears that if the borrower says, "Let the lender swear to me that I owe him," then the lender should seemingly have to swear. If he swears that he lent the money, he is not swearing in contradiction to the witnesses. On the contrary, the witnesses support him. Nor can one object that there is no need for an oath because witnesses support him. The lender himself admits that these witnesses are false, since they testified that the borrower repaid, while the lender says he was not repaid. How can he support himself with testimony that, according to his own position, was false testimony in beit din? Even where the debtor explicitly trusted the lender as equivalent to two witnesses, and actual witnesses testify that he paid, he must nevertheless pay and may impose an oath upon the lender, because he disqualified the witnesses for purposes of this dispute. All the more so here, where according to the lender's own claim he admits that the witnesses testified falsely in beit din. Why should we not require him to swear? For this reason the Rambam gives the reason that because the borrower has been established as a denier, Chazal did not institute an oath for him. They did not institute a shevuat heset at the demand of people established by witnesses in beit din as deniers and liars. This is a significant novelty, and the Raavad's objection was therefore unnecessary.
