And he denied in beit din. This excludes a denial made outside beit din, as explained in seif 9. Rivash writes that even if the witnesses testified in another beit din, the defendant becomes established as a false denier.More than one hundred witnesses. The Tur and Rambam do not say 'more than one hundred witnesses'; rather, they say that a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses.
The point is that although two witnesses who say he repaid are equivalent to one hundred, his admission that he did not borrow necessarily includes an admission that he did not repay. We set the testimony of repayment against his own admission and remove both, leaving the testimony that he borrowed, against which he was established as a false denier. Rama may have written 'more than one hundred witnesses' because of the later case where, after paying, he is sued again and says, 'I paid you twice,' and no hesset oath is imposed. Rama appears to understand that his prior admission that he had not repaid is stronger than the contrary testimony and weakens the new claim.
In my view that additional reason is unnecessary. The Sages instituted hesset because of the presumption that a person does not sue one who owes him nothing. Here the claimant's later demand that he was paid twice is weakened by his own previous admission in beit din.For he has been established as a false denier. This differs from an ordinary person suspected of false oaths, whose opponent does not collect without an oath. There the person was disqualified through another matter; here he was established as a false denier concerning this very money.We do not impose a hesset oath upon him, etc.
The Levush explains this because he was established as a false denier concerning this money. That is not precise. Even without that status, no oath is imposed because he already admitted that he had not paid initially. One who says, 'I never borrowed,' is treated as saying, 'I did not repay,' and the claimant cannot now assert that he had already paid then.Likewise if he produced his handwriting, etc.
The first ruling teaches a greater novelty: even where witnesses say he borrowed and repaid, he must still pay because his own statement prevents reliance on the repayment testimony. Here there is no testimony of repayment. Nevertheless, Rambam teaches that even through a handwritten obligation he can become established as a false denier. The Mechaber repeats this law later in seif 7 for the reason explained there.
