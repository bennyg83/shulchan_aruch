Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 78, discusses this and concludes that, since the Shach has ruled that one is not believed to claim payment against a written judgment, and since the prevailing custom is to issue written judgments and not to accept a claim of payment against them, certainly where the judgment itself contains a clause of credibility he would permit collection even from purchasers. He nevertheless leaves one point requiring analysis. Suppose a judgment states that the defendant must swear within a specified period and thereby be exempt, but if he fails to swear within that period he must pay a fixed sum without delay. If he does not swear, may the claimant collect from purchasers? Even if the defendant objected during the stated period, purchasers might argue that the principal judgment concerned the oath, and they assumed he would swear. Or perhaps they were obligated to consider the possibility that he would not. At first sight this resembles the responsum of the Rosh cited in siman 61, but there the underlying debt was already definite. Chavot Yair therefore favors the first view, that such a judgment does not enable collection from purchasers. He adds that even in a community with an enactment that no debt instrument or kinyan is effective unless witnessed by specifically designated witnesses, a formal court judgment remains effective. Such communal enactments are generally made to prevent fraud or to protect a wife’s ketubah from business debts, and they are not directed at the uncommon case of a judicial judgment. A judgment signed by judges is therefore treated, for this purpose, like a document attested by the designated witnesses. If the judgment predates the wife’s ketubah or the purchasers’ acquisition, it has priority according to ordinary law and may be collected from them.
