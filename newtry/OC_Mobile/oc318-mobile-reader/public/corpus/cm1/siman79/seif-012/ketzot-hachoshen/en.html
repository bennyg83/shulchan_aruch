What its market price was. It appears that the defendant becomes established as a false denier only regarding the unpaid balance. For example, if he owed one thousand gold coins and claims, "I repaid you with one hundred measures of wheat, each worth ten gold coins," but witnesses establish that each measure was worth only six, he must pay four hundred. As to the six hundred actually represented by the wheat, however, he has not been established as a false denier regarding a different sum. Yet the Tur's language does not sound this way. The Tur says that if he claims payment with merchandise worth a specified amount, and witnesses establish that it was worth less, and the creditor claims the difference, then if the debtor returns and says, "I later paid the difference, either with that merchandise or something else," he is established as a false denier because people pay attention to and remember market values. The Tur adds that if the creditor admits receiving the merchandise but says it did not satisfy the whole debt because it was worth less, the debtor is not believed to say he later paid the remainder, and the creditor collects the balance without an oath. This wording suggests that the creditor's admission matters specifically to exempt the debtor as to the amount actually covered by the merchandise. If the creditor did not admit receiving it, perhaps the debtor would have to pay the entire debt. Why, though, should that be, since he was established as a false denier only as to the shortfall? The Mechaber likewise does not make the creditor's admission a condition for limiting the remaining liability to the difference. Perhaps the Tur mentions the creditor's admission only to show that the borrower is exempt even without an oath as to the amount represented by the merchandise; without that admission, the borrower would at least need an oath regarding that portion.
That they sent for him. The Sema writes that the requirement that beit din have summoned him applies only to writing a document of admission or a written ruling, but for collection from purchasers the judgment is treated like a written loan even without a summons. He attributes this to the Tur in the name of Tosafot. The Shach disagrees and holds that a judicial act acquires publicity only where they actually summoned him. This appears correct from Tosafot at the beginning of Perek HaSholeach. The Mishnah says that originally a husband could convene a beit din elsewhere and annul a get, until Rabban Gamliel enacted that this not be done for the sake of social order. The Gemara explains that Rabbi Yochanan feared illegitimacy and Reish Lakish feared abandoned wives. Rabbi Yochanan follows the view that annulment can be before two and therefore lacks publicity; Reish Lakish follows the view requiring three and therefore says three have publicity. Tosafot asks that an oral loan does not collect from encumbered property even if made before many witnesses, and answers that one who borrows ordinarily does so privately. Rashba says the same. If every proceeding before a panel of three automatically had the publicity of a judicial act, Tosafot's question would not arise, because the Mishnah itself speaks of a beit din. Evidently even a proceeding before three is not treated as a public judicial act unless the defendant was formally summoned. Although Rav Ada bar Ahavah in Perek Zeh Borer says that where one party himself gathers three it is a beit din, we do not rule that way, and therefore Tosafot's proof remains. This supports the Shach.
