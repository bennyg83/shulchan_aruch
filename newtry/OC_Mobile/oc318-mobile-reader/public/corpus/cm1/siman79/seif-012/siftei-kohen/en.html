An admission in beit din, etc. When does this apply? When he did not accept the judgment, etc. Everything through the end of the seif is Rambam’s language at the end of chapter 7 of Hilchot To’en. Rambam appears to hold that when the defendant did not submit until summoned, he may not later say “I paid,” even if he left beit din and no written ruling was issued. Mahari ibn Chaviv, Lechem Mishneh, and Gedolei Terumah understand Rambam this way. The apparent reason is that once the matter was fit to be written, it is treated as written, like a kinyan that ordinarily stands for writing. Thus, a formal act of beit din would function like a written instrument even against purchasers. Upon careful examination, however, this does not accord with the Gemara or most authorities. The Rosh writes that although standing in judgment has the force of a written loan regarding collection from purchasers because it generates publicity, the debtor may still say “I paid” unless a judicial document was actually written. Where it is known that he did not pay, or he admits nonpayment, collection may be made from purchasers. The Tur, Nimukei Yosef, Ran in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Ephraim, Baal HaIttur, Ramban, Maharam, Raavad, Baal HaTerumot, and Rabbeinu Yerucham all indicate that even though beit din was authorized to write without the debtor’s instruction, the debtor remains believed to claim payment until it was written. A kinyan is different because the Gemara concludes that an ordinary kinyan inherently stands for writing. A judicial act does not necessarily do so. Ramban explains that a judicial act is “as though written” only in the sense that beit din may write it and it creates publicity, while payment may still be claimed before the creditor leaves beit din with written proof. It is difficult to reinterpret Rambam as merely assuming that one who resisted judgment certainly asked for a written ruling. Rambam instead contrasts such a case with two litigants who came voluntarily and the defendant simply answered, “Yes.” The Gemara itself does not mention such a verbal admission. Rambam evidently construed the Gemara’s voluntary case as an admission rather than an adjudicated liability, because according to his approach an actual judgment would already have the force of writing. Thus, when beit din merely records his answer, he may later claim payment after leaving. Baal HaTerumot may understand Rif differently, namely that one who had to be summoned ordinarily asks for writing, but if no writing was made he may still claim payment. Rif can also be read as concerning a defendant who resisted after judgment. In any event, the Gemara’s plain meaning appears unlike Rambam. The passage speaks of a liability imposed by beit din, not merely a voluntary “yes,” and the nearby discussion of a denier likewise concerns a judicial ruling. It is therefore surprising that the Mechaber follows Rambam against the many authorities whose reading better fits the Gemara. Mahari ibn Lev quotes Rabbeinu Yeshayah of Trani, who resolves the matter as follows. Although beit din may write without the debtor’s consent once he has been summoned, a judicial ruling does not ordinarily stand for writing, because many creditors do not ask for a document and the debtor pays immediately. If the creditor asks in the debtor’s presence that it be written, beit din writes despite the debtor’s objection. From that moment the debtor is no longer believed to say “I paid,” even before the document is physically written, because he knows it is being issued. If the creditor did not ask immediately and later returns, beit din first asks the debtor. If he claims payment, they do not write. If he does not, they may write despite his objection, but only from the later date, to avoid harming purchasers who had no notice during the interval. A kinyan is stronger because it inherently stands for writing, while a judicial act does not until the creditor expressly requests it. Rabbeinu Yeshayah’s words are correct and Mahari ibn Lev approves them. They also reconcile the Rosh, Tur, Tosafot, and the other authorities. Their references to a case “where they wrote for him” include a case where the creditor requested writing in the debtor’s presence and beit din agreed, because the essential point is that writing may occur without the debtor’s further instruction. Once beit din expressly says it will write, the matter has publicity like a kinyan, and the debtor is not believed to claim payment. This is also consistent with the rule that one who argues against a judicial act says nothing, for a formal judgment includes a ruling the creditor requested to have written. This appears the correct explanation.
When does this apply? When he did not accept upon himself, etc. The Sema says this qualification concerns only whether an admission or judicial ruling may be written. As to collection from purchasers, he says the proceeding is treated as a written instrument even if the defendant came voluntarily and nothing was written, provided he now admits nonpayment. He reads the Tur in siman 39 similarly. This is very difficult. How can one collect retroactively from purchasers where the debtor could previously have claimed payment? Even if he now admits nonpayment, we suspect collusion whenever payment was previously possible. This principle is repeated in Bava Metzia and throughout the authorities. Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch likewise rule that even where a debtor now says he did not pay, a creditor cannot collect from purchasers when the debtor had been trusted to claim payment, because of possible collusion. Rivam expressly explains that standing in judgment is treated like a written loan only when a judicial ruling was written. Ran, Rabbeinu Chananel, Rabbeinu Ephraim, and Rabbeinu Yeshayah of Trani likewise indicate that a later admission does not permit retroactive collection. The Tur’s statement in siman 39 means only that collection may be made from purchasers who acquired property after the debtor admitted before beit din that he had not paid, or where he made that admission immediately on the day of judgment while the creditor requested writing in his presence. The Bach also explains it this way. The purpose of Tosafot’s rule is that standing in judgment permits writing without the litigant’s command, not that one may collect from an earlier date despite a viable claim of payment. Rabbeinu Yeshayah adds that when writing is requested only later, the document is dated from that later time because beit din had previously generated no publicity. This may imply that even where nonpayment is independently certain, they do not write from the original date because purchasers lacked notice. Other authorities may disagree and allow collection from the original judgment date when nonpayment is conclusively established. Nimukei Yosef, citing Ramah, appears to say that where liability was adjudicated and it is established that payment did not occur, one collects from property sold after the judgment. This point requires further study. But where the only proof of nonpayment is the debtor’s present admission, it is clear that one may not collect retroactively from purchasers, contrary to the Sema and Mahari ibn Chaviv.
Therefore, if the claimant returned, etc. This is the ordinary situation: when the defendant is ready to pay, the claimant generally does not request writing; when he refuses, the claimant returns to beit din and asks for a document. The same rule applies if the claimant requested writing at the outset. Because the defendant admitted voluntarily without being summoned, beit din may not write without his instruction, as appears from Rambam and the Mechaber above in siman 39, seif 7.
