He became established as a false denier. This is very difficult. Once we see that one established as a false denier regarding a particular monetary claim is no longer believed in any exempting assertion unless he brings proof, all the more so should a thief or robber who took that very property require proof when he later says, 'I returned it.' Yet most poskim hold that one who robbed in the presence of witnesses need not return in the presence of witnesses, despite having been established as a robber regarding that very property. It appears that denial before witnesses, even if he said 'you are witnesses,' does not establish him as a false denier for that money. Only denial before beit din does, as explained in seif 9. A robber ordinarily is established as having robbed only before witnesses, not through a formal denial before beit din. He therefore retains credibility to say he returned the property. According to Rabbeinu Yonah, cited by the Tur, even one who denies before witnesses is established as a false denier. According to that view, a robber is no better and should have to prove return where the robbery itself was before witnesses. Perhaps this is the basis of the authorities who hold that one who robs before witnesses must return before witnesses, because they also regard denial before witnesses as enough to establish a person as a false denier. This would apply specifically where the robbery occurred before witnesses. If he robbed privately, there was no established false denial, and he may claim return directly even without a migo that he could have denied the robbery. This resolves the Ramah's difficulty against the view requiring a robber to return before witnesses. Ramah asks from the Mishnah in chapter HaIshah Shenitarmelah: why not teach that Rabbi Yehoshua agrees where one says, 'I robbed you and returned it,' that he is believed, but if witnesses prove the robbery he is not believed? According to what we have written, where there were no witnesses to the robbery there is no established false denial and his claim of return stands on its own, so it is not an example of the 'mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted.' From this it also appears that where a disputed object is under one person's control and another claims, 'It is mine,' and the possessor snatches it before beit din, as in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, he is not later believed to say he returned it. Once he became established as a robber before beit din, he is no better than one established as a false denier regarding that same property.
But not regarding other money. Rashba rules in a responsum that where Reuven produces several documents against Shimon and it is established that he stole some of those documents from Shimon, he is treated as a false denier regarding all the documents he produces against Shimon. The Sema cites this. The Shach objects that one established as a false denier regarding one sum does not thereby lose credibility regarding other money. He explains that Rashba follows his own view that a suspected person who produces a document loses his claim, and that according to our ruling that a suspected person does not automatically lose, Rashba's law should not apply. In my opinion the law remains valid even for us. If the debtor is alive and claims that the creditor stole additional documents from him, the debtor swears and collects under the rabbinic enactment for one who was robbed. If the debtor has died, the creditor resembles a suspected person producing a document against heirs, and he loses because the oath required against heirs was treated stringently, as explained in siman 92. Where the debtor is alive and makes a definite claim of theft, he must at least swear while holding a sacred object that these documents too were stolen, and is then exempt from the debts under the enactment for one who was robbed. Although the creditor is presently in possession of the documents, the debtor's special oath is the condition for invoking the rabbinic enactment. Until he takes it he has no benefit from that enactment. Therefore the debtor must take a formal oath, and Reuven loses his documents. This is clear.
