When is this said? When he obeys the judgment, etc. Mahari ibn Lev, in his responsa at the end of Book I, siman 123, raises a difficulty: the Tur disagrees with the Rif in one respect and with the Rosh in two. It appears from the Tur that only where the debtor does not obey the judgment may collection be made from encumbered property, because the matter becomes publicly known; but where he obeys the judgment, it does not become known. This is unlike the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, who makes no distinction between one who obeys the judgment and one who does not. The Tur also holds that where the debtor does not obey the judgment, he is not believed to say, “I paid.” This too is unlike the Rosh, for the Rosh states the rule without qualification in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and does not distinguish between one who obeys the judgment and one who does not. His words imply that even where the debtor does not obey the judgment, he is believed to say, “I paid.” A difficulty also remains against the Rosh: how could he state the matter without qualification and fail to explain that the Rif disagrees? These are Mahari ibn Lev’s words. In my humble opinion, there is no difficulty. The Tur already wrote above, in siman 39 in the name of Tosafot, that whenever an admission before beit din is established as unpaid, it has the status of a loan recorded in a document. He made no distinction there between one who obeys the judgment and one who does not. This is precisely the ruling of the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia; see there. Here, however, the Tur writes that where the debtor does not obey the judgment, even if it has not been established that he failed to pay, he is not believed, because once he has defied the court, the presumption is that he does not pay. This is the Rif’s ruling. The Tur plainly understood that the Rosh agrees with the Rif as a matter of law. The Rif merely offered two explanations of the case in which the litigant “stood in judgment”: either he did not obey the judgment, or it was established that he had not paid. The Rosh selected the latter explanation, that it had been established that he had not paid, because he did not understand the phrase “stood in judgment” to refer to one who refused to obey. Nevertheless, as a matter of law, he agrees with the Rif. There is clear proof of this. In the first chapter of Bava Batra, concerning the rule that one is presumed to have paid until the claimant proves that he did not, the Rosh writes: “It appears to me as Rabbenu Yitzchak wrote, such as where witnesses came and said that he was with us, etc.; alternatively, where he stood in judgment, beit din obligated him, and he refused the court’s directive, he is presumed not to have paid.” The Tur likewise rules below, siman 157, seif 25. There is no doubt that these words of the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Batra escaped Mahari ibn Lev’s attention. The Ramban too, in the chapter Chezkat HaBatim, regarding the discussion on page 40 whether a kinyan has the status of an act of beit din, first gives the Rosh’s explanation and then states the Rif’s rule that one who does not obey the judgment is not believed to say, “I paid.” This indicates that the Rif’s ruling is correct as a matter of law. This is the principal ruling.
And they placed him under a ban, etc. The Sema writes that the wording is exact, meaning that they actually placed him under a ban. The Bach, however, writes that this is not exact; the ordinary course of events was merely stated. See there. It appears to me that the Bach is correct. The Tur and the Mechaber write below, siman 157, at the end of seif 8, “He refused to carry out the directive of beit din,” without mentioning that he was placed under a ban. The language of the Rosh cited above, and that of Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 27, part 3, imply this even more clearly; see there. Moreover, the Rosh, the Tur, and Rabbenu Yerucham found it necessary to say there that if he was sued in the presence of witnesses, refused, and did not appear for judgment, he may later claim, “I reconsidered and paid,” because he did not refuse a directive of beit din. This proves that an actual ban is unnecessary. Otherwise, it would be sufficient to say that in the presence of witnesses he certainly had not yet been placed under a ban. Baal HaTerumot, Gate 12, likewise implies that the Raavad agrees with the Rif, and the Rif, in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, does not mention a ban. Rather, once he does not obey the judgment, he is no longer believed to say, “I paid.” This is also implied by the Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of the first chapter of Bava Kamma, concerning the statement that all of them entered in place of the owners, and the objection that damages are merely an oral debt. He answers in the name of the Ramah: “Alternatively, they obligated him to pay and he did not accept the judgment upon himself, thereby revealing that he did not intend to pay.” The Beit Yosef cites this below, siman 119, seif 4, and no ban is mentioned. This is the principal ruling.
