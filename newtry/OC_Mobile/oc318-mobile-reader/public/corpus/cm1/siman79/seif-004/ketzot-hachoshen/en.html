That I received a maneh from you. Shevuot discusses one who says, "I counted a maneh to you before so and so," while witnesses observed from outside. Rav Hamnuna asks what the recipient claims. If he says, "It never happened," the witnesses make him a false denier. If he says, "Yes, I took it, but I took what was mine," the witnesses do not defeat him, because they do not know whether the money was a loan, gift, or repayment of an earlier debt. At first this seems unlike objects ordinarily lent or rented. Where witnesses see such an object transferred without knowing whether it was a gift or loan, we ordinarily preserve the original owner's title and treat it as lent. Money differs because money is given for expenditure. Even if given as a loan, the specific coins immediately leave the original owner's ownership. There is therefore no continuing presumption of title in those coins. The recipient can consequently say the money was a gift or was received on account of some existing claim. The ordinary owner presumption governing an identifiable object does not apply.
And they heard that he gave it as a loan. If witnesses actually saw the physical delivery and heard that both sides treated it as a loan, the recipient cannot afterward keep the money on a claim that the giver owed him some separate debt while denying any such debt existed. A person cannot seize another's property merely on his own unsupported claim against a person who denies owing him. The Shach therefore correctly explains this seif as one where the concealed witnesses did not actually see the money pass from hand to hand. They heard only the conversation from outside. In that setting the recipient can explain his earlier words, "It is a loan," by saying he spoke that way only because otherwise he could not obtain payment of what was truly owed to him. His explanation qualifies only his own verbal admission. If witnesses actually saw the money transferred as a loan and saw the recipient accept it under that description, his later explanation cannot convert the loan itself into seizure for a different debt. This resolves Knesset HaGedolah's difficulty with the Tur. The Tur requires the concealed witnesses to hear that the money was being given as a loan because in the Tur's case they did not see the transaction itself. Without hearing anything, there would be no testimony at all. By contrast, in the first clause the witnesses actually saw the counting of money, so even without hearing the legal characterization they can defeat a total denial. Beit Yosef's statement that if witnesses testify the money was given as a loan, the recipient cannot say, "I took what was mine," refers to a case where they observed the actual delivery. It does not contradict the Tur's case, which concerns only overheard words. Accordingly, approaches that establish the Tur's case as one where witnesses saw an actual loan and yet the recipient can say, "I was forced to call it a loan," are difficult. That would undermine every witnessed loan, because any borrower could say he accepted the money as repayment of a separate claim and merely used loan language to obtain it. The proper rule is the Shach's distinction.
