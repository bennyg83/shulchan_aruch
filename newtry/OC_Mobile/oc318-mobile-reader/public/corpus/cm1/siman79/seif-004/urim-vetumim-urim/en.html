In the presence of so and so and so and so, etc. See Tumim. The wording is not literal. It is enough that the lender said, "Repay me in the presence of witnesses," because that should cause the borrower to pay attention to the matter. If witnesses were not mentioned at all, he ordinarily does not focus on the details of an event that was never a loan in the first place. So write Maharshal and Sema, and this is correct.
And witnesses came, etc. See Tumim. Maharshal holds that this applies specifically where the recipient knew witnesses were present. If he did not know of them at all and never saw them, he can say, "I paid no attention because I did not know there were witnesses." From this I wrote that if only one witness comes and the recipient contradicts him, the witness cannot impose an oath, because even if the recipient admitted the witness's factual observation, no monetary obligation would necessarily follow, since he could say that the money was a gift. Likewise, two witnesses who observed the event separately and successively do not combine, unlike separate witnesses to successive loans. I nevertheless left this ruling requiring study. If both witnesses were simultaneously present but one saw from one window and one from another, they do combine.
And if he later claims, etc. The Bach writes that this applies only where the claimant said, "I counted out a maneh to you." If he said, "I lent you a maneh," the recipient can answer, "Why should I have focused on the matter? You called it a loan, but in fact it was a gift." The Shach writes likewise. See Tumim. The language of the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber does not support this distinction, and I see no reason for it. See there.
He is certainly established as one who falsely denied. Although below in siman 81 a person who responds, "It never happened," to a mere verbal admission is not necessarily established as a false denier, there no concrete act occurred and people do not remember idle words. Here, however, witnesses saw money physically given to him, so he should remember the event itself. Sema and Shach. The formulation that he is established as a false denier when he says "it never happened" after witnesses heard the money described as a loan is puzzling, because earlier we said that even where witnesses saw only the transfer of money, his denial may establish him as false. We must therefore explain, as Maharshal does, that if he did not know witnesses were present he is not ordinarily established as a false denier unless the witnesses testify that they actually heard the money given as a loan. Knesset HaGedolah likewise writes that in a concealed witnessing arrangement they must hear that the money was given as a loan. One may reject the inference because the stronger formulation is needed for the concluding case, where even if they heard the money described as a loan the recipient may still give a valid explanation. I already wrote in Tumim that Maharshal's ruling requires study.
But if he claimed, etc. The Shach holds that an explanation helps only where the witnesses did not actually see a loan, but if they saw it, even through concealment, no explanation can help. See Tumim. Baal HaTerumot proves otherwise. If the recipient dies, however, we do not raise for the heirs the possibility that their father had some unexpressed explanation; we ordinarily retain the presumption that it was a loan. Gidulei Terumah.
