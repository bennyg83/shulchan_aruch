In the presence of so and so and so and so. The Sema and Bach write in the name of Maharshal that this applies specifically where the claimant said, "in the presence of so and so and so and so." Since the transaction was said to have occurred in their presence, the defendant should have paid attention to it and immediately told beit din, "That incident was not a loan. It was a gift," or the like. But if the claimant merely says, "I lent you one hundred there," and the defendant answers, "It never happened," and witnesses later testify that one hundred was counted out to him in their presence, the defendant may say that it was a gift. The Sema writes that this reasoning is sound. The Bach disagrees, and the Shach likewise appears to disagree. In my opinion this law requires study. Certainly Maharshal did not mean that the claimant must literally identify the witnesses by name. The Gemara speaks of witnesses standing outside, and Rashi explains that the defendant did not recognize them, yet he is nevertheless established as a denier. Rather, Maharshal means that the claimant specifically mentions that witnesses were present. Once the defendant hears that there were witnesses, and he himself knows that witnesses were present at that encounter, he should direct his attention to the incident. But if the claimant merely says, "I lent you one hundred," why should the defendant direct his attention to an event that was not a loan at all? What more should he answer than, "It never happened. You never lent me anything"? Must he act like a peddler and recount every transaction they ever had and every gift or repayment he ever received from the claimant? That is unreasonable. Rashi therefore precisely writes that the witnesses were "outside and the defendant did not recognize them," meaning that the defendant knew witnesses were there but did not recognize who they were. Rashi does not explain that the defendant was entirely unaware of their presence. He certainly knew witnesses were there, only not who they were. At minimum, he knew there were witnesses at the encounter, and when the claimant mentions having lent him money before witnesses he should consider that perhaps the claimant refers to that event. One can go even further. If the claimant says, "I counted out one hundred to you," the defendant says, "It never happened," and a single witness contradicts him, there may be no oath obligation at all, because a person does not carefully consider and retain a transaction on the basis of one witness. Therefore, if two separate witnesses testified one after the other to such an event, they would not combine, and the defendant would not be established as a denier. This differs from one loan after another, because where two witnesses were not present at the transaction a person does not direct his attention to remembering it in this manner, and therefore his denial does not establish him as a denier. There is also a proof from the Raavad cited in Baal HaTerumot, gate 11, part 2, section 2. The Raavad asks why we need the rule that separate testimony is valid in monetary cases, since even witnesses to one loan after another can combine. He answers that the rule is needed for a case where one says, "I counted out one hundred to you in the presence of witnesses," and the defendant says, "It never happened," while witnesses testify that they saw the transfer, even if one saw it from one window and one from another window. They combine. The question remains: even in such a case, why should witnesses who saw it one after the other not combine, just as with one loan after another? What novelty is being taught? The Gidulei Terumah indeed asks this. According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. In this kind of case separate witnesses do not combine, because the defendant does not direct his attention to know and remember the event unless two witnesses were present at the transaction. Maharshal's words therefore appear compelling. However, from what I write in the next seif katan it is evident that Baal HaTerumot holds the opposite, namely that even where witnesses were concealed and the defendant did not know of their presence, if he says, "It never happened," he is established as a denier. Therefore this law requires study.
Now, the Bach seeks to distinguish between a claim, "I counted out one hundred to you," where the defendant should consider the possibility of the transfer, and a claim, "I lent you one hundred," where he need not consider money that was never received as a loan. The Shach agrees with him. This does not appear correct. The Rambam, and likewise the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, explicitly state: "Give me the one hundred that I lent you." The Rambam is exacting in his language and would not unnecessarily alter wording upon which the law depends. Moreover, I do not understand the proposed distinction. If the claimant says, "I counted out one hundred to you," he necessarily means that the money was given as a loan. Otherwise, what is he demanding? Once he explains that it was a loan, the defendant can still say, "I did not direct my attention to a transfer that was not a loan at all." We therefore should not depart from the unqualified language of all the authorities. See Urim, seif katan 13, where I likewise explain the wording of the Tur and Mechaber precisely in accordance with Maharshal.
But if he claimed, etc. These words appear in Baal HaTerumot, gate 11, part 2, section 2. He brings proof from the Gemara's case where one says, "I counted out one hundred to you," while witnesses see the event from outside. The Gemara asks: What does the other party claim? If he says, "It never happened," he has been established as a denier. If he says, "Yes, I took the money, but I took my own money," then what difference does the arrival of witnesses make, since he already said, "Yes, I took it, but I took my own money," etc. One must understand the proof. The witnesses there do not say that the money was received as a loan. They merely saw money being counted out. It is therefore obvious that the recipient can say, "I received it as a gift," or, "I received it as repayment." But if the witnesses heard that he received it expressly as a loan, perhaps he would indeed not be believed to offer such an explanation. We must therefore say that otherwise the Gemara's question, "What does the other party claim?" is incomplete. Perhaps the original question was precisely a case in which witnesses saw him from outside and heard that the money was given as a loan, and the issue was whether the recipient could later provide an explanation. Rav Hamnuna canvasses all possible forms of the question and considers whether the defendant says, "It never happened," or, "I took my own money." Why did he not also include within the question the possibility that the witnesses heard that the money was delivered as a loan while the defendant offers an explanation? Necessarily, that possibility is already included in Rav Hamnuna's statement, "Yes, I took it, but I took my own money," and the defendant is believed. Let the witnesses have heard whatever they heard. Since the defendant did not know of the witnesses, an explanation is effective. If he knew there were witnesses but merely did not recognize who they were, it is obvious that such an explanation would not help, because otherwise every loan made before witnesses would be nullified. According to this, it is proven that even if the witnesses actually saw the transfer and heard that the recipient accepted the money as a loan, he may nevertheless be believed where he can provide a plausible explanation. The Gemara explicitly describes witnesses seeing him from outside, and the Raavad writes that separate testimony, one witness seeing from one window and another from another window, is valid. This shows that actual sight of the transaction is required. The same implication follows from Baal HaTerumot's proof that concealed witnesses do not prevent the defendant from being believed with an explanation, based on the rule that witnesses are concealed only for an inciter to idolatry. In the case of an inciter, the witnesses must see the person, as Sanhedrin 67a says, "They see him and hear his voice." Rashi explains that if they do not see him, even though they hear his voice they cannot testify against him. It follows that in our case, even if the witnesses actually saw him, their concealment does not prevent his explanation from being accepted. This is contrary to the Shach in seif katan 13, who writes that the rule applies only where the witnesses did not see the loan with their own eyes but merely heard that money was given as a loan. According to him, if they actually saw the loan, the defendant cannot make any alternative claim. The opposite is evident from every proof brought by Baal HaTerumot. The Shach's proof from siman 81, seif 12, that whenever witnesses saw a loan they may testify to it even if they were concealed, concerns only the defendant's inability to say, "It never happened," or a case in which he has no plausible explanation. Where he does have a plausible explanation, he is believed. The Gidulei Terumah therefore correctly concludes as a matter of law that we do not advance for heirs the speculative claim that had their father been alive he might have provided an explanation. In the ordinary course, the money is presumed to have been a loan, and witnesses therefore testify to it without qualification, as the Shulchan Aruch writes. But if the defendant himself supplies a plausible explanation, he is believed. According to this, since Baal HaTerumot holds that in the case of witnesses seeing him from outside the defendant may say, "I took my own money," even though the transfer was a loan, because the witnesses were concealed, it follows that "witnesses seeing him from outside" means concealed witnesses. Nevertheless, if the defendant says, "It never happened," he is established as a denier. This contradicts Maharshal's rule discussed above. It is strained to say that although the witnesses were initially concealed, their presence later became known to the defendant, as might be implied by the expression, "in the presence of so and so and so and so." Such an explanation is difficult.
