The reason a litigant may change from one exempting claim to another exempting claim is not the law of migo. Whenever a person seeks to retract his original words, he is not believed merely because he has a migo, as explained in Responsa Rivash, siman 193, and in Knesset HaGedolah, Rules of Migo, siman 133. Rather, the reason is that one who knows he is exempt does not necessarily formulate with precision every argument that could exempt him. We therefore treat the initial exempting formulation as though it had not been stated with binding precision. See there.
If he says, "I paid you afterward," he is believed. This means that he claims to have paid after the matter was litigated. For example, the creditor demanded payment before witnesses in Nisan, the debtor denied the debt and said that it never occurred, and in Tishrei the creditor brought witnesses before beit din and beit din obligated the debtor to pay. Before beit din itself the debtor did not repeat his denial. If in Cheshvan he then claims that he paid after the judgment, he is believed. But if he claims that he paid in Tammuz, before the judgment was entered, he is not believed. See what I wrote above in siman 72, seif 18, in my novellae there. Baal HaTerumot states this explicitly: if he claims that he paid afterward, after they had fully litigated the matter, he is believed. This is precisely the distinction stated here.
