Two. According to all opinions, contradiction by a single witness does not make a person an established false denier. Nevertheless, under the Rambam a single witness can create the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, as stated in siman 75, seif 14; the Rashba disagrees. The Tur here says that even when one witness contradicts him, the defendant can swear and be exempt, following the Rashba. The Sema's teacher objects that this would make the Tur inconsistently adopt two positions. He distinguishes that the witness here arrives only after the defendant has changed his claim to "I paid," so the change is from one exempting defense to another. The Bach agrees. The Shach adds that even if the witness came before the second claim, the Tur's present point may not concern the inability-to-swear rule at all, but only that a single witness does not establish the defendant as a false denier. If he later claims payment, he remains believed. The Tur's language about taking a heset that he paid or does not know should be read as applying to ordinary cases where he is not contradicting the witness. This also resolves the Bach's question regarding a claim of uncertainty against one witness.
To exempt him. The wording implies that if the defendant first says, "I do not know whether I paid," and is therefore liable under siman 75, he can no longer change to "I paid." This seems difficult because the Rama there allows him to say later, "I remembered that I paid," upon oath. The Shach distinguishes that there he claims his original uncertainty was genuine and that he later remembered. Here he is saying that his first claim was false. See simanim 80 and 146.
Who saw. The Sema says the Tur already records this at the end of siman 80 in the name of Ri Migash and Rambam, and wonders why Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite it as though newly found in a manuscript. The Shach answers that the Tur's wording can be understood differently, perhaps even where witnesses arrived only after the second claim and then contradicted it. The manuscript is explicit that the litigant actually saw witnesses approaching. Moreover, the Shach notes elsewhere that Responsa Rosh and Rabbeinu Yeruham disagree with that manuscript, so the Rama's ruling itself requires study.
Publicized. Darkei Moshe did not distinguish whether the rumor was widely publicized, and the Shach initially sees little logic in such a distinction. This is unlike siman 72, where a migo of return may be weakened by a public rumor because a person may fear asserting a claim contradicted by public knowledge. Here the defendant already made the first claim. Still, the Shach suggests that a change from one exempting claim to another is itself a weak form of pleading and is accepted only through the migo that he could have remained with the first claim. If the rumor is public, perhaps he would not have wanted to remain with that claim, so the migo disappears. Even so, the law requires study because a public rumor alone cannot extract money, and since he already made the first claim despite the rumor, it may still serve as a migo.
Believed. This demonstrates a difference between an admission made with "you are my witnesses" and a contradiction established in beit din. Once beit din itself establishes him as a false denier regarding this money, that status remains. The Taz adds that the Tur, citing Rabbeinu Yonah, holds that even a denial outside beit din can prevent a later payment claim. Although ordinarily a person does not reveal his full legal claims outside beit din, Rabbeinu Yonah follows his view elsewhere that an out-of-court statement intended to benefit oneself but containing an adverse implication can have the force of a hundred witnesses. Here, by saying "I never borrowed," he intended a favorable denial but thereby necessarily asserted that he never paid. The Taz therefore sees this as established law even though the Shulchan Aruch does not explicitly cite it here.
