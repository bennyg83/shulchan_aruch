He denied before beit din. This requires an actual beit din of three. An individual expert judge is insufficient for creating the formal status of an established false denier, as explained in siman 3. Even with three, at least one must be learned, because a panel lacking even one qualified person is not a beit din. Maharit argues that for admissions and the status of false denial, even three laymen none of whom is learned can suffice, because those matters do not depend upon judicial reasoning. He compares document validation, receiving testimony, annulment of vows, and chalitzah, where ordinary laymen can sometimes serve. In my view the comparison fails. Rif rules explicitly that reception of testimony requires three judges fit to adjudicate. If testimony was received by people unfit to judge, the reception is ineffective and the witnesses may testify again. The Shulchan Aruch codifies this in siman 28. Since formal reception of testimony requires a beit din fit to judge, an admission before beit din and establishment as a false denier should likewise require such a court. Annulment of vows is not proof. Rosh and Tur require knowledgeable judges there as well, and even if one follows the more lenient view, that is because annulment of vows is not ordinary adjudication at all. It can occur at night, on Shabbat, and among relatives. Chalitzah likewise rests upon its own special verse and special qualifications. Those categories cannot establish the requirements for monetary adjudication. Maharit's main analogy is to reception of testimony, saying that just as witnesses cannot retract after testifying before such a panel, a litigant cannot retract after his plea. But once Rif shows that testimony received by an unqualified panel may be repeated, the analogy supports the opposite conclusion. Therefore a litigant becomes formally established as a false denier only through a proper beit din fit to adjudicate.
When two witnesses came. The Sema cites two views where the defendant first said, "I never borrowed," one witness then says he did borrow, and he afterward changes to, "I borrowed and repaid." Is he now one obligated to swear but unable to swear? Tumim asks that according to the view that a migo can exempt from an oath, why was he ever subject to a Torah oath when he first said, "I never borrowed"? He had a migo that he could have said, "I borrowed and repaid," which would require only hesset. This is the classic question of the early authorities. Ran answers that one witness is to an oath what two witnesses are to money. Just as a migo does not overcome two witnesses for monetary liability, a migo does not overcome one witness until the defendant actually takes the oath against him. Therefore the one witness creates the Torah oath despite the possible alternative plea. That principle also resolves the present issue and does not require limiting the case to slaves, documents, or land.
Unless the rumor was public. This rule is difficult. So long as the defendant has not yet incurred liability, why should a public rumor prevent him from changing from one exempting plea to another? The Shach says that changing from one exempting plea to another is permitted only through the migo that he could have stood by the first plea. If the first plea contradicts a public rumor, he has no comfortable migo because he does not wish to continue asserting something publicly implausible. In siman 80, however, I will explain that the true reason a litigant may change from one exempting plea to another is not migo. Rather, so long as his first plea did not create liability, he may replace it with another consistent exempting position. If so, the public rumor does not itself create liability and should not prevent the change. Therefore I would not extract money from the possessor on the strength of this rule alone. The Shach himself leaves the law requiring study.
But if he says he paid afterward, he is believed. Baal HaTerumot distinguishes a denial before beit din from a denial before witnesses. A denial before witnesses, even when they were designated as witnesses, does not make the defendant formally established as a false denier regarding that money. Therefore he may later say, "I paid afterward," though he cannot say he had paid before the denial. Maharit asks from the Gemara: one first admits, "Yes, I owe you a maneh," and the next day says, "I gave it to you," he is exempt; if he says, "I owe you nothing," the Gemara interprets that as "I never borrowed," and applies the rule that one who says he never borrowed is as one who says he never repaid. Why does the Gemara need that rule? If the denial occurred before beit din, he should already be an established false denier. Maharit answers that a person is not made a false denier through his own earlier admission alone. Although the admission binds him monetarily, one does not establish himself as a dishonest denier through his own mouth. This is correct. The later denial contradicts only what he himself said earlier, not independent witness testimony. He owes the money because of his admission, but he does not thereby acquire the legal status of a false denier. There is no contradiction from Rambam's explanation of the case where the court said, "Go pay," and the defendant afterward says he paid. If witnesses later testify he did not pay, he becomes an established false denier concerning payment. Although the original debt itself may have arisen from his admission, the falsehood concerning the later payment is established by independent witnesses. The defect therefore comes from witness contradiction, not merely from his own earlier statement. This principle accords with the general rule that a person is not made a suspected wrongdoer solely through his own admission. Accordingly, denial before witnesses can still permit a later claim of subsequent payment, while a formal denial before beit din contradicted by witnesses creates the stronger bar described in siman 79.
