And two witnesses came, etc. The Sema records two views where a person first says, "I never borrowed," one witness then testifies that he did borrow, and he changes his claim to, "I repaid." Rambam, the Tur, and the ruling in siman 75 apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. Rashba disagrees and holds that the defendant takes heset that he repaid and is exempt. The Sema suggests that the Tur's ruling here, that he swears and is exempt, follows Rashba. Later authorities already objected that this would make the Tur contradict himself. There is an additional difficulty. The Tur holds that a migo can exempt from an oath. If so, even when one witness testifies that he borrowed, why would the defendant owe a Torah oath when he says, "I never borrowed"? He could have said, "I borrowed and repaid," and would have been believed with only heset. Yet we do not find that contradiction of one witness is exempt from a Torah oath through such a migo. The clearer explanation is that the Tur's main concern in this siman is whether the defendant became established as a liar. It therefore states the rule in a type of claim where the doctrine of inability to swear does not apply, such as a claim concerning documents or rent of land, where there is no Torah oath. The novelty is that even after initially saying, "That never happened," he may later swear heset that payment occurred, because he was not established as a liar. This also resolves the clause where he says, "I do not know," and is exempt. If the uncertainty were whether he repaid, he would ordinarily have to pay. If it were whether he borrowed while one witness testifies that he did, the ordinary rule would again be inability to swear. But in a category carrying no Torah oath, neither consequence follows. Even if the witness says he borrowed, the defendant who does not know remains exempt because there is no rule of payment for inability to take a Torah oath. At first I considered a different distinction: where the claimant himself knows the debt and one witness supports him, inability to swear might produce payment, while where the entire claim arises only from what the witness says and the claimant himself does not know, perhaps one would not extract money. This would explain why siman 75, where the claimant's definite claim preceded the witness, follows Rambam, while the unqualified language here could follow Rashba. But I found no clear support for such a distinction among the authorities, and the first explanation resolves all the language more naturally. As practical law, Rambam's view remains primary and the rule of inability to swear applies wherever the Torah oath itself properly applies.
Because he saw the witnesses coming. The Shach explains that the witnesses were visibly approaching and the defendant admitted only out of fear that their testimony was about to arrive. Above, the Shach says that Rosh and Rabbeinu Yerucham imply such an admission does not count as a valid admission and does not establish him as a liar. That may be their view, but practical law appears otherwise. They seem to follow Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Shimon, who says that one who admits a penalty only because he sees witnesses coming is not treated as one who voluntarily admitted. Nimukei Yosef similarly applies that view to modern penalty cases. Rif and Rambam, however, simply codify Rav's rule that one who admits a penalty and afterward witnesses come is exempt, without distinguishing fear based admissions. The anonymous Mishnah at the end of Shevuot is likewise interpreted according to the Sages who hold that even after seeing witnesses approach, the admission remains an admission for this purpose. The Gemara's effort to reconcile Rav with all opinions does not make the minority view practical law. The Tur in siman 1 discusses whether modern admission has effect in penalty matters and never invokes this special exception, which also indicates that he does not rule like Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Shimon. Maharshal rules likewise.
However. If witnesses came and testified that he borrowed, he then said, "I repaid," those first witnesses were subsequently disproved or rendered false witnesses, and afterward a second pair established that he did borrow, it appears that he is treated as one who admitted only after witnesses had already come and therefore becomes established as a liar. This parallels the dispute in Bava Kamma concerning one who admits a penalty after witnesses testified, those witnesses are later refuted, and afterward other witnesses arrive. The Sages hold that the admission is not treated as an independent confession freeing him from the penalty merely because the first testimony later fell away. Rashba explains that Rashi and Raavad rule with the Sages on this point even though they reject Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Shimon. Maharshal likewise rules this way. The same logic should apply to the present law of becoming established as a liar.
You are witnesses, etc. Below, siman 146, the Mechaber cites Rabbeinu Yonah that if outside beit din a person says, "It belonged to my fathers and not to yours," he cannot later explain in beit din, "my fathers bought it from yours," even without the formula "you are witnesses," because he sought to establish his own right and in doing so admitted a fact against himself. One might therefore ask why the same view is not cited here. When a person says, "I never borrowed," he is likewise asserting a position for his own benefit and implicitly admitting that he did not repay. The distinction is that there he said expressly, "and not to your fathers." There is no natural reason to reinterpret those words as mere imprecise speech. Here, however, the phrase "I never borrowed" can outside beit din be used loosely to mean, "I do not remain indebted." Tosafos itself asks why the defendant cannot explain that way and answers that in beit din a person speaks precisely. Outside beit din he need not speak with that precision. Therefore, unless he said "you are witnesses" or otherwise formalized the statement, the language does not conclusively admit nonpayment. This resolves the Taz's question.
