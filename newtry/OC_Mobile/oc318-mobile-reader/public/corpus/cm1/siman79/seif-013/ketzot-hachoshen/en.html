When he becomes obligated in an oath before beit din. It appears that even if beit din gave him a written ruling requiring him to swear, he remains believed to say, "I swore." Even according to those who say one cannot plead payment against a written judgment, that is where the judgment imposes a monetary obligation and property lien, so a person would not leave it outstanding in another's hand after payment. A writing that merely records an oath obligation carries no property lien. Indeed, several authorities allow a claim of payment against the borrower's own handwriting because it does not collect from encumbered property. All the more so a writing recording only an oath obligation should not be something a person carefully retrieves after performance. Rambam's position proves this. He holds that once a person is obligated by beit din he cannot say, "I paid," even if no written judgment exists. Yet the Gemara says that one who leaves beit din and later says, "I swore," is believed, and if witnesses prove he did not swear he becomes established as a false denier. This must involve an oath obligation imposed by beit din, because one who merely volunteered an oath would not become a false denier. Thus an oath obligation differs from a monetary judgment even when imposed by beit din. Chacham Tzvi discusses one who held a written ruling giving him the right to choose one of several houses, and after another house fell he claimed the claimant had already chosen that house. He ruled the claim unbelievable because the written ruling remained in the claimant's hands. According to what I have written, that proof is not decisive, because a ruling about an election rather than a monetary lien may not be something one insists on retrieving after making the choice. Independently, however, the person claiming that the election was already made bears the burden of proof. The house remains under its original ownership presumption, and seizure does not help with land.
