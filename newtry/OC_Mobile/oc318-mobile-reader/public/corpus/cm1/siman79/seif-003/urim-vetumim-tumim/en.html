But if the witnesses came first, etc. This is what the Nimukei Yosef writes to answer the Ran's difficulty with the Rambam from the beginning of Bava Metzia, where the Gemara says that one's own admission differs because it is not subject to contradiction or hazamah. Rashi explains that if a person says, "I owe you one hundred," even if witnesses contradict him and say, "You owe him nothing," he nevertheless remains obligated. The difficulty is: what exactly is the case? If even after the witnesses contradict him he continues to maintain his admission, then he is simply giving the claimant a gift. If he retracts in accordance with the witnesses and says, "I remembered," then according to the Rambam's own rule this retraction is effective. To answer this, the Nimukei Yosef distinguishes between a case in which he said, "I remembered," before the witnesses came and a case in which he said so only afterward. However, the Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot To'en, chapter 4, writes that the Rambam was troubled by the case where the borrower says that half the debt was repaid and witnesses testify that all of it was repaid. Why can he not say, "I remembered," etc.? This shows that the Maggid Mishneh does not accept the Nimukei Yosef's distinction, because otherwise there would be no question, since there the borrower did not say, "I remembered," until after the witnesses came. The Shach likewise writes that the Maggid Mishneh disagrees. The Maggid Mishneh's position is more reasonable, because if no witnesses came at all and the borrower merely said, "I remembered that I repaid," he would not be believed. His entire credibility here derives from the witnesses. Why then should his credibility become weaker merely because he says this after they arrive, when those very witnesses are the sole basis of his credibility? Furthermore, are the witnesses, God forbid, suspected of lying? We merely say that his own admission creates liability against him, but he now says, "I remembered." The Sema seeks to explain that perhaps the witnesses were speaking about a different payment. This has no basis. If so, even if he said, "I remembered," before the witnesses came, perhaps the witnesses later spoke about another matter, and his own statement would not be credible without the witnesses. Moreover, the witnesses explicitly testified concerning this very debt. How can we divert their testimony to a different matter in order to extract money? The Nimukei Yosef's entire rule rests on the above difficulty, and the Ritva already addresses it in Bava Metzia and gives a strained answer. The Shitah Mekubetzet cites an answer in the name of the Tashbetz that the case indeed concerns one who continues to maintain his admission. If one asks that this is simply a gift, the case may concern one who transferred all his property to another person, so that he cannot now make a gift, and he is obligated only by force of his admission. See there. In any event, the Tashbetz does not accept the Nimukei Yosef's distinction. In my opinion there is no difficulty at all. If a person says, "I owe you," and witnesses testify that he already paid, one can readily say that he forgot the payment, because forgetfulness is common. But if he says, "I borrowed today," and witnesses testify that he did not borrow that day because he was with them and never left their presence, then if he retracts and says, "The truth is as the witnesses say," even the Rambam would hold that he is not believed. What role can forgetfulness play in causing a person to say that an event occurred and that he borrowed, when no such event ever occurred and he did not borrow at all? Such forgetfulness is not common. In the case of a hired worker, Chazal say that an employer is preoccupied with his workers and may forget whether he paid, but he certainly remembers the amount originally fixed, because that is the beginning of the obligation and is not something that preoccupation causes him to forget. All the more so, he would not imagine that he borrowed when no borrowing ever occurred. The Gemara's statement, "What is unique about his own admission is that it is not subject to contradiction or hazamah," is therefore entirely correct. It concerns a case in which he says, "I borrowed," and witnesses say, "He did not borrow." See the Shitah Mekubetzet, which cites Rabbenu Chananel explaining why hazamah does not apply to one's own admission: he says, "On the first of Nisan I borrowed one hundred from Reuven in Lod," and witnesses testify that on that day he was with them in Jerusalem. The same applies to contradiction, where they say, "You never left our presence and you did not borrow." In such a case, even if he subsequently adopts the witnesses' account, forgetfulness cannot explain his original statement. The Gemara is therefore clear, the Rambam's words align with Rabbenu Chananel, and the use of the term hazamah is precise. Consequently, since most commentators do not accept the Nimukei Yosef's distinction, and it is independently unpersuasive, it is obvious that the borrower can say kim li in accordance with them. Even if he says, "I remembered," only after the witnesses came, he is exempt from payment. This is clear.
