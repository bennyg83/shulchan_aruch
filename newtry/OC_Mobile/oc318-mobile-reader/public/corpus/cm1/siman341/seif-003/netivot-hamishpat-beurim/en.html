For all the days of the borrowing. At first glance this is difficult according to Rambam, cited in siman 291, who treats reduction of guarding as negligence. If so, a borrower should not be permitted to hand the animal to his sons, and siman 363, seif 10, says that where one may not sublet, the rental profit belongs to the owner. If the borrower has no right during his lifetime to transfer use to others, how can his children inherit the use-right after his death? It therefore appears that the case necessarily concerns children who were dependent members of his household. Otherwise, if the children are not persons to whom the owner ordinarily lends, the lender could say: "I trusted your father to swear, but I do not trust you. Perhaps you will later falsely claim an accident. I do not wish the animal left in your hands at all." The ruling must therefore concern children who are members of his household. Anyone who lends or deposits does so with awareness of the borrower's household. During the father's lifetime he was certainly permitted to let his wife and children use the animal for his household needs, even when he was not personally present. Although their own custodial status is only that of unpaid custodians and thus the formal level of guarding is lower, the owner's deposit is understood as made on that basis. During the father's lifetime the profit from their use belonged to the father. After his death, the children inherit that benefit. Since they were permitted to retain and use the animal in his household during his life, the lender cannot object merely because the economic benefit now belongs to them.
The days of its borrowing. See Sema, seif katan 6, who asks from a recipient of an iska who dies, where the owner can remove the heirs. He answers that borrowing differs because throughout the borrowing period the object is like the borrower's own property, all benefit belongs to him, and he is liable for accidents. This is difficult. An iska recipient's half-loan portion is certainly also like his own property, with all benefit belonging to him and accident liability upon him, yet the owner can remove the heirs even from that half. The distinction is simpler. During the borrower's lifetime he had permission to allow his household members to use the borrowed animal for his purposes, as explained above. Therefore after his death the lender cannot remove those household heirs from use during the remaining term. An iska is different. An iska recipient must keep the capital available and may not use it for his own beverages or allow another person to trade with it, as explained in siman 176. Since even during his lifetime he could not turn the enterprise over to his children, after his death the owner can remove them from continuing to trade with it.
