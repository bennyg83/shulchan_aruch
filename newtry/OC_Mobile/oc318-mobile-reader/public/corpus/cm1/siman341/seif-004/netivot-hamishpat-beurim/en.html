And they slaughtered it. See Sema, seif katan 13, who says the father should have informed his heirs. There is a major practical difference between Rambam's reason and this reason. According to Rambam, the father had already incurred borrower liability from the time of meshichah. But with an unpaid or paid custodian whose heirs slaughtered and ate the animal, the father could not have incurred liability in the same way because his death made it impossible for him to continue guarding. There is no greater bodily accident than death. According to the reason that the father was negligent for failing to inform his heirs, even an unpaid or paid custodian could be liable. This makes the omission of the distinction by the poskim difficult, and also revives Shach's question: how can failure to warn before death be called negligence when death itself prevented him from acting? In my view it is not treated as ordinary negligence. The accident that resulted in the heirs' eating began during the father's lifetime, at the stage when he had already lost the ability to speak. Because he had not informed them earlier, the animal passed into their hands under the assumption that it belonged to him. The unavoidable chain of events thus began before his actual death. For a borrower this can preserve his accident liability. But an unpaid or paid custodian should remain exempt, even if the father left assets. Their heirs' slaughtering and eating constitutes an accident affecting the object itself, because the heirs reasonably believed it belonged to them. Even according to authorities who make a paid custodian liable where the custodian personally suffers an unavoidable bodily event, here there is also an accident in the object itself. In such a case the paid custodian is exempt, as explained in siman 310.
They pay its value from his estate. See Shach, seif katan 6, who writes that once they slaughtered it they became thieves. This is very difficult. The Gemara and Rambam state that a custodian who slaughters or sells is liable in double and fourfold or fivefold payment only after first making a false theft claim and swearing. If mere slaughter made every custodian a thief in the full statutory sense, that condition would be unnecessary. Moreover, if the slaughter occurred without an act of acquisition, it is merely damage. If it occurred through lifting or another acquisition, then the theft began at the earlier acquisition and double payment should already arise then. Shach's intent may be that where the custodian performs a bodily act of acquisition in the object for purposes of theft, such as removing it from a place designated by the owner, he can become a thief even without a prior theft claim. A shepherd who takes a lamb from the flock for himself can owe double payment, as Rambam rules. But that cannot explain Rambam's language here. Rambam writes that where a borrowed cow is slaughtered on Shabbat "by way of theft," the borrower is exempt even from double payment because the Shabbat liability and theft liability arise together, and without theft there is no slaughter or sale liability. Lechem Mishneh understands this as exemption even from principal theft liability. In my view Rambam means as follows. Because the animal was already borrowed, the borrower remains liable for the depreciation caused by slaughter under borrower law. A borrower is assessed with the broken remains. Thus he need pay only the reduction in value caused by slaughter. Full thief-liability would be different. A thief becomes liable for the entire original principal at the moment of theft, and later fluctuations in the value of the remains are governed by robbery law. That additional theft-liability arises at the moment of slaughter. But because the same act incurs the severe Shabbat liability, the rule of kam lei bid'rabbah minei removes the monetary liability that depends specifically on the theft occurring then. The borrower therefore remains liable only under the earlier borrowing relationship, namely for the depreciation caused by the slaughter, not for the entire body as a thief. Once the statutory theft element is absent, there can be no fourfold or fivefold payment for slaughter or sale, because those payments require a theft whose body is already chargeable as theft. The Gemara says this disagrees with Rav Pappa because Rav Pappa would exempt even the depreciation caused by slaughter under the broader application of kam lei bid'rabbah minei. Rambam rejects Rav Pappa only to the extent that ordinary borrower liability for the depreciation remains, while he still exempts the distinct theft liability. Rambam therefore needed the reason "there is no theft here" rather than merely saying the Shabbat violation exempts the slaughter payment. If someone else performed the slaughter as the borrower's agent, agency can exist for the special statutory act of slaughter. But ordinary theft itself has no agency for a transgression. Without a theft attributable to the borrower there can be no fourfold or fivefold payment.
