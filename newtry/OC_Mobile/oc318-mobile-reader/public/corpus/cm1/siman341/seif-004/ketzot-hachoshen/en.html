And they shall pay the entire value of the hide. The Sema explains that the heirs have a choice: if they wish, they may retain the hide and pay its value to the owner, because they acquired it through the change created by slaughter and skinning; and if they wish, they may return the hide itself. In my opinion this is incorrect. We find acquisition through change only with a thief or robber. These heirs did not act as thieves or robbers; they slaughtered inadvertently, thinking the animal belonged to their father. The hide therefore remains entirely the owner's if he wants it. Indeed, if they slaughtered the animal but had not yet eaten the meat, the owner could take all the meat itself, because a physical change does not create ownership in this case. The Shulchan Aruch's wording that they 'pay the entire hide' is therefore imprecise, or else it speaks where the hide is no longer present, such as where they sold it, and they must pay the full amount they received for it. This is simple and clear.
And if he left them property. The Gemara in chapter Eizehu Neshech and in chapter HaGozel states: if a borrowed cow died while in the heirs' possession, they are not liable for unavoidable accidents; if they slaughtered and ate it, they pay the reduced value of the meat. If their father left them property subject to collection, they must pay. One version applies this final clause to the first case, unlike Rav Pappa; another applies it to the latter case, in accordance with Rav Pappa. Rav Pappa's position is that one who had borrowed a cow and slaughtered it on Shabbos is exempt from monetary payment, which seemingly requires saying that a bailee's liability becomes actual only at the later event. Rambam rules in chapter 1 of Hilchot She'eilah that if the father left property and the cow died or the heirs slaughtered it, its value is collected from the father's property. This appears not to follow Rav Pappa. Yet in chapter 3 of Hilchot Geneivah Rambam rules that one who slaughtered a borrowed cow on Shabbos is exempt, apparently following Rav Pappa. Maggid Mishneh discusses the difficulty. Kesef Mishneh answers that Rambam did not have the textual reading that makes the rulings dispute Rav Pappa. Rav Pappa's statement that liability applies at the time of slaughter does not mean that no liability whatsoever existed from the time of borrowing. Rather, from the moment of borrowing there was a conditional potential liability: perhaps no unavoidable accident would occur, but if one did occur the borrower would then owe. Thus the potential obligation begins at borrowing, and the father's property became conditionally encumbered from that time. If the cow later dies while with the heirs, the condition is fulfilled and the property is collected retroactively. In my opinion I have not understood this explanation. If even death in the heirs' hands activates a conditional obligation beginning from the father's borrowing, despite the fact that after death there is no new personal obligation upon the father, then slaughter on Shabbos should likewise activate the same earlier conditional obligation. We would not be charging him for the slaughter on Shabbos, but rather enforcing the obligation that began at the time of borrowing once its condition was fulfilled. If one says that because on Shabbos there can be no actual monetary liability, the condition does not activate, because the original undertaking was only to become liable retroactively if the accident occurred at a time when liability could attach, the same should apply where the cow died after the borrower's death, for a dead person can incur no new personal liability. In short, I do not understand the distinction between slaughter on Shabbos and slaughter by the heirs. Mishneh LaMelech also follows Kesef Mishneh's path, but I do not understand it. The clearest explanation is that stated in Shitah Mekubetzet in chapter Eizehu Neshech. Rambam distinguishes between an ordinary borrowed cow and a borrowed cow that the borrower later slaughtered as an act of theft. So long as the borrower remains alive and the cow remains under the framework of borrowing, wherever it is it remains in the owner's domain and no complete monetary obligation rests upon the borrower. Rather, if it is later stolen or suffers an unavoidable accident, his property becomes retroactively encumbered from the time of borrowing. If the borrower slaughtered it on Shabbos while still acting under the framework of borrowing merely in order to pay the owner its value, one could understand the later liability as arising from the original borrowing. Likewise, where the heirs slaughtered it inadvertently, the later event can reveal retroactively the father's liability from the time of borrowing. But where the borrower himself slaughtered it as theft, his status as borrower ends and a new legal event has occurred, namely theft. We cannot say that he became retroactively liable for this theft from the time of borrowing, since the object originally came to him permissibly as borrowed property. The theft creates a new obligation only at the time of the theft. Shitah Mekubetzet develops the sugya according to this distinction. The reasoning is sound: the earlier conditional liability can relate only to negligence or unavoidable accident arising from the law of a bailee. When he changes status and becomes a thief, the earlier relationship of bailment has ended. We rule like Rabbi Akiva that once a bailee sends his hand against the deposit, the original guarding relationship ends. Thus a new liability begins and does not operate retroactively. However, Shitah Mekubetzet's suggestion that if he slaughtered merely intending to pay the owner he might still be treated under the law of borrowing is difficult. Even one who takes another's property intending to pay has the law of a thief or robber, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, siman 359. Only the heirs who slaughtered inadvertently are not thieves or robbers, and therefore the property remains under the framework of the father's bailment until it is returned.
They pay its value from his property. The Gemara specifically discusses a borrowed cow rather than an ordinary bailee. An unpaid bailee is exempt from unavoidable accidents, so if the father dies and the object is later lost through an unavoidable event, the heirs are exempt even if the father left property, even according to the version that treats the obligation as retroactive. The father's death is itself the greatest unavoidable event with respect to his ability to guard. The same applies to rented property: an unavoidable loss after the father's death should be exempt. Although chapter HaMafkid indicates that a renter can be liable for theft even where the theft occurred through a powerful external event, if the bailee himself suffers a bodily event such as illness that makes guarding impossible, he is certainly exempt. Death is the strongest possible bodily accident. With an unpaid bailee, however, if the object was lost through negligence after the father's death, perhaps the estate is liable because the father may himself have been negligent in failing to instruct his heirs that the object was a deposit, so that they would guard it properly. But if it was stolen or otherwise lost while in the heirs' hands, even where the father had been a renter or paid bailee, his death is an unavoidable accident and the estate is exempt, even under the view that a bailee becomes obligated from meshichah.
