And they slaughtered it. The wording is deliberate and follows the Rosh, excluding the Rambam's view. The Rambam holds that even if the borrowed animal died naturally in the heirs' hands they must pay, because the father's property became encumbered from the time of meshichah. The Rosh follows the other version of the Gemara and holds that the encumbrance does not attach until death or another accident occurs. By then the father had already died. If the heirs slaughtered and ate it, however, they benefited from it and the father had been negligent in failing to tell them that it was borrowed, so they must pay. They are not liable for natural death merely because the father's original negligence created the possibility that they might eat it, since that negligence did not cause the accidental death.
