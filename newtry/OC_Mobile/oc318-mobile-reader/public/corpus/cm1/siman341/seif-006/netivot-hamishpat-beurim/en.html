According to Rashi, the Gemara concerns one who says "with its goodness," meaning for as long as the object remains good, and he may use it indefinitely. Rashi further distinguishes whether there was a kinyan: with a kinyan the borrowing can revive even after the object is returned; without one, the original meshichah has ended and cannot support a later new borrowing. If so, from where can we independently create a rule concerning the phrase "with your goodness" and say that it does not mean indefinite borrowing without a kinyan? If that phrase itself naturally meant forever, ordinary meshichah should suffice. We cannot invent from our own reasoning both the linguistic meaning and the legal distinction. Therefore, according to Rashi, the law concerning "with your goodness" remains at least doubtful. The burden of proof lies on the one seeking to extend the borrowing. As to "with its goodness," however, when accompanied by a kinyan everyone should agree that it means for as long as the object remains good. Nimukei Yosef's objection to Rashi is only that even ordinary meshichah should suffice. Accordingly, without a kinyan there is a safek din. According to Rashi the original meshichah does not support renewed borrowing after return; according to Nimukei Yosef it does. With a kinyan, everyone agrees the borrower can retake the object when needed.

Even if he returned it to the lender. See Sema, seif katan 18, who says this applies specifically where he said "lend it to me with its goodness," but not "with your goodness." At first glance that is very difficult. According to Rif and Rambam, the phrase "with your goodness" creates an indefinite borrowing just as "with its goodness" does according to Rashi. Why should return operate differently? The answer depends on the different conceptual models. According to Rif, the phrase "with your goodness" by itself does not necessarily mean forever. The kinyan strengthens the wording into a continuing obligation. Once the object is lent forever in that sense, the borrower need not return it at times he does not need it. According to Rashi, "with its goodness" means that the object is lent whenever the borrower needs it, for as long as it remains good. That does not mean the object stays physically with the borrower forever. When he does not need it, he returns it to the lender; when he later needs it again, the lender must lend it again. This is proved by Rashi's requirement of a kinyan. If the borrower never had to return the object while he did not need it, then even without a kinyan the lender could not retract so long as the object remained in the borrower's hands, because the first meshichah would still be continuing. If the borrower voluntarily returned it as a complete termination of the borrowing, then even a kinyan would not revive it, just as a borrower for a fixed term who fully returns during the term can end his accident liability and need not receive it back. Therefore Rashi must mean that the ordinary arrangement itself includes return during periods of non-use, followed by renewed lending when needed. Without a kinyan, the first meshichah is exhausted and cannot support that later new borrowing. With a kinyan, the lender has a personal obligation to lend again whenever needed. This is what Sema means. Under Rif's model of "with your goodness," where the object remains continuously lent and need not be returned at all until it breaks, there is no separate rule that after return the borrower can retake it. If he returned it merely temporarily as a deposit with the lender, of course he can later reclaim it. If he returned it as a complete termination of the borrowing, he cannot reclaim it even with a kinyan. Nimukei Yosef's objection to Rashi, that the first meshichah should suffice even after return, is not compelling. Rashi regards each renewed period of use after an intervening return as a new borrowing. This resembles "draw the cow now, but it shall become yours only after thirty days," where the original act may be exhausted. A generic donkey rental differs. Rosh, cited by Sema in siman 308, says that drawing the first donkey creates a personal obligation to provide another if necessary. Rental, which is for payment, can create a broader personal obligation than a gratuitous loan.
