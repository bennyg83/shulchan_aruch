The borrower may use it forever. Nimukei Yosef cites the Ran, who is uncertain whether the lender may use the object at a time when the borrower is not using it. One can say that the lender may tell him, 'I lent it to you only for your needs. Now that you do not need it, give it to me so I may use it.' Or perhaps the borrower may say, 'Since you lent it to me for all of my uses, I may prevent you from using it, because your use may diminish or break it.' A further practical difference concerns liability for unavoidable accidents. Even though the object was lent forever, if it is stolen or lost through an unavoidable accident the borrower must pay, because he did not receive it as a gift and remains a borrower. If the lender has no right at all to use it even when the borrower does not need it, then if it is destroyed the borrower need pay only the value of the broken remnants, because while the object remained whole the lender had no usable right in it. If the lender retains a right of use and the object is stolen or destroyed, the borrower must pay according to the value of that retained right. The Tur cites this uncertainty and concludes in the name of Ramah that it is reasonable that the lender cannot take the object even when the borrower is not presently using it. Accordingly, if it is destroyed, the borrower need pay only the value of the broken remnants. See what I wrote in siman 346, seif katan 5.
