Meaning, "You are not lending it to me, etc." This is Rambam's wording. In the Derishah I cited the Beit Yosef that this interpretation removes the Rosh's objection, recorded by the Tur, against Rif. The Rosh writes: it is not clear to me, for merely because he said, "as a favor," this does not remove the transaction from the category of an ordinary unspecified borrowing. People commonly say, "Do me a favor and lend me your utensil." One can answer that Rif too understood the phrase like Rambam. The Gemara's statement that where he says, "lend it to me as a favor," he may use it indefinitely, concerns specifically a borrower who knows that the lender is a generous person who does not ordinarily insist on a fixed term. The lender then lends without a term in response to that formulation. Thus Rambam's explanation, "meaning, you are not lending it to me, etc.," means that the Gemara's phrase "lend it to me as a favor" speaks of a lender about whom the borrower can plausibly mean, "because I know that you are not like ordinary lenders who insist upon a term." That requires familiarity with the lender's habits. It is possible that the Rosh agrees and objected only because he understood Rif to say that the phrase is effective even with an ordinary person whom the borrower does not know to be lenient about time. According to this, Rama's statement, "and some say that one who says, 'lend it to me, etc.,'" need not be intended as a dispute with Rambam as cited by the Mechaber. Each authority addresses a different circumstance and they need not disagree.
And he returns its broken pieces or remnants. "Broken pieces" means literally where the utensil broke into pieces. "Remnants" means that it did not break but was crushed or worn down by extensive use until only a small part of what had been substantial remained. This is straightforward.
The borrower is not permitted, etc. If he repairs it and uses it again, he may ultimately ruin it completely. The lender lent him only this utensil as it stood. Nimukei Yosef writes this according to Rif, excluding Ran's view that under Rif's interpretation of "lend it to me as a favor," the borrower may repair it and continue using it. See the Derishah.
Even if he returned it to the lender, etc. This applies specifically where he said, "lend it to me through the favor of the utensil," meaning on the basis of the utensil's continued usability. If he said, "lend it to me as a favor from you," this does not apply. The Tur concludes that if the lender wishes to take it from the borrower at a time when the borrower is not using it, the borrower may prevent him. Nevertheless, the borrower has no right to lend it to others. See the Perishah for the rationale.
If it broke, he returns the broken pieces. Meaning, he does not repair it and continue using it. This also refers to the first clause, where he retains the utensil rather than returning it to the lender. The Tur likewise writes this and explains that the lender lent it only for as long as it retained its usable condition.
