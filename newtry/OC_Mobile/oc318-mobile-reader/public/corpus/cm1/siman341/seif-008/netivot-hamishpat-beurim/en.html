He may rebuild it. This implies that the borrower cannot compel the lender to provide another trough, and the lender himself has no personal obligation to rebuild it. Rather, if the borrower wishes, he may rebuild and continue using it. Otherwise Mechaber should have written as he does in siman 312, seif 17, concerning a generic house, that the landlord must rebuild or provide another house. The distinction is between a renter and a borrower. When a house is rented, the renter becomes obligated to the landlord for rent through the acquisition, and the landlord correspondingly undertakes to provide the rented use. A paid rental therefore creates reciprocal personal obligations. This resembles siman 39 concerning one who writes a loan document: by undertaking the transaction, the lender can become obligated to advance the loan in the circumstances there. A gratuitous lender, however, does not ordinarily assume a personal obligation to keep providing property to the borrower. The borrower acquires only the particular object or use that was delivered to him by meshichah or chazakah. There is proof from siman 310. In rental, if an animal dies and the carcass contains value sufficient to purchase another, the renter can use that value toward replacement. In borrowing, by contrast, when the object breaks the borrower returns the broken remains, as siman 344, seif 3, states. The borrowing ends with the destruction of the object. If the borrower rebuilds the trough himself, the lender should reimburse the rebuilding expense at the end of the borrowing period. The borrower has improved with authorization. The rebuilding benefits the borrower now and the lender later. Where an expenditure benefits both parties, they can be compelled to contribute as partners, as explained in siman 178.
