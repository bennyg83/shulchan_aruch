This is a partial admission, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that since Rambam and the Rosh agree, the halachah follows them, especially because Ramban answered Raavad and the Maggid Mishneh found Raavad's words astonishing. The Bach cites this. Their words are themselves astonishing. Ramban answered Raavad only concerning a claim of one maneh as a loan and one as a deposit where the defendant admits one entire maneh. On that point the Maggid Mishneh objected. But in the present law, where the claimant demands one hundred as a loan and the defendant admits fifty as a deposit, Ramban explicitly agrees with Raavad that he is exempt. The Rosh quotes Ramban's exact words, the Beit Yosef cites the Rashba's students saying that Ramban agrees with Raavad, and the Maggid Mishneh himself says that Rashba appears to agree with Raavad in this case. Nevertheless, as a matter of law, Rambam is correct. Ramban's principal proof comes from the end of HaManiach, where the Gemara compares two damaged and two damaging animals to wheat and barley. The Rosh answers that people do not confuse wheat and barley, so omission of barley indicates waiver or admission, whereas people may confuse whether money came as a loan or deposit. This distinction is strained. If confusion suffices here, it should certainly suffice in the case of the large and small animals, where the injured party may have mistakenly reconstructed which animal damaged which. The better reason is the one I established above, seif 12. Rabbah bar Natan's exemption is not based on waiver or admission, but on the fact that a barley response to a wheat claim deflects the claim and may be jesting. Where the claimant demands money and the defendant admits money, there is no deflection. He gives the claimant the same type of thing demanded and merely clarifies whether the obligation arose as a loan or deposit. The Gemara's challenge from the two animals is different because the tortfeasor admits precisely the event the claimant has no interest in hearing him admit. The claimant was not present and knows that the tortfeasor ordinarily does not know which animal caused the damage. The volunteered alternative therefore looks like deflection, and the challenge is apt. Rabbeinu Yerucham records distinctions between an unspecified maneh and a specifically identified maneh, where the defendant says, “That very maneh you call a loan was actually a deposit.” His conclusion appears to follow Rambam that there is no distinction and a partial admission is created in every loan and deposit formulation. Baal HaTerumot likewise rules like Rambam, reasoning that the claimant demands fifty owed to him, and it makes no difference whether the defendant's obligation arose through a loan, deposit, or damage. Baal HaTerumot's separate answer, that a tam pays only from its body while a muad pays from all assets, does not address Ramban's proof. Ramban's proof concerns two damaging animals that are both tam, where the defendant admits partial liability in the very same animal but attributes it to the smaller damage. The Gemara still compares it to wheat and barley. Ramban states this explicitly in the passage quoted by the Rosh. Thus Baal HaTerumot's answer cannot stand. The explanation I gave for Rambam is clear and true. Heaven left room for me to clarify this matter.
