You have a deposit of mine in your possession. The Rosh, in the sugya of the borrowed and rented animals, distinguishes between wheat and barley, which people do not confuse, and a loan and a deposit, which people can confuse. The same applies to borrowing and renting. Therefore, in the latter cases the admission is considered of the same kind as the claim. The Shach, in seif katan 39, asks that if so, in HaManiach, where the dispute is whether the larger or smaller ox caused the damage, perhaps people likewise confuse the larger and smaller ox. He also questions the Baal HaTerumot, who writes in Shaar 7 that he too was troubled by the case where one says the mu'ad caused the damage and the other says the tam did. Why is this not like one claiming a maneh as a loan and the other admitting a maneh as a deposit? The Baal HaTerumot answers that a tam ox is unlike a deposit. With a deposit, in any event all the bailee's assets are subject to his monetary obligation to return or pay for it. A tam ox, however, creates no personal monetary liability on the tortfeasor at all. Payment comes only from the body of the ox itself. The tam stands before us, and the defendant's admission therefore does not relate to the same type of claim at all. One must say according to the Baal HaTerumot that the Gemara could not simply answer that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Yishmael, who treats the injured party as a creditor and would seemingly make the tam analogous to a deposit, because the first clause, where both oxen belong to one person and both are liable, is established according to Rabbi Akiva, unlike Tosafot in chapter HaParah. The Shach asks further. In a case of one large and one small tam ox, where the claimant says the larger ox damaged the larger injured animal, he is claiming, for example, fifty zuz against the larger ox. The tortfeasor says the smaller ox caused the damage and thereby admits, for example, forty zuz. Why should this not be considered an admission of the same kind as the claim? In truth, the Rosh's distinction is readily understood. Wheat and barley differ in their very substance. One is wheat and the other barley. How could a person confuse them? By contrast, there is no physical difference in the act of giving money as a loan and giving money as a deposit. In both cases coins were handed over. The distinction lies only in the legal designation, whether they were called a loan or a deposit. People can therefore err about the classification. Accordingly, the Shach's question from a large and small ox does not arise. Whenever the things themselves are different, whether the difference is large or small, people are not presumed to confuse them and were required to be precise. Thus, in the sugya concerning an exchange, where one claims a large slave and the other admits a small slave, the Gemara treats them as different kinds. Any objective difference between large and small is enough. The Baal HaTerumot likewise agrees with this principle. His difficulty was not from the case of the large and small ox. It was specifically from the case of tam and mu'ad, because the two oxen can be physically identical in every respect, with the only difference being their legal status. One bears the name tam and the other the name mu'ad. The distinction is therefore merely qualitative and legal, just as the distinction between a loan and deposit is based on legal designation rather than physical substance. He therefore asks why we do not say that the parties may have confused them and why the admission is not of the same kind as the claim. His answer is that the issue is not merely one of possible confusion. The defendant has not admitted any obligation of the category that the claimant asserted. The claimant asserted a personal monetary obligation against the tortfeasor's general assets, while the defendant responds with an obligation limited to a particular ox. Since the admitted liability belongs to an entirely different legal category, the question of confusion does not arise. This resolves the Shach's objection. One might ask that Rambam appears to hold, as the Baal HaTerumot writes explicitly, that a tam is even less similar to a general debt than a deposit is. With a deposit, the bailee necessarily bears a personal obligation toward all his assets if he cannot return the deposit, since responsibility for safeguarding rests upon him. With a tam, by contrast, the injured party is a partner in the ox and has responsibility for his own share; the tortfeasor is not a bailee for that share, as stated in Bava Kamma. Why then does Rambam rule, as cited below in siman 400, that where the claimant says the mu'ad caused the damage and the defendant admits the tam, the defendant must take a Torah oath? Should the admitted tam not be heilech even more readily than a deposit, since it is entirely in the injured party's legal possession and even its safeguarding rests upon him? The Shach's answer that the tortfeasor can hide the ox is strained. The ox is legally more in the injured party's possession than an ordinary deposit, and the tortfeasor has no proprietary hand in the injured party's share. As I wrote above in explaining Rambam, this is not such a difficulty. Rambam holds that even a deposit that remains intact is not considered heilech until the bailee actually delivers it in beit din, as the Maggid Mishneh explains at the end of chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en VeNitan. According to Rambam himself, therefore, the tam ox need not be heilech automatically. The Shulchan Aruch's adoption of Rambam still deserves attention. One can explain the case as one in which witnesses came only after the defendant's admission and established that he was the tortfeasor. At the time of the admission itself, the admitted ox was not yet regarded as fully in the injured party's possession, because absent the later witnesses the defendant would have been exempt as one who admits a fine. Only afterward, when witnesses came, did his earlier statement become retrospectively relevant as an admission of a monetary obligation. Rambam holds, as the Maggid Mishneh explains, that one who admits a fine and afterward witnesses come is liable. At the moment of the original admission, however, the admitted property was not yet heilech. Examine this, though the matter still requires study. Nor is there a difficulty from the Mechaber's ruling above in seif 13 that if one claims a maneh and the defendant admits that he gave him fifty secured by a specifically designated item as an apotiki, the admission is not of the same kind as the claim. There too the original transfer was in money, but because the money was specifically given in connection with the designated object, it is treated as payment tied to that object rather than as an ordinary loan. People do not confuse ordinary loan money with money whose legal identity is the value of a designated object. Here, by contrast, if the deposit itself consisted of a physical object, it is obvious that it would not be of the same kind as a monetary loan. The rule that loan and deposit can be considered one kind concerns money deposited, which is physically the same as money lent. If a deposit was accepted in a manner carrying no responsibility whatever, perhaps it would be comparable to the tam and mu'ad distinction described by the Baal HaTerumot. Alternatively, one can still distinguish. The bailee received the deposited property directly from the claimant, and the claimant now demands it back from him, so the transaction is easily confused with a loan. With a tam ox, the tortfeasor never received anything from the injured party. He merely points to the ox and, in effect, says, "Here is your share of the ox." The first distinction appears more persuasive, especially since many authorities disagree with treating a loan and deposit as the same kind. We should not extend the rule beyond its clear scope. Indeed, Rambam's wording itself does not conclusively prove that an intact deposit is of the same kind as a loan. He writes: "You owe me a maneh that I lent you," and the debtor responds, "There was no such loan, but I owe you fifty dinars as a deposit, or because of damage," and Rambam says that his teachers ruled that he is liable. By grouping a deposit and damages together, Rambam may mean a deposit that has already been consumed or lost in a manner creating a present personal obligation to pay, just as a damage obligation is a present personal debt. He may be stating only the source from which the monetary debt arose. This is also how the Nimukei Yosef in the sugya of the borrowed and rented animals cites Ritva's explanation of Rambam: the defendant is merely indicating the source of the obligation. See there. If the deposit itself remains intact, however, it may not resemble a loan even according to Rambam. Therefore, where there is no responsibility at all for the deposit, it certainly appears not to be of the same kind as a loan. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 98, s.v. mashkachat, also appear to treat separate legal sources of liability as different kinds. They ask why the Gemara needs to multiply the number of cows in its case. Two should suffice: one cow for the admission and another concerning which the defendant says he does not know, thereby creating the denied amount and the unavoidable accident claim. Tosafot answer that Scripture requires that one cow not serve simultaneously for two different roles. This answer is sound because there the claimant actually has only one substantive demand concerning that cow, but supports it with two possible legal grounds. He says, in effect, "Pay me for this cow because it died while borrowed, and even if it died while rented, perhaps it died at the time that would still make you liable." Either way, he seeks only one payment for that cow. One payment would satisfy both alternative grounds, and therefore Tosafot explain why the verse nevertheless requires distinct cows. But there was another possible formulation. The owner could say that the rented cow still exists and demand continued rental payment, while also claiming with respect to the borrowed cow that even if the rented cow died, the bailee must swear that he was not negligent. Those are genuinely different claims, one concerning rental money and one concerning responsibility for the body of the animal. Payment of one would not satisfy the other. If the rented cow died through negligence, the rental claim may cease while liability for the animal remains. Such a case would already contain distinct denial and accident claims without requiring four cows. The fact that the Gemara does not rely on that formulation indicates that a claim for the body of the borrowed cow and a claim for rental money are different kinds. This also explains the Mishnah's wording that the defendant must swear that the rented cow died. Tosafot ask what relevance it has that the rented cow died. According to what I have written, the point is that the oath also defeats the separate rental claim. We must therefore conclude that claims rooted in distinct legal obligations can be treated as different kinds even where they relate to the same property. Examine this carefully.
