A quantity filling ten jars, etc. Rambam writes this, followed by Baal HaTerumot, Semag, and the Mechaber. In my humble opinion it is not correct. The Gemara says that when one claims, “I have with you ten jars full of oil,” all agree that he claims both oil and jars. It is implausible to distinguish between “the fill of ten jars of oil” and “ten jars full of oil.” Rather, Rava's statement that when one says, “I have the fill of ten jars of oil in your pit,” he claims oil but not jars, depends specifically on the words “in your pit.” Rava did not mention the pit gratuitously. It is the pit before the press, as Rashi explains, and contains oil without jars. The claim therefore concerns oil alone. Rambam and those who follow him omitted this condition, apparently because their text read “in your possession” rather than “in your pit,” as in the Rif. But the Gemara must read “in your pit.” Otherwise, why would Rava invent a thin and counterintuitive distinction between “the fill of ten jars” and “ten jars full,” when the dispute could be explained without it? Rava is instead returning to the Gemara's first explanation, a claim for a measure of oil in the pit, and clarifying that Admon does not obligate an oath there because jars were never claimed. When one says, “Ten jars full of oil are with you,” or simply, “Ten jars of oil are with you,” both oil and jars are claimed, and the admission to jars creates an oath under Rav Nachman in Shmuel's name. The later repetition of “with you,” rather than “in your hand,” likewise proves that the first phrase was “in your pit.” The Rif's “in your hand” should therefore be corrected to “in your pit,” or understood as “in your hand, in your pit.” Rav Hai Gaon in Mishpetei Shevuot supports this. He writes that if the claimant claims jars of oil and the defendant admits only the vessels, an oath is imposed, but if the claimant claims oil alone and the defendant admits jars, there is no oath because oil may be stored in skins or similar receptacles and the jars are not of the kind claimed. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise writes that one who claims jars of wine or oil and receives an admission to the jars, even saying “I have ten jars of oil with you,” creates a Torah oath. He makes no distinction based on word order. The case of oil in the pit is too obvious to require mention. Tosafot in Ketubot 108b asks why the Gemara finds Admon's view difficult, since Rabban Gamliel obligates an oath even when the admission is of a different kind. Their answer is strained. The difficulty is simple: Admon expressly bases his ruling on the admission being of the kind claimed. All editions of the Mishnah, Gemara, Rif, Rosh, and Rav Hai's Mishpetei Shevuot read, “Admon says: since he admitted of the kind claimed, he must swear.” Admon therefore cannot follow Rabban Gamliel's broader rule. Where the claim is only a measure of oil in the pit, the admission to jars is not of the kind claimed. This is clear.
But if he said to him, “Ten jars,” etc. The Sema's statement that the Rama follows the Beit Yosef's explanation is incorrect. The Rama necessarily rejects the Beit Yosef and must follow the Bach, unless the Sema contains a scribal error. Yet the Beit Yosef, Rama, and Bach all seem incorrect in their reading of the Tur. According to the Beit Yosef, the difficulty he himself leaves unresolved remains, and the Tur's statement that the admission is not of the kind claimed is unexplained, while the supposed true reason, that only oil was demanded, is absent from the Tur. The Bach emends the Gemara and the Rosh because the Tur's wording conflicts with them, but that is untenable. One cannot alter all editions of the Gemara and Rosh to read that the claimant demanded jars but not oil, for then the Sages' statement that the admission is not of the kind claimed would make no sense, since the Mishnah says the defendant admitted jars. One would have to corrupt the Mishnah as well, contrary to every text, including Shevuat HaDayanim 48a. The clear solution is that the Tur contains a scribal error. It should read as the Mishnah, Gemara, and Rosh do: “he admitted the jars and denied the oil.” The error entered from the abridged rulings of the Rosh, where “jars” and “oil” were interchanged, and the Tur copied that faulty text. Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah also contains a scribal error in this matter. As a matter of law, the primary rule is that a Torah oath is imposed in every formulation, except where he expressly says, “I have the fill of ten jars in your pit,” as I explained above.
