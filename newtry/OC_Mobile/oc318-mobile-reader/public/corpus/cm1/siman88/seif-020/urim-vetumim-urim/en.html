Regarding his father’s debt. Meaning that he knows with certainty that the claimant’s father remained indebted to him. The reverse is likewise true: the claimant sued him for a debt that he himself owed, and he admitted that the claimant’s father had remained indebted to him. One might have thought that this is also comparable to one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley. It must therefore speak of a case in which both he and his father borrowed from the claimant at the same moment. Now that his father has died and the son is responsible for payment, one might have thought that the obligations arose together and should be claimed together like wheat and barley. The ruling teaches otherwise. Since one obligation derives from his father and the other from himself, they are not comparable to wheat and barley. But if the obligations did not arise together, the Mechaber already wrote above in seif 12 that even in the case of wheat and barley he is not deemed to have waived the other claim. This is obvious. And he takes a shevuat heset, etc. This means that the claimant sued him for one hundred that he himself had borrowed, while he admitted one hundred arising from his father’s debt. There is therefore no partial admission of the claim itself. But if the claimant sued him for one hundred arising from his father’s debt and he admitted fifty from his own debt, this is a partial admission and he must take a Torah oath, since both are obligations of loan. Shach. This is obvious. All the more so, if he claimed both the defendant’s own debt and his father’s debt and the defendant admitted one of them, it is a partial admission. This too is obvious.
