He sued him for his father's debt, etc. The case must be one in which the claimant asserts with certainty that the heir knows his father owed the debt, as the conclusion indicates. Otherwise, even if the heir admits a personal debt, he need not pay unless he maintains the admission; he may retract and say, “I was jesting.” Although I wrote in the preceding seif that where he admits the same kind claimed, such as money in response to money, jesting does not apply, that is where the claimant says, “You know that you owe me money.” An heir may simply answer that he knows nothing of his father's affairs. Therefore, a volunteered admission can be a deflection. It resembles the case of two damaged animals, where the defendant admits liability in the very tam animal involved but changes which damage it caused, and is nevertheless exempt because he was not expected to know. It likewise resembles a claim that the muad damaged the large animal and an answer that it damaged the small one. Although both are ordinary monetary liability payable from all assets, the defendant may say that he was jesting because he had no reason to know the facts. The same applies here.

If he claims against him with certainty, etc. Although an heir ordinarily need not swear in order to be exempt, as stated above at the end of siman 69 and in siman 71, seif 22, that applies where the claimant does not assert with certainty that the heir knows the facts. Here, since he claims with certainty that the heir knows, the heir must take a hesset oath. This is also the explanation of the Sema, seif katan 32, and the Bach, and it is clear. Since, with God's help, many laws have been clarified from my discussion of seifim 12 through 20, and it has also become clear that the words of the Tur, Mechaber, and Rama in several places there are not correct, I shall summarize the conclusions in the order of those seifim. 12. If one claims two items, such as wheat and barley, and the defendant admits one, he must give what he admitted and take a Torah oath concerning the remainder. But if he claims wheat and the defendant admits barley, the defendant is exempt from a Torah oath even if he maintains his admission and wishes to give the barley. He takes only a hesset oath that he does not owe wheat. If he does not wish to give the barley, he is exempt even from the barley. There is no distinction whether the wheat and barley concern the same occasion, such as when he says, “Those wheat were really barley,” or separate occasions, such as, “I owe you barley from another loan.” The reason is that the defendant may say, “I was jesting with you.” Therefore, when the claimant demanded wheat and the defendant admitted barley, and they left beit din and returned to hear the ruling, beit din may state simply that the defendant is exempt, even though he did not plead that he was jesting. But if the claimant then says, “At least give me the barley you admitted,” the defendant must himself plead that he was jesting. If he does not, beit din does not plead it for him. Consequently, if witnesses establish the barley, he must pay it. Likewise, if the claimant seized property equal to the value of the barley, whether before litigation or after litigation but before the defendant retracted, even if the seizure was witnessed, then since the defendant knew that the claimant forcibly seized the value of the barley and nevertheless maintained his admission, he must give the barley. A witnessed seizure does not help with respect to the wheat. If the claimant seized without witnesses and therefore has a migo that there was never such a seizure or that he returned the property, the seizure is effective for both wheat and barley even after the defendant retracted. All this applies where the claimant asserted certainty. But if he said, “Perhaps you owe me wheat or barley,” and the defendant answered, “I certainly owe you barley,” he must give the barley and cannot say that he was jesting. 13. If one claims, “I lent you one maneh,” and the defendant says, “You have only fifty zuz with me, because you gave that money for a particular object that you purchased. The object still exists, is yours, and remains at your risk wherever it is,” or, “You lent me upon that object on condition that you could collect only from the object itself,” the case resembles a wheat claim answered by an admission of barley. He may say that he was jesting and is exempt even from the object he admitted. If he maintains the admission and wishes to give the object, or witnesses establish the facts as he stated, then in the first case, where the object belongs to the claimant, he is exempt from a Torah oath because it is a case of “here it is.” In the second case, where the loan was made on condition that collection be only from the object, he must take a Torah oath. If the admitted object remains the defendant's responsibility, such as where he must furnish another object of that kind, the case follows the rule explained above in seif 9: whenever a maneh is claimed, any monetary admission creates partial admission, and he cannot retract by claiming that he was jesting. The reverse is likewise true. If the claimant demands a particular object worth one maneh, alleging that the defendant sold it to him or that he lent money upon it on condition that payment be made specifically with that object, and the defendant admits only a fifty-zuz loan, it resembles a wheat claim answered with barley. But if the claimant says that the defendant designated a particular object as an apotiki, even an express apotiki, then because the defendant may discharge it with money, an admission of fifty zuz is partial admission. He pays fifty and swears a Torah oath on the remainder. 14. If the claimant began, “You owe me a kor of wheat,” and before he could add, “and also a kor of barley,” the defendant hurriedly answered, “I owe you only a kor of barley,” and the claimant said, “I intended to ask you about the barley as well,” then if the defendant acted evasively, rushing to answer before the claim was completed in order to avoid an oath, he must give what he admitted and take a Torah oath on the remainder. We treat the matter as though both items had already been claimed. If he answered innocently, he is exempt from a Torah oath, but must give the barley if he maintains the admission. If he retracts and says that he was jesting, he is exempt. Even if the claimant says, “You also owe me barley, but I intended to claim it only on another occasion,” the defendant takes a hesset oath that he owes nothing. 15. If the defendant first said, “I owe you barley,” and only afterward the claimant demanded wheat, which he denied, there is no partial admission, because a Torah oath for partial admission applies only when the admission follows the claim. The same applies to one kind. If the defendant entered beit din and first said, “I owe you fifty zuz,” and the claimant answered, “You owe me an additional fifty,” which the defendant denied, he is exempt from a Torah oath. He may also retract the barley or fifty zuz admitted before the claim, though he cannot say that he was jesting. Some say that here too beit din examines whether he rushed to admit the barley evasively in order to avoid the oath. If so, he must give what he admitted and take a Torah oath on the remainder. 16. Likewise, if the claimant demanded wheat, the defendant admitted it, and only afterward the claimant demanded barley, which the defendant denied, the admission preceded the barley claim and is not partial admission. He pays the wheat and takes a hesset oath concerning the barley. Some say that if he hurried to admit evasively, he must take a Torah oath on the barley. 17. If the claimant demanded wheat and the defendant answered that he does not know whether he owes wheat or barley, he must at least pay barley. He may not retract and say that he knows nothing or owes nothing. He takes a hesset oath that he does not know that he owes wheat and is exempt from the wheat. To satisfy his obligation before Heaven, he must give wheat. Likewise, if he first answered that he knows nothing or that the matter never occurred, and witnesses testify that he owes either wheat or barley, he must at least pay barley and take a hesset oath that he does not know about wheat. The same applies in comparable cases. 18. If one claims jars of wine or oil and the defendant admits the jars, he pays what he admitted and must take a Torah oath in every formulation, because the claim includes both oil and jars, whether the claimant said, “Ten jars of oil,” or, “The fill of ten jars of oil.” The exception is where he says, “I have ten jars of oil in your pit.” There his intent concerns only the oil in the pit, and he mentioned jars merely as a measure. It is therefore an oil claim answered by an admission of jars, like wheat answered with barley, and he is exempt as explained in seif 12. 19. If the claimant says, “You owe me one maneh as a loan,” and the defendant answers, “I never borrowed from you, but you have fifty dinars with me as a deposit, damages, or the like,” this is partial admission. He must give the fifty, may not retract, and takes a Torah oath on the remainder. The same applies to borrowing and rental, and certainly where the claim consists of fifty as a loan and fifty as a deposit and he admits one of them. 20. If one claims the defendant's personal debt and he admits a debt owed by his father, or claims the father's debt with certainty that the defendant knows his father owed it and the defendant admits his own debt, he pays the personal debt he admitted and takes a hesset oath concerning the father's debt, where his personal debt equals the amount claimed from the father. But if the claimant initially demanded one hundred as the father's debt and the defendant admitted fifty as his own debt, this is partial admission and he takes a Torah oath concerning the remaining fifty. If the claimant does not assert with certainty that the defendant knows his father owed him, the defendant need not take a hesset oath concerning the father's debt and may retract the admission of his own debt. Since only the father's debt was claimed and he could have answered, “I do not know,” he may say that his admission of his own debt was merely a deflection and jest.
