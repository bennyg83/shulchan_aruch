The Taz writes that this does not appear correct, because the verse mentioning "silver or utensils" applies only to partial admission, not to one witness or bailees. If so, from where do we know to impose liability for utensils in a case of one witness? His position has no basis at all. We see that if one admits owing one needle and denies another needle, he is liable for an oath. Surely his admission itself does not create the oath. Consider a case where he admits one hundred maneh and denies half a perutah. Would he be liable for an oath? Necessarily, the denial of the needle is itself treated as a denial of a perutah. If so, one witness should likewise obligate an oath. What difference does the admission of a needle make? Regarding bailees, however, there is room for analysis. Since the Torah imposes an oath because of doubt, perhaps it imposed that oath only regarding property of monetary value, but not regarding a utensil that has no monetary value. A utensil is not considered money for every purpose. Could one betroth a woman with such an item? Would one who finds a lost utensil worth less than a perutah be obligated to return it? Certainly not. If so, perhaps even a bailee would not be obligated to swear regarding such a utensil because the matter remains doubtful. Yet it appears that there is another point that must first be understood in the reasoning of Rambam and Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash. Why should bailees not require two silver pieces when the principal verse is written in the section dealing with bailees, as the Ran asks? Indeed, the phrase "silver or utensils" is expounded in connection with bailees. It therefore appears that they follow their own view, as I wrote above in seif 1, that by Torah law "two silver pieces" means two perutot. Two perutot are required because one is needed for the denial and one for the admission, since neither an admission nor a denial counts for less than a perutah. With bailees and with one witness, where an admission is not required and only the denial is relevant, the same rule applies as with a loan: just as with a loan a denial of one perutah suffices, so too with bailees, concerning the claim that the bailee was negligent. Although according to Rav the admission must equal a perutah in addition to the two perutot denied, one may say that Rambam holds that this too is rabbinic. In truth there is a difficulty: if "this is it" requires an admission in addition to the "two silver pieces" stated in the section, then two utensils should likewise require three utensils, two that he denies and one that he admits, since "silver or utensils" refers to the denied amount apart from that which he admits. In particular, Scripture juxtaposes silver and utensils. Why, then, do we say that if he claimed two needles and the defendant admitted one of them he is liable? In the Gemara, Shmuel indeed says that one claimed two needles, and Shmuel follows his own view that the admission need not be in addition to the two silver pieces of the claim. But the Rif, Rambam, and all the authorities who rule like Rav nevertheless cite the law of two needles. Their reasoning requires explanation. We must therefore say that they hold that assigning the term "silver" exclusively to the denied amount is only an asmachta. By Torah law, the admission is actually included within the two silver pieces. This is precisely why the Gemara says that the Mishnah is precise in accordance with Rav while the verse supports Shmuel. If the verses actually ruled like Shmuel, what significance would there be that the Mishnah's wording points toward Rav, and which authority would yield to which? Rather, both are true. The verses support Shmuel for the Torah law, whereas rabbinically both amounts are required and therefore the Mishnah is precise in accordance with Rav. This clearly explains Rambam's intent in calling the requirement of a denial worth two silver pieces rabbinic. By Torah law, he certainly holds that one perutah for the admission and one perutah for the denial suffice. The Sages instituted the greater amount only for silver, while with utensils no such enactment was made, so one utensil suffices for the denial and one for the admission. Accordingly, the Ran's question from bailees falls away. The Sages made no enactment concerning bailees and left the Torah law in place. Thus the denial in a case of bailees has the same measure as the denial in partial admission: just as there one perutah suffices by Torah law, so too with bailees. This also resolves Tosafot's difficulty. They ask why one witness obligates an oath for a denial of one perutah when, in a case where partial admission is exempt, such as land, one witness is likewise exempt. Why then, where a claim of less than two silver pieces does not create liability through partial admission, should one witness create liability? For this reason Tosafot in Shevuot hold that this rule applies specifically according to Shmuel and not according to Rav. According to the explanation above, however, there is no difficulty. By Torah law, even according to Rav, a denial of one perutah suffices, and in the case of one witness the Sages left the Torah law in force. This is clear and resolves all the questions and difficulties. Accordingly, the phrase "silver or utensils" indeed applies even to bailees. Therefore, just as utensils of any value suffice for partial admission, they suffice for bailees and all the more so for one witness. Everything is thus properly explained.
