The same applies to an oath of custodians. Shaar Mishpat writes that where one claims a deposit worth two silver ma'ah and a perutah, and the defendant admits the perutah, denies one ma'ah and says that the matter never occurred, and regarding the second ma'ah claims that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances, he is liable to take a Torah oath concerning both ma'ah. Although an admission in part requires a denial of two silver ma'ah, and an oath of custodians likewise requires that amount, nevertheless, the reason two silver ma'ah are required is that the Torah imposed an oath only concerning a significant amount, namely two silver ma'ah. Here, since the total denial is ultimately two ma'ah and is a significant amount, the two claims are combined, and he is liable to take a Torah oath concerning both ma'ah together, one on which he claims that the matter never occurred, and the other on which he claims unavoidable loss. See there, where he brings proofs for this. See Beit Yehudah on this siman, who disagrees and writes that the matter does not depend on significance. Rather, it is a Scriptural decree that the denial itself must be two silver ma'ah. Since in each of the two oaths there is no denial of two silver ma'ah, they cannot be combined. He rejects the proofs of Shaar Mishpat. See there.
