He does not know whether wheat. Rambam rules that if the claimant says wheat and the defendant says, "I do not know whether I owe wheat or barley," the defendant must pay the barley. Raavad objects from the sugya of the two oxen, where even a certain claimant against an uncertain damager appears not to recover the smaller claim. Under Rama's waiver explanation, the result is simple. When the claimant says wheat, he has effectively waived the barley. The defendant's uncertainty therefore concerns only whether a wheat obligation ever arose, and he is exempt as one uncertain whether he became liable. Rambam follows the admission rather than waiver theory. The claimant's wheat plea is evidence against a barley obligation, but the defendant's own uncertain response does not contradict that evidence with a certain competing admission. Once the defendant admits uncertainty between wheat and barley, he cannot retract from the fact that at least one obligation exists. The claimant has not actually waived barley. Therefore the smaller barley amount remains payable. The difficult Gemara can be read, following a version preserved in Shitah Mekubetzet, as one where the injured party says with certainty that the large ox damaged, while the damager says the large ox certainly did not damage and is uncertain only whether the small ox damaged at all. His uncertainty is therefore not about which of two admitted liabilities exists. He denies the claimant's large ox theory and is uncertain whether any smaller liability arose. On that reading Rambam is consistent.
