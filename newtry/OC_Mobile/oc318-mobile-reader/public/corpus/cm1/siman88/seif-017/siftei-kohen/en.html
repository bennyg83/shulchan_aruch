He swears and pays barley. Baal HaIttur, Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot challenge this ruling from the end of HaManiach, where the Gemara asks that even when the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor uncertain, the Mishnah should refute Rabbah bar Natan. The Maggid Mishneh likewise says that the sugya is difficult for Rambam, and the Rama follows them by citing a dissenting opinion and ruling it primary. In my humble opinion, Rambam is correct and the difficulty can be fully resolved. It is unreasonable that the defendant should be entirely exempt after admitting that he owes either wheat or barley. According to the Rosh and Baal HaTerumot, who understand the sugya as involving witnesses, Rabbeinu Yerucham even concludes that if witnesses establish that the defendant owes either wheat or barley but cannot identify which, he is entirely exempt. That is astonishing. According to what I proved above, seif 12, Rabbah bar Natan's rule is not based on waiver or the claimant's admission, but on the defendant's ability to say, “I was jesting with you.” When the claimant demanded wheat, an item the defendant says he did not owe, he may say that he toyed with him by admitting barley. That rationale plainly does not apply when the defendant says, “I do not know whether it was wheat or barley.” He acknowledges that the claimant may be entirely correct and merely lacks certainty. Even according to Rashi and Tosafot, who use the language of waiver or admission, there is no waiver here. The claimant maintains his wheat demand and can say: you admit that wheat is possible; if you will not pay wheat because you are uncertain, at least pay barley. The Gemara's question can be resolved in two ways. Raavad gives the first. He writes that before the enactment of hesset, the tortfeasor could retract and say, “I know nothing of any damage; I was jesting.” After hesset was enacted, if he swears specifically that he does not know whether the debt was wheat or barley, he has made a genuine admission that at least one is owed, and must pay barley. Raavad says this explanation has substance, though Rambam should have stated it explicitly. Baal HaIttur and Maggid Mishneh may object only to Rambam's broad wording, which sounds as though the defendant can never retract. Yet Raavad himself necessarily agrees that if the defendant stands by his uncertainty he must pay barley. Baal HaTerumot likewise cites Raavad that even in an ordinary wheat and barley case, if the defendant stands by his barley admission, he must pay it. The Maggid Mishneh understood Raavad in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'elah as exempting the defendant entirely, save for a heavenly obligation, and in chapter 1 of Hilchot To'en as later inclining to the post-hesset distinction. In my view there is no retraction. Raavad's objection in Hilchot She'elah is to Rambam's rule that the claimant swears and collects wheat. The Gemara proves only that the claimant cannot collect wheat by his own oath. It does not prove that the defendant, while maintaining his uncertainty, is exempt from barley. There is, however, a second and stronger explanation, which reflects Rambam's actual ruling. Raavad's own criticism remains: Rambam did not limit the law to a defendant who maintains his answer after hesset. The Maggid Mishneh also says that reason itself dictates payment of the lesser amount, and only the sugya appears difficult. Since reason and Rambam's ruling are compelling, the sugya must be read differently. In the animal-damage case, the tortfeasor ordinarily was not present and had no duty to know which animal caused the damage. Therefore, if Rabbah bar Natan allows a defendant to dismiss an irrelevant admission as jesting, the tortfeasor too may retract his volunteered guess that the smaller animal caused the damage and say that he knows nothing at all. The Gemara's challenge is not that a person who genuinely remains uncertain must pay nothing. It is whether he may withdraw the nonrequired answer as mere deflection. This is the same explanation I established above, seif 12, and below in siman 400. It also explains why the Gemara can compare the muad case, although muad damages are ordinary money, to wheat and barley. The issue is not the monetary category but whether the defendant answered something he was not required to know and may therefore characterize it as jesting. Where the defendant himself continues to say, “I owe either wheat or barley but do not know which,” no notion of jesting, waiver, or implied admission can exempt him entirely. He must pay the lesser amount. Rambam therefore states the rule without qualification. The same principle appears in chapter 20 of Hilchot Mekhirah and below, siman 223: if the buyer says, “I bought the large item,” and the seller says, “I do not know,” the buyer must prove his claim or the seller swears hesset that he does not know, and the buyer receives at least the small item. The Rama there does not dissent, nor do the later authorities. This compels the same result here. Baal HaTerumot's suggestion that Rambam speaks only of one who wishes to satisfy Heaven is therefore incorrect. Migdal Oz's attempt to derive the law from the Rif's rule concerning a claim of wheat and barley with an admission to one of them likewise confuses distinct cases. Gidulei Terumah's discussion does not alter the matter. Rambam's words in chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en, that the claimant is as though he said “you have no barley of mine,” address the ordinary case in which the defendant gives a nonresponsive barley admission and may have been jesting. They do not apply to a defendant who himself says that one of the two items is certainly owed. The law and the sugya are therefore clear as explained.
Except according to Baal HaIttur. The Maggid Mishneh writes that Baal HaIttur holds that one who wishes to satisfy Heaven must pay barley. This is puzzling, for one who wishes to satisfy Heaven must pay even wheat, as the Gemara, all the authorities, and the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 75, seif 9, state. The Maggid Mishneh himself says so later regarding one who says, “You lent me a maneh,” and the defendant answers, “I do not know.” Lechem Mishneh raises this and answers with great difficulty. It appears clear that the Maggid Mishneh, in the sharpness of his analysis, did not read Baal HaIttur precisely. Baal HaIttur writes that the Babylonian authority's ruling requiring barley is not clear from HaManiach, which implies that where the defendant too is uncertain he is exempt from barley, but that one who wishes to satisfy Heaven is liable “as explained below.” Below he writes that one who says “I do not know” regarding the principal loan is liable when satisfying Heaven. His meaning is therefore liability even for wheat, not merely barley. The Maggid Mishneh may instead mean that because the defendant knows that he owes at least barley, his obligation to pay barley is not merely an act of piety. Although beit din may not extract it under that view, he knows that he owes it and remains wicked until he pays. The broader heavenly obligation concerning wheat is a higher measure of piety. If so, the Maggid Mishneh does not accept waiver as the reason. This makes the Rama difficult, for above, seif 12, he rules that even one who knows he owes barley is exempt because the claimant waived it, like the law in siman 75, seif 11, where even the heavenly obligation is removed. Perhaps the Rama distinguishes this uncertainty case from the ordinary wheat and barley case, though the end of HaManiach suggests one rationale throughout. This requires study. In any event, I already challenged the Rama's ruling above and established that the primary reason is jesting, not waiver or admission.
