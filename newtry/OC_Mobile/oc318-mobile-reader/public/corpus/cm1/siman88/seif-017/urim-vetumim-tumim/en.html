And he pays him barley. Many have expressed astonishment at Rambam's ruling that he pays the barley. In HaManiach the Gemara says that if the injured party makes a definite claim and the tortfeasor is uncertain, this would still refute Rabbah bar Natan. Thus, even in such a case, the defendant should be exempt from the barley. The Shach writes that earlier authorities left room for him to distinguish himself by resolving this difficulty, but his answer is strained. Moreover, all his strained reasoning follows his view that Rambam agrees with the Ramah that the exemption from barley is based on the defendant's ability to say, "I was merely mocking you." I have already clarified that this is not Rambam's view, so the difficulty remains according to the Shach's approach. I am astonished, however, that this was treated as such a great difficulty that the Rama ruled against Rambam because of it. Tosafot explicitly write that when the Gemara establishes the first clause as a case where the injured party says he is certain and the tortfeasor says he is uncertain, this is so even though the Mishnah says "No, rather," a phrase that implies two definite claims. Tosafot therefore explain that the Mishnah includes two definite claims as well. Maharsha clarifies that Sumchus's dispute is not actually relevant in the two definite claim case, since Sumchus agrees there according to this view. The novelty of the Mishnah concerns the definite and uncertain case, where Sumchus and the Sages disagree, while the wording incidentally includes two definite claims as well. Accordingly, Rav Pappa establishes the middle clause as a definite and uncertain case. The Gemara asks which party is certain. If the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor uncertain, the Mishnah would teach that even though there is partial admission the Sages still say that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. But the phrase "No, rather" would then force us to say that the clause also includes two definite claims, even though the main dispute between Sumchus and the Sages concerns the definite and uncertain situation. In that event, the Gemara properly asks that the Mishnah would still refute Rabbah bar Natan. The objection is not from the uncertain case itself. It is that the Mishnah necessarily also includes the two definite claim case, in which it states that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. If the claimant cannot recover according to the tortfeasor's admission there, the Mishnah's ruling would fail. The Gemara therefore establishes the case with the injured party uncertain and the tortfeasor certain. That allows the words "No, rather" to be understood directly without requiring the clause also to include the problematic two definite claim case. If so, I do not understand the difficulty against Rambam. It is true that one could argue that even if the Mishnah includes both definite and uncertain claims, there is no difficulty from the two definite claim case. In that case perhaps the injured party indeed does not recover according to the tortfeasor's admission because of Rabbah bar Natan. The Mishnah says that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant rather than simply saying "exempt" because in the definite and uncertain case the injured party does recover the amount admitted by the tortfeasor according to Rambam. The same phrase could then have two applications. Yet it is very difficult to interpret the identical words of the Mishnah in two different ways within the same clause, saying that with an uncertain tortfeasor the injured party recovers according to the tortfeasor's statement, while with two definite claims he recovers nothing at all. Neither the language nor sound interpretation comfortably bears such a double meaning. Thus Rambam's ruling is entirely plausible. It may also be that those authorities who challenged Rambam follow the Rif and Rambam themselves in holding that seizure helps in the wheat and barley case even where the claimant has no simultaneous valid claim to both items. They therefore ask why the Gemara did not establish the middle clause as a case of seizure. The answer must be that if so the middle and final clauses would be redundant. They can then argue that the first clause should be established as a definite injured party and uncertain tortfeasor, and although it also includes two definite claims, the two definite claim situation could be handled through seizure without making the clauses redundant, because seizure is not the principal subject of the first clause. Even so, this is not sufficient reason to reject Rambam. And there is another satisfactory basis for Rambam even according to the questioners. Rambam's teacher, Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash, holds regarding a deposit where the claimant says what it contained and the bailee does not know how much, that the claimant swears and collects because a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one. Although we do not generally rule like Rabbi Yehudah that a definite claim prevails, Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash holds that where there is an inherent monetary basis for liability, a definite claim is stronger. The commentators write that Rambam cannot fully agree with this position, because in our very case, where the defendant does not know whether he borrowed wheat or barley, Rambam rules that he pays only barley. According to Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's broader rule, since the defendant certainly owes something, the claimant's definite claim should prevail even for more than the admitted lesser amount. Rambam does not go that far. He limits the inherent monetary basis to the amount that is certainly owed. But with respect to that amount, there is indeed a definite monetary obligation and the defendant was required to be precise. For example, suppose the claimant demands wheat worth one hundred and the defendant admits owing barley worth fifty. As to fifty, there is certainly an existing monetary obligation. The defendant himself admits that amount. He therefore had reason to know whether the obligation was wheat or barley. Regarding that amount, the claimant's definite assertion should prevail over the defendant's uncertainty. As to the additional fifty, however, there is no established obligation at all, and therefore the claimant's definite assertion does not prevail. Thus, where the defendant says he does not know whether he borrowed wheat or barley and the claimant says definitely that it was wheat, even though the claimant has by his formulation waived the barley as such, Rambam still obligates the defendant to pay up to the value of the barley in wheat because, to that extent, there is a definite monetary basis and the definite claim prevails over uncertainty. Accordingly, there is no difficulty from the sugya in HaManiach. The rule that a definite claim prevails over uncertainty in this setting applies only where the uncertain party had reason to know. Where he had no reason to know at all, everyone agrees that a definite claim does not prevail. See above in siman 75, where I cited many authorities holding that even in a case of "I do not know whether I paid," if the defendant had no reason to know, he is not liable. This is all the more true where knowledge was entirely impossible. The Shach himself writes below in siman 400 that in the sugya of HaManiach, all agree that the rule requiring one who cannot swear to pay does not apply because the tortfeasor could not have known which ox caused the damage. Thus the Gemara can properly say that the case would still refute Rabbah bar Natan, while Rambam's law regarding wheat and barley remains intact.
Along this path, I said that Rambam has a proof from the Gemara for his ruling. The Shach himself notes that it is strained to say that the Mishnah concerns witnesses who saw the goring. If they did not see it, however, what claim is there at all, since even if the tortfeasor admitted liability he would be one who admits a fine and would be exempt? The Shach therefore sought to resolve the matter by saying that the claimant asserts, "I already brought you to beit din and it was established that the larger ox gored," while the tortfeasor responds, "No, beit din established only that the smaller ox gored." According to this approach, the entire Mishnah concerns a prior adjudication of liability, and the problem of admission to a fine disappears. This answer of the Shach would be sound were it not for Tosafot's question. Tosafot ask how the Gemara can establish the case as one where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor certain, since a definite claim should prevail over an uncertain one. As I wrote above, this question applies even according to Rav Nachman because the tortfeasor is the possessor and his definite claim is supported by possession. Tosafot answer that this is a matter the injured party had no reason to know. That answer works if the dispute concerns the factual event of the goring. But according to the Shach, the dispute concerns what judgment beit din had previously issued and which ox beit din established as the one that caused the damage. That is something the injured party certainly had reason to know. He was present for the judgment and should know how he was adjudicated. Tosafot's answer would therefore fail, and their original question that the definite claim should prevail would remain. Accordingly, Rambam's position is strongly supported. Rav Pappa can establish the middle clause as a case where the injured party is uncertain only because Rav Pappa holds that half damages are compensatory, so there is no need either for witnesses or for a prior adjudication. But according to the final ruling that half damages are a fine, the Mishnah cannot be understood merely as a fresh admission by the tortfeasor unless one uses the Shach's prior adjudication device. Once one does so, however, the uncertain claimant should lose because he had reason to know. Nor can we use seizure for the middle clause, because that would duplicate the final clause, as explained. Rambam therefore holds that the case can instead be understood through his own law. One who does not know whether the debt was wheat or barley must pay the lesser value when the matter was one he had reason to know. The middle clause can then involve an injured party making a definite claim and a tortfeasor who is uncertain. We rule like Rav Nachman that possession defeats the claimant's definite assertion, so the claimant does not automatically prevail. Yet the case does not refute Rabbah bar Natan because, where the tortfeasor had reason to know, Rambam himself obligates payment of the lesser amount. This resolves the sugya according to the final halachah. One may ask that Rambam rules that if the injured party says the mu'ad caused the damage and the tortfeasor says the tam did, the defendant must take a Torah oath, as discussed above regarding the Raavad and Maggid Mishneh. If the injured party is definite and the tortfeasor uncertain, should this not become a case of one who cannot swear and therefore must pay? We cannot answer that he had no reason to know, because under the prior adjudication approach he did have reason to know. The answer is that the rule of one who cannot swear and must pay applies only where a definite assertion by the defendant would have required an oath. Here, however, if the tortfeasor made a definite claim that the smaller ox caused the damage, he would be entirely exempt from an oath under the rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley. His liability for the lesser amount arises only because he says that he does not know. Since a definite response would have created no oath at all, his uncertainty cannot trigger the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. Examine this carefully. This is what appears to me as the correct halachic explanation of Rambam. There is also another independent basis. Rambam holds that the distinction between "I do not know whether I owe you" and "I do not know whether I paid" is that in the first case the underlying obligation was never established, whereas in the second the obligation was established and the defendant seeks to remove it by doubt. A definite obligation cannot be displaced by a doubtful discharge. The same principle applies not only to a personal obligation but also to the lien upon property, since liens are of Torah origin. If particular assets were certainly subjected to a lien, a doubt cannot remove that lien, just as a doubt cannot remove an established personal obligation. Now, even if the actual loan consisted of barley, assets worth the value of the barley, including wheat belonging to the debtor, were certainly subjected to the debt, because a monetary lien extends to all the debtor's assets. The Rosh explicitly writes that one may seize barley for the value of wheat or wheat for the value of barley. Therefore, where the defendant says that he does not know whether he owes wheat or barley, the debtor's wheat assets are certainly subject to a lien up to the value of the barley, even if the original loan consisted only of barley. The lien extends to the wheat as property available for collection. The lien is thus certain. The defendant seeks to remove it only by a doubt: if the loan was barley, perhaps the claimant's formulation waived the barley claim and thereby removed the lien; if the loan was wheat, the lien certainly remains. A doubtful removal cannot displace a definite lien. The defendant must therefore pay up to the value of the barley, just as one who says "I do not know whether I paid" remains liable because the underlying obligation is certain and the discharge doubtful. This reasoning applies to wheat and barley, but not to two tam oxen. A tam pays only from its own body. There is therefore no certain lien upon the larger tam ox if perhaps only the smaller ox caused the damage, even up to the value of the smaller. One tam ox does not become subject to payment for the damage caused by the other. The lien on each particular ox is therefore doubtful from the outset. This is equivalent to "I do not know whether I borrowed," and the tortfeasor is exempt. Perhaps only the smaller ox gored and any claim regarding it was waived. Rambam's distinction is therefore precise. There is yet another possible supporting approach. Tosafot, as I discussed at length in the treatise on seizure, hold that where there are two independent doubts and the defendant himself is uncertain, property can be extracted from the possessor. Rambam may hold that the rule that the claimant's omission amounts to waiver or admission is not absolutely certain but itself doubtful, as the Sema writes in seif katan 25 and as I explained above at length in reconciling the Rosh's distinction between seizure before and after the doubt arose. We would then have two doubts: perhaps the claimant did not waive the barley at all; and even if he did, perhaps the actual debt was wheat. In both respects the defendant himself is uncertain. Under the double doubt principle, one can extract from the possessor up to the value of the barley. Again, this works for wheat and barley because the same general assets can satisfy either obligation. With two tam oxen, however, where payment comes only from the body of the particular damaging ox, when one seeks to collect from the smaller ox there is only one relevant doubt, whether the claimant waived the right to it. The second doubt, perhaps the larger ox caused the damage, does not help, because even if the larger ox caused it, the smaller ox is not subject to that damage at all. The reverse is equally true when attempting to collect from the larger ox. Each ox is subject to only one possible source of liability, so there is no double doubt. The case therefore remains governed by the rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley, and the tortfeasor is exempt. These are clear distinctions. The Rama's rejection of Rambam is therefore not decisive. Rambam's ruling is well grounded and correct, as the Shach himself ultimately indicates that the Rama's contrary determination is not compelling.
