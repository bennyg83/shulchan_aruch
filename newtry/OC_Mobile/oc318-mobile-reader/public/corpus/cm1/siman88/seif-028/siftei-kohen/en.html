Rather, he pays the fifty that he admitted, and concerning the amount in the document that he denied, the holder of the document swears and collects. The borrower is not believed through a migo that he could have denied the oral debt and admitted the documented debt. Although a migo may sometimes be asserted even against a document, as below, siman 296, seif 2, here the document always remained fully effective. In siman 296, by contrast, had he claimed unavoidable accident, the document would have had no force.
For a document is treated like land, etc. The Sema writes that even if the borrower owns no land at all, including encumbered property, the document is nevertheless treated like land because it was written for collection from land. He infers this from the Tur's later distinction that a handwritten obligation is treated only as an oral loan. This inference is not correct. Just as an oral loan is not treated as land according to the Rosh where the borrower has no land, despite the Torah lien, so too a documented loan is not treated as land where there is no encumbered property. Tosafot explicitly states at the beginning of Bava Metzia that if the borrower has no encumbered property, even a documented loan creates a Torah oath. The Beit Yosef likewise writes that without land the document is not comparable to produce attached to land. The Bach and Gidulei Terumah also state this plainly. The Rosh's case is one where the borrower has no unencumbered land, or the creditor waived the lien on it, but encumbered land exists and its lien was not waived. Thus an oral loan requires a Torah oath, while a document does not, because the document permits collection from the encumbered property. The Tur's ruling concerning a handwritten obligation likewise concerns the same case, where there is no unencumbered property but there is encumbered property. Tosafot explains Rabbi Yochanan's distinction in precisely this setting. The Beit Yosef further writes that a document is “here it is” even when the borrower has no land at all, though if he has no assets whatsoever, including movables, it may not be. Rashi appears to ground “here it is” in the document's lien upon land, but Rashi discusses a case where the defendant does not admit the debt. Where he admits, the Beit Yosef is correct that the documented debt is “here it is” even without land, because many authorities hold that any obligation that cannot be denied is treated as “here it is.” See what I wrote below, seif 29, and above, siman 75, seif 4, in the gloss, seif katan 12.
It is like an oral admission, etc. The Sema notes that the Tur, seif 38, cites Tosafot and the Rosh that, since we rule that a lien exists by Torah law, partial admission concerning an oral loan does not require a Torah oath unless the borrower has no unencumbered land or the creditor waived the lien on his land. The Mechaber and Rama did not mention this view and ruled generally like Ramban and other Geonim, who do not distinguish. This means that they hold that a denial of land exists only with a document that permits collection from encumbered property. Maharam of Lublin likewise rules this way. See Tosafot, Bava Batra 175a.
