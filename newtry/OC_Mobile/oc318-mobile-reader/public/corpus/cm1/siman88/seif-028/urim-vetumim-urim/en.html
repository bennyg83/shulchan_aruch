If one does not employ a migo from one monetary matter to another, the issue is simpler still.
A document is like land. Sema explains that even if the debtor presently owns no land, the written obligation is treated as a land-secured claim because if he did own land the document would create a lien on it. Shach objects. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length and concluded that actual land is required, whether unencumbered or already sold. If he owns no land, the claim is not treated as land-secured. It is possible that even if he owns land acquired only later, but the deed did not include an after-acquired-property clause, the claim is not treated as land-secured, because he may sell that land and purchasers cannot be foreclosed upon. This remains unresolved. See Tumim.

Sema writes that Tur cites Tosafot and Rosh as holding that even an ordinary partial admission does not generate an oath according to the view that property liens are Torah law, unless the debtor owns no land. Mechaber and Rama omitted this. See Tumim. I wrote that no authority actually holds that in the broad form stated. The real dispute concerns where there had been a document that is not authenticated, or was lost, and the debtor admits it. Some authorities treat even that as a land-secured obligation. See there. Although Mechaber rules here that it is not land-secured and therefore a Torah oath applies, in Even HaEzer 96:15 he brings a dispute concerning this issue.
