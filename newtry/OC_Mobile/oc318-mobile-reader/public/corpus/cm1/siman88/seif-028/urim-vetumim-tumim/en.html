That a promissory note is treated like land. The Sema holds, as he explains at length in the Derishah, that even if the debtor never had land and presently has none, a debt evidenced by a promissory note is nevertheless considered a claim secured by land merely because a lien on land is mentioned in the note. The Taz follows him. Their proof is from the Tur, who cites Tosafot and the Rosh as saying that according to the view that a lien on property is of Torah origin, one cannot find a Torah oath in a case of partial admission and the like except where the debtor has no land. They infer that a note must be treated as a land secured obligation even where the debtor has no land. If the debtor actually had land, then even an oral loan or a handwritten acknowledgment would also be secured by land. The distinction must therefore be that a formal promissory note itself is treated as a land secured claim even where there is no land. The Shach disagrees, and the Bach appears to agree with him. In their view, if the debtor has no land, even a promissory note has no land lien and cannot be called a land secured claim. The reason a promissory note is sometimes called a land secured obligation, while an oral loan and handwritten acknowledgment are not, is that the creditor may waive his right against the debtor personally without waiving his right against encumbered property. It is for that reason that the formal note is described as secured by land. It is true that Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Metzia, in the sugya of heilech, write a distinction of this kind. Yet it is difficult to fit into the Tur's language. The Tur should not have stated without qualification that a promissory note is a land secured obligation. He should have specified the circumstances described by the Shach, where the creditor waived recourse against the debtor personally but not against encumbered property. There is another difficulty. The Tur first cites Ramban's position that if the note was lost or cannot be authenticated, it is not considered a land secured claim. The Tur then writes: but Tosafot and the Rosh say that partial admission requiring a Torah oath can be found only where the debtor has no land, etc. This clearly indicates that Tosafot and the Rosh disagree with Ramban. According to the Shach, however, Ramban could fully agree with their statement. Ramban may simply be speaking about the kind of note described by the Shach, where the creditor waived recourse against the debtor personally but retained recourse against encumbered property. So long as the note exists and is enforceable, he can collect from purchasers and it is therefore a land secured claim. If the note is lost or cannot be authenticated, he can no longer collect from purchasers and it is no longer a land secured claim. There would be no dispute at all. It therefore appears that the Derishah was troubled by Tosafot in Shevuot. Regarding Rabbi Yochanan's statement that one who denies a loan supported by witnesses is liable for a false oath offering, whereas one who denies a loan evidenced by a promissory note is exempt because it is a claim secured by land, Tosafot and the Rosh ask that Rabbi Yochanan holds that liens are of Torah origin. If so, even a loan supported by witnesses should be a claim secured by land. Tosafot answer that the debtor has no land, or that the creditor waived the land lien. The question still remains: if the debtor has no land, then the loan evidenced by a promissory note should likewise not be land secured. Tosafot in Bava Metzia themselves notice this and answer, as the Shach does, that the creditor may have waived recourse against the debtor personally without waiving recourse against encumbered property. But in Shevuot they do not raise or answer this second question. Maharam of Lublin already expresses astonishment at this in his novellae to Bava Metzia. The Derishah sought to prove from the omission that Tosafot there hold that even without existing land, a formal promissory note itself is considered land secured. In truth, the Shach is correct that such a proposition has no sound basis. The difficulty in Tosafot and the Rosh can be resolved easily. They may hold, like most authorities, that after the Sages enacted that an oral loan cannot be collected from purchasers, there is no operative lien at all upon the borrower's land as against purchasers. At every moment the borrower may sell his land without the creditor being able to prevent it. The fact that the creditor can collect from land that remains in the borrower's possession does not reflect a continuing property lien. It is merely the general rule that collection from the debtor himself may be made even from the cloak on his back. Thus an oral loan no longer has the status of a land secured obligation. Tosafot's question there, however, concerns the Torah's original law. The Torah imposes an oath in a case of partial admission. According to the view that liens are of Torah origin, an ordinary debt under Torah law would be secured by land. Where, then, is the Torah oath of partial admission found? Tosafot answer that under the Torah law one finds such an oath where the debtor has no land or where the creditor waived the land lien. Rabbi Yochanan's statement, by contrast, concerns practical halachah after the Sages enacted that an oral loan does not collect from purchasers and thereby removed its land lien. Therefore, even where the debtor owns land, one who falsely denies an oral loan supported by witnesses can be liable for the oath offering because the debt is no longer treated as a land secured claim. A formal promissory note is different. Its lien against purchasers remains in force because the Sages did not remove it. Everything is thus properly explained. This, in my view, is clearly the Tur's understanding. For this reason, throughout siman 75 and siman 87, where the Tur discusses the rules of Torah oaths and the distinctions between Torah and rabbinic oaths, he never states that the borrower must specifically be a person who owns no land or that the creditor must have waived the lien. As practical halachah, no such condition exists. Even where the borrower owns land, the Sages removed the purchaser lien from an oral loan. This applies to an oral loan or handwritten acknowledgment. A formal promissory note, however, retains its land lien when the debtor has land. This is the actual distinction between a formal note and an oral or handwritten debt. The Tur cites Tosafot and the Rosh to establish precisely this conceptual point. The Ran in Shevuot, whose words I discussed at length above in siman 75, answers the original difficulty differently. What does it matter that the debtor's property is legally subject to the debt? If the creditor later seeks to collect from purchasers, the purchasers will deny the debtor's admission and say, "What does your admission matter to us? You are speaking falsely. You never borrowed, or you already paid." Accordingly, the admitted portion is not practically collectible from purchasers and should not be considered an admission concerning land, even if the Torah recognizes a general land lien. Ramban too accepts this answer, as the Rosh cites in his name at the end of chapter Shevuat HaPikadon. Tosafot and especially the Rosh there reject this answer. They hold that once the underlying obligation is one for which land is legally encumbered, it is treated as a land secured claim even if, in practice, the creditor cannot collect from purchasers because they will not accept the debtor's admission. Therefore, under Torah law, an ordinary loan secured by land does not generate a Torah oath of partial admission, and we must establish the Torah's oath in a case where the debtor has no land. The practical difference between these approaches emerges with a formal promissory note whose purchaser lien normally remains intact but which has been lost, while the debtor admits that it existed. According to Ramban, the debt is no longer a land secured claim because the creditor cannot collect from purchasers. They are entitled to reject the debtor's admission and say that perhaps there never was a note or perhaps the debt was repaid. The debtor's partial admission can therefore generate a Torah oath. According to Tosafot and the Rosh, however, the underlying debt is of a type that carried a land lien from its inception. The inability to collect from purchasers because the note was lost does not change the nature of the debt. This is exactly like the Torah law of partial admission discussed above, where the purchaser may not practically believe the debtor but the claim is still characterized as land secured because of the underlying lien. Thus, even when the note has been lost or cannot be authenticated, if the debtor admits its existence, no Torah oath of partial admission arises. This is clear and correct. See the Rosh at the end of chapter Shevuat HaPikadon and his words will explicitly support this explanation. The same is evident in chapter 2 of Ketubot. The Rosh discusses a husband who says, "I married you as a widow and your ketubah is one hundred," while the woman says, "I was a virgin and my ketubah is two hundred." He explains that the husband does not take a Torah oath as one who partially admits because the ketubah is a land secured obligation. The Rosh cites the Yerushalmi concerning a note stating unspecified sela'im where part of the writing was erased. The creditor says the amount was five and the debtor says it was three. Ben Azzai says that since the debtor partially admits the claim he must swear. The Sages say that this is not an admission of the same kind as the claim. The Rosh explains that the admission concerns land. Although the remaining text of the note itself proves only two sela'im, once the debtor admits a third, it is treated as though the third had also been written into the note. The denial concerns movable money while the admission concerns a land secured debt. The Yerushalmi then asks from the ketubah case. There, seemingly, the husband's admission is of the same kind as the woman's claim. She no longer holds a note in her hand. She says two hundred and he says one hundred. The Yerushalmi answers that everyone agrees she is entitled to one hundred, meaning that he cannot deny that basic amount. Therefore, it is treated as though she has a note for that one hundred, and that portion is land secured. At first glance this passage is very difficult. How can the Rosh cite the Yerushalmi when it appears to contradict the Bavli in Bava Metzia? In the Bavli's case of a note saying merely "sela'im," where the debtor admits three, the third sela is not treated as a land secured obligation. The Gemara explicitly distinguishes the first two sela'im, which are evidenced by the note, from the third, which exists only by the debtor's oral admission. There is another difficulty. The Mishnah concerning the ketubah applies both in a place where ketubot are ordinarily written and in a place where they are not, as is clear from Ketubot 16b, where Rabbi Elazar establishes the Mishnah in a place where ketubot are written and derives from it that a receipt may be written. In a place where ketubot are written, if the woman does not present her ketubah, the Rosh follows the Rif that Rabbi Yochanan's rule that one cannot plead payment against an obligation imposed by beit din does not apply. The husband can claim that he paid. How then can the Yerushalmi say that he cannot deny the first one hundred and that it is treated as though a note exists? According to what I have written, everything is correct. The baraita cited in the Bavli concerns a note that originally stated only the unspecified word "sela'im." An unspecified plural means two. No formal note ever existed for the third sela. Thus, when the debtor admits a third, that third amount is only an oral admission. There never was a land lien evidenced by the note for it. The Yerushalmi, however, speaks of a note that contained an amount which was later erased. If the note originally stated three, then the third sela was originally secured by the written note exactly like the first two. The fact that the writing is now erased and purchasers will not accept the debtor's admission does not matter according to the Rosh's view. The underlying third sela was originally a land secured debt. It is therefore treated as an admission concerning land, just as I explained above regarding a lost or unauthenticated note. The same applies to the ketubah in a place where ketubot are ordinarily written. Originally, when the ketubah existed, the basic one hundred was land secured. Even though the woman now cannot collect from purchasers because she lacks the document and they may claim it was paid, the underlying obligation was originally secured. According to the Rosh, that is sufficient to characterize the husband's admission as concerning land. Why then does the Yerushalmi emphasize that the husband cannot deny the basic one hundred? Because the Mishnah also applies in a place where no ketubah document is ordinarily written. In such a place, if the husband could plead payment, there may never have been any written instrument or enduring land lien at all. The obligation could resemble an oral debt whose purchaser lien was removed. Therefore the Yerushalmi explains that everyone agrees the woman is entitled to the basic one hundred. In a place where no writing is customary, the obligation created by beit din itself gives her an unavoidable basic claim. But in a place where ketubot are written, once the obligation was originally secured, it makes no difference that he may now plead payment. The Rosh's words are thus entirely clear. The Rosh does not explain the Sages in the Yerushalmi the way the Bavli explains its own baraita, namely that the first two sela'im are a land secured admission and therefore, had the debtor merely admitted two, there would be no oath, while an admission of three still leaves him as one who voluntarily returns a lost item because he could have admitted only the two already stated in the note. The Raah explains along those lines. The Rosh does not, because in the Yerushalmi case the note originally contained more and was later erased. There is no written support for the proposition that only two were owed. Thus the debtor is not like one voluntarily returning a lost item by admitting the third. In the Bavli's case, by contrast, the note itself says merely "sela'im," which ordinarily means two. That writing supports the debtor's claim that no more than two were owed. He could therefore be viewed as voluntarily admitting the third, and Tosafot explicitly use the fact that the note supports him to explain why he is considered one returning a lost item even though ordinarily one who partially admits has a migo to deny everything. The note's text gives him unusual boldness to deny beyond the two because it supports his position. In the Yerushalmi, where the original amount was written and later erased, there is no such support from the document. The debtor cannot be considered one returning a lost item merely because he admits three. The Rosh therefore explains the Sages' exemption by saying that the third sela itself is treated as a land secured admission. Everything fits precisely. From this Yerushalmi the Rosh could indeed derive his rule that where a debt was originally secured by a note, the loss or erasure of the note does not remove its characterization as a land secured obligation for purposes of a Torah oath. One may ask according to this approach from the Gemara's question against the opinion that heilech exempts: why did Scripture need a special exclusion to exempt land from an oath? Even if heilech generally exempts, the exclusion for land should still be necessary in a case of partial admission where land was originally encumbered but later sold, according to Tosafot and the Rosh. Purchasers will not accept the debtor's admission, so the creditor cannot actually collect even a needle's worth of land from them. The admission would therefore not be heilech. Yet according to the Rosh it remains a land secured admission because the original lien existed. Tosafot themselves explain that when the Gemara asks why a verse is needed to exclude land, the question is not literally whether the Torah could have used the verse for some other law. The question is why the Mishnah needed to teach the land exclusion as an independent application. By the era of the Mishnah, the Sages had already removed the purchaser lien from oral loans. Thus the ordinary cases before the Mishnah would not involve a surviving land lien on an oral partial admission. This removes much of the difficulty. Still, one can ask from a formal promissory note that was later lost. According to the Rosh and Tosafot, if the debtor admits the debt it remains a land secured claim, but because the note is lost the creditor cannot collect from purchasers who reject the admission. It is therefore not heilech. Why then was the Mishnah's exclusion of land not needed for precisely such a case? We must say that the proposition that a formal note is a land secured claim is itself accepted even according to the opinion that liens are not of Torah origin. The Gemara routinely treats a formal promissory note as land secured without making that characterization depend upon the Torah origin of liens. Even if liens are not of Torah origin, the Sages established a purchaser lien for formal notes, and actual land becomes legally subject to the debt. But this is so only when the note exists and is enforceable. If the note is no longer present and only the debtor admits that it once existed, according to the opinion that liens are not of Torah origin there is no original Torah lien to preserve. The rabbinic lien was enacted only so long as the creditor possesses the enforceable note. Once the note is lost, the very rabbinic framework that created the purchaser lien no longer applies. The Sages said that he may collect from purchasers when the note is in his hand, and they equally determined that the lien is not enforceable when it is not. The Rosh and those who agree with him say that a lost note remains a land secured claim only according to the view that liens are of Torah origin. Under that view, the underlying debt carried a Torah lien from the outset. Although the Sages removed the lien from oral loans, they left the Torah lien intact in the case of a formal promissory note. The loss of the document may prevent practical collection from purchasers, but it does not change the original Torah character of the debt. According to the view that liens are not of Torah origin, however, once the rabbinic purchaser lien becomes unenforceable because the note is absent, no land secured status remains. The Gemara's question concerning heilech can therefore be understood according to the opinion that liens are not of Torah origin. Indeed, the sugya concerning an exchange indicates that Rav and Shmuel hold that heilech exempts, and they also hold that liens are not of Torah origin. Under their view, there is no case of a lost note that simultaneously remains a land secured claim and yet fails to be heilech. If the note exists, the claim is land secured and collection can proceed against the encumbered property. If the note is lost, the rabbinic lien itself no longer gives the claim land secured status. The Gemara's question is therefore properly framed. This also provides one way to answer a point I raised above at the beginning of siman 75. Tosafot there ask from Rabbi Yochanan's statement in Shevuot rather than asking internally from the baraita itself why there should be any difference between the first two sela'im stated in the note and the third sela admitted orally. One may say that the baraita's tanna could hold that liens are not of Torah origin, as I wrote above in siman 39 regarding Rabbi Meir. The first two are nevertheless secured because they are in the formal note, while the third admitted orally is not. Tosafot therefore challenge from Rabbi Yochanan, who himself holds that liens are of Torah origin. This is straightforward, although, as I wrote above, the matter may still be somewhat strained.
If he cannot establish it, the Mechaber states the law without qualification in accordance with the Ramban. As I wrote in the preceding seif katan, Tosafot and the Rosh disagree, for in any event there was a lien upon land, and it makes no difference that the purchasers would not believe him. I likewise found in the Asufot Zekenim to Bava Metzia, in the name of one of the great authorities, an explanation of the statement, “But the document that he admits to is heilech,” as referring to an uncertified document, so that his admission is required. According to that authority, the explanation must be as follows. One might ask why the Gemara assumes that the baraita concerns an uncertified document. The Gemara holds that something which cannot be denied is not considered an admission at all. If so, how could Rabbi Meir say concerning one who says, “three,” that he is returning a lost object because he has a migo that he could have denied everything? His admission to two would not be an admission in the legal sense at all. Therefore the Gemara establishes the case as one of an uncertified document. Then he is indeed returning a lost object, because even the two are within the category of an admission, since he could have denied them by claiming that the document was forged. The Gemara then asks why, regarding the two themselves, he should be exempt from an oath. If the reason is that he could have claimed forgery, then he has a migo that he could have denied everything. Rather, once he admits to the document, it is heilech. The Gemara answers that he is exempt either because the document supports him or because it is a claim involving a lien on land. Even though he could have claimed forgery, now that he admits the document it is nevertheless a land lien, as Tosafot and the Rosh wrote. This also resolves the question raised by all the commentators, namely, what did the questioner initially think? Did he not know that one does not take an oath concerning denial of a land lien? According to this explanation, it is clear. The questioner thought that since the document was uncertified and was established only through the borrower’s own admission, it was not a land lien, which is in fact the view of the Ramban. The Gemara answers that it nevertheless is a land lien, in accordance with Tosafot and the Rosh. This also resolves another difficulty. According to the initial assumption, before the Gemara knew that it was a land lien and exempted the defendant only because of heilech, why did it seek support from the baraita rather than from the Mishnah at the beginning of the second chapter of Ketubot, as the Rosh asks? There too the husband should have been obligated to take a Torah oath as one who admits part of the claim, and necessarily he is exempt because of heilech. Likewise, according to the answer that the document supports him, without relying either on heilech or on a land lien, the Mishnah in Ketubot remains difficult. There the document cannot be said to support the husband, because the woman’s ketubah does not itself establish whether she is entitled to one hundred or two hundred. According to what was explained, however, there is no difficulty. With a certified document the Gemara already knew that there is a land lien, and there the obligation is certified because a woman has a ketubah by stipulation and by act of beit din. Only here did the Gemara initially assume that the case concerns an uncertified document, because it had not yet introduced the rationale that the document itself supports him. We, however, rule that even with a certified document he is considered one who returns a lost object, because the Gemara introduced the additional rationale that the document supports him, and in every form of the case he is therefore returning a lost object, as Tosafot wrote. The inference is thus sound. If so, the great authority cited above likewise follows the approach of Tosafot and the Rosh. Perhaps that authority held like Tosafot in the chapter Eizehu Neshekh, that where the borrower himself does not claim forgery, beit din does not make a forgery claim for the purchasers and we do not suspect collusion. In that case it is indeed a land lien. According to the Ramban, however, and as I explained at length below in siman 106, we nevertheless make the forgery claim on behalf of the purchasers. If so, there would no longer be any lien upon land. This is somewhat strained, however, because it would mean that the questioner and the answerer in the Gemara disagree on that separate rule. However, however the matter is understood, Tosafot and the Rosh certainly disagree with the Ramban. I am nevertheless surprised that the Tur cites the words of Tosafot and the Rosh as practical law. The Rosh wrote at the beginning of the second chapter of Ketubot, as cited above, that the husband does not swear because his admission concerns a lien upon land. He then wrote that even after the enactment that a woman may collect from movable property, since one who makes a claim contrary to an act of beit din accomplishes nothing, this is heilech because he cannot deny the obligation. The Rosh thus taught that after the enactment of the Geonim a document is no longer called a land lien, and the exemption from a Torah oath rests instead upon heilech. Accordingly, where a document was lost and the debtor can deny the obligation, there is neither a land lien nor heilech, since he can deny it. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes in chapter 17 of Hilkhot Ishut concerning the Rambam’s ruling where she says, “I was a virgin,” and he says, “you were a widow,” that the husband must take a Torah oath as one who admits part of the claim. The Raavad objects that this is a lien upon land. The Maggid Mishneh explains that the Rambam holds that after the enactment of the Geonim permitting collection from movable property there is no longer a land lien. He adds, however, that ordinarily the husband still cannot deny the obligation because one who contests an act of beit din is not believed. He therefore concludes that in a place where ketubot are written, if the ketubah was lost and the husband is believed to say that he paid it, he can deny the obligation, and because the enactment of the Geonim allows collection from movable property he is obligated in a Torah oath. See there. It is explicit from his words that without the Geonic enactment, even though the husband could claim payment, it would nevertheless be a land lien. This is precisely what I wrote concerning the Rosh and Tosafot. Yet after the Geonic enactment it is no longer a land lien. If so, according to our law the ruling concerning a document that was lost has fallen away entirely, because nowadays movable property may be collected under the Geonic enactment. It is true that in Even HaEzer siman 96 the Tur cites the Rosh’s view concerning the case where he says that she was a widow and she says that she was a virgin, ruling that in every place, even where ketubot are written, the husband takes only a heset oath. There too he is believed to claim payment and the principle concerning one who contests an act of beit din does not apply. The later authorities have already addressed this and explained that the Tur is speaking of a case where the husband had warned her not to accept payment except before witnesses, so that he can no longer deny even in a place where ketubot are written. But here the difficulty remains. One might answer, though with difficulty, that the Lechem Mishneh already asked why the Geonic enactment should matter at all. Any ordinary creditor may collect movable property under Talmudic law, and nevertheless a debt evidenced by a document is called a land lien. We must say that although the creditor may collect movable property as a matter of Talmudic law, movable property is not subject to a Torah lien. The Sages merely empowered him to collect movable property and descend to the debtor’s assets, while by Torah law collection is only from land, as the commentators wrote. Therefore a document is called a land lien even though movable property may also be collected, because the essential lien rests upon land. A ketubah may be different. Since the Geonim enacted collection from movable property, and according to the Rosh the enactment has a root and basis in Talmudic law, such as the case of the camels of Arabia from which a woman collects her ketubah, the obligation is not exclusively a lien upon land. If one were to say that the primary Torah lien is nevertheless upon land, that applies to ordinary creditors. A ketubah itself is rabbinic, and the Sages who created it also defined its mode of collection. There would therefore be no stronger reason to characterize the land than the movable property as the principal lien. Still, this distinction is strained. Regarding the Lechem Mishneh’s underlying question, it seems that when an ordinary creditor collects movable property, it is not because the movable property itself is encumbered. If it were, he should also collect it from orphans, just as when a person expressly designates his ox as an apotiki, thereby expressly encumbering movable property, the creditor may collect it from the heirs, as the Rashba writes. Rather, movable property is not subject to the lien at all. The debtor himself is liable even from the garment upon his shoulders because repayment of a debt is a mitzvah, but the orphans are not personally obligated in that mitzvah. Consequently, movable property is not part of the lien. Even when the creditor presents his document, his secured claim is only against land, not against movable property, and it is not a claim that combines land and utensils. By contrast, under the enactment of the Geonim, which permits collection even from the movable property of orphans, it is clear that they instituted an actual lien, as though the debtor had written that movable property would be encumbered to the creditor without attaching it to land. Such movable property is encumbered and may be collected from heirs, although not from purchasers because it lacks publicity, as the Rashba writes. Once movable property enters the lien in the same manner as land, a claim upon the document becomes like a claim upon land and utensils together. The Lechem Mishneh’s difficulty is therefore resolved on its own. Nevertheless, it appears that even apart from this, the later authorities already noted a difficulty in the Tur’s unqualified ruling that even in a place where ketubot are written, if the ketubah is not in her possession, the husband takes only a heset oath. The explanation that the Tur is speaking of a case where he warned her not to accept payment except before witnesses is strained, as is evident there. It may therefore be that the Tur was troubled by the Lechem Mishneh’s question: what practical difference is created by the Geonic enactment if every creditor already collects movable property? He did not accept the distinction offered above. He therefore understood the Rosh differently. The Mishnah teaches that where witnesses exist the husband is believed, which implies that without witnesses he is believed without a Torah oath because the admission concerns a land lien. One might ask how the Mishnah can state the rule without qualification. Granted, where he owns land, but where he owns no land what can be said? There the husband should have to take an oath. The answer is that if he owns no land the ketubah cannot be collected at all, because originally a ketubah was collected only from land. If he does own land, it is again a land lien. The difficulty is only with the Rif, who reproduces the ruling without qualification that the husband is believed. After the Geonim enacted collection from movable property, there is now a practical case where the husband owns no land yet the ketubah may still be collected. The Rosh therefore explains that even then he cannot deny, because a claim contrary to an act of beit din is ineffective. Alternatively, the Rosh intended to teach an independent law, that even if the husband has no land he does not swear. This is the meaning of his statement that “even after the enactment of the Geonim” the result is unchanged. There now exists a case where he owns no land, yet there is still no oath because he cannot deny the obligation. Accordingly, all the words of the Rosh apply where the debtor has no land. Where he does have land, the fact that movable property may also be collected does not remove the status of the claim as a land lien. Therefore the Tur, who speaks in the ordinary case where the debtor has land, as I explained in the preceding seif katan and as the Shach also wrote, rules here that the document remains a land lien. So too in Even HaEzer the Tur speaks ordinarily of a husband who owns land. It therefore always remains a land lien even though collection is also possible from movable property. This is why the Tur rules that the husband is obligated only in a heset oath. Similarly, in his Kitzur Piskei HaRosh he writes that where a ketubah was lost, namely in a place where ketubot are written, the husband takes only a heset oath, and he does not say that the law changed after the enactment. This understanding resolves the difficulty. Furthermore, a clearer and more correct explanation follows from what I write below in siman 107 in resolving the apparent contradiction in the words of the Tur and the Rosh. In one place they write that the Geonic enactment was not merely an enactment but follows from the underlying law, as above, because movable property is like the camels of Arabia. Elsewhere they write that it is not the underlying law but only an enactment. There I explained that in damages and similar obligations, where the creditor had no opportunity to stipulate and write security terms, we say that collection from movable property is rooted in the law itself. But where a document is written, it cannot be said that the parties relied upon movable property. Had they intended that, the document should have expressly mentioned both movable property and land. See there at length. Accordingly, the Rosh may hold that where reliance upon movable property and collection from it exist by the underlying law, a document is no longer called a land lien, because movable property too is truly encumbered. The claim is then like one who claims utensils and land and the defendant admits part of both, which produces a Torah oath. But where movable property is not collectible by the underlying law, and the parties did not rely upon it, and collection exists only because of the enactment, the original lien evidenced by the document never loses its character as a land lien. The Rosh’s words are therefore precise. When he writes that even after the Geonim a woman collects from movable property, he is not speaking of a place where ketubot are written. In such a place the parties could have written the security expressly, and thus collection from movable property is not by the underlying law. His question concerns a place where ketubot are not written, where the case is truly comparable to the camels of Arabia and movable property is legally encumbered. In that setting he answers that the husband cannot deny the obligation, because Rabbi Yochanan said that one who contests an act of beit din accomplishes nothing. The Rosh’s rulings thus apply in all circumstances. In a place where ketubot are written, the Geonic enactment does not alter the character of the original lien. In a place where they are not written, the husband cannot deny the obligation. Therefore the Tur rules without qualification, both in his Kitzur Piskei HaRosh and in the Tur itself, that there is never a Torah oath, for one of these two reasons always applies. It follows that the Tur acted correctly in not stating here that after the Geonic enactment, where a document was lost and the debtor can deny the obligation, it ceases to be a land lien. With an ordinary written loan, the rule resembles a place where documents are written. Movable property of heirs is collected only because of the enactment, not under the underlying law, as the Tur himself writes in siman 107. Therefore the document remains a land lien. The same is true even if we adopt only the explanation of the Sema and the Shach that Tosafot and the Rosh hold that even nowadays an admission of part of a documented claim is a land lien where the debtor owns land. If this were not so, the Geonic enactment itself would determine the law and there would be no land lien. Examine this carefully. This, however, is the view of the Tur and the Rosh. The Maggid Mishneh understands the Rambam differently. According to him, even in a place where documents are written, once the document is lost and the debtor can deny the obligation, after the Geonic enactment there is no longer a land lien. The Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 96 cites both opinions, the Rambam’s view and the Rosh’s view, whereas here the Mechaber rules without qualification like the Rambam. This is thus a case of an anonymous ruling followed elsewhere by a dispute. It also appears from the foregoing that where we say that if the debtor owns no land even a document is not considered a land lien, there is a question regarding land that he acquired later, where the document did not expressly include a clause encumbering property to be acquired in the future. Is that later acquired land enough to characterize the claim as a land lien? It seems that this depends upon a dispute. According to the Ramban, if the borrower dies the creditor cannot collect such property from the heirs at all, only from the borrower himself. If so, there is no true lien upon that property, and the case resembles movable property under the Talmudic rule governing ordinary creditors. Since the property never entered the lien, the claim cannot be treated as one upon both land and movable property. According to those who disagree with the Ramban and hold that even from heirs such later acquired property may be collected, it resembles movable property after the enactment of the Geonim. Although the creditor cannot collect it from purchasers, it nevertheless forms part of the lien, and the claim is therefore treated as one involving land.
