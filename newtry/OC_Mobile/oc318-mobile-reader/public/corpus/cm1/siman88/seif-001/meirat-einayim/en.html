Until he admits at least one perutah, etc. In the Perishah and Derishah I cited Tosafot, who explain that this is derived from the verse, "If a man gives his fellow money or utensils to guard." Money is compared to utensils. Just as "utensils" is plural, meaning at least two, so too money must be two units; and just as utensils are considered significant, even a utensil worth only a perutah has significance because workmanship was invested in it, so too the money must be significant, and less than a ma'ah is not called significant money. The verse then says, "that this is it," from which we learn that for the oath of partial admission both plurality and significance are required at the point of denial. It cannot refer merely to the initial claim, for if so the Torah's mention of money would be unnecessary, since significance could be learned from utensils. Rav teaches this, while Shmuel disagrees, but the halachah follows Rav. Thus if the verse is not needed for the initial claim, apply it to the final stage, the denial: the denied amount must be both plural and significant. It follows automatically that the original claim was greater, because the case is one in which the defendant admits part. See the Perishah and Derishah for more, unlike the explanation of the Ir Shushan and in accordance with Tosafot.
A perutah is the weight of half a barleycorn, etc. In the Perishah I wrote that according to Rama in Yoreh Deah at the beginning of siman 305, the five selaim for redemption of the firstborn, which are likewise pure silver and are calculated there by barleycorn weight, amount to two Polish gold coins. Since each sela is four dinarim, five selaim equal twenty dinarim. Two Polish gold coins equal twenty Polish dreier, so one dinar equals three Polish groschen, and two ma'ot, which are one third of a dinar, equal one Polish groschen, namely eight Lithuanian pfennigs, likewise eight Vienna pfennigs. One ma'ah is half a Polish groschen, or four pfennigs, and contains thirty two perutot, each equal to half a barleycorn of pure silver. Accordingly, one perutah would equal one eighth of a Vienna or Lithuanian pfennig. It is difficult to say that one eighth of a pfennig should be considered money sufficient for kiddushin or for beit din to entertain a claim. Perhaps in their days produce and other goods were inexpensive and a perutah could purchase a meaningful quantity, and therefore it was treated as money. If so, in our time, when a perutah buys almost nothing, perhaps kiddushin should not suffice with a perutah and redemption of the firstborn should not suffice with two gold coins. This requires study.
