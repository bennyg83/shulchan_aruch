And the denial must be at least two ma'ah of silver. Rambam, chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en VeNitan, writes: although every mention of silver in the Torah means a sacred shekel, which is twenty ma'ah, and every mention of silver instituted by the Sages refers to the Jerusalem coinage, etc., nevertheless, because the requirement that the amount denied in the claim be two silver pieces is of rabbinic origin, they established it as two silver pieces of Jerusalem, which are two ma'ah. The Maggid Mishneh questions Rambam's statement that this requirement is rabbinic and writes that it requires study, since it is derived from Scripture. As for why the amount is two ma'ah rather than two shekalim, the Gemara in the first chapter of Kiddushin raises this question and answers it, and perhaps Rambam had a different approach there. I do not understand his words, because Rambam follows the version of Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash, as the Ritva writes in Kiddushin in his name. The Gemara asks: is it not a rule that every mention of silver in the Torah means Tyrian silver, namely shekalim? Yet we learned that the claim must be two silver pieces. According to the version of Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, the answer is as follows: Scripture states "silver or utensils." Just as utensils means two and something significant, so too silver means two and something significant. The question was why two ma'ah suffice rather than two shekalim. The answer is that Scripture juxtaposed silver to utensils. Just as a utensil, once it qualifies as a utensil fit for its function, is considered significant and is called a utensil, and one takes an oath concerning it, so too any silver coin that is legal tender is significant for this purpose and one takes an oath concerning it. Nevertheless, there remains the difficulty noted by the Ritva in the name of Ramban: if every legal tender coin counts as silver, why is a woman betrothed with a perutah, which is called silver, whereas for a claim two ma'ah are required? The juxtaposition should establish that every legal tender coin is called silver, just as in betrothal. Ramban answers this with difficulty. Therefore, to resolve this difficulty, Rambam holds that by Torah law one indeed takes an oath over two perutot, since that amount is called silver just as in betrothal. The Sages, however, saw fit not to impose a Torah oath over so small an amount and instituted specifically two ma'ah. Rashi likewise writes there, in one explanation, that the Torah entrusted the measure to the Sages and they fixed it by their assessment. This is straightforward. This is Rambam's intent in saying that the requirement that the denial of the claim be two silver pieces is rabbinic: by Torah law two perutot suffice, but the Sages required two ma'ah and established ordinary provincial coinage rather than Tyrian coinage. This is straightforward and correct.
