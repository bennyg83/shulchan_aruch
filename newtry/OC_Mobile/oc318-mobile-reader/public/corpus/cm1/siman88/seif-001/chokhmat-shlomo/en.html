Seif 1: One who admits part of the claim is obligated to swear, etc. Note: For a long time I was troubled by Tosafot in the first chapter of Bava Metzia 3a, who ask from where we know that one who denies the entire claim is exempt from a Torah oath. Tosafot say that one cannot derive it from the law of one witness because perhaps the witness case is one where the claimant sues on the basis of the witness. Why did they not answer more simply that the law of one witness is needed for a case where the defendant denies less than two silver pieces? Such a defendant would otherwise be exempt, because one who denies everything should not be more stringent than one who admits part, yet one witness obligates him to swear even on a perutah. I later saw that Maayan HaChochma on Bava Metzia cites this point in the name of Shita Mekubetzet. It is therefore difficult why Tosafot did not write it. With Hashem’s help, the matter can be explained from Shevuot 40a. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak said in the name of Shmuel that the minimum of two silver pieces applies only to a lender’s claim and a partial admission, but where the lender’s claim is supported by one witness, even if the defendant denied only a perutah he must swear. The reason is the verse, “One witness shall not rise against a man for any iniquity or sin.” He does not rise for monetary punishment, but he does rise for an oath. The baraita also teaches: wherever two witnesses obligate a person to pay money, one witness obligates him to swear. Why does the Gemara need both derivations? The verse itself, “he rises for an oath,” might still refer only to a claim of two silver pieces. Therefore the Gemara cites the baraita that wherever two witnesses impose monetary liability, one witness imposes an oath. But one may then ask from where the baraita itself knows this. For that purpose the verse is necessary. The verse’s formulation assumes a possible comparison of one witness to two with respect to monetary liability, and then limits one witness by saying that he does not rise for iniquity or sin but only for an oath. Just as, under the initial possibility that one witness would be like two for money, there would have been no distinction between a claim of two silver pieces and a claim of a perutah, so after the verse teaches that one witness rises only for an oath, his power to impose an oath applies in all amounts. This is the meaning of the sugya. Accordingly, Tosafot’s question is resolved. Once we already know that one who denies the entire claim is wholly exempt, yet two witnesses can obligate him to pay, there is no reason to distinguish with two witnesses between two silver pieces and less. Two witnesses therefore impose monetary liability even for a perutah, and by the rule of the baraita one witness imposes an oath even for a perutah. But if we had assumed that one who denies the entire claim is also obligated to swear, we could have said that even two witnesses are effective in monetary matters only where the claim is at least two silver pieces. The reason would be that the presumption of possession is extremely strong. Just as we do not follow a majority to extract money, perhaps witnesses themselves do not directly extract money where the claim is below the threshold. Their effect would instead be indirect: the defendant is already obligated by Torah law to swear, and two witnesses establish that his oath would be false. We therefore cannot administer the oath to him, and he becomes one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, who must pay. On that theory, two witnesses would impose payment only where there is an underlying Torah oath, namely a claim of at least two silver pieces. Below that amount, where there is no oath, even two witnesses would not generate liability by this route. One witness likewise would not impose an oath below two silver pieces. For this reason Tosafot properly say that one witness can serve as the proof that complete denial is exempt. Once complete denial is exempt, two witnesses must impose money independently, even below two silver pieces, and one witness therefore imposes an oath even on a perutah. Tosafot then answer that perhaps the case of one witness is where the claimant sues on the basis of the witness. In that circumstance, even under the alternative theory, two witnesses can create a sufficiently certain claim to trigger the monetary consequence. A claim based on two witnesses is not treated as an ordinary uncertain claim. At the beginning of the second chapter of Ketubot the Gemara even entertained that a statistical majority might convert the matter into the equivalent of a definite claim. Although that is not the final halacha, two witnesses certainly render the factual assertion as definite for this purpose. Thus, even if complete denial otherwise required an oath only on a definite claim, two witnesses could obligate payment, and one witness could correspondingly impose an oath. Tosafot’s formulation is therefore precise. Examine this carefully.
