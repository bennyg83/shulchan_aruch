And he admitted one. If the admitted utensil is still intact, it should be "here it is," because an intact deposit remains in the owner's legal possession. If it is no longer intact and the custodian must pay its value, then if the utensil itself was worth less than a perutah the monetary obligation is now less than a perutah, and beit din ordinarily does not attend to such an amount. It is strained to establish the verse specifically with a loan of needles rather than a deposit, since the verse itself speaks of items given for safekeeping. It appears that where an item had the legal status of a significant utensil when entrusted, the later monetary replacement can remain legally cognizable even if its current value is below a perutah. Bava Kamma teaches that one who stole property worth a perutah and it later depreciated must return either the object or its value even if the current value is less than a perutah, because the duty of restitution arose when it had monetary significance. The same principle can apply to a lost utensil. With custodians the matter is less clear, because the custodian's property may become liable only at the time of negligence or loss, when the value may already be below a perutah. Still, as I wrote in siman 87, even a liability that beit din cannot directly enforce can count as partial admission where the person remains genuinely obligated before Heaven. Thus the original status of the utensil can preserve the character of a valid admission even though the present monetary replacement is under a perutah.
