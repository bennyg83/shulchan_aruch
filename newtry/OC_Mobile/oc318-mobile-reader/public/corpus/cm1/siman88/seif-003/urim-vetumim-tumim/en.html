Ten needles worth a perutah. This is the view of Rambam and those who follow him. The Rosh and Tur, however, as well as Tosafot in Kiddushin, disagree. They hold that utensils were excluded so that they need not equal two ma'ah in the amount denied, but in any event each utensil must be worth a perutah, both for the denial and for the admission. This is also evident from Tosafot in Bava Kamma 103, who ask concerning the law that one who robs another and swore falsely to him concerning an item worth a perutah must carry it after him: does not the claim have to equal two silver pieces? They answer either that he swore on his own initiative, or that the claim was for two silver pieces and the admission was a perutah. Their explanation is that both parties made false assertions: in truth he owed only two perutot, the claimant demanded two silver pieces from him, and he admitted only a perutah, thereby becoming liable for an oath, which he then falsely swore. Thus he robbed him of a perutah. See there. They did not answer that the case concerned a utensil, for which an oath may be imposed even over a perutah. Rather, they evidently hold like Rashi that a utensil need not even be worth a perutah. If so, why does the Mishnah teach that he swore falsely concerning a perutah? It could have taught even less. It is likewise evident that they hold there like Tosafot in Shevuot, that the rule that one witness obligates an oath on a claim of a perutah is not accepted by all opinions, but only by Shmuel and not by Rav. Had they held as Tosafot write in Ketubot, that all agree to this rule, they should simply have explained that he swore to contradict the witness and a perutah suffices. Rather, they hold that Rav does not accept this rule, and therefore they explain the Mishnah even according to Rav so that the Mishnah should not pose a difficulty for him. Accordingly, there was no need for the author of Tosafot Yom Tov to explain the Mishnah as Tosafot did, because since we rule that this law concerning one witness applies according to all opinions, even Rav, the Mishnah may simply be explained as a case in which he swore to contradict the witness. The same view appears in Tosafot, Bava Metzia 3, s.v. mipnei, where they ask how we know that one who denies everything is exempt from an oath. If the proof is that otherwise Scripture would not need a verse obligating an oath through one witness, perhaps that verse is needed where the claimant makes his claim on the basis of the witness. This depends upon the dispute whether a claimant can impose an oath on the basis of one witness. Tosafot do not give the simpler answer that the verse is needed where the amount denied is less than two silver pieces, since one witness nevertheless obligates an oath, whereas total denial does not. One cannot answer that even total denial might have been thought not to require two silver pieces, for then why would partial admission require two silver pieces? If the reason is that he admitted part, that itself would be an artifice that enables him to escape the oath. We must therefore say that Tosafot hold that according to Rav even one witness requires two silver pieces. Tosafot therefore sought to formulate the question even according to Rav, and rejected the distinction based on a claim made through a witness.
Now, according to what I wrote in the preceding seif katan, that Rambam holds that by Torah law even silver worth a perutah suffices, it necessarily follows that when the Sages said that utensils are excluded "for whatever they are," they meant even if worth less than a perutah. Otherwise utensils would be no different from silver. Rambam thus has a conclusive proof for his position.
