Ten needles worth one perutah. In his novellae to the chapter Kol HaNishba'in, Rashba is uncertain whether a woman can be betrothed with a utensil worth less than one perutah, since a utensil has independent legal significance as though it were worth a perutah. Ketzot HaChoshen challenges this from the Gemara in Kiddushin, which says that a woman is not acquired through chalipin because chalipin can be effected with less than one perutah, while a woman does not consent to acquire herself for less than one perutah. Chalipin is effected only with a utensil, so this seems to show that even a utensil does not effect kiddushin if it is worth less than one perutah. There is no difficulty. In a kinyan sudar, it is effective even if the recipient grasps only part of the utensil, because grasping part is treated as grasping the whole, as stated in Bava Metzia. A mere part of a utensil certainly is not treated as a utensil for purposes of partial admission or kiddushin. Moreover, even an animal is called a utensil for purposes of kinyan sudar, and according to one opinion even a coin can be used. Thus the "utensil" of kinyan sudar is not comparable to the utensil considered significant for partial admission. For kiddushin, a genuinely significant utensil is required.
And he admitted one of them. Ketzot HaChoshen asks: if the utensil still exists, this is "here it is," and if it was lost, the defendant owes only money. For money, the admission must be worth one perutah. According to what I wrote above in siman 87, however, in a case of theft an object standing in the thief's house is not "here it is," because stolen property does not stand in the owner's legal domain. The case can therefore arise where the custodian misappropriated the deposit, making him a thief, so that the admission is not "here it is." It also appears that just as the Torah excluded land from the law of oaths, and siman 95 explains that even if he dug pits, ditches, and caves in the land, so that the present admission is merely a monetary obligation, he remains exempt because that monetary obligation arose from land, so too when the Torah included utensils even below the value of one perutah, the law follows the source of the obligation. Even if the utensil was lost and the present admission is monetary, he is liable because the liability arose from utensils. In addition, since if the utensil is later found it belongs to the depositor, the admission can still be characterized as an admission concerning a utensil.
