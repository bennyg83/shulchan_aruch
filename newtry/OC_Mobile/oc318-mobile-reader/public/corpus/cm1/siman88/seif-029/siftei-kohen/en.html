Even if it contains a clause of credibility, etc. In my humble opinion this ruling requires study. It appears that the Mechaber ruled this way based on what he wrote in Bedek HaBayis, that perhaps the Rambam also agrees. In truth, however, it appears to me that the Rambam holds that a handwritten obligation containing a credibility clause is heilech, because the Rambam explains the reason as being that the defendant cannot deny it. The Maggid Mishneh and Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia also explain the Rambam this way, contrary to Bedek HaBayis's understanding. Lechem Mishneh, chapter 4 of Hilchos To'en, likewise understands the Rambam this way. His challenge there to the Nimukei Yosef, as though the Nimukei Yosef did not understand the Rambam that way, is puzzling, for the Nimukei Yosef actually writes exactly this. I later saw that the Kesef Mishneh in chapter 22 of Hilchos Edus likewise understands the Rambam as I have written, and I cited his words above in siman 31, seif 2. Rabbeinu Yeruham also reaches this conclusion in Nesiv 3, chelek 2, writing that some authorities hold that whenever one admits something he could not have denied, such as a handwritten obligation with a credibility clause, it is heilech and he is exempt from a Torah oath, and that the Rambam writes so. Rabbeinu Yeruham later states this ruling without qualification at the end of that section. The Rosh at the beginning of the second chapter of Kesubos likewise holds that something which the defendant cannot deny is heilech, as cited by the Beit Yosef above at the beginning of siman 87. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 5 of Hilchos To'en holds the same, contrary to the marginal note here in the Sema that cites him for the opposite. See there. The gloss in siman 87, seif 1, likewise appears to rule this way, following the Tur. The Semag explicitly writes that a document is heilech because denial cannot avail against it, without mentioning any land lien. Maadanei Yom Tov at the beginning of Bava Metzia likewise writes that according to the Rosh and Rambam, whenever the defendant cannot deny the admitted portion it is not considered a partial admission, even without a formal document. The Bach also writes that this is the view of the Rambam, Rosh, and Tur.
I later saw that in Even HaEzer, siman 96, seif 15, the Rama himself cites the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 16 of Hilchos Ishus as holding that where the defendant cannot deny a matter, it is not a partial admission even when there is no land lien, consistent with the Maggid Mishneh's approach in chapter 4 of Hilchos To'en, as cited by the Beit Yosef there. The responsa of Mabit, volume 2, end of siman 205, likewise appear to support this. Accordingly, the practical ruling is plainly that he cannot be obligated in a Torah oath. At the very least it is a matter of legal doubt, and in a doubt concerning an oath we rule leniently for the defendant.
