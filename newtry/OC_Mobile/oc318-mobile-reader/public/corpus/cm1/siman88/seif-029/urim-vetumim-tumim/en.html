Even if it contains a clause of credibility, etc. The Shach objects that the Rambam and the Rosh hold that whenever a person cannot deny the obligation, his admission is not considered an admission that can generate an oath. See his lengthy discussion. We must first examine the Rambam’s words. In chapter 4 of Hilkhot To’en he writes that if one admits fifty by virtue of a document, this is not considered an admission of part of the claim, because his denial would not help against the document, all his property is encumbered to the creditor, and even if he denied the debt he would still have to pay. From this it has been inferred that the Rambam holds that whenever one cannot deny, his statement is not considered an admission. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes in chapter 15 of Hilkhot Ishut that in a place where ketubot are not written, since the husband cannot deny the ketubah obligation, his statement is not considered an admission, and the Rama cites this in Even HaEzer siman 96, seif 15. I am surprised by this inference. If that were the entire reason, why did the Rambam add that all of the debtor’s property is encumbered? He should simply have written that denial is ineffective. According to the Shach’s understanding, the same law would apply even to a handwritten note containing a credibility clause, although such a note creates no lien upon property. Rather, it is clear that the inability to deny prevents the statement from being an admission because the admitted portion is heilech, as the Rosh wrote in the second chapter of Ketubot. This must be so. Otherwise, why did the Gemara in Bava Metzia labor to explain why, where the lender says that the loan was two and the borrower admits one, he is exempt from an oath? It could simply have said that he cannot deny the documented amount, as the Ran asks. The reason is that the exemption is heilech. If the debtor could deny the obligation, the admitted sum would not be heilech, because from what could the creditor certainly collect? If the debtor sold his property, the purchasers could dismiss the creditor and his lien would disappear. A document without an effective lien upon land is not heilech, as the Ir Shushan notes, since many people hold documents and nevertheless never receive payment. See above at the beginning of siman 87. The essence of heilech here is that the assets are encumbered and the sum is regarded as already collected, as Rashi writes and as I explained above in siman 87. Thus, where the debtor cannot deny, the assets are encumbered and the admitted amount is heilech. Where he can deny and there is no effective lien upon the assets, so that he can sell them, the debt is not regarded as already collected and there is no heilech. This is why the Rambam writes both that denial is ineffective and that all the debtor’s property is encumbered. Both elements are required: an effective lien upon the assets and the inability to deny. Only then is it heilech. One cannot object that if there is a land lien the defendant should in any event be exempt because one does not swear concerning land. This very point led the later authorities to think that the Rambam must be referring even to a handwritten note containing a credibility clause. But that conclusion is unnecessary. The Rambam wrote his ruling for the present law, after the Geonim enacted collection from the movable property of heirs, or for a document that expressly includes movable property together with land. In such cases the claim is no longer exclusively one involving a land lien, as the Rosh and Maggid Mishneh write, because it is like a claim upon both utensils and land. The Rambam therefore explains that even so, the admitted amount is heilech because the debtor’s land remains encumbered and, if movable property cannot be found, the creditor will necessarily collect from the land. The debtor cannot escape payment of the admitted amount. The Rambam’s words are therefore clear and straightforward, and accord with the Rosh, who writes that even after the Geonic enactment removed the exclusive status of a land lien, the admitted amount is nevertheless heilech. The Maggid Mishneh and the Rama also write correctly concerning a ketubah in a place where ketubot are not written. Since the husband cannot deny the obligation, and both movable and immovable property are subject to collection, the admitted amount is heilech. I am therefore surprised that the Shach cites that case as proof that even without any lien upon property the mere inability to deny creates heilech. There is a further proof from the Rambam’s ruling in chapter 9 of Hilkhot Nizkei Mamon. Where two animals pursued another animal, one large and one small, and witnesses saw the goring but did not know which animal caused it, the Rambam rules that if the injured party says the large animal gored and the owner says the small one gored, or one says the mu’ad animal caused it and the other says the tam caused it, the defendant takes a Torah oath because he admits part of the claim. Yet with respect to the goring by the small animal or the tam he cannot deny the event, because witnesses establish that some goring occurred. It is strained to say, as discussed above, that the Rambam refers only to a case where the owner admitted before the witnesses arrived, so that at the moment of admission he could still deny. Had this limitation been intended, the Rambam should have stated it. Moreover, according to such reasoning, whenever a person admits and a document is later discovered, his earlier admission would be treated as one made while denial was possible, even though the obligation is ultimately established by a document or by witnesses. This is especially difficult in the case of damages, where the legal force of the defendant’s statement itself depends upon the witnesses, for without them it could be an admission to a fine and would also raise the problem of claiming wheat and admitting barley. Thus the essential admission depends upon evidence that simultaneously prevents denial. In my view, this is nearly conclusive proof of the Rambam’s intent. Having clarified the Rambam, we return to the Rosh. The Ma’adanei Melekh, followed by the Shach, understood the Rosh’s statement in the second chapter of Ketubot, that because the husband cannot say “I paid” in the face of Rabbi Yochanan’s rule that one who contests an act of beit din accomplishes nothing, the one hundred that he admits is heilech because he has no defense against it, as a general principle that whenever denial is impossible, even with a handwritten note containing a credibility clause, there is no admission capable of generating an oath. In my view, the Rosh means the same as the Rambam. Quite apart from the difficulty of calling something heilech merely because denial is impossible, when the debtor may hide all his property and there is no lien at all, the Nimukei Yosef and the Ran, who understood the Rambam as teaching that inability to deny means there is no legally significant admission, did not explain the matter as heilech. Their rationale is that the admission lacks the element of brazenness described by Rabbah, because the defendant is compelled to concede that amount. The Rosh, however, expressly invokes heilech. It is difficult to apply heilech to a mere handwritten note where there is no lien upon any property. The Rosh also supports his words from the Yerushalmi, which says that the husband does not take a Torah oath because everyone agrees that he owes her one hundred. The Rosh explains that he cannot deny the obligation because of the rule concerning an act of beit din, and therefore it is as though she has a document for that one hundred and there is a lien upon land. The Rosh thus expressly emphasizes the land lien. According to the Shach’s approach, why is that needed? It would have been enough to say that the husband cannot deny. The gaon Rabbi Shmuel Kaidanover already noticed this in Tiferet Shmuel and wrote that, were the text not uniform in all manuscripts, he would delete the words referring to a land lien. Heaven forbid that such an emendation be made when all texts agree. The truth is that without a lien upon land, the inability to deny would not by itself create heilech, because there is no assured property from which collection may be made. Both elements are required, exactly as in the Rambam: he cannot deny, and there is a land lien. The Rosh’s use of the Yerushalmi still requires explanation. The Yerushalmi discusses the law before the Geonic enactment. At that time a ketubah was collectible only from land, so the exemption could simply rest upon the rule that one does not swear concerning land. Why does the Rosh need to derive from the Yerushalmi the later rule that after the enactment, when the claim is no longer exclusively a land claim, it is nevertheless heilech because denial is impossible? What support does the Yerushalmi provide for that later situation? One possibility is that the Rosh understood the Yerushalmi’s phrase “everyone agrees” as referring specifically to Ben Azzai and the Sages, who disagree where a document contained a sum and part of it was erased. Ben Azzai, who obligates an oath there, necessarily holds that an oath may apply despite the original land lien evidenced by the document, as explained above at length. If even Ben Azzai agrees that the husband is exempt in our Mishnah, the reason must be heilech rather than merely the land lien. This explanation, however, is strained, because the phrase “everyone agrees” ordinarily means that the matter is universally known, not that it refers to those particular disputants.
Therefore, it appears more likely that the Rosh was troubled by a different point. Surely the Yerushalmi knew that a ketubah is a claim involving land, since under the original law it was collected only from land, even in a place where no written ketubah was used. Why, then, did the Yerushalmi need another reason? In that sugya the Yerushalmi asks what help witnesses provide, since perhaps the woman is a woman who lost her virginity through an injury. One answer is that the Sages did not concern themselves with such an uncommon possibility. Another answer is that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that even such a woman receives two hundred. The Rosh may follow the Ran rather than Rabbenu Tam in understanding Rabbi Meir to hold that movable property is legally encumbered for a ketubah, even from orphans. If so, according to Rabbi Meir the husband should take an oath, because the claim is not exclusively a land lien. What for us is true only after the Geonic enactment was, according to Rabbi Meir, already true under Talmudic law. The Yerushalmi must therefore exempt him because he cannot deny the admitted amount and it is heilech. This provides the Rosh with a direct precedent for the post enactment law. Admittedly, in the chapter HaOmer in Kiddushin the Rosh writes that even according to Rabbi Meir a woman does not collect movable property from orphans, and Rabbi Meir does not disagree on that point. However, that passage appears to be copied from Tosafot, as its wording indicates, and perhaps the Rosh himself did not accept that detail. This requires study. In any event, the sound understanding of the Rosh is as stated: he requires both the inability to deny and an effective lien so that the admitted sum is heilech. The Ma’adanei Melekh therefore objected without basis that the Tur contradicts the Rosh. The same applies to the Rosh’s statement in Bava Metzia that one does not swear regarding a document either because it is heilech or because it is a land lien. The Shach wished to infer that the heilech rationale must refer to a case where there is no land lien. That is not necessary. The practical difference can be after the Geonic enactment, when movable property of heirs is collectible, or even before the enactment where the document expressly encumbers movable property together with land. In those cases the claim is not exclusively a land claim, yet it is still heilech because an effective lien remains. After clarifying the Rambam and the Rosh, we may examine the other authorities. Rashi explicitly explains the Gemara’s statement that “the document which he admits is heilech” by saying that this is because land is encumbered. The Giddulei Terumah cites this as proof, and the Shach in seif katan 47 attempts to reject it by saying that Rashi spoke only where the debtor does not admit the document. This is forced. Rashi is explaining the very phrase, “the document which he admits is heilech.” Since he does admit it, why should Rashi need to mention the land lien unless the lien is essential to the heilech rationale? Indeed, there is an additional difficulty for the Shach. Why did Rashi erase the older reading that explicitly says “since he admits it”? According to the Shach, Rashi could have retained it and explained that the Gemara’s initial assumption, before it concluded that there was a land lien, was that an ordinary document can be one without a property guarantee, as the Mishnah in Bava Metzia recognizes a document without such guarantee. Rashi could then have said that such a document is heilech only when the debtor admits it, precisely as the Shach maintains. Why did Rashi reject that reading? Necessarily, Rashi held that if there is no lien upon property, the admitted amount is not heilech in any form, because there is no assured source of collection. If there is a lien upon property, the creditor does not need the debtor’s admission. Rashi therefore removed the phrase “since he admits it.” This is clear support for the Giddulei Terumah. Still, one can distinguish Rashi’s view. Rashi elsewhere holds that even in an ordinary loan, if the defendant places the admitted money before beit din, that is not heilech. Only a deposit already in the claimant’s domain in a legal sense is heilech. According to Rashi, mere inability to deny certainly cannot be superior to physically placing money before beit din. He therefore had to explain that an existing lien upon property makes the sum as though already collected and continuously standing in the creditor’s possession, like a deposit. According to the halakhah, however, we do not follow Rashi on that point, and one could argue that whenever denial is impossible it is as though the defendant hands the money over before beit din, which would suffice for heilech. This somewhat weakens the proof from Rashi. Nevertheless, the overall argument remains compelling, and the Asufot Zekenim cites many authorities who expressly require not only inability to deny but also a lien upon land. One should therefore not depart from the Mechaber’s ruling, and he deserves praise for stating it as he did.
