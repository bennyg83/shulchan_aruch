He is exempt from an oath and from payment even, etc. This is the correct reading, and so it appears in the Tur, as the Sema writes in seif katan 29. In Maadanei Yom Tov, Perek Shevuas HaDayanim, the author writes that the Shulchan Aruch omitted the words "and from payment," and that Maharuach added them in the Shulchan Aruch, but he is astonished because we already struggle to explain those words in the Tur and Maharuach added them to the Shulchan Aruch as well. The Bach likewise writes that the Mechaber intentionally omitted the word payment. Their words are not correct. It is obvious that those words must be added to the Mechaber, for in the latter clause he writes, but if after he admitted, etc., he must pay the value of the barley, implying that in the first clause he is exempt even from the value of the barley. The Levush also states this explicitly. The Bach further lengthens the discussion and writes that in the first clause the defendant does not maintain his admission, but now says, I was merely jesting with you even regarding the barley, whereas the later statement, but if after he admitted, etc., he must pay the value of the barley, concerns a defendant who continues to maintain his admission regarding the barley. This is not correct for several reasons. Rather, it appears to me that the Tur here discusses a case where the claimant did not specify a particular time. If he did specify a time, the defendant is exempt from the barley in every case, even if the claimant later says, certainly, as you say, that is so, but I am not presently demanding the barley, as follows from the Rosh in Perek HaManiach and the Tur above in seif 12. Therefore, where no time was specified it helps if the claimant says, certainly, as you say, that is so, etc. Nevertheless, it does not help if, immediately after the defendant's admission, within the time required for speech, he says: I too intended to demand the barley now, but you anticipated me and admitted it before I finished speaking. We are certain that he is lying, because the defendant did not rush to respond. The claimant had time to speak and could have demanded the barley too if he had indeed intended to demand it now. His silence and failure to demand the barley are therefore as though he admitted that the defendant does not owe him barley. This appears to me to be the Tur's view. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 7, chelek 2, also leaves the Tur's words difficult, but in my humble opinion there is no difficulty, as I have explained. All this, however, follows their approach that the defendant is exempt from the barley because the claimant is treated as though he admitted that the defendant does not owe him barley. According to what I wrote above in seif 12, however, the primary view is that of the Ramah, that no admission by the claimant is relevant here. Rather, the reason for exemption is that the defendant may say he was jesting. If so, there is no distinction. Merely because the claimant did not initially demand barley, we do not exempt the defendant when the defendant himself maintains his admission and says that he owes barley. Any plausible explanation by the claimant for why he did not initially demand the barley is sufficient, because we do not release the defendant from a clear monetary obligation except by an actual waiver, not by such a weak inference. But if the defendant says, I was merely jesting with you, then even if the claimant says, certainly, as you say, that is so, but I am not presently demanding them, etc., the defendant is exempt even from the value of the barley. It also appears that this may be the intent of the Semag and Hagahos Maimoniyos. When they first write that he is exempt even from payment, they mean that beit din tells him generally that he is entirely exempt because the claimant did not demand barley. But if the claimant later demands the barley and explains that he had not demanded it because he did not wish to demand it then, the defendant must pay the barley if he maintains his admission. Even so, the defendant can later claim, I was merely jesting with you. This appears to me the primary ruling.
He must pay him the value of the barley. Nevertheless, he is exempt from a Torah oath. This is simple.
