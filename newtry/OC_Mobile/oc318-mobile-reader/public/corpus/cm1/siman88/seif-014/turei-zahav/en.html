(In the Tur, seif 38: “But Tosafot wrote that even an oral loan,” etc.) I have a great difficulty from the case in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, cited by the Tur at the end of siman 82, where the borrower claims that half the note has been paid and we rule that he must take a Torah oath because he admits part of the claim. Tosafot’s question here should apply there as well: even an oral loan creates a lien upon land, and we have already ruled that liens are of Torah origin, as the Tur writes here. One cannot answer, as Tosafot do here, that the case is where the borrower owns no land or where the lender waived the lien, for there the Gemara says that the creditor collects half from unencumbered property, proving that the borrower owns land. Moreover, it says that he cannot collect from purchasers because they say, “We relied upon the witnesses,” proving that there is at least some lien. Nor can one object from the Tur’s statement below concerning a handwritten note, that it is not treated like a formal note, even though an oral loan also creates a lien. A formal note exempts the borrower from the oath even if he presently owns no land, because the note at least possesses the legal force to collect from encumbered property if land exists. Thus a handwritten note is correctly treated as an oral loan and obligates an oath where the borrower has no land, because it lacks such force even if land existed. That presents no difficulty. But the cited case certainly remains difficult, because no oath is imposed either upon an admission concerning land or upon denial concerning land. Whoever can reconcile these authorities will rightly be called a master and will receive great reward from Heaven. Later, the answer to this question was revealed to me in a dream. The case is where the lender afterward waived the lien. Thus there is no difficulty. Whenever he lacks power to collect from purchasers, the obligation is treated like movables rather than land. Tosafot’s question is specifically according to the view that a lien is of Torah origin for an oral loan, meaning that the creditor can collect from purchasers. As for unencumbered property, even the view that a lien is not of Torah origin agrees that the creditor may collect from it. Therefore, Tosafot’s statement that the lender waived the lien means that he waived only the power to seize from purchasers, while retaining collection from unencumbered property. One should not ask why, at the beginning of Bava Metzia, where the Gemara asks that all land is like “here it is” and answers that the debtor dug pits and caves in it, it did not answer that the creditor waived the lien. There the principal claim is directed at the land itself, because the claimant seeks specifically to take land. Here, after the witnesses came, the claimant saw that otherwise the defendant would not be obligated to swear, and therefore declared before beit din: “I waive the lien that would permit seizure from purchasers with respect to the half that he will be found obligated to pay me. I will collect only from unencumbered property that remains in his hands before he sells it.” Property already sold need not be included in the waiver, because in any event the creditor cannot collect it, as the purchasers can say, “We relied upon the witnesses.” This is precisely the novelty taught: he need not waive the lien upon property already in the purchasers’ hands, since he cannot seize it in any event. But as to property presently unencumbered, beit din’s judgment would allow him to seize it even if later sold, and he must therefore waive that lien.
