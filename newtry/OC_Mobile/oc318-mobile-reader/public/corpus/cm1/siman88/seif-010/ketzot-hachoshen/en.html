And some disagree. Tumim objects to presenting Ran as a lone dissenting view. He cites a textual version in Gittin that interprets Rav's concern for a buried maneh as concerning a deposit. If one who says simply "a maneh" always means value, why would there be any concern for a particular buried maneh? This seems to show that even with a deposit the word can refer to a specific sum. The proof is not decisive. According to our standard text, Rav speaks of a gift. The early authorities therefore had to explain why a general gift of a maneh could generate concern for a particular buried maneh if the donor normally means value. Ran answers by distinguishing a donor, who can mean a specific object, from a claimant, whose general monetary language is broader. According to the alternate text, however, Rav speaks of a deposit. Then the difficulty disappears even if "a maneh" means value. A person can say, "A deposit worth a maneh is with me," and because an intact deposit remains in the owner's possession wherever it is, one can still fear that the actual deposited object worth a maneh is buried. The statement need not identify a particular group of coins in order for the concern to arise. Thus the alternate text does not disprove Ran.
