(Seif 17) “He takes a hesset oath that he does not know.” This requires study. The defendant’s uncertain claim is no stronger than a definite claim. It still concerns wheat and barley, and he should be exempt regarding the barley. Sema wrote that the claimant too was uncertain, but I do not understand his view, because if so there is no hesset oath. One cannot answer that afterward the claimant says, “I have now remembered that you certainly owe me,” for then in every such case we could say that he was initially uncertain and therefore did not claim the barley. Rather, this ruling of the Rambam rests upon an explicit passage in the chapter HaMani’ah, 35, where the injured party claims with certainty and the tortfeasor is uncertain, yet the tortfeasor is exempt. Raavad and Sefer HaTerumot, gate 7, already raised this difficulty. Raavad wrote in his glosses to chapter 1 of the Laws of Pleading: “Some reject the proof, because nowadays there is a hesset oath. If he swears, ‘I do not know whether it was wheat or barley,’ he has admitted in a complete manner that he owes something, and this is not merely an attempt to dismiss the claimant. Therefore he pays. This reasoning has substance, but the Rambam should have stated it expressly.” The explanation of Raavad’s words, in defense of the Rambam, appears to be as follows. The Rambam holds that one who is claimed for wheat and admits barley is exempt because his statement is regarded as a way of dismissing the claimant: “You do have barley with me, so why are you claiming wheat?” Mordechai likewise wrote in the name of Rashi in HaMani’ah, comparing this to the plea, “I was jesting with you,” discussed in siman 32. This also resolves the question I raised against the Rambam in seif 12, why seizure helps. The claimant may say, “I know with certainty that you hold my barley. Whether you formally admitted it or not is immaterial, and I never waived it.” On this basis, the Rambam’s words are coherent. Ordinarily, a definite admission is treated as an admission creating liability, except where circumstances prove that he intended merely to fend off the claimant. Such proof exists when he mentions an item that was not claimed, for that cannot be viewed as an ordinary admission and must have been said merely to dismiss him. Therefore, whether he says with certainty, “I owe you barley,” or says, “I do not know whether it was wheat or barley,” he could have said only, “I do not know whether it was wheat,” and would thereby have been exempt. Since he added unnecessary words, they were certainly said merely to dismiss the claimant. All of this applies before the enactment of the hesset oath, which is the case in HaMani’ah. After the enactment, however, there is no such proof. We say that he realizes he must swear concerning what he claims not to know, and therefore he is exact in saying “I do not know,” so that his oath will be truthful. The Rambam therefore speaks after the hesset oath was enacted. This is what Raavad meant when he wrote that the Rambam should have explained that wherever there is no proof of dismissal, the statement is treated as a complete admission, and he becomes liable for the lesser amount after taking a hesset oath that he does not know.
