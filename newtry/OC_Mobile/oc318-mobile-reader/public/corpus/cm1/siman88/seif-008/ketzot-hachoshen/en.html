A specified object. Ran writes that if the claimant demands a particular object and the defendant admits only monetary value in another form, the admission is not of the same kind as the claim. If the claimant demands value and the defendant admits a specific object, however, the admission can be included within the broader value claim. Thus a claim of a kor of produce with an admission of a half kor of legumes can be partial admission because the value claim includes the admitted specific property. The Shach's wording suggests that if one claims a maneh of a specified object and the defendant admits another specified object worth half a maneh, the admission is not of the same kind. Ran's language supports this even more broadly: once the claimant demanded a specific item, an admission merely of equivalent value does not match the claim. A difficulty arises from Kiddushin, where a woman traveling with two men and a bundle claims both men are her servants and the bundle is hers, while each man claims she is his wife and the other his servant. The bundle remains until Elijah comes unless both give her a get, and then the get creates at least a ketubah claim. Why may she recover a ketubah amount from the bundle by an either way argument? She claims the specific bundle itself, while they claim only a monetary ketubah obligation. Under the rule of wheat and barley, the forms of claim do not match. If the reason the defendant is exempt from barley is that the claimant effectively waived it by not claiming it, the woman can avoid waiver by stating from the outset: "According to me the entire bundle is mine, and according to you I am at least entitled to a ketubah amount from it." She therefore expressly preserves both positions. Ritva explains the case in this manner. But if the exemption rests on the claimant's admission that no barley is owed, difficulty remains, because her own claim of ownership in the bundle seems to deny a mere ketubah right. The issue remains unresolved under that theory.
