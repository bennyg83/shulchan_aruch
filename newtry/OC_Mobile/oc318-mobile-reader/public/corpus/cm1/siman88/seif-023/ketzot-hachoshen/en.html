Measured and weighed. Ran discusses a case where one says, "Everything up to this beam is mine," and the other says, "You may take whatever you left there." He asks why the defendant is exempt once the produce can later be measured and the size of the admission determined. Ran answers that the Torah partial admission oath requires the admission to emerge initially in something measured or weighed. If the original statement was unmeasured and therefore exempt, later measurement does not retroactively create the oath. This is like an admission that preceded the claim and therefore left the defendant exempt when made. See siman 75.
And he admits something measured. Rambam rules that if a woman says, "You raped me," and the man says, "No, I seduced you," he pays humiliation and depreciation and takes a Torah oath concerning the pain component because he admitted part of the claim. Yet humiliation and depreciation are not fixed amounts. How can this satisfy the requirement that partial admission concern something measurable? Ran writes that where one claims ten vines and the other admits five, a Torah oath applies even though neither side yet knows the precise money value of the vines. The stated numerical quantity is enough. He proves similarly from a claim of a kor of produce with an admission of a half kor of legumes. The law requires a defined quantity, not necessarily a presently appraised market value. On that basis Rambam may understand the rape case as a claim containing three defined categories of damages, humiliation, depreciation, and pain, while the defendant admits two and denies one. The categories themselves may be sufficient definition. Still, because no actual number is stated, the matter requires study. Mishneh LaMelech discusses Raavad's objection that pain belongs to the girl while humiliation and depreciation can belong to her father. Kesef Mishneh understands the case after the father's death, when she claims all components herself. Mishneh LaMelech objects that if she was seduced while already entitled to the payments herself, voluntary seduction generally waives them. The case can instead be one where the event occurred in the father's lifetime and she later became orphaned before judgment, so the claims passed to her. Where she was a minor at the time, voluntary seduction does not necessarily amount to full waiver, especially because a minor's power of waiver is only rabbinic and does not readily apply in this context.
