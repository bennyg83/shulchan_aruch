What you left, you take. Ran writes in Shevuot that even though, after the fruit is inspected, the extent of the defendant's admission becomes known and is measurable, his original admission had already emerged in a form that created no Torah oath. Ran later distinguishes testimony by witnesses: when witnesses later establish the measurable amount, the liability rests upon their testimony, and the defendant cannot argue that no witnesses were present earlier. At first glance this implies that an admission concerning something that still requires appraisal is not a modeh bemiktzat admission. Ketzot HaChoshen therefore asks from siman 72 seif 16, where a moneychanger admits that the ring in his hand is worth as much as the lost ring and is treated as a modeh bemiktzat. Ketzot answers that the ring is before beit din, but this is strained. The decisive issue is whether the defendant's verbal admission concerns a measurable amount, as Tosafot in Shevuot write, even if the object is not physically before beit din. When one says, "up to the window," the window is not before beit din and its dimensions may not yet be known, yet the admission is measurable. Likewise, siman 400 rules that when one says the larger ox and the other says the smaller ox, there is a d'oraisa oath, although neither carcass nor damaging ox is necessarily before beit din and appraisal remains necessary. Ketzot also asks from the claim of rape or seduction, where the amount for pain still requires appraisal, yet there can be a d'oraisa oath. It therefore appears that in the case of "what you left, you take," although the fruit has rotted, the litigants did not present the rotting as a quantified part of the claim. One says that he left a full house, and the other says only, "what you left, you take," in a situation where the diminution per measure may be generally known. The court is asked only to determine the quantity originally deposited. Alternatively, the rotting became known only after the admission. Even if it is discovered later that the property is not intact and therefore the case is not one of heilech, at the time the defendant said, "what you left, you take," his response was equivalent to saying, "your property is before you." Nothing remained for beit din to do other than direct the plaintiff to take his own property, and the admission and exemption were already complete before any measurable deficiency was established. Therefore he is exempt. This differs from rape and seduction, where beit din cannot determine the amount of liability until the pain is appraised, and from siman 400, where the carcass and damaging ox must be appraised before judgment can be entered. It likewise differs from siman 72, where the ring must be appraised. In all of those cases the admission is not complete until the appraisal, and at the moment when the amount becomes judicially determinable it is a measurable admission. Plainly, if one is sued for one hundred and answers, "I owe only forty or fifty, or slightly less, as I will determine from my ledger," we would not say that the admission is nonmeasurable merely because the exact number remains to be checked. So long as the admission is not complete until the amount is clarified, and at completion it is measurable, a d'oraisa oath can attach. Shach in siman 72 seif katan 3 cites the Rosh in Shevuot. The Rosh distinguishes the statement "I delivered a house full to you" and the answer "I know only that whatever you left you may take." The defendant is not saying that he is uncertain about an acknowledged shortfall. Rather, he says that he did not attend to whether the house was literally full and that people commonly describe a house nearly full as "a full house." What he knows with certainty is that nothing has been taken from what the plaintiff left. Thus there is not yet a definite partial denial. The plaintiff's own claim of "full" is not precise enough to establish a measured amount, and the defendant denies only that any fruit is missing. But if the plaintiff says, "I deposited such-and-such quantity of fruit," and the custodian says, "You deposited fruit, and I do not know how much; I know it was worth at least a perutah and do not know how much more," there is a d'oraisa oath if the plaintiff's asserted amount is at least two silver ma'ot. If the custodian admits merely "fruit" without acknowledging even a perutah's value, the admission has no definite monetary measure, because even less than a perutah may be called fruit. The Rosh's reasoning is that even where the exact amount admitted above a perutah is unknown, if it is certain before us that the defendant admits part and denies part, a d'oraisa oath applies. In "what you left, you take," by contrast, the meaning is: I did not focus on whether the house was literally full, but I know that I did not diminish what was deposited; if you left it full, it remains full. Thus there is no known denial before beit din at the time of the original admission. It is possible that the full quantity remains and that there is no denial at all. The Torah decree of modeh bemiktzat requires an actual partial denial together with a partial admission. Since no partial denial was established at the time of the first admission, that admission emerged in exemption even if later events clarify the deficiency, as Ran explains. When the Rosh says that the defendant is not making a shema claim, he means that "what you left, you take" should not be interpreted as: I know the house is now short of being full, but perhaps you never deposited more. If that were his claim, he would be liable to a d'oraisa oath, because the admission and denial are objectively present. Since he can be required to pay only the fruit he certainly acknowledges while his uncertainty prevents a more definite denial, the case resembles "fifty I know and fifty I do not know," concerning which one who cannot swear must pay, as the Rosh explains regarding a pledge. The Rosh's final statement about merely admitting "fruit" is a separate rule. He found Ramban's distinction difficult. Ramban had distinguished a lost pledge, where the custodian knows it was worth more than a perutah but does not know the balance, from "what you left, you take," where the fruit remains and can ultimately be measured. The Rosh objects that at the moment of admission beit din still cannot impose more than the minimum certain amount. If the depositor died then, the court could recover only that amount from the heirs. The Gemara indicates that the decisive moment is the time of admission. Therefore, if at that moment the defendant certainly admits at least a perutah and the remainder is uncertain, there is no reason to distinguish it from the pledge merely because the exact quantity can later be measured. And if one looks to the later clarification, then at that later point the matter itself is measurable. Ran likewise teaches that when witnesses later establish the amount, their testimony does not require the original admission itself to have been measured.
