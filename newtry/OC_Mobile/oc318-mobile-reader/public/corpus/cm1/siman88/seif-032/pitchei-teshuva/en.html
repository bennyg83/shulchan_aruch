It follows that he admitted only what is stated in the document. In Rabbi Akiva Eiger's glosses to the Shulchan Aruch he writes: "I am surprised why the early authorities did not codify the rationale of the other answer in our sugya, that he is exempt because the document supports him. A practical difference arises with a handwritten note that contains no clause of trustworthiness. According to all opinions, his admission would obligate him to swear. Nevertheless, in a case of 'selas are stated in the document and he says two,' he would be exempt because the document supports him. This requires great study."
Nevertheless, he is exempt. See Baer Heitev, who writes that in any event he must take a heset oath, etc., until: "and for that reason he wrote there explicitly that he is exempt from heset, whereas he did not write so in the first ruling, and the reason is," etc. In Rabbi Akiva Eiger's glosses to the Sema he writes: "It can simply be said that here, since had he claimed two he would have been liable to a heset oath, therefore even when he claims three he must take a heset oath. Otherwise one could say that he is acting deceptively in order to exempt himself from heset. This is straightforward." See Tumim.
For if he wished, etc. Shaar Mishpat writes that the same applies if he admits three and regarding the remainder says, "I do not know." Although no migo applies when he says that he does not know, we nevertheless do not say that this is like the case of "fifty I know and fifty I do not know," in which one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. Rather, since had he denied the matter with certainty he would have been exempt from a Torah oath, so too when he claims that he does not know, the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay does not apply. See there.
And even from a heset oath. See Baer Heitev, who writes that the Shach leaves this ruling requiring study. See Tumim, who challenges the Shach from the end of siman 267. Netivot HaMishpat also writes that the Mechaber's view is primary. See there. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 29, who writes: "In my view, the words of the Shach are correct. There, in the case of one who finds a lost object, both what he returns and what is claimed from him arise only because of the finder's own admission. Here, however, according to his present claim, he now remembers the matter himself, and this does not arise from the claimant's admission. It may therefore be that what he now remembers and asserts with certainty does not render him one who returns a lost object. This is evident to one who examines the Shach, who writes that it is difficult to say that because he said, 'I did not remember,' etc." Apparently, however, the words of the Taz in siman 267, which I cited there in seif katan 4, indicate that he does not accept this distinction.
