Even so, he is exempt from a Torah oath. He must nevertheless take a hesset oath. The Maggid Mishneh states this in the name of earlier authorities, and Nimukei Yosef writes similarly in Ramban's name. Although Nimukei Yosef says that Rashba and Ran rejected Ramban's proof, they may agree with his ruling. The Sema suggests that the Tur also agrees, and the same is written by Agudat Ezov and Gidulei Terumah. The Bach's objection to the Sema is unpersuasive.
Therefore, if he said, “You have one maneh,” etc. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that where one advances an uncertain claim, but the defendant's admission turns the claimant's position into a certain claim and the defendant admits part, Rashi appears to require a Torah oath, while leading teachers disagree. The authorities, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch here follow those teachers. See what I wrote above, siman 82, seif 13, and Responsa Maharit, simanim 112 and 151.
Or to your father, etc. Rav Hai Gaon writes that if an heir claims, “You owe my father such and such,” and the defendant says, “Your father never had more than fifty against me,” he is exempt as one returning a lost object. This applies where he says that the deceased originally lent only fifty. But if he says, “I originally owed one hundred and paid fifty,” he is liable, at least where the heir claims with certainty that one hundred was lent and is uncertain only whether fifty was repaid. Rav Hai further distinguishes a spontaneous admission made before the heir's claim. Such an admission ordinarily exempts as one returning a lost object, even if the heir later claims with certainty, because the admission preceded the claim. Yet beit din must determine whether the defendant rushed to admit evasively in order to avoid an oath. If so, he is liable. If he spoke innocently, he is exempt. Rav Hai adds that an admission that one originally owed one hundred and repaid fifty is treated as returning a lost object only when three conditions are met: he admitted in beit din before the heirs claimed and without evasion; there were no witnesses establishing monetary dealings with the deceased in a manner that would have obligated him independently; and the transaction was not publicly known or widely rumored. Where witnesses establish the underlying transaction, Rav Hai compares the case to one who says, “This field belonged to your father, but I purchased it from him,” after witnesses establish the father's ownership. Tosafot in Ketubot appears to disagree with Rav Hai concerning witnesses, holding that against the son the defendant remains believed through a migo that he could have said he paid everything, because one is willing to deny the son's claim. The same disagreement applies where there is only public rumor or the heir claims with certainty. Rabbeinu Yerucham cites Rav Hai but notes that Tosafot indicates that a spontaneous admission remains exempt even according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, and that Rambam also seems to hold broadly that only a certain claim by the heir creates partial admission. Nevertheless, no one is found to dispute Rav Hai's distinction between an evasive and an innocent advance admission. Thus, where the heir claims with certainty that one hundred remains owed, an evasive advance admission of fifty creates a Torah oath, and this is certainly so in a direct claim against the debtor himself. Ramban and Baal HaTerumot, who require the claim to precede the admission for a Torah oath, speak of an innocent admission. The Mordechai likewise cites Rav Hai that the Sages' exemption applies only where the defendant did not rush forward evasively.
He is exempt, even from a hesset oath, etc. The Tur indeed writes this, but the source in Ramban, cited by Baal HaTerumot, says only that a Torah oath for partial admission requires the claim to precede the admission. Where the claimant says, “You reminded me, and I now know that you paid me nothing,” the claimant now advances a certain demand. Although a Torah oath is absent because the admission preceded the claim, it is not clear why a hesset oath should also be absent, since we find no rule that hesset requires the claim to precede the admission. Rabbeinu Yerucham's language likewise limits the requirement of prior claim specifically to a Torah oath. The case resembles seif 15 above, where a hesset oath is required concerning the wheat. A distinction based on the defendant's saying that he had forgotten is strained. The matter requires study.
