Exempt. The Maggid Mishneh writes in the name of the Rambam that he must nevertheless take a hesset oath. It can be said that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch agree. Although they conclude, "Therefore, if one says to another, 'You owe me one hundred,' and so forth, he is exempt, even from a hesset oath," they did not fully equate the two cases. They meant only that there too he is exempt from a Torah oath. For that reason they expressly state in the latter case that he is exempt even from hesset, but do not say so in the first case. The reason is that in the first case there is at least a document against him, so he is not fully comparable to one who returns a lost object. The latter case is different, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch explain in siman 267. So writes the Sema. The Shach agrees and notes that the Nimukei Yosef writes the same in the name of the Ramban, and Gidulei Terumah likewise explains that this appears to be the Tur's view, contrary to the Bach. Remember. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes: If one makes an uncertain claim against another, and from the defendant's admission the claimant's allegation becomes definite, and the defendant has admitted part of it, the defendant is obligated in a Torah oath. This appears from Rashi in chapter HaNizakin, although leading teachers disagree. The Beit Yosef cites this at the beginning of siman 87. It appears that the authorities, and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch here, follow the leading teachers who disagree. See siman 82, seif 13, and Responsa Maharit, simanim 112 and 151. Shach. See also there for what he cites in the name of Rav Hai Gaon.
Hesset. The Shach leaves this ruling requiring study. Granted that the defendant is not obligated in a Torah oath because the claim did not precede the admission, nevertheless, who says that he is exempt from a hesset oath? We find nowhere that a hesset oath requires the claim to precede the admission. See siman 75, seif 3.
