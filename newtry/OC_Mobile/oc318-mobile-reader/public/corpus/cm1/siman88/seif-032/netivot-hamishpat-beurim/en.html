Therefore he said to him, one hundred, until it appears to me. This applies specifically where the heir is certain that the debtor borrowed fifty but does not know whether it was repaid, while the debtor says, "I paid him fifty." Otherwise there is no intelligible basis to distinguish between "I owe you nothing" and "I paid." Why should a defendant be exempt when he says he never borrowed and the heir contradicts him with certainty, yet liable when he says he repaid and the heir cannot contradict the repayment with certainty? Rather, the Shach understands Rav Hai's latter case as one in which the defendant says he repaid and the heir states with certainty that he did not, as the Shach concludes: the practical difference is where the heir says with certainty that the money is still owed. Therefore the defendant is liable because he lacks a migo. The earlier case, where the defendant says he never borrowed, is where the heir knows with certainty that the loan occurred but does not know whether repayment occurred. The defendant is exempt even though the heir contradicts the denial of the loan, because he has a migo that he could have said, "I repaid everything to your father," which the heir could not contradict with certainty. The Shach's text should accordingly read that the heir is certain his father lent one hundred but does not know whether it was repaid, and the defendant says he owes only fifty. Rav Hai's rule is therefore this. If the heir says his father lent one hundred and the debtor admits only part of the original loan, he is exempt from the d'oraisa oath because he has a migo that he could have denied everything by saying he repaid the father in full. This is not a migo of brazenness, since the heir does not know whether repayment occurred. If the heir states with certainty that the debt remains unpaid and the debtor admits part, he is liable to a d'oraisa oath. If the defendant's admission preceded the heir's claim, however, then even though the heir later says with certainty that the debt remains unpaid, the issue is the dispute of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages in Ketubot 22, and we rule like the Sages that the defendant is like one returning a lost object. Since he did not know whether the heir knew of the debt and nevertheless volunteered the admission, he is treated as a meshiv aveidah. Three conditions are necessary. The prior admission must not be an obvious device made in anticipation of the claim. There must not already be witnesses or a public report of the loan, for if there are witnesses or a kol he is not a voluntary finder, since he knows the heir is likely to sue on that basis. The Gemara explains the Sages by saying that a person is more willing to be brazen toward a son than toward the father. Rav Hai can follow Rabbeinu Tam, cited by Tumim in the rules of migo in siman 74, who holds that even a migo of unavoidable accident can be a migo of brazenness, because a person is internally reluctant to deny another's property completely even if the other person cannot detect the lie. Toward the father who benefited him he is reluctant to deny everything, while toward the son who did not benefit him he can be more brazen. Therefore a voluntary admission to the son can be treated as returning a lost object. Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov holds that even toward the son he is not sufficiently brazen to deny everything. Where the denial concerns a fact known personally to the claimant, however, even the Sages agree that such a denial is brazen, as appears from the cases of goats that ate barley and of a deposit. The Shach therefore writes that Tosafot disagree and that even if the heir knows with certainty of the fifty and also knows it was not repaid, they would still consider the alternative denial a migo of brazenness. As a matter of halachah, however, where the heir claims with certainty from personal knowledge that the debt remains unpaid and the debtor admits part, he is liable to a d'oraisa oath, unlike that reading of Tosafot. This follows from the Mechaber here, who exempts only where the debtor says, "You reminded me," in the case of money owed to the claimant's father, implying that an admission after a direct certain claim is liable. It also follows from siman 78 seif 21, where only a claim of "My father told me that you owe him" is exempt, implying that if the heir himself knows with certainty that his father is owed the money, liability applies. The dispute between Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages is therefore limited to certainty based on the father's statement. If the heir knows personally, the partial admission creates a d'oraisa oath. Still, that is only where the heir knows that the debt was not repaid. If he knows of the loan but does not know whether it was repaid, the debtor is exempt even after a partial admission because he has a migo that he could have said he repaid everything, and we do not follow Rabbeinu Tam's broad view of brazenness. Likewise, when a genuine unsolicited admission preceded the heir's claim and was not evasively timed, we rule that even vis-a-vis another person the defendant is treated as a meshiv aveidah and is exempt.
