Even so, he is exempt, etc. The Sema explains that he is exempt from a Torah oath, but he must take a heset oath. The Bach disagrees and holds that he is exempt even from heset. The Shach in seif katan 53 agrees with the Sema and writes that the Nimukei Yosef says the same. Although the Nimukei Yosef writes that the Rashba and the Ran rejected the Ramban’s proof, the Shach suggests that they may nevertheless agree with him as to the law, and that the Tur can likewise be understood this way. I do not understand his words. The dispute brought by the Nimukei Yosef between the Ramban and the Rashba does not concern this case. It concerns the preceding case, where the defendant admits only two, as to whether he must take a heset oath. Yet the Tur and the Mechaber have already ruled above that one who admits two must take heset. I am therefore surprised, especially at the Bach. Since the Tur concludes that one who admits only two must take heset, how could one think that if he admits three he would be exempt even from heset? He could then evade the oath by artifice: if he says two he must swear, so he will say three and be wholly exempt. Plainly, if the former case requires heset, so does this one. The Bach’s ruling was an inadvertent error.
Or to your father, etc. The Shach in seif katan 55 quotes the words of Rav Hai Gaon. I will state their essential point briefly. If an heir claims, “You had one hundred zuz belonging to my father,” and the defendant says, “I have only fifty of his,” he is exempt from an oath because he is returning a lost object. But if he says, “It is true that I had one hundred of your father’s in my possession, but I repaid fifty,” he must swear, unless his admission preceded the claim, provided that he is not acting deceptively. If there are witnesses or a public report that he had financial dealings with the deceased, then even if his admission preceded the claim he must swear. These words are puzzling. If Rav Hai Gaon rules like the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and hold that a person can brazenly deny a claim made by the creditor’s son, then even when he says, “I repaid fifty,” he should be regarded as returning a lost object. Likewise, why does Rav Hai say that where there are witnesses or a public report he must swear even if his admission preceded the claim? He is still returning a lost object. Even if, as the Shach understands, witnesses establish with certainty that the deceased lent him the money, he could still have claimed that he repaid the entire amount, since according to the Sages a person is capable of brazenly denying the son. The Shach attempts to explain that the heir claims with certainty that his father lent the money, but does not know whether it was repaid. This makes the matter even more difficult. The heir then has no definite claim regarding repayment, and according to everyone no oath can be imposed because there is no definite claim. Moreover, the Gemara itself speaks of a defendant who claims repayment, saying, “I fed him half,” and nevertheless the Sages, whose view Rav Hai follows, consider him one who returns a lost object. The Shach thought that Rav Hai understands the dispute as concerning whether the admission preceded the claim, while the Sages would agree that if the claim came first a Torah oath is required. Apart from the fact that this does not solve the problem, for then even if the defendant said merely, “I never borrowed more than fifty,” perhaps the Sages would obligate him once the claim preceded the admission, the distinction between a claim preceding the admission and an admission preceding the claim has no special connection to the dispute concerning a creditor’s son. The same issue would apply between the original litigants themselves. The Ramban, in the name of Rav Hai Gaon, indeed states as a general rule that an admission of part generates an oath only when the claim precedes the admission. That principle is independent of the dispute here, which itself concerns a case where the claim did precede the admission. Thus, despite the Shach’s lengthy discussion, I do not find that he has resolved even part of the Gaon’s words. One possible explanation is that Rav Hai’s phrase “and he repaid him” does not mean that the defendant repaid the father. It means that he repaid the heir. The language is in the third person rather than addressing the heir directly, but such usage is not unusual in Rav Hai Gaon’s Sha’arei Shevuot, as anyone familiar with that work will recognize. Perhaps there is simply a scribal error and the text should read, “I repaid you.” Rav Hai would then hold that when we say a person can brazenly deny the son, that applies where he says, “Your father never lent me,” or, “I repaid your father,” because these assertions do not directly contradict the heir himself. Even if the heir cries out that he knows the loan occurred and knows that his father was not repaid, the defendant can evade the accusation by saying that there were private matters between himself and the father. The father’s statement in the son’s presence that the money was a loan may have been inaccurate, or the transfer may have been repayment, a gift, or something similar. Likewise, repayment to the father need not have occurred openly. But if the defendant says to the heir, “I repaid you,” that is a direct act of brazenness. How can one living person deny another living person to his face regarding a payment supposedly made directly to him? In that case the defendant is one who admits part of the claim, and he is not regarded as returning a lost object, because his present defense is brazen. We do not say that he has a migo that he could have claimed that he repaid the father. Once he presently admits part of the claim, such a migo does not exempt him from the oath imposed upon one who admits part. Rav Hai has support from the chapter HaNizakin. There, if the claimant says, “You found two purses,” and the finder says, “I found only one,” he is exempt because he is returning a lost object. But if he admits, “I found two, but I already gave one back to you,” he must swear, even though he has a migo that he could have said, “I found only one.” Since he now admits finding two and claims to have returned one, while the claimant denies receiving it, he is one who admits part of the claim. See Tosafot there. The same applies here. Even though he initially could have said that no loan occurred or that he repaid the father, once he says that he repaid the heir and the heir directly denies it, a Torah oath applies. This is provided that the claim preceded the admission. Otherwise, an admission of part made before any claim generally does not create an oath, and certainly not here. Rav Hai adds that if there are witnesses or a public report, it appears that the defendant is acting deceptively, anticipating that the heir will claim against him and therefore hastening to make an admission before the formal claim. Such a preemptive admission is treated like one made after a claim, and he must swear. On the Shach’s approach, Rav Hai’s words can therefore be understood without any unusual result. A simpler explanation, however, appears more likely. Rav Hai certainly does not impose a Torah oath in this case at all. Since we rule like the Sages that a person can brazenly deny the son, the defendant is exempt from a Torah oath. The entire question concerns heset. Rav Hai holds, as Tosafot in Bava Kamma and the Mordechai in the chapter Kol HaNishba’in report in his name, that the halakhah follows the second version of Rav Nachman, under which heset was instituted only where there is a concrete basis for a monetary claim. Accordingly, where the heir claims with certainty, “You owe my father one hundred,” and the defendant says, “I owe only fifty, because I never borrowed more than fifty,” there is no admission of part for a Torah oath, since one may brazenly deny the son. There is also no heset under Rav Hai’s rule, because with respect to the denied fifty there is no concrete basis for the claim. But if the defendant says, “It is true that I borrowed one hundred, but I repaid fifty,” then although there is still no Torah oath, heset is required. A repayment defense creates a concrete monetary basis, and Rav Nachman imposed heset in such a case. This distinction applies where the claim preceded the admission. If the defendant first volunteered the admission before the heir claimed anything, he is returning a lost object and is exempt even from heset, because had he remained silent the heir might never have demanded anything. Rav Hai therefore adds that there must be no witnesses or public report. If witnesses or a public report exist, the defendant cannot be considered a genuine returner of a lost object, since he knows that the heir is likely to hear of the transaction and make a claim. His early admission may be an artifice designed to precede the claim. He therefore remains obligated in heset. On this reading Rav Hai Gaon’s words are clear, straightforward, and correct. As a matter of practical law, although we rule like Rav Nachman that heset applies even where there is no prior concrete basis for the monetary claim, we nevertheless learn from Rav Hai an important qualification to the Mechaber’s ruling. The Mechaber treats the defendant as one who returns a lost object where there is no public report. Where such a report exists, however, he should not be believed without heset, because he is not truly returning a lost object. This requires study because the later authorities did not reproduce the qualification, but in my view this is the central and correct intent of Rav Hai Gaon.
He is exempt, even from a heset oath. The Shach asks why he should be exempt from heset. The Ramban’s reason is that the admission preceded the claim, which explains why there is no Torah oath, but why should heset also be absent? His objection is not clear. If one says, “I found two purses and returned one to you,” and the claimant denies that any purse was returned, then although the claimant now has a definite claim based upon the finder’s own admission, since had the finder remained silent the claimant would have received nothing, the finder is regarded as returning a lost object and is exempt even from heset. The Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber all rule this way below in siman 267. The present case is identical. The claimant now says with certainty, “You did not repay me,” but had the defendant remained silent the claimant would not have received even part of the money, because he did not know at all that the other person owed him anything. The defendant is therefore returning a lost object and is exempt even from heset. See Baal HaTerumot, Sha’ar 7, part 2, which indeed compares this case to the two purses. They are identical both in law and in reasoning, and there is no difficulty. The Shach in seif katan 54 cites Rabbenu Yerucham, who writes that if the claimant’s certainty arose only from the defendant’s admission, whereas his earlier claim had been merely uncertain, Rashi’s view in the chapter HaNizakin is that a Torah oath applies, though Rabbenu Yerucham’s great teachers disagreed. At first glance I am surprised, because Rashi’s words do not appear to prove such a practical ruling. Rashi explains Rabbi Yitzchak’s case where the claimant says, “You found two purses,” and the defendant says, “I found only one,” and must swear because purses are not usually separated from one another. Rashi explains that the claimant did not actually see or know that the finder found them. Rather, the finder admitted finding one purse, and the claimant then said, “According to your own words, you found two.” Rashi thus holds that this can constitute an admission of part. But there is an obvious difficulty. The finder is returning a lost object, and the Mishnah states that one who returns a lost object does not swear, as Rashi himself explains in several places. Had the finder remained silent the claimant would have had nothing. Rashi said what he did only in explaining Rabbi Yitzchak’s own statement. Rabbi Yitzchak does not accept the Mishnah’s exemption for a finder based upon the concern for tikkun olam, as is evident in the Gemara. Rashi therefore explained Rabbi Yitzchak correctly. According to the halakhah, however, we follow the Mishnah that a finder does not swear. Even in this case he should therefore not swear as a practical matter. If Rabbenu Yerucham copied Rashi’s explanation as a practical ruling, that appears difficult and requires study. What appears possible in defense of Rabbenu Yerucham is as follows. Rashi does not read the Gemara’s continuation with the text “this was also taught,” as Tosafot do. This raises the question: according to whom does Rabbi Yitzchak state his rule? One must say that Rashi understands the final conclusion to depend upon the dispute between Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages. Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov holds that a person does not brazenly deny the creditor’s son even though he did not receive the money directly from the son. If so, in a lost object case as well he would not brazenly retain the lost object for himself. His concept of brazenness follows Rabbenu Tam, who holds that it is considered brazenness even when the claimant cannot personally recognize the lie, as I explained at length in the rules of migo, simanim 112 and 113. We do not worry that perhaps the finder would simply refrain from picking up the object. Why should he not pick it up? It may belong to a non Jew, or it may belong to a Jew where the finder discovers that the owner had already despaired before he found it, in which case it belongs to the finder. Accordingly, for Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov there is no concern for tikkun olam that imposing an oath will deter finders from returning lost property. Rabbi Yitzchak can therefore require an oath. The Sages, however, hold that a person can brazenly deny the creditor’s son because he did not receive the money directly from him. Certainly they would say the same of a lost object. There is therefore a real concern for tikkun olam, and the finder does not swear. According to this, if the claimant originally did not know that the defendant had borrowed from him, and the defendant himself admits the loan but says, “I repaid half,” after which the claimant says with certainty, “I know you did not repay me at all,” there is no tikkun olam concern according to anyone. The concern is not that a person will refrain from taking possession of the object in the first place, only that he might refrain from returning it. Here, withholding repayment is a direct act of brazenness. Although the claimant may not independently know the defendant’s falsehood, the defendant received the money directly from him. The principle that one may brazenly deny because he did not receive anything from the claimant applies only to a son, whereas here the original creditor himself gave the money. Everyone therefore agrees with Rabbi Yitzchak that the tikkun olam rationale does not exempt the defendant, and he must swear. This, in my view, is the best defense of Rabbenu Yerucham, though it still requires study. The Maharsha’s words in the second chapter of Ketubot also tend in this direction. He addresses the Maharshal’s question that the Mishnah could have taught, according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, that Rabbi Yehoshua concedes where the claimant had not first made a demand. The Maharsha answers that the case cannot be established where witnesses exist. At first glance this answer is difficult to understand. The proposed case is that the claimant made no demand and did not know that anything was owed to him. The defendant voluntarily admitted owing one hundred but claimed that he had repaid fifty. Necessarily, the claimant then says with certainty that the fifty was not repaid, because otherwise there would be no definite claim capable of generating an oath. If he now has a definite claim, the defendant’s only migo is that he could have remained silent and never admitted the debt. If witnesses establish the original loan, that migo indeed disappears, because the defendant cannot deny the loan, while the claimant directly denies repayment. This seems to explain the Maharsha’s answer. According to the approach above, however, the matter is clearer. If Rabbenu Yerucham is correct, then according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov the son is treated like the original creditor, because Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov holds that even toward the son the defendant does not brazenly deny. Thus, if no claim was initially made and the claimant’s certainty arose only from the defendant’s admission, the defendant is still one who admits part and must swear. What then is the Maharshal’s question that the Mishnah should teach that Rabbi Yehoshua concedes and exempts him where the claim was not made first? Necessarily, after the voluntary admission the claimant would have to say with certainty that no repayment occurred. But in precisely that case, according to Rabbenu Yerucham’s reading of Rashi, a Torah oath applies. There is therefore no possible formulation in which Rabbi Yehoshua would concede exemption, and the Maharshal’s question would never arise. It appears, however, that the Maharsha follows those authorities who hold that Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages disagree only where the son says, “I stood there and saw that you did not repay, because I never moved away from them,” or an equivalent claim based upon his own observation. If the son says only, “My father told me so,” then even Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov agrees that the defendant can be brazen toward him. They derive this from the Gemara in Shevuot, where the first clause, “I owe your father one hundred and fed him half,” is exempt because the defendant is returning a lost object, and the Gemara establishes it as a case where the son did not make a fully independent definite claim. If the son’s claim were wholly uncertain, however, why would the Gemara need the reason that the defendant is returning a lost object? No oath is imposed on an uncertain claim in any event, as the Mordechai asks. One must therefore say that the son’s claim is definite in the sense that he says, “My father told me,” yet it is not based upon his own observation. Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov agrees that in such a case the defendant can be brazen toward the son. At the same time, the statement “my father told me” is sufficiently definite to support an oath where the other requirements exist, for otherwise the Gemara could simply have said that the claim itself is insufficient rather than invoking the lost object rationale. Accordingly, the Maharsha can distinguish two versions of the Maharshal’s proposed case. If the defendant voluntarily says, “I owe your father one hundred but repaid fifty,” and the son says, “I stood there and saw that you never repaid,” there is no question at all. According to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov the defendant must indeed swear, exactly as Rabbenu Yerucham maintains, so the Mishnah could not present this as a case in which Rabbi Yehoshua concedes exemption. If instead the son says only, “My father told me that you did not repay,” then even Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov agrees that the defendant can brazenly deny him. In that setting there would be no oath because the defendant is returning a lost object, although absent that rationale the son’s statement is a definite claim. The Maharsha therefore correctly answers that such a case cannot be reconciled with the clause involving witnesses. If witnesses establish the loan, the defendant can still say that he repaid the entire amount to the father, because even according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov he can brazenly deny the son where the son did not personally observe the matter. Thus the witnesses would not produce the result stated in the Mishnah. The Maharsha’s words are therefore clear and compelling, and they assume Rabbenu Yerucham’s understanding of Rashi. The Rosh in Gittin writes that the Rif, who holds that Rabbi Yitzchak’s rule does not accord with Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov after the Gemara concludes that a person can be brazen toward the son, does not follow Rashi’s explanation. Rashi maintains that even at the conclusion the case concerns an actual minor, because the lost object was handed over after the claimant had reached adulthood. This is difficult. What practical difference should the claimant’s actual age make? The Maharsha explicitly writes that even according to Rashi, Rabbi Yitzchak’s rule is not inherently tied to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov. It seems that the Rosh understood that if the claimant is an adult, the case necessarily concerns one who claims with certainty based upon what his father told him, rather than one who personally stood and saw the event. Otherwise, why would the Gemara call him “a minor in his father’s matter”? If he personally saw everything, his claim is not dependent upon his father’s matter at all. He is called a minor in his father’s matter precisely because his knowledge rests upon his father’s statement. This also resolves another point. According to the view that the Sages agree that a defendant cannot brazenly deny the son where the son personally stood and saw, why does the Gemara establish the Mishnah in Shevuot concerning oaths to a minor specifically according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov rather than the Sages, by saying that the son stood and saw? If the claimant is truly an adult, that would not fit the Mishnah’s designation “a minor in his father’s matter,” because his claim rests on his own observation. According to Rashi, however, the claimant is an actual minor. It is therefore entirely possible that he stood and saw and is still called a minor simply because of his age. The Rosh certainly understood the comparison stated above. If a defendant can brazenly deny the son, he can likewise brazenly retain a found object, creating the concern that an oath will deter him from returning it. If he cannot brazenly deny the son, there is no comparable concern regarding a found object. But where the son personally stood and saw, everyone agrees that the defendant cannot brazenly deny him, and the lost object case likewise concerns one who stood and saw, as the Maharshal and Rashba explain, such as where the claimant stood on his roof and saw the finder lift the object. Rashi can therefore compare the two cases because even with a son who personally observed the matter the defendant can be brazen if the son is an actual minor. According to Tosafot, however, the claimant is not an actual minor, and the phrase “minor in his father’s matter” must mean that his knowledge depends upon his father’s report. The lost object case, which involves direct observation, is therefore not comparable to the case of the son at all. Examine this carefully.
