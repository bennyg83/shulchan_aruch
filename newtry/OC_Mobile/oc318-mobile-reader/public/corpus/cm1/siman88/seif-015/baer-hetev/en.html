Wheat. The Taz writes that the text should read "wheat and barley," for otherwise the defendant would in any event be exempt because his admission is not of the same type as the claim. In the Tur, however, the reading is actually "of the same type as the claim"; Beit Yosef emended it from "type" to "part." See there.
Before. The Ran explains that a partial admission generates a Torah oath only where the admission comes after the claim. It follows that even where both amounts are of one type, the defendant is exempt from the Torah oath if he admitted first. For example, they come to beit din and before the plaintiff speaks the defendant says, "I owe you fifty zuz." The plaintiff replies, "You owe me another fifty," and the defendant denies the additional fifty. Ramban and Baal HaTerumot state explicitly that the defendant is exempt from a Torah oath. See siman 75, seif 3 and below seif 32. Shach.
And pays. The Shach writes that because the defendant volunteered the original admission, he may later say, "I was mistaken in my admission and now remember that I owe you nothing," as explained at length in siman 81, seif 22. He cannot, however, say, "I was merely jesting." Although in seif 12 the Shach allowed a jest defense, that was because the plaintiff had first sued him for wheat, allowing the defendant to say, "Since you made a false claim against me, I jested back by admitting something else." Here there was no prior demand at all, so a volunteered admission before beit din cannot naturally be explained as jest. Conversely, in seif 12 the defense available is specifically jest, not mistake, because there the admission came in direct response to the wheat claim. The rule allowing a volunteered admission before beit din to be retracted for mistake applies only where it was made without any prior claim.
