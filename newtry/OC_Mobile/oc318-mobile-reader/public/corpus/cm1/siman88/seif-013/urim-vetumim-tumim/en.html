That he designated it as an apotiki, etc. The Shach asks that the Nimukei Yosef derived this rule from the Gemara's treatment of a case where the injured party says that a mu'ad ox caused the damage and the tortfeasor says that a tam ox caused it as analogous to one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley. This is so even though a mu'ad is collected from the owner's superior assets and all his property is subject to the claim. See Nimukei Yosef to Bava Kamma, who likewise implies that even a claim for the monetary value of wheat and an admission of the monetary value of barley is considered claiming wheat and admitting barley, because we follow the underlying object. I brought some support for this from the sugya concerning reversal and conspiring witnesses. Where witnesses testify that a master blinded his slave's eye and knocked out his tooth and are then found to be conspiring witnesses, if the sequence was reversed they pay the value of the eye to the slave and the value of the tooth to the master. According to what I wrote, this is understandable because the matter is treated as claiming wheat and admitting barley, so the master is exempt even from the tooth despite the existence of witnesses. I discussed this at length in my novellae. In the case of the tam ox, even though the owner's other assets are not subject to payment, he cannot discharge the claim with money instead of the ox. By contrast, in our case of an apotiki the debtor can ordinarily redeem the designated property with money, so it seems merely like collateral. The Shach therefore seeks to force the Shulchan Aruch's case into one where the apotiki was designated in such a way that the debtor cannot redeem it at all. This is strained. According to Tosafot at the beginning of chapter HaParah, however, who write that the Mishnah could have been established according to Rabbi Yishmael, another difficulty remains. The Gemara asks from the case where one party says the mu'ad caused the damage and the other says the tam caused it, treating this as claiming wheat and admitting barley. Why not answer that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Yishmael, according to whom the tortfeasor may satisfy even the tam obligation with money and the ox is merely an apotiki? We must conclude that even in such a case the rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley applies. This supports the ruling here. As to the underlying reason, another point first requires explanation. Rambam writes in chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, and the Mechaber cites him below in siman 400, seif 3, regarding one tam and one mu'ad, or one large ox and one small ox, that if there is no proof, the tortfeasor must take a Torah oath concerning the balance that he denies. The Raavad explains Rambam as referring to the case where the injured party says that the mu'ad caused the damage and the tortfeasor admits only the tam. The Shach there asks from the Shulchan Aruch's ruling here, following the Nimukei Yosef, that if one claims a maneh and the defendant admits an item designated as an apotiki, the admission is not considered of the same kind as the claim. Lechem Mishneh similarly questions the Raavad. Why does the Gemara challenge from the case where one says that the mu'ad caused the damage and the other that the tam did, as though it were claiming wheat and admitting barley? Should this not be considered an admission of the same kind as the claim? The Shach there follows his own approach and seeks to reconcile everything according to the Ramah, who holds that the exemption in the wheat and barley case is based upon the defendant's ability to say that he was merely mocking the claimant. Even if such an explanation can be said according to the Raavad, it cannot explain the Shulchan Aruch, which rules against the Ramah. Nor does it appear to be Rambam's position in Hilchot To'en VeNitan. The simple explanation is as follows. I already wrote in seif katan 8 that if people do not confuse the relevant times, then even where one claims wheat lent in Nisan and the defendant admits wheat lent in Tishrei, the rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley can apply according to the view that it applies even to two obligations arising at different times. Accordingly, where the dispute concerns two distinct bodies, with the claimant saying that this mu'ad ox caused the damage and the tortfeasor saying that this tam ox caused it, people certainly do not confuse one ox with another, nor a tam with a mu'ad. Even if the two liabilities are called the same type for purposes of the oath, just as wheat and wheat are the same type, the claimant's assertion that this particular mu'ad caused the damage constitutes an admission that the other, tam ox did not cause it. Since he cannot plausibly have confused them, his own admission exempts the tam. The tortfeasor, meanwhile, denies liability based on the mu'ad. The fact that both obligations are of the same general monetary type therefore does not help. If witnesses exist, however, Rambam holds that the claimant is not exempted by his own statement, as explained above. The defendant's admission then becomes a genuine partial admission and he must take a Torah oath. This is straightforward. Accordingly, our case is also clear. With respect to the claimant's statement operating to exempt the item admitted by the defendant, it makes no difference whether the admission is formally considered of the same kind as the claim. The only question is whether people could have confused one with the other. A claim for an ordinary maneh and an item designated as an apotiki are certainly not matters people confuse. By claiming only the ordinary maneh, the claimant effectively admits that the defendant does not owe him the apotiki item, and the defendant is exempt regarding it. It is true that the Raavad appears internally difficult. He rules that where the claimant says a mu'ad caused the damage and the defendant says a tam did, there is a Torah oath because a mu'ad is paid from superior assets. Yet the Rosh, in the sugya of the borrowed and rented animals, cites the Raavad as holding that even a loan and a deposit are two different kinds. All the more so tam and mu'ad should be different kinds. Indeed, the Baal HaTerumot goes so far as to say that a tam is even further from an ordinary deposit. No one says it is closer. However, as appears from the Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 96a in the Raavad's discussion of the borrowed animal, the Raavad may distinguish between one claim and two claims. If the claimant demands a loan and the defendant admits a deposit, the deposit may count as an admission toward the loan because the distinction between them is relatively small. But if the claimant separately demands both a loan and a deposit and the defendant admits only the deposit, that admission has no relationship at all to the loan because it answers only the independent deposit claim. If so, there is no contradiction in the Raavad. The Tur, however, does not appear to understand him this way, and the matter requires study. In truth, the law could have been stated even more broadly. If one claims an ordinary maneh and the defendant admits a maneh secured by an apotiki, the defendant should be exempt, because the two are not matters people confuse. The Baal HaTerumot explicitly writes similarly regarding a claim for a dark coin and an admission of a light coin. See what I wrote above in siman 30, seif katan 5, in his name. It appears, however, that the present formulation was chosen to teach that the defendant is exempt even from an oath, and therefore the case is framed as partial admission. Still, the plain wording of the Shulchan Aruch seems to imply that the rule is specifically where one claimed wheat and the other barley, and this requires study. The Maggid Mishneh, however, explains Rambam as holding that even regarding two tam oxen the defendant must swear when witnesses exist. This requires study. Two distinct bodies should certainly count as two different kinds, as the Gemara in the sugya concerning an exchange says regarding two slaves. Rashi explains there that two distinct slaves are treated as two kinds. What difference is there between two slaves and two oxen? Their bodies are equally distinct. The Shach already raises this difficulty in siman 400, seif katan 6. It appears to me that the Maggid Mishneh's explanation of Rambam rests upon a point stated in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia. The question is raised there why the verse "for this is it" is needed to exempt one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley from an oath. Once the defendant is exempt from paying the barley, there is no effective partial admission, so what oath could exist? Likewise, how do we know that Rabban Gamliel obligates an oath? Perhaps Rabban Gamliel obligates only payment of the barley because he rejects Rabbah bar Natan's rule, but would agree that no oath is generated. The answer is cited in the name of Mahari Katz that the two issues depend upon one another. The defendant is exempt from the oath because there is no enforceable payment obligation on the admitted item. If payment were due, the admission would count as partial admission even if it were not formally of the same kind as the claim. The particular kind would make no difference. The Torah's phrase "for this is it" therefore teaches that he is exempt from payment of the admitted different item, and once there is no payment obligation, he is automatically exempt from the oath. Rabban Gamliel, who does not expound "for this is it" in that way, holds that payment is due and consequently an oath is due as well. Accordingly, if the formal sameness of the claim and admission is irrelevant to the oath and the decisive question is whether payment is owed, then where witnesses establish the goring and Rambam holds that payment of the lesser amount is due, the issue of whether the admission is of the same kind as the claim no longer matters for the oath either. A Torah oath is required. This is the meaning of the Maggid Mishneh's statement that the oath applies because witnesses exist. Once witnesses create the payment obligation, the oath follows. If so, according to the Rosh's view discussed above, where one claims wheat from one time and the defendant admits barley from another time and the latter obligation is not extinguished, a Torah oath should likewise result. I have not found the authorities state this explicitly. Nevertheless, it is clear that this is the Maggid Mishneh's intent in explaining Rambam. It also appears to be the Mechaber's view, for below in siman 400 he cites without qualification that the defendant must take a Torah oath and does not make the Raavad's distinction. Here too he writes that the defendant is exempt from both payment and a Torah oath. The Shach, however, in siman 400, seif katan 6, asks concerning two tam oxen, one large and one small, why there should be any Torah oath. Even if the tortfeasor admitted the claim, he would not become liable for money, because it would be an admission to a fine. Tosafot at the beginning of chapter HaParah similarly write concerning the case of an ox that gored a cow that even if the injured party makes a definite claim and the tortfeasor is uncertain, the rule that one who cannot swear must pay does not apply, because even an admission would not create monetary liability, since one who admits a fine is exempt. This difficulty is not only against the Maggid Mishneh. It applies equally to the Rosh and Tur. The Rosh writes that where witnesses saw the goring but did not know which ox, the larger or smaller, caused it, and the tortfeasor admits the larger, this is not an admission to a fine. Why not? Their words are entirely correct. The Mishnah says that one who admits a fine is exempt. It does not say that one who admits facts relevant only to payment of a fine is exempt. Consider a thief whose theft was witnessed and who therefore became liable for double payment, but whom beit din believes to be utterly penniless and from whom nothing can presently be collected. If he then comes to beit din and reveals that he has hidden funds from which the double payment can be collected, would anyone call him one who admits a fine and exempt him? Such a position should be forgotten and never stated. He is merely revealing the means of payment for a fine already established by witnesses. The same applies here. The fine imposed by the Torah is for the goring. When an ox gores another ox and causes one hundred in damage, the Torah imposes a fine of fifty. That is the fine arising from the goring itself. From what property is that fine to be collected? The Torah further says that it is collected only from the body of the damaging ox. If the ox is worth less than fifty, the injured party loses part of his fine. If it is worth enough, by whatever means that fact is established, he collects the full fine. The value and identity of the particular ox therefore concern payment of the fine, not the creation of the fine itself. Thus, where witnesses saw Reuven's ox gore Shimon's ox, the Torah's fine for the goring has already been established by witnesses. They saw both the goring and damage worth one hundred. Their only uncertainty is which of Reuven's oxen, the larger or smaller, caused it. That uncertainty affects only the extent to which the injured party can collect the already established fine. If the tortfeasor then admits that the larger ox caused the damage, thereby enabling the injured party to collect the full fine, he is not admitting the fine itself. The fine was already established by the witnesses. He is merely admitting facts determining payment of the fine, and such an admission is binding. By contrast, in the case of an ox that gored a cow while its fetus was beside it, the witnesses saw the goring of the cow but did not see or know whether the ox also caused the loss of the fetus or whether the cow miscarried independently. The uncertainty there concerns whether any fine exists regarding the fetus at all. If the ox did not cause the miscarriage, there is no fine for the fetus. Thus the doubt concerns the very existence of the fine. If the tortfeasor admits that his ox also caused the loss of the fetus, that is an admission creating the fine itself, and one who admits a fine is exempt. Tosafot's rule there is therefore entirely consistent. With due respect, the Shach wrote his objection too quickly and failed to distinguish between these different subjects. As for the separate question that the admitted amount might be heilech, see what I wrote at length above in seif katan 8.
However, regarding the underlying law in the case of the ox that gored the cow, there is room for further analysis. Tosafot give two answers. One is that the tortfeasor is not considered one who cannot swear and therefore must pay because he had no reason to know. In Bava Metzia, in the sugya of the borrowed and rented animals, Tosafot give only this answer and do not mention at all the answer that his admission would be an admission to a fine. Rashba too, in his novellae to Bava Kamma chapter HaParah, answers only that he is not one who cannot swear because he had no reason to know, without mentioning admission to a fine. It is strained to say that according to the latter answer there would be a difficulty from Rav Pappa, who holds that half damages are compensatory rather than a fine. One can simply say that Rav Pappa follows Tosafot's other establishment of Shmuel's statement, that it concerns one who sold an ox, etc., rather than the injured party saying he is certain. Tosafot explicitly write in chapter Yesh Nochalin that Abaye, who holds that a definite claim prevails even where the uncertain party had no reason to know, establishes Shmuel's statement as referring to the case of one who sold an ox. The same can be said for Rav Pappa. It therefore appears that Tosafot and Rashba may hold as Maharsha writes, that once witnesses saw part of the event of goring, even if they did not see every element, the tortfeasor's later statement is no longer classified as an admission to a fine. Maharsha goes even further, deriving from the Mishnah's wording that where an ox was pursuing another and the injured party says it was gored while the owner says it was struck by a rock, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. This implies that if the owner admitted the goring he would be liable rather than exempt as one who admits a fine. Maharsha therefore concludes that even where witnesses saw only the pursuit and did not see the goring at all, a later admission is not treated as an admission to a fine. The Rosh, it is true, does not write this way. He uses the need for witnesses to the actual goring as an objection to the Ramah's approach, reasoning that without witnesses the owner's admission would be an admission to a fine. How then would the Rosh explain the Mishnah's case of an ox pursuing another? Maharsha understands the Rosh as establishing the first clause with a mu'ad ox. By contrast, his question against Rashbam, who says that Sumchus does not apply where the disputed property is in the possessor's house, necessarily concerns a tam ox, because with a mu'ad there is no relevance to the ox standing in a neutral field, since payment comes from superior assets. From there Maharsha concludes that witnesses to pursuit alone can be enough to prevent the admission from being classified as one to a fine. Although Maharsha's view is not stated by the decisors, and I already wrote above that there is another way to resolve the difficulty, one should nevertheless take note that so great an authority held that witnesses to pursuit can suffice. All the more so, witnesses to the goring itself should clearly suffice for the distinction I have made. In the case discussed above, therefore, there is no doubt in my view that the tortfeasor's later statement concerns only payment of an already established fine and is binding.
And from payment. This is the wording of the Tur, and the Mechaber's language likewise indicates that the correct reading is "from payment," as the Sema and Shach write. They ask, however, why according to the Tur the defendant should be exempt from payment. The entire reason is that because the claimant failed to demand the barley, he is treated as having admitted that it was not owed. Here, however, the claimant protests that he intended to demand it but the defendant hurried to answer before he could do so. The Tur also writes that if the claimant says that he did not wish to demand the barley at this time, his statement is accepted. But according to the Rosh and Tur the case of wheat and barley concerns obligations that arose together, for otherwise the Rosh already holds that the defendant is not exempt since a person does not necessarily demand all his claims at once. The Shach answers that this case concerns obligations arising at different times, one in Nisan and one in Tishrei. The Rosh's rule that the defendant is not exempt in such a situation applies when the claimant actually says that he intended not to demand the second claim at this time, as the Tur states. He then retains the barley claim. But if the claimant says that he intended to demand it immediately and was prevented only because the defendant hurried to answer, we do not accept his assertion, even though the two loans did not arise together. The Shach's words are terse and require explanation. The case must be one where the defendant did not answer literally within the brief period immediately following the claimant's words. Rather, several intervals of speech had passed, or it was otherwise apparent to beit din that the claimant had finished speaking and, from the manner of his statement, did not intend to add another claim at that time. Therefore, if after the defendant's response the claimant says, within the brief period following that response, that he had intended to demand another obligation, we do not believe him. His assertion appears false because his earlier pause and completed formulation indicated that he did not intend another immediate demand. By contrast, if he admits that he did not intend to demand the barley at that moment because he wished to wait and demand it later, he is believed. This appears to be the Shach's intent, though his wording is terse. This explanation is nevertheless strained, because it gives the present rule a different factual framework from the ordinary case of claiming wheat and admitting barley discussed everywhere by the Tur and Rosh, which concerns obligations arising together. Had the Tur intended a materially different case here, he should have said so explicitly. According to what I wrote above in explaining the Rosh, the matter is straightforward. Even where the obligations arose together, the claimant's initial failure to mention the barley is not itself yet the decisive admission. The admission occurs only if, after the defendant mentions the barley, the claimant returns to his original demand and again insists on the wheat. But if instead he says that he does not wish to demand the barley at present, he is believed, even though the two obligations arose together. The present rule can therefore concern the ordinary case where both arose simultaneously. This creates no difficulty and is further proof for what I wrote above regarding the Rosh. One passage illuminates the other, and the explanation stated there is the correct one. Still, one must examine where the Tur derived this rule from the Rosh's intent. When the Rosh says "exempt," perhaps he means only exempt from the oath, since that is the subject he was discussing, as many authorities explain. Maadanei Melech also appears to understand him in that manner. See there. It appears that the Tur was compelled to his interpretation by the following difficulty. The Rosh rejected Rashi's explanation based on waiver because that reasoning does not resemble the Mishnah in HaManiach concerning a tam and a mu'ad. He therefore explains the wheat and barley case as one in which the two obligations arose together. Since they arose together, the claimant should have demanded both at once, and his failure to do so is treated as an admission. But this itself is somewhat strained. Even a person who made two loans at the same moment may not wish to collect both simultaneously. It may be difficult to extract payment, so he may choose to collect one and later the other. Why, then, did the Rosh not explain the matter in the simplest possible way, namely that both parties agree that there was only one loan, while the claimant says it consisted of wheat and the defendant says it consisted of barley? In such a case, the claimant's formulation is necessarily both an admission and a waiver regarding the barley. He admits that no barley was ever lent, while the defendant denies the wheat. This is exactly parallel to the case in HaManiach where one says the larger ox gored and the other says the smaller ox did, when there was only one goring. Such an explanation would simplify many aspects of the sugya. We must therefore say that the Rosh understood the statement in Shevuot concerning one who claims wheat and the defendant preemptively admits barley, where if the defendant acted strategically he is liable and otherwise exempt, as applying to payment as well. Since the baraita states simply that he is "exempt," it implies exemption even from payment. If the ordinary wheat and barley rule concerned only a single loan, whereas where there were two loans everyone would agree that the defendant must pay the barley, then the Shevuot case necessarily concerns two separate loans. Otherwise, what strategic maneuver could there be? If there was only one loan, the claimant cannot demand more than the value of the wheat, so the notion of preemptively admitting barley as a strategy has no place. Yet the baraita states without qualification that he is exempt, implying exemption from payment. Thus Rabbah bar Natan's rule must apply even to two distinct loans. The Rosh was therefore forced to explain that the two loans arose together, making the case somewhat analogous to HaManiach and allowing the claimant's omission to be treated as admission. The Rosh's words are thus compelled. The Tur's interpretation that the exemption includes payment is also compelled, because if the baraita meant only exemption from an oath due to the admission not being of the same kind as the claim, it would provide no proof that Rabbah bar Natan exempts payment where two loans exist. See what I wrote in the preceding seif katan regarding the Maggid Mishneh's view, following Mahari Katz, that liability for the oath and liability for payment depend upon one another. According to that approach, the Tur's ruling is likewise necessary. Since the Mechaber below in siman 400 follows the Maggid Mishneh's approach, his words here too must be read as "from payment," in accordance with the Sema's emendation. Nevertheless, as a matter of final halachah the ruling requires study. Many great later authorities disagree. Moreover, numerous early authorities hold that the entire exemption applies only because the defendant can retract his admission, whereas if he stands by his words he must pay. According to Rabbenu Yerucham, even the Rosh agrees with that. We should therefore not extend the exemption further and free the defendant from payment in a case such as this. Bach likewise rules this way, and this is the position that should be followed.
