(Seif 24) “Even if he claimed a specific house full of produce.” Initially Abaye held in Shevuat HaDayanim, 43, that if the claimant says merely “a house,” the claim is not measured because the house is not identified, but if he says, “this house full,” the claim is known and is measured. Rava concludes that even then it is insufficient. Although the claim itself is measured, the law requires that the claimant state a measured amount and that the defendant admit a measured amount. Tosafot asked why this is not measured, since one can see how much is missing. They answered first that a house containing a small deficiency is still called full, and the amount separating the claims is therefore unknown. Accordingly, the defendant must expressly name a measure in his admission. This answer requires examination. Why did Rava need to formulate a new rule that the admission too must be measured? He should have ruled exemption simply because the claim is itself unmeasured, since “full” is not exact. It appears that Rava holds that the claim is still considered quantified, because even if the house is not literally packed to its utmost capacity, the term “full” still constitutes a measure for this purpose. Indeed, had the defendant answered, “You gave me half a house,” that too would be a quantified admission. This is evident from the Mishnah’s failure to teach the more novel rule that he is exempt because the claimant’s assertion itself is unmeasured. The same can be said of the latter clause, where one claims “up to the beam” and the other “up to the window.” Even if a slight deficiency would still be called “up to the beam,” once the defendant answers “up to the window,” both statements are treated as quantified. The present case differs, because the defendant says, “I do not know; take what you deposited.” He personally has admitted no measured amount. It is only we who would measure the contents and determine what is missing, thereby creating a measurement after the fact. Tosafot therefore explain that measurement is impossible here, because even if some amount is missing the house may still be called full. Their phrase, “the difference between them is unknown,” means that the very measurement one seeks to perform cannot be fixed. We rule in accordance with Tosafot’s second answer, that the defendant must mention the name of the measure in his admission. According to the first answer, which rests on inability to determine the difference, if the claimant says, “You have fifty dinars of mine,” and the defendant answers, “Take what you deposited,” he would be obligated in a Torah oath because the difference can be determined. Yet seif 23 states expressly that he is exempt. Thus the second answer is primary. This also proves what I wrote immediately above: even if the bailee originally received the deposit by count but now does not know the number, he is exempt, because he did not state a measure in his present admission. Sema cited Tosafot’s first answer as saying that even if it is not completely full it is still called full, “and therefore the claim is not known.” This implies that he understood Tosafot to mean that the claimant’s assertion is unmeasured. That is difficult for the reason already stated: if so, why did Rava need to mention that the admission must be measured? Sema also cited Tosafot’s second answer as requiring the claimant to mention a measure in his claim. In the Tosafot text before us, however, it says that the measure must be mentioned in the admission, as I have written.
