That you gave that money for a certain object, etc. This applies specifically in such a case, where if the object is lost the defendant is exempt because the risk is borne by the claimant. But if the utensil remains the defendant's responsibility, such as where he owes him a utensil in any event, the case is like one who claims one hundred, and whatever amount he admits he owes, as above in seif 9. This is why the Mechaber also writes here that collection is to be made only from the body of that object. Otherwise it would resemble the case above in seif 9, where the Gemara and poskim explicitly indicate that he becomes fully liable and cannot retract. Examine carefully.
Or that you lent me upon it, etc., he takes a heset oath. He swears that he does not owe him as the claimant asserts and is exempt. This is where he retracted his admission. But if he maintains his admission, or there are witnesses in accordance with what he admitted, he is obligated to take a Torah oath regarding the remainder, as follows from the Rambam in chapter 9 of Hilchos Nizkei Mamon, the Raavad and Maggid Mishneh there, and the Mechaber below in siman 400, seif 3, as I explain there. Examine carefully. In the first case, however, where the claimant says that he gave that money for a certain object, even if the defendant maintains his admission he is exempt from a Torah oath, because it is heilech, as I wrote above in siman 87, seif 1, and below in seif 24. But where he lent upon the object on condition that collection be made only from the object itself, this is not heilech, since the object does not become the claimant's until after appraisal by beit din, like an innocuous ox, as I write below in siman 400, seif 3.
A certain object that he designated to him as an apotiki, etc. This is indeed the wording of the Nimukei Yosef there, but it is difficult. Even with an explicit apotiki, the borrower can satisfy the creditor with money, as below in siman 103, seif 6, and at the beginning of siman 117. In the Nimukei Yosef one could perhaps say that these are the words of the Ramah, and perhaps he holds that with an explicit apotiki the borrower cannot discharge the lien with money, as appears from Rashi in Perek Eizehu Neshech regarding asmachta. But if the borrower can pay money, the claim is effectively: give me this object or its monetary value. Accordingly, when the defendant admits that he borrowed fifty zuz, it is an admission of the same type as the claim, since in the end he admits an obligation of money. It is entirely implausible to say that because the borrower can force the creditor to take the object, or because the borrower would have been exempt had the object been lost, the admission is not of the same type as the claim. Rather, because he can ultimately discharge the debt with money, the claimant is demanding either the object or one hundred zuz, and the defendant admits fifty zuz. This is a complete partial admission. The Nimukei Yosef's comparison to an innocuous ox is not analogous, because with an innocuous ox the tortfeasor cannot discharge the claim with money, since the injured party and tortfeasor are partners in the ox according to Rabbi Akiva, as I write below at the beginning of siman 401. It therefore appears that the statement here that he designated it as an apotiki is imprecise. It means a case like the first clause, where he lent money upon it on condition that collection be made only from its body, analogous to an innocuous ox. Namely, he stipulated that when the due date arrives he must give the creditor the specific object after appraisal and will not be able to discharge the obligation with money. This appears correct. Examine carefully.
