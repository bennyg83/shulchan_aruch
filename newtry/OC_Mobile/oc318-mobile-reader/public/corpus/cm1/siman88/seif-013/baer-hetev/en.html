From its body. The defendant takes a hesset oath that he does not owe according to the claimant's version and is exempt. This is where he retracts his admission. If he maintains the admission, or there are witnesses in accordance with what he admitted, he is obligated in a Torah oath concerning the remainder, as is evident from the Rambam, Raavad, and Maggid Mishneh in chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon. In the first case, however, where the claimant says that he gave the defendant an article and the defendant returns that very article, even if he maintains his admission he is exempt from a Torah oath because this is "here it is," as stated in siman 87, seif 1, and below in seif 24. But where he lent him money on condition that collection be made only from the body of a particular object, it is not considered "here it is," because the object does not become the claimant's until after appraisal by beit din, comparable to an innocuous ox, as stated in siman 400, seif 3. An apotiki. The Shach writes: It is true that this is the wording of the Nimukei Yosef, but it is difficult. Even with an explicit apotiki, the borrower can discharge the lien by paying money, as stated in siman 103, seif 6, and at the beginning of siman 117. With respect to the Nimukei Yosef itself, one could say that the passage appears to record the Ramah's view, and perhaps he holds that with an explicit apotiki the debtor cannot discharge it with money, as is implied by Rashi in chapter Eizehu Neshech regarding asmachta. See there. If so, when the claimant says, "Give me this object or its monetary value," and the defendant admits that he borrowed fifty zuz, his admission is of the same kind as the claim, because ultimately he admits a monetary debt. This should therefore be a complete admission of part. The Nimukei Yosef's comparison to an innocuous ox is not apt, because there the tortfeasor cannot discharge the liability with money. The damaged party and owner of the ox are partners in the ox according to Rabbi Akiva, as stated at the beginning of siman 401. It therefore appears that the term "apotiki" here is not precise. Rather, the case is like the first one, where he lent money against an object on condition that payment be made only from that object, comparable to an innocuous ox. That is, he stipulated that when the due date arrives the debtor must give him the object itself after appraisal and has no right to discharge the obligation with money. This appears correct. Shach.
