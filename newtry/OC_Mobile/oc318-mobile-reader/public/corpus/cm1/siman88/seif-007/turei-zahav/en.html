(Seif 14) “If he did so innocently.” The wording implies that he likewise hurried to answer, but did not do so as a stratagem; rather, he responded innocently. Thus “if he did so” means that he hurried to reply, but did so innocently. The wording of the Rosh cited by Beit Yosef, “and not like one who jumps,” also implies that he in fact responds quickly, but does not appear to be leaping to do so. Otherwise, the Rosh should have written simply “and he does not jump,” without the comparative letter kaf. The Rif’s language likewise implies this. Yet I find difficult the Tur’s wording here, that he is exempt from both the oath and payment. What proof do we have to exempt him from a definite debt? With respect to the oath, it is understandable, because perhaps this is not a partial admission. But payment of a clear debt cannot be removed merely because of doubt, for perhaps the claimant intended immediately to add the barley to his claim. The Rosh wrote only that he is exempt, which may refer solely to the oath, while the Rif expressly wrote that he is exempt from a Torah oath, implying that he is not exempt from payment. The Mechaber, whose language here follows the Tur, also omitted the words “and from payment”; Sema inserted them by emendation. I found this reading also in Semag, which likewise mentions “and from payment.” It appears to me that there is indeed proof that the claimant waived the barley. He said, “You have a kor of wheat belonging to me.” Had he intended also to claim barley, he should have included it in the same statement, saying, “You have a kor of wheat and barley belonging to me,” rather than use the imprecise expression, “and also barley.” Still, one could argue that this person was not exact in his wording. Accordingly, the law distinguishes: if he responded as one acting strategically, meaning that we see a defect in his conduct because he jumps to answer deceptively, we conclude that he was not exact in his wording and intended also to mention barley. But if he acted innocently, there is no defect before us. We therefore assume that he spoke precisely in the ordinary manner, and since he did not state both claims in one clause, he certainly waived the barley. According to this, if he merely said, “A kor of wheat,” and stopped, doing so innocently, that does not exempt the defendant from payment. Likewise, if he said, “You have a kor of wheat belonging to me,” it is possible that he intended to continue, “and barley”; in that case the defendant remains liable for payment. This appears correct to me.
