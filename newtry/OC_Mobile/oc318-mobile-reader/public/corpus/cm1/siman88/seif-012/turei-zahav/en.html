(Seif 23) “I have nothing of yours except a bundle.” Sema asked from siman 75, where it is stated that if one claims, “I lent you one hundred,” and the other answers, “It is true that you lent me, but I do not know how much,” some say that his admission is presumed to include at least one perutah and he is liable. Sema answered that there the defendant responded to a quantified claim by admitting that he had borrowed a quantified amount, though he does not know how much, and is therefore liable. Here, however, he says that he never received the items by count, but only as a single bundle without counting, and says, “Take what you deposited.” According to Sema, if he admitted here that he originally received the items by count, though he now does not know how many, and says, “Take what you deposited,” he would be liable. This is exceedingly difficult. At present there is still no admission of a quantified amount, and what benefit is there in the fact that it was originally counted? Moreover, if so, Rava’s challenge to Abaye, that the Mishnah should distinguish within the same case, is even stronger: it should have taught in the latter clause that where one says, “up to the window,” he is liable, since there he knows the measure even now; according to Sema he would be liable even if he no longer knew the measure, provided he knew the count originally. Plainly this is not so. Sema overlooked the Rosh’s words in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. The Rosh first cites Rabbi Yosef Halevi regarding one who says, “I know that the pledge was worth more than I lent you, but I do not know how much more.” He rules that this is a case of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and therefore must pay. Great authorities objected that he is not obligated to swear, because he did not admit a quantified amount. Why is this different from one who claims, “I delivered to you a house full of produce,” and the other says, “Take whatever you left,” who is exempt? The Rosh answered: “There is no difficulty. Since he says, ‘I know it was worth more than the amount owed to me,’ he has admitted that it was worth at least one perutah more. Less than a perutah is not counted as value in a single object. Thus, one who says that it was worth more than a sela has necessarily admitted at least one perutah. There is therefore an admission of money. Since he admitted without specifying, the admission includes only the smallest monetary amount, and we can extract from him only one perutah. It is as though he expressly admitted one additional perutah, and regarding the balance he is obligated to swear but cannot swear. This differs from the claim, ‘I entrusted you with a house full of produce,’ where the other says, ‘I do not know; take whatever you deposited.’ There the defendant is not making an uncertain answer. Rather, he says: ‘As to your assertion that you entrusted me with a full house, I did not pay attention to whether it was literally full.’ Nor is the claimant’s assertion entirely definite, because common speech calls a house ‘full’ even when it is only nearly full. The defendant nevertheless knows with certainty that the claimant may take exactly what he deposited. There is therefore no admission at all, since neither claim nor answer states a measure, weight, or count. The claimant alleges only a shortage of produce, while the defendant says that nothing is missing. But if the claimant says, ‘I entrusted you with such-and-such an amount of produce,’ and the bailee says, ‘I know it was worth one perutah, but I do not know how much more,’ there is an oath obligation, provided that according to the claimant the produce was worth two silver ma’ot. If, however, he merely says ‘produce’ and the defendant does not admit one perutah, his admission does not imply a perutah, because even less than a perutah is called produce.” To understand the Rosh, note that where he says, “it was not literally full,” rather than “I do not know whether it was full,” and where he later says that no more than one perutah can be extracted, which seems unnecessary, his intent is this: in the pledge case, the one additional perutah is a fixed minimum, no less and no more, because one can never extract more on the basis of his admission. It is therefore as though he expressly said “one perutah.” In the deposit case, even though the admitted items are certainly worth a perutah, they may be worth more, and he will have to return them because they belong to the claimant. His statement is therefore not a fixed quantified admission. One should not answer that the amount can be determined by examining the difference between the parties, as Tosafot’s first answer, cited below, might suggest. The Rosh already answered this by saying that “full” does not necessarily mean literally full, so the difference cannot be objectively measured. This resolves a claim phrased in a manner that may not mean literally full. But where the claimant states an exact number and the defendant says, “Take the bundle you deposited,” there is partial admission, because we can examine the difference and it necessarily includes at least one perutah. The Tur nevertheless rules that he is exempt even there, because he follows Tosafot’s second answer, cited below, that the defendant must expressly state a number; it is not enough that beit din can ascertain one. Therefore, there is no contradiction to siman 75, for there the defendant has admitted a fixed one perutah that cannot mean more. The entire question of Sema never begins. Nor is it difficult that the Tur ruled differently from his father, because the Rosh intended to teach that even according to Tosafot’s first answer, one is at least exempt in the produce case.
