And he swears concerning the remainder. He takes a Torah oath like any other case of partial admission, since the claimant demanded both items.
Exempt. This is where the claimant makes a definite claim. But if he says, "I do not know whether he owes me wheat or barley," and the defendant admits that he owes barley, he must at least pay the barley. This is stated in the Gemara, Bava Kamma, at the end of HaManiach.
Therefore, even if there are witnesses, etc. Shach asks that the Gemara there compares this to two oxen that pursued one ox and damaged it, where the injured party says the larger ox caused the damage and the owner says the smaller ox caused it. The defendant is exempt even for the smaller ox, just as one who is sued for wheat and admits barley is exempt for the barley. The Gemara assumes that the two cases have the same law. Yet below in siman 400 the Mechaber rules like Rambam that if witnesses saw the goring but do not know which ox gored, he is liable for the smaller ox. If so, the same should apply here. Shach answers by distinguishing between witnesses who do not know whether the large or small ox caused the damage, and witnesses who testify clearly that the small ox caused it. According to him, here too, if witnesses testify that they saw a loan but do not know whether it consisted of wheat or barley, he would be liable for the value of the barley. See Tumim, where I discussed Rambam's words at length and concluded that there is a distinction between the case below in siman 400 and the present case. According to that distinction, where the claimant says, "I deposited wheat," the defendant says, "You deposited barley," and there are witnesses such that the defendant cannot claim that the deposit was lost through unavoidable accident, because the witnesses saw it in his possession, it is possible that he must pay the value of the barley. This resembles a case of seizure, since the barley is in his possession in any event and he cannot deny it. See there. This requires study.
For the admission of a litigant, etc. Shach discusses this at length and rules in accordance with Ramah that the reason is that the defendant can say, "I was merely jesting with you." But if he maintains his admission, and certainly if there are witnesses, he is plainly liable. See Tumim, where I wrote that one should not depart from the Mechaber's ruling under any circumstances. See there.
It is as though he forgave, etc. Even though the debt is actually owed, since a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses, we assume that he forgave it, Sema. See Tumim, where I wrote that Rabbeinu Yerucham understands Rosh to distinguish as follows: where there are witnesses and the defendant does not maintain his statement, he is exempt, but if he maintains it, he must pay. That is his understanding of Rosh. Nevertheless, from the plain language of Tur and additional proofs I concluded that even if he maintains his statement, he is exempt from payment. See there.
And not like the dissenting view. Shach explains that this refers to Ramah's view. In my opinion it refers to Rabbeinu Yerucham, as I wrote at length in Tumim.
On a particular day. All the more so where according to both parties there was only one loan, and one says it was wheat while the other says it was barley. He is exempt, for this is identical to the case where one says the large ox damaged and the other says the small ox damaged, concerning which the Gemara in Bava Kamma says that he is exempt even for the value of the smaller one.
For a person does not forgive. According to Rosh, who holds that the issue is admission rather than forgiveness, it should have said that a person does not admit. It can be explained that the point is: the reason cannot be forgiveness, since a person does not forgive, and therefore the reason must be admission, which requires that both statements occur at the same time. So writes Shach. According to what I wrote in Tumim, that the ultimate effect of the admission is forgiveness, the matter is clear even without this distinction, for they are one and the same.
Only one. Shach writes that nevertheless, even if the two claims did not occur at the same time, if the claimant first demanded wheat, the defendant admitted barley, and the claimant then returned to his original claim and again demanded wheat without mentioning barley, Rosh agrees that the defendant is exempt for the barley. In Tumim I demonstrated from Rosh's own words that even in the case of an admission he is discussing a claimant who afterward returns to his original claim, yet he still requires that the two matters have occurred at one time. This requires study. If the claimant said, "No, only wheat," everyone agrees that even if the statements did not occur simultaneously he has waived the barley, because he expressly said, "No." See Tumim.
If the claimant seized the value of the barley. This does not mean specifically its monetary value. The same applies if he seized actual barley, and this is obvious.
We do not remove it from him. Sema explains that since he is now in possession he can say, "I never admitted and never forgave." See Tumim, where I wrote that this is not the reason. Rather, beit din itself is uncertain whether the earlier statement constituted a complete admission and forgiveness, and therefore once the claimant has seized property we do not remove it from him. I also gave another reason, as stated above.
Before he sued him in court, etc. See Tumim. According to the view that seizure after a doubt has arisen is ineffective, this can be explained as above: we are uncertain whether there was an admission and forgiveness, and therefore before they stood before beit din it is considered seizure before the doubt arose, whereas afterward it is seizure after the doubt arose. According to the Mechaber, however, who holds generally that seizure is effective, another reason is needed. Tur writes that the claimant can say, "I did not demand the barley because I already had barley of yours in my possession." Therefore, it must specifically have been in his possession at the time of the claim. See Tumim, where I explained why this is not difficult even if both parties agree that there was only one loan, with one saying wheat and the other barley. Why should seizure help? Merely because he has barley in his possession, does his admission disappear, when he still admits and maintains that no barley debt existed? See there, where I wrote that it is as though he stipulated that even though he is demanding wheat, an admission by the defendant concerning barley should not constitute an admission against him. This requires study.

With respect to the barley he has no migo, and it is a migo against witnesses. Sema and Shach.
