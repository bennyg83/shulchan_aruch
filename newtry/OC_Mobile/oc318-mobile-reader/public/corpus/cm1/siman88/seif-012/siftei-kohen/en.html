And some say that the reason is, etc. The Sema explains that this excludes the Ramah, who says that the defendant's admission of barley is not a binding admission, because he did not say, “You are my witnesses,” and did not admit before beit din. The Sema's words are incorrect, for the Gemara discusses an admission before beit din. When the Ramah adds, “or before beit din, where he was sued and admitted,” he means to exclude our case, in which he was not sued concerning barley and did not admit to the claim presented, since the claimant demanded wheat and he admitted barley. Perhaps there is a scribal error in the Sema. Below I establish the Ramah's view with clear proofs.
That the reason is, etc.; therefore, even if there are witnesses, etc. The Mechaber's ruling is difficult, for below, siman 400, seif 3, he rules without qualification like Rambam that when there are witnesses the defendant is liable, and cites no dissent. Perhaps he distinguishes that case because the witnesses do not know which damage occurred and therefore do not contradict the claimant. It resembles one who claims wheat while witnesses testify that the defendant owes either wheat or barley. He must at least pay barley, for the claimant can say: I maintain that their testimony concerns wheat, as I claim; if you say it concerns barley, then at least give me barley. Here, by contrast, the claimant is deemed to have admitted that no barley loan occurred, and a litigant's admission is stronger than one hundred witnesses. The witnesses' testimony that barley was lent would then be nullified because he contradicts them. This explanation is not primary. I establish below that when there are witnesses he is liable in every case, and the true reason for exemption absent witnesses is that the defendant may say, “I was jesting with you.”
For it is regarded as though he admitted, etc.; therefore, even if there are witnesses, etc. The Rosh, at the end of HaManiach, writes that the entire discussion applies even where there are witnesses. He rejects the Ramah, who explains that the defendant may say, “I was jesting with you,” based on the Tosefta that one who admits on his own may retract. The Tur, Maharshal, and Baal HaTerumot follow the Rosh. Baal HaTerumot nevertheless cites Raavad that exemption from barley applies only where, after admitting barley, the defendant says, “You have with me neither wheat nor barley; I was jesting.” If he maintains his admission of barley, he must pay it. Responsa Ramban, siman 117, cites Raavad similarly and concludes that an admission before beit din is like one hundred witnesses only where the other party first sued him. This accords with the Ramah. Halachot Gedolot also links this law to the Tosefta's distinction between an admission made in response to a claim and one made voluntarily. Raavad, in his glosses to chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en, the Maggid Mishneh there, Rambam in chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, the Nimukei Yosef, and the Mordechai all support the Ramah's approach. It follows that where witnesses establish the barley, the defendant must pay. Rambam's position is clear, as the Tur, Maggid Mishneh, Mechaber below in siman 400, and the later authorities understood it. The contrary reading in Gidulei Terumah is untenable. The Rosh, Baal HaTerumot, and Maharshal challenge the Ramah from the Mishnah concerning two damaged animals and two damaging animals. They assume witnesses saw the goring, because otherwise an admission would be an admission to a fine, and argue that jesting cannot apply where witnesses establish the event. This objection is not decisive. The Nimukei Yosef and Maggid Mishneh explain that the sugya follows the opinion that half damages are monetary, and thus can discuss a case without witnesses. Maharshal and Lechem Mishneh object that this would make the anonymous sugya follow a nonaccepted view. In my opinion, however, the whole sugya plainly concerns monetary liability without witnesses, and there is no difficulty. Indeed, if witnesses are present, the Rosh's explanation based on the claimant's implied admission is itself impossible. In the case of two damaged animals and two damaging animals, the claimant demands both animals and seeks more, not less. He has not waived or admitted away the smaller animal. If witnesses establish that both animals gored but do not know which damaged which, it is inconceivable that the defendant should pay nothing. The claimant may say: I maintain that the larger damaged the larger and the smaller damaged the smaller; the witnesses do not contradict me. If you reject my reconstruction, then pay at least what their testimony indisputably establishes. The same difficulty exists in the first clause, and is even stronger where the damaging animals are two. Nor can one reasonably say that the claimant's words erase clear testimony. The Gemara further asks where the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor uncertain. According to the Rosh's approach, even with witnesses the defendant would be exempt from both possibilities because the claimant supposedly admitted away the lesser claim. Rabbeinu Yerucham indeed writes this, but it is astonishing. If a claimant says, “I lent you a barrel of wheat,” the defendant says, “I know it was either wheat or barley,” and witnesses testify that a barrel of one or the other was lent, no reasonable court would exempt him even from barley. The same is true if witnesses establish two barrels, one wheat and one barley, but cannot identify which was larger. He must at least pay the smaller wheat quantity and the larger barley quantity. Likewise, where one animal was muad and one tam, the plain Gemara indicates that even the amount admitted for the muad's damage is included in the challenge, despite the fact that muad damages are ordinary monetary liability payable from all assets. If the Rosh's theory were correct, this would conflict with the law of one who claims the value of a large slave and receives an admission of the value of a small slave. The correct explanation is therefore the Ramah's. Rabbah bar Natan exempts because the defendant did not answer the claim but deflected it and may say that he was jesting. The Gemara challenges him from the Mishnah. According to the opinion that half damages are monetary, the Mishnah naturally includes a case without witnesses. According to the opinion that half damages are a fine, it may concern a case where liability had already been adjudicated on the basis of witnesses, although those witnesses are no longer present. In either event, the present claim is litigated without witnesses capable of resolving the uncertainty. Thus the Gemara can ask why the defendant's admission is not treated as mere deflection. The sugya need not follow the rejected opinion. It tests whether the accepted law of Rabbah bar Natan can coexist with the Mishnah under the view that half damages are monetary, and there are many such challenges in the Gemara. This also explains the case where the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor says, “I do not know whether the large or small animal caused the damage.” If the tortfeasor maintains that uncertainty, he must pay the lesser amount. The Gemara asks only whether he may retract and say that his prior answer was jesting, because ordinarily he was not present and had no duty to know which animal caused the damage. Similarly, where the claimant demands wheat and the defendant says, “I do not know whether wheat or barley,” if he maintains that answer he must pay barley. But where he answers a matter he was not required to know, the Gemara considers whether it was merely a deflection. The same reasoning explains the muad case: although the amount is ordinary money, the tortfeasor could originally have said that he knew nothing of the event, so his volunteered specification of the smaller damage may have been a jesting response. Where, however, the injured party claims that the large animal caused the damage and the tortfeasor admits, “Yes, the large animal caused it,” or where the claimant is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain, the admission is plainly binding. Jesting applies only where he contradicts and diverts the claimant, not where he fully confirms the claim. Maharshal's additional objection, that admitting barley in response to a wheat claim should count as partial admission because wheat is more valuable, is likewise answered: an admission to an item never claimed is not an admission to the claim but a diversion. This interpretation also explains the Gemara's phrase “he is fit to collect, but has nothing.” It means that if the tortfeasor stands by his admission, the claimant is fit to collect; if he retracts and says he was jesting, he has nothing. Tosafot's interpretation, that “fit to collect” means if he seized, is strained, because the Gemara introduces seizure only later in response to a baraita. The Gemara first attempts to explain the Mishnah without seizure, and only when pressed resorts to it. Even Rashi and Tosafot, who use the language of waiver or admission, may agree that witnesses obligate payment of at least the lesser amount. Baal HaTerumot, despite explaining the law by admission, rules that seizure helps even if made before witnesses. The claimant can say that he did not sue for barley because it was already in his possession. By the same logic, where he has witnesses he may say that he did not sue for barley because the witnesses already secure that amount, and he sued only for the additional wheat. Rambam's language in chapter 3 of Hilchot To'en, comparing the case to one who is told “I owe you a maneh” and answers “you owe me nothing,” means that beit din cannot compel payment on the basis of the defendant's unsolicited admission when the claimant did not demand it. It need not mean that the claimant conclusively admitted away the debt. Rambam's rulings in Hilchot Nizkei Mamon and below in siman 400 show that witnesses nevertheless obligate the lesser amount. Thus even on Rashi's and Tosafot's wording, the better understanding is that the defendant's admission lacks legal force because it did not respond to the claim, and he may retract as the Ramah says. Accordingly, Maharshal's conclusion that even witnesses to the barley do not help is incorrect. Since the claimant may reasonably have omitted the barley because it was independently provable, there is no implied admission. Nor must Rabbah bar Natan be limited, as the Rosh does, to wheat and barley claimed for the same date and hour. His unrestricted formulation means that even if the barley obligation arose at another time, the defendant's response is nonresponsive and may be withdrawn as jesting. This accords with the Rif, Rambam, and the other authorities. The later discussion of seizure also proves that the basis is jesting, as the Ramah and those who follow him maintain.
For it is as though he waived it, etc. The Levush, Sema, and Bach understand the Rosh and Tur to mean waiver, because an implied admission is a waiver. This is incorrect. The Rosh at the end of HaManiach expressly rejects waiver and adopts the language of admission. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 6a, Maharshal, Responsa Maharshal, and Responsa Maharan Lev likewise state that the reason is not waiver. Indeed, the Rosh indicates that if the basis were waiver, the rule would apply even where the two loans did not arise together. This differs from siman 75, seif 11, where one expressly says, “I owe you,” and the other answers, “You do not.” There, because he had occasion to consider the matter and nevertheless denied the debt, we treat it as a wholehearted waiver, so that even his heavenly obligation is removed, as though he had given a gift. Here that reasoning does not apply. Therefore, according to the Rosh and Tur, if the defendant knows that he owes barley, he must pay it and is a robber if he refuses, although beit din cannot extract it because he may rely on the claimant's apparent admission. Rabbeinu Yerucham states this expressly. The Bach's contrary reading of the Tur is incorrect. In any event, I have already established above that the primary reason is neither waiver nor admission, but jesting, as the Ramah and those who follow him explain. On a certain day and at a certain hour, etc. Maharshal explains Rabbah bar Natan according to its plain wording. He answers the Rosh's concern, that a person may have several claims and not wish to present them all at once, by saying that once the defendant admitted barley and the claimant responded, “You owe me wheat,” he thereby indicated that no barley was owed. Had barley also been owed, he should have said, “True, you owe me barley as well, but I did not intend to claim it now.” This by itself is not sufficient, for the Rosh too would agree that if the claimant expressly persists, “You owe me wheat and not barley,” he loses the barley even without specifying an hour. Rabbeinu Yerucham says so explicitly. The Rosh's difficulty was rather that it is strained to assume such a response. According to Maharshal's approach, one could instead say that by leaving beit din without claiming the admitted barley, the claimant indicated that no barley was owed. Gidulei Terumah writes similarly. Still, the Rosh did not limit the case to a specified hour only because of this concern. He also asked why the Gemara challenges Rabbah bar Natan from the Mishnah. In the Mishnah, the claim concerns a specific event, and the defendant may have shifted his admission to the smaller animal merely to gain and avoid the larger liability. If Rabbah bar Natan concerned an unspecified time, no such motive would exist. The Rosh therefore understood Rabbah bar Natan as parallel to the Mishnah. Maharshal could answer that Rabbah bar Natan applies both to a specified time and to an unspecified time; the challenge concerns the former. Yet Maharshal's language does not clearly say this. According to what I established above, following the Ramah, that the basis is jesting, the unrestricted wording presents no difficulty. Rabbah bar Natan applies in every case, and the Gemara's challenge is likewise straightforward.
For a person does not, etc. This is also the Tur's language, and it led the Rama, Levush, Sema, and Bach to explain the rule as waiver. The Tur can be understood differently: absent the requirement that both claims arise together, one could not explain the exemption as waiver, because a person plainly does not waive one debt merely by suing for another. Therefore the reason must be the claimant's implied admission, and the case concerns claims arising together. This is the Rosh's meaning at the end of HaManiach.
And if he seized, etc. The Rosh challenges the Rif: since the Gemara did not answer the earlier clause by saying that the claimant seized, seizure should not help, except in the case of two damaging oxen, where he has a claim against both. Tosafot and Maharshal follow this. In my humble opinion, the Rif is primary. The Gemara's plain answer, “where he seized,” implies that seizure distinguishes the Mishnah from Rabbah bar Natan generally. Once the Gemara gives this answer, it no longer needs its earlier explanations. It is common for the Gemara first to resolve a difficulty without seizure and later to offer seizure when pressed. The earlier answer, “fit to collect but has nothing,” likewise shows that the Gemara had not yet introduced seizure. There is also a direct explanation. When the Gemara says that he seized, it naturally means that the claimant seized according to his claim. In the latter clause he seized both animals and seeks recovery from both. Since he seized before witnesses, the burden of proof remains on him, and he may retain only the amount admitted by the tortfeasor. In the first clause, where he claims that the large animal caused the damage, seizing the large animal does not help him collect the defendant's admission concerning the small animal, because a tam pays only from its own body. The Rosh cites those who attempted this distinction and rejects it, arguing that if seizure of the small animal would not help there, it should certainly not help in wheat and barley. But the distinction should be stated in the opposite direction. The Gemara cannot answer that he seized in the first clause, because seizure ordinarily follows the claimant's own demand, namely the large animal. That seizure does not encompass the small animal admitted by the defendant. If he actually seized the small animal and then demanded the large one, his seizure of the small animal would indeed secure the defendant's admission. But the Gemara does not assume such irrational conduct. Moreover, the defendant could demand return of the small animal before answering the claim to the large, since a tam pays only from its own body. Accordingly, seizure of the barley, the small animal, or any other property worth the barley is effective. This is especially clear according to the Ramah, because seizure before the defendant retracts shows that the admission was genuine and not jesting. But even if the Rif understood the reason as the claimant's implied admission, the claimant can say, “I did not sue for the barley because I already held it.” The Tur records this distinction. Thus seizure before witnesses is effective for the value of the barley, contrary to Tosafot, the Rosh, and Maharshal, and in accordance with the Rif, Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, the Geonim, Raavad, Baal HaTerumot, Baal HaIttur, Semag, Ramah, Nimukei Yosef, and Agudah. This is the halachah.
He seized, etc. This too proves that the Ramah's explanation is primary, namely that the barley exemption rests on jesting rather than on an admission by the claimant. The Gemara challenges Rabbah bar Natan from the case of two injured and two damaging animals, then answers that the claimant seized. If the reason were the claimant's admission, seizure would not cure the problem, for he also held the large animal and nevertheless claimed it. The distinction would be forced. According to the Ramah, however, the answer is clear. If the claimant seized after the defendant's admission and before the defendant retracted, and the defendant remained silent, his silence confirms that the admission was genuine, so he can no longer say that he was jesting. The Ramah's rule applies likewise where seizure occurred before the parties stood in judgment, provided it was taken by force because of this claim and with the defendant's knowledge. Then the admission is shown to have been genuine. If the claimant already held barley for another reason, even under an arrangement requiring return only before witnesses, and then sued for wheat while the defendant admitted barley, the defendant may still say that he was jesting. His knowledge that the claimant held barley for another reason does not make his response genuine. Even if the claimant immediately says, “Indeed you owe me barley, and I already hold it,” the defendant may retract and say that he was jesting. The Ramah and Nimukei Yosef say that seizure before retraction is effective because it is as though the defendant voluntarily delivered the item. Their language implies seizure with the defendant's knowledge for this specific claim. It further appears that according to the Ramah, beit din raises the jesting defense even if the defendant does not initially articulate it. The rule in siman 81, that beit din does not raise “I was jesting” for him, concerns one who answered “yes” to a direct claim and is later sued on that prior admission. Here, after a wheat claim and a barley response, the court may simply rule that he is exempt because the claimant never sued for the admitted item. If the claimant then demands the barley and explains why he omitted it, the defendant must himself answer, “I was jesting.” If he does not, beit din does not raise it at that later stage. This reconciles the Ramah's language with the general rule, and these distinctions appear clear.
We do not remove it from his possession. The Nimukei Yosef writes at the end of HaManiach that if he seized before the defendant retracted his admission, the seizure is effective even if made before witnesses. If he seized only after the defendant retracted, we remove it unless he has a migo that he could have denied the seizure or claimed that he returned it. The Sema cites this, but not precisely. He should not have attached the Nimukei Yosef's rule to the Mechaber and Rama. The Nimukei Yosef follows his own explanation that the original exemption is based on jesting. Therefore, seizure before retraction helps, because the defendant's silence when the claimant seized demonstrates that he maintained his admission and intended a genuine admission. The Mechaber and Rama, however, understand that the claimant is deemed to have admitted that no barley is owed, or to have waived it. According to that theory, seizure after the judgment cannot help. Seizure before judgment may be explained by saying, “I did not claim the barley because I already held it,” as the Tur records in the name of those who distinguish. But after judgment, the implied admission has already taken effect. That is why the Rama writes that some say seizure helps only before the parties stood in judgment. This is the necessary conclusion once he adopts waiver as the reason. According to what I established above, however, the Ramah's explanation is primary, and therefore seizure helps even after judgment, provided it occurred before the defendant retracted. The Sema's citation of a migo to deny or claim return is likewise inconsistent with the Mechaber's and Rama's acceptance of the Rosh's theory. If the claimant has already admitted that no barley is owed, a later migo cannot undo that admission. The Sema himself says immediately afterward that according to the admission theory, even seizure without witnesses does not help as to the barley. The Bach says similarly. Maharshal's statement that a migo helps must be limited to retaining the value of the wheat claim, or to a case in which the barley exceeds the wheat, or where the claimant later admits that no wheat is owed, as the Levush and Sema explain. Shiltei Gibborim writes several things here that appear incorrect. He says that witnesses to the barley make seizure effective according to all opinions, because the claimant can explain that he did not sue for barley since he had witnesses. But according to the Rosh, even in the Mishnah's case where witnesses exist, seizure did not help in the earlier clause. He further writes that seizure without witnesses allows recovery of barley or even wheat through migo according to all opinions. According to the Rosh, migo helps only concerning the wheat claim, not the barley, unless his words are interpreted as I interpreted Maharshal. According to the Ramah, by contrast, even seizure after the defendant's retraction is effective if made without witnesses and accompanied by a migo, even as to the barley itself.
