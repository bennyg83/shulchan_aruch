Therefore, even if there are witnesses, etc. The Shach asks from the ruling below in siman 400 concerning two oxen that gored, where the injured party says that the larger ox caused the damage and the owner says that the smaller ox caused it. The Gemara at the end of chapter HaManiach compares this to one who claimed wheat and the other admitted barley, where he is exempt. Yet the Mechaber rules, following Rambam, that if there are witnesses to the goring, the injured party collects from the smaller ox. The Shach seeks to distinguish between the cases. What is stated here, that even if there are witnesses he is exempt, means that they testify clearly concerning barley. Below, however, the witnesses saw a goring but did not know which ox gored. So too here, if they know that there was a loan but do not know whether it was wheat or barley, the claimant should at least recover the value of the barley, because he can say: whichever way you look at it, if the witnesses are telling the truth, give me at least the barley. These are his words. This is difficult, however, for if so, why did the Mechaber omit the rule that where witnesses testify merely that there was a loan, the claimant at least recovers the barley? More fundamentally, I do not understand the reasoning. What sort of whichever way you look at it argument is there? If the witnesses testified to wheat, give him wheat, and if they testified to barley, give him barley. But where they testify explicitly to barley, the claimant has no right to the barley. What difference is there whether they speak doubtfully or decisively? In the end, if the truth is that the debt was barley, then by claiming wheat he admitted and waived the barley. How can money be extracted because of a doubt? This is particularly difficult in light of what appears from the Gemara there, and as I will write below in explaining Rambam, that where the tortfeasor says he is uncertain and the injured party makes a definite claim, it is treated as one who claimed wheat, etc. What difference is there between an admission by his own mouth and testimony of witnesses? An admission by a litigant is stronger than one hundred witnesses. I therefore do not fully understand the basis for this distinction. There is also a precision to be noted in Rambam himself. Not only did he fail to mention in Hilchot To'en VeNitan, in the discussion of one who claims wheat and the other admits barley, the rule that where witnesses exist he pays the lesser amount, but even in Hilchot Nizkei Mamon he inserted this rule in the middle of the paragraph dealing with two pursuing oxen, one large and one small, saying that if there are witnesses, etc. He did not place it at the end after all the cases taught in the Mishnah, namely one tam and one mu'ad, and two oxen pursuing two others. Seemingly this rule should apply to all the cases, as the Maggid Mishneh writes. It therefore appears that Rambam deliberately preserved this order, because one must explain why witnesses should help more than the defendant's own admission. He himself admitted that the smaller ox caused the damage, or that he owed barley, yet he is nevertheless exempt. What, then, do witnesses add? It appears that the reason is that Rambam holds that where the injured party seized the lesser amount he is not deprived of it, and we rule like Rabbi Akiva, who says that the injured party and the owner are partners in the offending ox. If the injured party consecrated his share it is consecrated, because it is already his. It follows that his half share is regarded as though it had come into his possession. How can waiver apply to something that is already his? Waiver applies to a claim against another person, but not to property that belongs to the claimant himself. With respect to property that is already his, the language of waiver is ineffective. One would have to say explicitly, in the language of a gift, that it is given to the other party. A claim that the larger ox caused the damage cannot be interpreted as saying, "The smaller ox is given to you." Since his portion of the smaller ox is already deemed in his possession, the rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley does not apply. This is even stronger than an actual seizure. This is true, however, only once the tortfeasor has already admitted the matter or witnesses exist. At that point the injured party can bring the ox at any time and sell it, and therefore half the ox is conclusively his. Tosafot likewise write at the beginning of the chapter Shor Shenagach Arba'ah VaChamishah, s.v. shor, that because the injured party has the ability to bring witnesses immediately, he acquires rights in the ox with respect to its safeguarding. See there. But as long as he has no witnesses and the defendant has not yet admitted anything, how can the ox be conclusively his? The owner can still deny liability and say, for example, that the injured ox was struck by a rock, or say that he does not know. In such a case the injured party is certainly still lacking collection, and the ox is not considered in his possession at all. It is not yet conclusively his. Therefore, when the Gemara speaks of a claimant who says the large ox caused the damage and the owner admits that the small ox did, this means that when the claimant first asserted his claim regarding the large ox, the tortfeasor had not yet admitted anything and collection was still lacking. At that moment, by asserting only the claim concerning the large ox, he waived the claim against the smaller ox, which was still entirely in the tortfeasor's possession. Once it was already waived, the owner's subsequent admission concerning the smaller ox cannot restore it. This is properly explained. The Gemara there does not deal with witnesses to the goring, because that discussion follows the opinion that half damages are compensatory rather than a fine, as the authorities explain, and this is straightforward. By contrast, where witnesses testify to the goring, it makes no difference whether they testify specifically about the smaller ox or testify only that one of the oxen gored. In either case the injured party's rights attach to the smaller ox, because in a case of doubt he can recover no more than from the smaller ox. It is therefore equivalent to testimony specifically concerning the smaller ox. The Rosh similarly writes regarding one who says, "I know that the pledge was worth more than a sela, but I do not know how much more," that this is regarded as an admission of a quantified amount, because an unspecified admission is treated as an admission of a perutah, under the principle that where grasping the larger amount would be excessive, one grasps the smaller amount. All the more so here, where the witnesses testify doubtfully, the injured party has no established right except in the smaller ox. That smaller ox, however, is certainly within his legal possession. He can bring it for collection at any time and is a partner in it, so waiver cannot apply to it at all. See below, seif katan 14, where I cite the Baal HaTerumot as explaining Rambam's view that a deposit creates a stronger obligation upon the bailee than a tam ox creates upon the tortfeasor. Therefore, in the case of a tam ox, the owner's admission is not considered an admission of the same kind as the claim at all, because the tortfeasor has no personal monetary obligation. For this reason Rambam stated his ruling specifically in the case of two tam oxen pursuing, etc. Had he placed the ruling at the end, after the case of one tam and one mu'ad, it would have appeared to apply also where the owner says that the mu'ad, smaller ox caused the damage. In that case the injured party certainly would not collect the value of the smaller ox, because by claiming that the larger ox caused the damage he waived that lesser claim. With a mu'ad ox there is no proprietary seizure and no share of the ox is conclusively the injured party's, since payment comes from the owner's superior assets. The injured party is merely an ordinary creditor of the tortfeasor. Had Rambam placed the rule at the end, it would have appeared to apply to this case as well, which would be incorrect. He therefore advanced the ruling to its proper place in order to prevent the reader from making this mistake. All the more so, this rule cannot apply where the claimant says that he lent wheat and the defendant admits barley, while witnesses testify to barley or merely testify that there was a loan without knowing which commodity was lent. In such a case none of the reasoning just stated applies. It is comparable to the case of a mu'ad ox, because the claimant has only a personal claim against the borrower and nothing is regarded as already seized or conclusively his. This is straightforward, and therefore Rambam made no mention of such a rule in Hilchot To'en VeNitan. The Mechaber's words are likewise readily explained in this manner. It is indeed possible that if the claimant says that wheat was deposited and the defendant admits a deposit of barley, and witnesses testify concerning barley or testify to the deposit without knowing which commodity it was, while also knowing that no mishap occurred so that the bailee cannot claim that the property was lost through an unavoidable accident, then the barley is not lacking collection. Wherever the deposit is, it stands in the depositor's legal possession. In that circumstance waiver truly should not apply, nor should the ordinary rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley. We should not extend the exemption beyond its established scope. Quite apart from this, many authorities hold that the entire basis for the exemption is that the defendant may say that his admission was made merely to mock the claimant, and that reasoning does not apply when witnesses exist, namely in the circumstances described where he cannot deny the matter at all. Nevertheless, I have already written that a tam ox is weaker than a deposit, as the Baal HaTerumot states. It appears that Rambam has a proof from the Gemara. The Gemara says: if the injured party does not bring proof, he takes according to what the tortfeasor says. It then asks why, since this should be a case of claiming wheat and admitting barley. The Gemara answers that he is fit to take, but in fact has nothing. Tosafot already ask why the Mishnah did not simply say that he is exempt, and they answer according to their second resolution concerning seizure, which Rambam does not accept. Rambam therefore explains the Mishnah as follows. The principle that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant means that for anything he wishes to extract, he must bring proof. It is obvious that if he seeks to collect according to his own claim, namely the value of the larger ox, he must bring proof. The Mishnah adds that even if he seeks to collect only the value of the smaller ox, which the tortfeasor himself says is the amount due and which would seemingly be recoverable as a matter of law, he still has nothing until he brings proof. For every amount that he seeks to extract, large or small, he needs proof. Without such proof, it is a case of claiming wheat and admitting barley. Once he brings proof, however, he collects according to what the tortfeasor says. This is precisely Rambam's ruling that where witnesses exist he collects according to the tortfeasor's admission. The Mishnah therefore teaches a substantial point through the phrase that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. It tells us that with witnesses he collects according to what the tortfeasor says. It could not simply teach "exempt," because that would imply exemption even where witnesses testify to the goring. Everything is thus properly explained and precise. The Shach, in seif katan 16, discusses this at length and holds that not only where witnesses exist can the barley be recovered, but even where the defendant remains by his admission and continues to acknowledge that he owes the barley, he must pay it. In his view, the entire reason that one who admits barley after being sued for wheat is exempt from the barley is the view of the Raavad and Ramah, that the defendant may say: "I was mocking you because you sued me for something I do not owe." Although the admission was made in beit din, it can still be explained that way. But if the defendant acknowledges that he truly owes the barley and does not claim that he was mocking, then he certainly must pay. Despite the Shach's lengthy discussion, I have not found in his words a decisive argument sufficient to rule against the great and profound authorities who disagree. His principal point is that the Gemara's discussion follows the opinion that half damages are compensatory, or alternatively that the case is where the injured party says, "The large ox caused the damage, and you have already stood with me in judgment and become liable to me for the value of the large ox," while the owner responds, "No, only the value of the smaller ox." It is true that the authorities write that the discussion follows the opinion that half damages are compensatory. As for what the Shach writes about a prior judgment, see what I wrote above. Even so, it is difficult to say that the Mishnah's statement, "the other says that the smaller ox caused the damage," means that he is now retracting what he said and claims that he intended merely to mock the injured party. Even if we ourselves needed to explain the Mishnah this way, the interpretation would be difficult, because the Mishnah states simply that the other party "says," which implies his present and continuing position. It does not say that he "had said" this earlier. All the more so, how could the Gemara derive a difficulty from the Mishnah and then force the case into a situation of seizure or the like, when there is no indication of that in the Mishnah, rather than explain it simply as a case where the tortfeasor stands by his statement and does not retract? The Mishnah is concerned only with which ox caused the damage, the smaller or the larger, not with a later retraction in which the tortfeasor claims that his ox caused no damage at all. After all, the injured ox lies before us. If one answers that perhaps it was struck by a rock, that possibility is already the subject of the first clause, so what would the latter clause add? I have found no indication for the Ramah's explanation in the sugya except the Gemara's answer that the injured party is fit to take but in fact has nothing, an expression that Tosafot found difficult. According to the Ramah it can indeed be explained well: he is fit to collect if the tortfeasor stands by his statement, but he has nothing if the tortfeasor retracts and says that his earlier admission was intended merely to mock him. The Mishnah thereby teaches something significant, namely that the reason is the possibility of mockery, so at times he collects and at times he does not. Although this explanation is attractive, it contains its own refutation. According to the Ramah, why did the Gemara feel forced, in the middle clause, to explain the case as one in which the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain? Why did it not give the same answer that the injured party is fit to take but has nothing? The baraita stating that he collects from the smaller ox was not cited concerning the middle clause. This is similar to the difficulty that must be raised according to Rashi, who asks why the Gemara did not say in the middle clause that he is fit to take, etc. It necessarily follows that Rashi does not understand "fit to take" as referring to seizure. Otherwise there would be two separate questions. Rather, he understands the phrase in its simple sense. The question regarding the middle clause is therefore that, since no baraita contradicts it, the Gemara should have explained it by saying that he is fit to take. As for the last clause, where the baraita explicitly says that he collects, the Gemara could have explained it as a case of seizure. Likewise, according to the Ramah, the Gemara could have explained the middle clause as a case where the owner stands by his admission and therefore he pays, while the last clause, in light of the baraita, could be a case of seizure. Both rulings would then be necessary. The latter would teach that even if the tortfeasor does not stand by his statement, seizure is effective, while the former would teach that even without seizure, if he stands by his statement, he must pay. One cannot answer that the Gemara knew of a baraita concerning the middle clause as well, saying that he pays from the smaller ox. Had that been so, when the Gemara sought to challenge Rav Nachman it should have cited that baraita explicitly. From the Mishnah itself there would be no difficulty, since it could be explained that he is merely fit to take. This is necessarily Rashi's intent, as explained above, for otherwise Rashi's question would have no force either. Nor can one say that the Ramah follows Rashi's answer that, since he holds that Sumchus did not state his rule where both parties make definite claims, it was preferable to explain the middle clause as a case where the injured party is uncertain. The difficulty would remain: why, according to Rav Pappa, who holds that Sumchus applies even where both parties are certain, does Rav Pappa challenge from the middle clause? According to him, the middle clause could indeed involve two definite claims, while the injured party is merely fit to collect but in fact has nothing. In truth, this question is difficult at first glance even according to Rashi and requires study. Yet Rashi's approach has several possible explanations, as I wrote in my novellae. One of them is that Rashi holds that the Mishnah says, "the burden of proof rests upon the claimant," rather than simply "exempt," in order to teach, as I explained above according to Rambam, that where there are witnesses the injured party does recover. The reason is Rabbi Akiva's view that the parties are partners in the ox. At first glance, however, the Mishnah cannot be attributed to Rabbi Akiva. Rashi holds that one who totally denies a deposit is liable to take an oath. If, according to Rabbi Akiva, the injured party is already a partner in the offending ox and can validly consecrate his share, then the case is as though the tortfeasor holds a deposit belonging to the injured party. Accordingly, in the first clause, which the Gemara establishes as a case where both parties make definite claims and the tortfeasor says that the injured ox was struck by a rock, there would be a Torah oath. How, then, could the Mishnah be teaching a ruling against Sumchus? Sumchus agrees that his division rule does not apply where a Torah oath is required, as stated in Bava Metzia in the sugya of one who exchanges a cow, page 100. We must therefore say that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Yishmael, who does not hold that they are partners but only that the injured party is a creditor. There is then no deposit and no Torah oath. Although the Gemara appears to imply that the law concerning two oxen belonging to one person follows Rabbi Akiva, it is still possible that the other Mishnah follows Rabbi Yishmael, or perhaps this very Mishnah follows Rabbi Yishmael. Tosafot at the beginning of chapter HaParah also ask that the Mishnah could be established according to Sumchus and Rabbi Yishmael, showing that they too regard attribution to Rabbi Yishmael as possible. Yet one may answer differently. The Maharsha asks at the beginning of chapter HaParah regarding the Mishnah where the claimant says that the defendant's ox caused the damage and the defendant says that the injured animal was struck by a rock. According to the Sages, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. But according to the opinion that half damages are a fine, even if the owner admitted that his ox caused the damage he would be exempt, because he would be one who admits liability for a fine. Why then, according to Sumchus, should they divide? See there, where the Maharsha seeks to establish a novel rule contrary to the Rosh and the other authorities. In truth, however, there is no difficulty according to Sumchus. According to the opinion that Sumchus applies even to two definite claims where there is an inherent monetary doubt, as Tosafot write, then if the tortfeasor admits that his ox gored, his admission exempts him from one quarter of the damage that otherwise would have been payable as half of the disputed half damages. Had he not admitted anything, the rule according to Sumchus would have been to divide, which would yield one quarter damages. His admission could create liability for up to half damages, but as an admission to a fine it cannot create that additional liability. Still, he must pay the quarter that would have been payable even without his admission, because that amount does not arise from his admission at all. Even if we say that Sumchus does not apply where both parties make definite claims, so that one might argue that this is an admission to a fine because had the tortfeasor denied everything he would have been exempt from all damages, the argument is still incorrect. The rule that one who admits a fine is exempt applies where, had he remained silent, he would have been exempt, and only his own words create the liability. In such a case we say that his admission cannot create the fine. But if his silence would itself have exposed him to liability, as where his silence would make him an uncertain litigant and Sumchus would require division, the fact that he could have lied and denied the claim does not convert his truthful statement into an exempting admission to a fine. We say only that where silence itself leaves him exempt, the movement of his lips cannot create the fine. Where silence would not leave him exempt, this principle does not apply. This is straightforward and correct. Accordingly, the Sages indeed need the Mishnah's rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant in order to reject Sumchus. Sumchus would require payment of at least one quarter of the damage, whereas the Sages teach that the claimant collects nothing. It is true that according to the Sages, even an admission by the tortfeasor would not obligate him, because it would be an admission to a fine. Nevertheless, the Mishnah states the rule in order to exclude Sumchus, according to whom the claimant would recover one quarter. This is a clear and correct resolution of the Maharsha's difficulty. We may now return to the original difficulty. Sumchus agrees that his rule does not apply where there is a Torah oath. But a Torah oath exists only where the defendant's admission actually creates the monetary obligation while his denial would exempt him, and therefore an oath is imposed upon him so that he should admit the truth. Here, however, his statement cannot itself create liability for the fine, because an admission to a fine is exempt. What purpose would the oath serve? Whatever amount cannot be imposed without his own statement cannot be imposed through his statement either. His oath therefore accomplishes nothing. This is not comparable to the case of one who exchanges a cow for a donkey, where we say that Sumchus agrees in the presence of a Torah oath, because there the defendant's admission creates a full monetary obligation. There is therefore no difficulty. According to this, Rashi's words are precise and refined. Rav Pappa properly challenges from the middle clause. The explanation that the injured party is merely fit to take where witnesses exist, according to Rabbi Akiva's partnership theory, cannot be used there, because Rav Pappa holds that half damages are compensatory. If the Mishnah followed Rabbi Akiva, then, as explained, it would involve the equivalent of a denial concerning a deposit and would generate a Torah oath. In that event the Mishnah could not serve to reject Sumchus. The alternative explanation based on an admission to a fine is unavailable to Rav Pappa because he holds that half damages are compensatory rather than a fine. Therefore the Gemara establishes the case as one where the injured party says he is uncertain, etc. Rashi's question, however, addresses the final halachic conclusion, according to which half damages are a fine. At that stage the Mishnah can indeed follow Rabbi Akiva. Rashi can therefore ask why the anonymous Gemara challenged only from the last clause rather than from the middle clause as well, and why it did not explain the middle clause by saying that the injured party is fit to take in the presence of witnesses, thereby rejecting the earlier interpretation that the injured party is uncertain. Rashi answers that according to the final ruling, Sumchus does not apply where both parties make definite claims, so it is preferable to preserve the Gemara's establishment of the middle clause in the case of an uncertain injured party, thereby making the clauses parallel. Examine this carefully, and see also what I wrote elsewhere on the subject. None of this, however, is sufficient to sustain the Ramah's explanation. According to him, "fit to take" means simply that the claimant recovers if the tortfeasor stands by his admission, as explained above. The difficulty therefore remains, especially since Tosafot themselves struggled to explain "fit to take but has nothing" as referring to seizure rather than interpreting it like the Ramah. This indicates that Tosafot did not accept the Ramah's approach, contrary to the Shach's assertion that Tosafot also agree with it. There is no need to rely on this inference alone. The view of Tosafot is plainly contrary to the Ramah, as any reader of their words can see. Tosafot explicitly write that the defendant's admission is treated as an admission followed by the claimant's waiver. According to the Ramah, what waiver is there? Moreover, how could it be that even Rabbah bar Natan would agree with Rabban Gamliel that there is an oath of partial admission here if the defendant may say that he was merely mocking the claimant? What partial admission remains upon which to impose an oath? The matter is likewise explicit in Rambam in Hilchot To'en VeNitan. Rambam writes that this case is comparable to one person saying, "I owe you a maneh," and the other responding, "You owe me nothing," in which case the debtor is exempt. In that case the debtor is always exempt even if he continues to stand by his admission. Maharam and the Rosh derive this rule in chapter Yesh Nochalin from the statement, "You admit that you are not our brother." Accordingly, all the Shach's effort to reconcile Rambam with the Ramah was unnecessary, because Rambam's own comparison proves that the exemption remains even where the defendant continues to stand by his admission. Rambam's commentators likewise write this explicitly. What need is there for further length once Rambam, Tosafot, the Baal HaTerumot, the Rosh, the Tur, the Mechaber, Maharshal, and Rama all point in one direction, and, according to what I have written, the Gemara itself strongly supports their view? We therefore rule in accordance with them. The Ramban's view is also clearly the same. The Rosh cites Ramban in Bava Metzia, in the sugya concerning the borrowed and rented animals, as holding that the borrower need not swear that the rented animal died in the ordinary manner. Since the owner claimed concerning the borrowed animal, it is a case of claiming wheat and admitting barley, and he is exempt regarding the barley. See there. In that case the defendant necessarily continues to stand by his statement that the rented animal died normally. Thus Ramban too holds that the exemption applies even where the defendant does not retract. Therefore the primary law is exactly as stated in the Shulchan Aruch, without qualification.
For it is as though he waived it, etc. Know that the Rosh, at the end of chapter HaManiach, writes that Rashi and Tosafot explain that the reason the claimant is exempt from the barley is that he is treated as though he waived the barley. The Rosh writes: I am astonished at this reasoning, because it is common for a person to have several claims against another and not wish to demand all of them at once. He may demand only one of them and nevertheless has not waived the others. Furthermore, this is not comparable to our Mishnah. In the Mishnah there is an admission, for by claiming that the larger ox caused the damage he admitted that the smaller ox did not cause it. This is not a waiver. Therefore, even if we say that in the Mishnah the defendant is liable because the claimant's statement is not treated as an admission, since he intended only to enhance his recovery by claiming the larger ox, it can still be that one who claims wheat is exempt from the barley because his failure to claim the barley constitutes waiver. Conversely, even if we say that in the Mishnah the defendant is exempt because the claimant's statement is an admission, it can still be that in the case of wheat and barley the defendant remains liable for the barley because we do not say that the claimant waived it. I therefore explain the case of claiming wheat as comparable to the Mishnah only where the claimant says, "I lent you wheat on that day and at that hour," and the other responds, "It was barley." If he had lent him both commodities at that same time, he would have claimed both, since they arose simultaneously. His failure to claim the barley therefore constitutes an admission that the defendant does not owe him barley. These are the Rosh's words. Many explanations have been offered regarding the Rosh's intent and the difference between admission and waiver. Rabbenu Yerucham, his student, writes in Netiv 3, part 2, that according to the Rosh, if the defendant stands by his words and affirms them, saying, "You have barley in my possession," he is liable for the barley. His words require study. The Shach explains Rabbenu Yerucham to mean that beit din does not compel the defendant to pay, because the defendant can say that the claimant already admitted that he owes him nothing, but if the defendant himself fails to pay he is considered a robber. This is incomprehensible. Whichever way one looks at it, if we say that the claimant's statement is not an admission that effects waiver, why should beit din not compel payment? The defendant has admitted owing barley. How can he escape by saying, "The claimant admitted that I owe him nothing," when we know that his present contention is false and foolish? We know that he now states in beit din, just as he stated before, that he owes barley. Even if the claimant intended to obtain wheat instead of barley, the defendant himself knows that he owes the barley. On what basis could he be exempt unless he asserts that the claimant's admission operated as a waiver? But that is exactly the point under discussion. If an admission is in fact equivalent to waiver, then the defendant is not even a robber for retaining the money, because nothing remains after a valid waiver. I therefore do not understand the distinction between what beit din may compel and the defendant's obligation toward Heaven. One who examines Rabbenu Yerucham will see that he first cites the view of the Ramah, that the defendant can say his words were uttered merely to mock the claimant. He writes that according to this approach, if the defendant stands by his admission he is liable for the barley. He then cites the Rosh's explanation based on the claimant's admission and writes that according to him likewise, if the defendant stands by his words he must pay. The phrase "likewise" indicates that Rabbenu Yerucham understands the Rosh's rule to be the same as the Ramah's in this respect. According to the Ramah, however, beit din certainly compels payment, as the Shach himself writes. In truth, Rabbenu Yerucham's view requires careful study. According to it, the same should apply where witnesses testify. What difference is there between the defendant himself admitting and standing by his admission that he owes barley and witnesses testifying that he owes barley? If so, the Rosh's question against the Ramah returns against the Rosh himself. The Mishnah in HaManiach necessarily concerns a case where witnesses saw the goring, for otherwise the tortfeasor would be one who admits liability for a fine and would be exempt. If witnesses exist, then according to Rabbenu Yerucham the defendant should be liable. Moreover, the Mishnah certainly concerns a tortfeasor who stands by his statement that the smaller ox gored. There is no question there of retracting and claiming that the injured ox was struck by a rock, for that is the first clause, as I wrote above. This is especially so where witnesses exist, because then he cannot retract at all. If so, the comparison to claiming wheat is no difficulty. Why would we need the strained interpretations that the Gemara gives rather than explaining the Mishnah simply as a case where the tortfeasor continues to stand by his statement? Rabbenu Yerucham's words therefore appear very difficult at first glance. What appears correct in explaining him is that this is precisely the distinction between the Rosh and the Ramah according to Rabbenu Yerucham's understanding. The Rosh does not hold that the claimant's admission itself constitutes waiver. Rather, since an admission by a litigant is equivalent to one hundred witnesses, his admission disqualifies any witnesses who would testify contrary to it, just as the Gemara in Gittin says, "She is believed by me," by which one accepts her statement and effectively rejects contrary witnesses. So too, when the claimant says that the defendant does not owe him barley, he rejects any witnesses who would say that barley is owed, because his own admission is stronger than one hundred witnesses. Nevertheless, if the defendant himself admits that he owes barley, payment is certainly extracted from him. Here the situation is reversed: the defendant's own admission rejects any witnesses that would support his exemption. His own words are accepted. What difference does it make that the claimant previously admitted that no barley was owed? The defendant can impose an obligation upon himself even where no preexisting obligation exists, as stated above in siman 40. Thus his present admission is certainly effective. The claimant may have intended to enhance his claim, as the Rosh writes, and we can preserve both parties' statements. We may say that in truth the defendant never owed barley originally, just as the claimant admitted, but that the defendant now obligates himself through his own admission even though he was not previously liable. This resembles the legal admission made by Issur the convert, who knew that Rav Mari had no actual share in the deposit held by Rava, yet was still able to confer the money through an admission. The same applies here. This is the Rosh's intent. The claimant did not waive anything. Rather, we accept his statement as true that no barley was ever owed, and therefore his claim excluded barley and contrary witnesses are ineffective. But if the defendant stands by his own admission, he is certainly liable, even though we know that no barley was ever lent. According to this, there is no difficulty in comparing the case of two pursuing oxen to the case of wheat and barley even though the Mishnah has witnesses. The existence of witnesses changes nothing, because the claimant's own admission is stronger than the witnesses and is treated as having rejected them, as explained. The second difficulty, that the Mishnah concerns a tortfeasor who stands by his words, can also be resolved. This is obvious according to the Rosh's presentation of the sugya as following the rule that half damages are a fine. The Gemara's question is then straightforward. Whichever basis one might use to obligate the tortfeasor to pay for the smaller ox fails. If the obligation is based upon the witnesses, the injured party's own admission has rejected their testimony. The witnesses are therefore treated as nonexistent. The only remaining basis is the tortfeasor's own admission. But that is an admission to a fine, and one who admits liability for a fine is exempt. Once the witnesses have been neutralized by the claimant's own admission, if the tortfeasor were to deny everything and say that the injured animal was struck by a rock, he would be exempt and the witnesses could no longer help the claimant.
But even according to the version of the Gemara in which Rav Pappa asks whether the Mishnah refutes Rabbah bar Natan, and Rav Pappa holds that half damages are compensatory, the matter can still be explained. Quite apart from our issue, one must understand the Gemara's establishment of the middle clause as a case where the injured party says he is uncertain and the tortfeasor makes a definite claim. Tosafot ask why this clause is not also needed to reject Sumchus. They answer that since there is partial admission, Sumchus would say that the parties divide. Tosafot later write, s.v. ra'ui litol, that the clauses are both necessary. The first clause teaches that even where the injured party makes a definite claim and the tortfeasor is uncertain, the Sages still apply the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. The case of the larger and smaller ox teaches that where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain, Sumchus nevertheless requires division. At first glance this is difficult from the Mishnah in Bava Metzia 97, where the renter says that the rented animal died and the owner says that he does not know, and the renter is exempt. The Gemara establishes that Mishnah, according to Rav Nachman, as a case of partial admission: the owner claimed three cows and the renter admitted one. Accordingly, in the first clause he is one who cannot swear and must pay. If so, according to Sumchus, why should it matter that the owner says he is uncertain? Since there is partial admission, Sumchus should require division rather than a complete exemption. Yet the Gemara there establishes the Mishnah according to Sumchus. Similarly, in Bava Metzia 100, where the Mishnah discusses one who exchanged property and where there were two slaves, the seller says, "I sold the smaller one," and the buyer says, "I do not know," the smaller slave is awarded to the buyer. That case too involves partial admission, as the Gemara establishes in explaining the clause requiring an oath, namely that the dispute concerns the value of the smaller slave, etc. If so, Sumchus should require division there as well, yet that Mishnah too is according to Sumchus. It is strained to answer merely that Tosafot there already ask, s.v. midsifa, that in a definite claim against an uncertain claim the definite claim should prevail. This question exists even according to Rav Nachman, who does not generally hold that a definite claim prevails. Rav Nachman rejects that principle only where the definite claimant seeks to extract money from the possessor, because the possessor's monetary chazakah is powerful. But where the possessor himself makes the definite claim, even Rav Nachman agrees that the definite claim is superior, since the possessor's chazakah supports it. Accordingly, where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain, the tortfeasor is in possession and everyone should agree that his definite claim prevails. This also resolves the Maharsha's question why Tosafot did not ask from the first clause, where the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor uncertain. There the question exists only according to Rav Yehudah, not according to Rav Nachman. Tosafot therefore ask from the latter clause, where the possessor himself makes the definite claim, so the question applies according to all opinions. The answer must then be Tosafot's second resolution, that this is a matter the injured party could not have known. That answer works in the Mishnah in HaManiach, but in the Mishnah concerning the borrowed and rented animals and in the Mishnah concerning the exchange, where the claimant says he does not know, the defendant is exempt because the definite claim is stronger for all opinions and Sumchus does not require division. Yet from Tosafot's wording in s.v. midsifa it appears that once we use the answer that Sumchus agrees in a case of partial admission, there is no need for the second answer that the claimant could not have known. Otherwise, why would Tosafot formulate the latter point as a separate question and answer, "But is not a definite claim superior?" If that distinction were independently necessary to reconcile the other Mishnayot, Tosafot should have stated immediately, after saying that Sumchus agrees in partial admission, that this is true only where the claimant could not have known. This is especially difficult according to the authorities, as discussed above in siman 75, who hold that even where the claimant could not have known, a definite claim is superior. There is an even greater difficulty. Rav Pappa sought to prove that the Mishnah does not deal with two definite claims because Sumchus does not apply where both parties are certain. Nevertheless, he establishes the middle clause as a case where the injured party is uncertain, and Tosafot's explanation requires that, because there is partial admission, Sumchus would require division. Likewise, Tosafot's necessity analysis in s.v. ra'ui, which applies according to all opinions, must mean that even according to the view that Sumchus does not apply where both parties are certain, the rule of division applies in a case of partial admission where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain. If that is so, all the more so where the injured party also makes a definite claim. A definite claim by the injured party is stronger than an uncertain claim. It would therefore follow that where there is partial admission, everyone agrees that Sumchus requires division even when both parties are certain. But this is difficult from Bava Metzia 100. The Gemara there asks from the Mishnah: one says that he sold the larger slave and the other says the smaller, and the defendant must swear. This is understandable according to the view that Sumchus does not apply where both parties are certain, for then he swears. But according to the view that Sumchus applies even where both are certain, why should he swear? They should divide. Yet that very Mishnah involves partial admission, as indicated by the obligation to swear. According to the reasoning above, Sumchus should require division there according to all opinions, even according to the view that he does not ordinarily apply his rule to two definite claims. The Gemara's question should therefore arise according to everyone, even before it knows the answer that Sumchus agrees where there is a Torah oath. It is difficult to answer that the questioner certainly anticipated the Gemara's eventual answer that Sumchus agrees where a Torah oath exists, but nevertheless reasoned that if the claimant protests, "What do I care about your oath? Give me half under Sumchus's rule," we should listen to him. He could argue that if the defendant had denied everything, the claimant would have received half, so why should the claimant's position be weakened and he lose everything merely because the defendant admitted a small part? Perhaps the defendant is strategically admitting a perutah in order to escape division. The questioner would then be understood as saying that they should divide, while the answer would be that in any event, once the Torah imposes an oath, Sumchus's division rule does not apply. If the claimant had demanded an oath rather than division, the defendant would have had to swear. Conversely, when the claimant seeks division, the defendant may say, "I am prepared to swear, and we shall accept whichever result the Torah gives." This might explain why the question is raised only according to the view that Sumchus applies to two definite claims even in total denial. According to the view that Sumchus does not apply except where there is partial admission, the possibility of strategic partial admission creates no difficulty, for if the defendant denied everything he would owe nothing in a case of two definite claims. Still, this entire explanation is strained. It therefore appears that a recognized rule is decisive here: a migo that would require brazenness is not accepted to exempt one from an oath, but it is accepted with respect to monetary liability, as I discussed at length above in the rules of migo. This is almost unanimously accepted by the authorities. Accordingly, consider the case discussed above where the injured party says he is uncertain and the tortfeasor says he is certain. If the tortfeasor totally denied liability, Sumchus would not require division, but where he partially admits liability Tosafot say that Sumchus does require division. This seems difficult because the tortfeasor has a migo that he could have denied everything. He is therefore like one who returns a lost item voluntarily. One might answer, following Rabbah's explanation of why the Torah imposes an oath on one who partially admits a claim, that a person generally does not have the brazenness to deny the entire claim. But that answer works only if we seek to exempt him from an oath. Here we seek to exempt him from monetary liability, and with respect to money we do accept even a migo that involves brazenness. We must therefore say that the Mishnah concerns a case where witnesses saw the goring but did not know whether the larger or smaller ox caused it. The tortfeasor then has no migo to deny everything, because he cannot contradict the witnesses. Where there are no witnesses, everyone agrees that even in a case of partial admission Sumchus does not require division, because the tortfeasor has a migo that he could have denied everything, and with respect to money we disregard the brazenness involved. Accordingly, all the Mishnayot cited above are resolved. Those Mishnayot do not concern cases supported by witnesses. Sumchus therefore agrees that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, and this is why they teach complete exemption. The Gemara's question is also precise. According to the view that Sumchus does not apply where both parties make definite claims and the defendant totally denies liability, there is no question that they should divide. Even if the defendant partially admits, he has a migo that he could have denied everything, and since Sumchus does not apply in two definite claims with total denial, that migo defeats division. This applies in the absence of witnesses. Even though Rav Pappa holds that half damages are compensatory, so that it is not necessary to say that the Mishnah specifically deals with witnesses, the Mishnah is stated without qualification and includes both a case where witnesses exist and one where they do not. It is therefore necessary to teach the Sages' position against Sumchus in the case where witnesses do exist. Sumchus would require division there, and the Mishnah teaches that even there the Sages say that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. This properly explains the matter. Since the first clause involves two definite claims, the latter clause should likewise involve two definite claims. If so, why are both clauses necessary? We might answer that the first teaches the strength of Sumchus's rule, that even with two definite claims they divide, while the second teaches the strength of the Sages, that even with partial admission they disagree and require proof. But that would itself be obvious, because in the absence of witnesses the tortfeasor has a migo to deny everything. If the Sages reject Sumchus in the first clause, then certainly they reject him in the latter clause. Conversely, if Sumchus requires division in a case of partial admission even though the defendant could totally deny, then he must also apply his rule where there is total denial. The two clauses would again be redundant. If we say that witnesses exist, so that the defendant cannot deny everything, then the case should constitute a refutation of Rabbah bar Natan. The claimant's assertion of wheat is an admission that rejects the witnesses concerning barley. If so, once the witnesses are rejected by his admission, the tortfeasor could again deny the barley entirely, and the migo problem returns. Thus both clauses would remain unnecessary. If the middle clause instead concerns a definite claim by the injured party and uncertainty by the tortfeasor, the wording "No, rather" becomes difficult. Tosafot therefore explain that even if the first clause includes a case of definite claims and the latter clause a case of uncertainty, the first may still have been stated alongside the second. But then the comparison to Sumchus does not work unless the latter clause, because of its partial admission, is a case in which Sumchus applies even where both parties make definite claims. That makes the phrase "No, rather" meaningful, and the first clause is then taught incidentally because of the second. The Gemara can consequently challenge Rabbah bar Natan precisely. Since the defendant has a migo to deny everything, and if witnesses exist the claimant's own admission has rejected those witnesses, how can the partial admission generate a different result? The Gemara therefore establishes the case as one where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain. The phrase "No, rather" is then precise, and the first clause is taught incidentally because of the second even though Sumchus does not apply to the first in exactly the same manner. This resolves the matter. Examine it carefully, for the reasoning is subtle. All of this follows the version that reads Rav Pappa in the sugya. From the Rosh's words, however, it appears that he did not have the reading of Rav Pappa and understood the entire discussion as following the opinion that half damages are a fine. According to that version the matter is, in any event, readily explained as stated above. This, at least, provides a clear and correct explanation of Rabbenu Yerucham. There is in fact support for Rabbenu Yerucham's understanding that the Rosh does not identify admission with waiver. In chapter 2 of Ketubot the Rosh discusses one who says, "This promissory note is a note of accommodation." Where this statement causes a loss to others, he is not believed. The Rosh asks why he is not believed through a migo that he could simply waive the debt. He answers that the creditor does not wish to lose the debt. If he waived it, the borrower would no longer have to pay him, whereas by saying that the note is merely a note of accommodation he knows that the borrower is not a robber. This is difficult. Even now, by saying that the note is fictitious and admitting that he never lent the money, his statement should itself amount to a waiver. The borrower would therefore still not be a robber. What difference is there between admitting that no barley was lent and admitting that the loan represented by this promissory note never occurred? I see no distinction. It is strained to say that there his admission was made only to keep the debt away from his own creditors and is therefore not a complete admission. If so, the same should apply to an ostensible waiver, for in his heart he does not intend to waive. It is difficult to distinguish between them. Nevertheless, the plain implication of the Tur is that he does not accept Rabbenu Yerucham's view. If he did, he should have stated that where the defendant stands by his admission, he must pay. How could the Tur omit such a significant law, especially if, as explained above, there is a distinction between a case with witnesses and a case where the defendant himself continues to stand by his admission? There is also a difficulty in the Rosh himself. If there is a genuine distinction between admission and waiver, why did he depart from Rashi and Tosafot, who explain the exemption as resulting from waiver? His objection was that it happens every day that a person has several claims and demands only one of them. But the Rosh could have answered Rashi exactly as he answers for himself, namely that the case is one where the two claims arose at the same time and in the same transaction. In that circumstance, by failing to claim both at once, the claimant has waived the second, just as the Rosh says that his omission constitutes an admission. Why then reject Rashi's explanation at all? Rather, it is clear that the two concepts are really one. The claimant's admission operates because it is treated as waiver. This is why a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses, because through his admission he relinquishes his right, as explained above in siman 75 and in my discussion of the admission of Issur the convert. Indeed, waiver is the stronger and more natural concept here. If the reason were only admission, it could apply even to a deposit, just like Issur's admission, whereas pure waiver is more difficult to apply to property that remains legally in the depositor's possession, as I wrote above. Rashi too certainly agrees that the claimant's failure to demand the barley is treated as an admission, just as the Rosh says regarding the Mishnah in HaManiach, where waiver does not naturally apply and the operative idea is admission. The difference is only that Rashi understood the general statement, "he claimed wheat and admitted barley," to apply even where the two alleged debts did not arise at the same time. As Maharshal writes, in such a case there is no inference from the fact that the claimant did not demand barley, because a person does not necessarily demand all his separate claims at once. Rashi therefore explains the waiver differently. Since the defendant admitted barley and the claimant did not immediately respond, within the time required for speech, "You also owe me barley," his silence demonstrates that he waived the barley. The Rosh objects that there is no such proof. Since a person need not pursue all his claims at once, there was no reason for the claimant to say anything about the barley when he was not presently demanding it. The Rosh therefore concludes that the case must involve claims arising at the same time and in the same transaction. Once we reach that conclusion, there is no need to describe the result as an independent waiver. The claimant's omission itself is an admission, exactly as in the case of one ox pursuing another. But in truth, where both liabilities arose at the same time, there is no practical distinction between admission and waiver. On the contrary, the concepts reinforce each other. There is further proof from Bava Metzia in the sugya of the borrowed and rented animals. Ramban sought to compare that case to one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley. The Rosh rejects the comparison and writes: in the case of wheat and barley, the claimant waived the barley, as Rashi explains in chapter HaManiach, etc. He concludes there that because the claimant did not claim the barley, he waived it or admitted that no barley was owed. Had the Rosh disagreed with Rashi's concept of waiver, there would have been no reason for him to cite Rashi's explanation. The Rosh's formulation, "he waived it or admitted that it was not owed," likewise shows that, as a matter of law where the claims arise at the same time, there is no difference between the two formulations. They are two descriptions of the same legal effect. This is especially compelling against Rabbenu Yerucham's rule that if the defendant stands by his admission, the rule of wheat and barley no longer applies. In the case of the borrowed and rented animals the renter unquestionably stands by his statement that the rented animal died in the ordinary manner. Yet Ramban applies the wheat and barley principle, and the Rosh discusses the comparison without saying that continued adherence to the admission removes the exemption. It is therefore clear that Rabbenu Yerucham's qualification is not correct. The Rosh's own words, which the later authorities did not cite in this connection, explicitly show that even an admission is treated as waiver for purposes of this law.

In Tosafot's second answer, which establishes the case as one involving a tam, there is no need to invoke heilech because there is no oath at all, since an admission to the fine would exempt him. Moreover, according to one establishment in Shevuot, Shmuel holds that where one claims wheat and barley and the defendant admits one of them, he is exempt from an oath, while heilech itself creates liability according to Rabbenu Tam, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 5, s.v. i nami. Yet Shmuel himself is the one who says that the rule applies even where the injured party makes a definite claim, etc. Tosafot therefore needed an answer to Shmuel's statement that works according to every establishment. They could not rely on heilech, since according to Shmuel heilech may obligate rather than exempt. There is thus no proof from that passage. Here, however, the sugya does indicate that Tosafot's answer depends upon treating the tam ox as heilech. There remains a difficulty regarding the middle and final clauses. In the final clause, where the claimant says the larger mu'ad ox caused the damage and the defendant says the smaller mu'ad did, even if we disagree with the Nimukei Yosef, it is impossible to say that the Mishnah follows Rabban Gamliel unless we say that an oath is required, because there is no heilech with respect to a mu'ad. We must therefore say that Tosafot agree with the Nimukei Yosef that this is an admission of the same kind as the claim. If so, the final clause independently must involve one party being uncertain. Yet Rav Pappa does not derive his argument from that clause. This shows that parallelism to the final clause is not essential. Tosafot's question is therefore sound: perhaps the Mishnah follows Rabban Gamliel, while the middle clause involves heilech. In that case, however, what room is there for the Rosh's question against the Rif, who does not accept the Nimukei Yosef's view, as I will explain below in siman 400, and Rambam likewise does not accept it? According to the Rif, full parallelism throughout the Mishnah can indeed be required, and the objection disappears. There is another possible resolution. Rav Pappa does not establish the middle clause as involving seizure because it is certainly strained to interpret a Mishnah that says only that the claimant must bring proof as secretly referring to property already seized by the claimant. Seizure is not mentioned at all. But if, independently, we must say that even though the Mishnah says that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant it nevertheless concerns a case where the injured party had seized the ox, then it is reasonable to say that the seizure occurred from the outset before the claims were made. Once we already know that the Mishnah deals with seizure, we can use that fact throughout. This is relevant according to the view that half damages are a fine. The Rosh asks how the Mishnah can involve the owner's admission, since even if he admitted every detail he would be exempt as one who admits a fine. The Rosh's answer, that there were witnesses but they did not know which ox gored, is strained, like the Gemara's question in Bava Batra: if there are witnesses, let us see what they say. Here too, the difficulty is obvious. The Maggid Mishneh therefore explains that the injured party had seized the ox. Seizure prevents the tortfeasor's admission from exempting him from half damages even according to the view that half damages are a fine. Once the Mishnah must in any event be understood as involving seizure, it is reasonable to say that the injured party seized before making his claim, and then the rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley does not apply. Accordingly, the Rif may hold that the anonymous Gemara, which follows the ruling that half damages are a fine, answers with seizure, and that answer in truth applies to the middle clause as well. The Gemara does not ask from the middle clause because it could have answered that the injured party is fit to take there and has nothing, since no baraita was cited to contradict such an explanation, as I wrote above. But in substance the seizure answer can apply there too. Rav Pappa, by contrast, holds that half damages are compensatory and therefore has no independent reason to introduce seizure into the Mishnah. For him, seizure remains unmentioned and strained, so he cannot use that answer. He therefore establishes the middle clause as one where the injured party is uncertain. The same applies to the final clause. The Rif need not accept Rashi and Tosafot's claim that the clauses would then be redundant, because many distinctions make each clause necessary, as Tosafot themselves discuss. The anonymous Gemara that answers with seizure follows Rabbah bar Mamal and, as explained, can maintain that Sumchus applies even to two definite claims. The entire Mishnah can therefore remain a case of two definite claims. Everything is properly explained.
And there is a clear proof for the Rif that the seizure explanation applies to the first clause as well. We must explain Rav Pappa's establishment of the middle clause as one where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain, and the first clause in the reverse, with the injured party certain and the tortfeasor uncertain. Although the Mishnah says "No, rather," which ordinarily indicates two definite claims, Tosafot and Maharsha explain that the clause includes such a case but that Sumchus's disagreement is relevant only to the case of uncertainty. At first glance, why was Rav Pappa forced to make the first and middle clauses only imperfectly parallel, with uncertainty on opposite sides? It would seem simpler to establish both as cases where both parties are uncertain. In such a case Sumchus and the Sages disagree directly. It is true that the middle clause also includes the case where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor is certain, which explains the words "No, rather," but there would then be no novelty in saying that Sumchus does not require division in that subcase. One could hardly say that the entire first clause is superfluous, because if it comes merely to teach that Sumchus requires division where both are uncertain, many Mishnayot already establish that rule, such as the beginning of chapter HaParah and the Mishnayot concerning the borrowed and rented animals and concerning an exchange. Nor is it necessary to teach that the Sages disagree even there. If, in the middle clause where there is partial admission, the Sages nevertheless say that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, then all the more so in the first clause where the defendant totally denies liability. We must therefore say that the first clause concerns an injured party making a definite claim and a tortfeasor who is uncertain. It teaches that even there the Sages apply the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, contrary to Sumchus. The middle clause must correspondingly be understood in that way as well to the extent the wording allows, which is strained, as Maharsha notes. Were it not for the implication of "No, rather," we would have established the whole matter as uncertainty on both sides. There is, however, a way to explain the wording. In the first clause, although the main dispute between Sumchus and the Sages concerns the case where the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor uncertain, the Mishnah still says "No, rather" because it also includes two definite claims. It teaches that even where the ox was a mu'ad that was pursuing the injured animal, the Sages nevertheless allow the tortfeasor to say that the animal was struck by a rock, as Rambam rules, contrary to the view of Maharshal. The Mishnah says simply "an ox," which includes a mu'ad. But in the middle clause, if the actual dispute were only where both parties were uncertain, why would the Mishnah say "No, rather" merely to include an additional case where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor certain? There would be no practical dispute in that additional case, since Sumchus too would agree that the possessor's definite claim prevails. The phrase would teach nothing. Therefore the middle clause itself must involve a case where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor certain, and even there Sumchus requires division while the Sages apply the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. If so, the final clause likewise necessarily involves the injured party uncertain and the tortfeasor certain, at least according to Rabbah bar Mamal, who maintains that the first clause involves two definite claims and whose approach still requires the middle clause to have the tortfeasor making a definite claim in order for the wording to be meaningful. But this creates a major difficulty according to Abaye. Tosafot in Bava Batra 135, s.v. Abaye, write that Abaye holds that even where the uncertain party had absolutely no reason to know, a definite claim is still superior. How then can the first clause, where the injured party is certain and the tortfeasor uncertain, conclude according to the Sages that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant? The claimant's definite claim should prevail. All the more so in the middle clause, where the injured party is uncertain and the tortfeasor certain, why should Sumchus require division? Tosafot's answer that the uncertain party had no reason to know does not work according to Abaye. This is a powerful difficulty. The Rif therefore must hold as I have written, that once the Gemara introduces seizure, that answer applies to the middle clause as well and the clauses can be understood as cases of two definite claims. Abaye can explain the middle clause as two definite claims, where Sumchus and the Sages disagree, without any difficulty from the principle that a definite claim is superior to an uncertain one. The rule of seizure also removes the difficulty from Rabbah bar Natan. Thus the Rif's interpretation is necessary for Abaye according to the view attributed to him by Tosafot in Bava Batra. This is, in my opinion, a clear and powerful proof for the Rif. Tosafot in HaManiach, who do not apply seizure to the first and middle clauses, follow their own view in chapter HaParah that even Abaye agrees that where the uncertain party had no reason to know, a definite claim is not superior. They therefore face no difficulty. The Rif, however, follows the other approach recorded by Tosafot in Bava Batra, as explained above, and his position has a clear proof.
The value of the barley. The Sema writes that the reason is that since the claimant is already in possession, he can say, "I neither admitted nor waived the barley. I merely did not wish to demand everything at once." These are his words. At first glance they are incomprehensible. According to the Rama, seizure helps specifically if it occurred before the admission, but not afterward. If the Sema's reasoning were correct, seizure afterward should also help, because the claimant could say, "I know that I did not waive it." One cannot answer that this would be seizure after the doubt arose, because where the possessor makes a definite claim, seizure is effective even after the doubt arose, as the Rosh writes in Ketubot and Bava Metzia. See what I wrote above in the rules of seizure. This is particularly so according to the Mechaber, who holds that seizure even after the doubt arises is effective. We must therefore say that the Sema's reason was written only according to the opinion that seizure is effective even afterward, not according to those who distinguish based on the timing of seizure. Yet the Sema himself, in seif katan 26, cites the Nimukei Yosef's view distinguishing between seizure before the claimant's retraction and seizure afterward. His words therefore remain difficult. The Tur explicitly gives a different reason. He writes: since the claimant seized the barley, his failure to demand it is not considered an admission, because he can say, "I did not demand the barley because I was already in possession of it." This is a different explanation from the Sema's. Still, even the Rosh requires explanation. According to his own view, seizure helps only in the case of the two oxen. He writes: therefore, if the injured party seized before coming to beit din, even in the presence of witnesses, he collects according to what the tortfeasor says. Why does the Rosh require seizure specifically before coming to beit din? In the case of the two injured animals, the explanation that "I did not demand the smaller one because I was already in possession of it" seems inapplicable. The injured party cannot truthfully mean to demand both the large and the small ox, because the two claims are mutually exclusive. If the larger ox caused the damage, the smaller did not. Once he asserts in beit din that the larger ox caused the damage, he can no longer say that the smaller ox did. One might answer that this reason is needed only where the claimant could have claims regarding both items, whereas where the claims are mutually exclusive no such reason is necessary. But if so, the Rosh's objection to the Rif would fail. With wheat and barley it is possible that both claims are true, whereas with one large and one small ox both claims cannot be true. What then does seizure help there, since the Tur's explanation cannot apply? It is strained to say that the injured party could have come to beit din and said explicitly, "In my view the larger ox caused the damage, but if it is established otherwise, or if the owner admits only the smaller ox, then at least give me the value of the smaller." Such a formulation indeed would avoid both admission and waiver. If the claimant had seized, it could be treated as though he had made that reservation, because according to the Rosh both oxen are in his hands, while according to the Rif at least the smaller one is in his hands. This also resolves another difficulty in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. They state generally that seizure helps. That is understandable if, according to the claimant, there were two separate loans, one of wheat and one of barley, even if they occurred on the same day and at the same hour. But what if, according to both parties, there was only one loan, and they disagree whether it was wheat or barley? The defendant is exempt, exactly like the case of one large and one small ox. In such a case seizure should seemingly not help even according to the Rif. The claimant cannot say, "Because I had already seized the barley I did not demand it," for he did demand this very loan and admitted by his formulation that it was not barley. According to what I have written, however, the matter is resolved. Seizure is treated as though the claimant had expressly reserved his rights by saying, "I am certain it was wheat, but if in fact it was barley, I do not waive or admit away that claim." Such a reservation works in every case. Yet even if we can interpret the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in this way according to the Rif, it is difficult to explain the Rosh this way because he grounds the rule in the claimant's admission. What reservation can overcome an express admission that the smaller ox did not damage the larger animal? Unless we say that the ultimate effect of such an admission is waiver, so that seizure is treated as though the claimant expressly stipulated that he does not waive. This itself is proof against the Shach's explanation of the Rosh above. All of this is strained if we distinguish between situations where the claimant could have claims against both items and situations where he could not. The same reasoning should apply even where the claims are mutually exclusive. There is also a difficulty in Tosafot. At the beginning of chapter HaParah, Tosafot ask according to Rashbam's view that Sumchus did not state his division rule where both parties make definite claims except when the disputed property stands in a neutral place, whereas when it is in the possessor's house Sumchus agrees that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. If so, why did Rabbah bar Mamal feel compelled to say that the Mishnah does not follow Sumchus? Let the Mishnah follow Sumchus and concern a case where the tortfeasor's ox remained in the tortfeasor's house, in which case Sumchus agrees that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Yam Shel Shlomo discusses this question at length and treats it as a strong argument against Rashbam. But how can the Mishnah be explained that way? In the final clause involving the two injured animals, the difficulty from Rabbah bar Natan's rule of claiming wheat and admitting barley would remain. We would then have to say that the injured party seized both oxen, as the Rosh writes, and that the seizure occurred before he came to beit din. But if the ox is in the injured party's house, that is stronger possession than if it stood in a neutral field. Surely his position should not become weaker merely because he brought it into his own house. According to Sumchus, the Mishnah should therefore require division rather than simply apply the burden of proof to the claimant. The result could not be that the injured party either takes according to the tortfeasor or takes nothing where he did not seize, while if he did seize they divide. How then can the Mishnah simply state that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, implying that with proof he takes according to the tortfeasor's admission? It follows that the Mishnah cannot follow Sumchus and must follow the Sages. According to the Sages, the Mishnah must be teaching their rule specifically against Sumchus, and therefore we establish the case as one where the ox stands in a neutral place, where Sumchus would require division. This entirely resolves Tosafot's question. Why, then, would Tosafot force any strained answer that undermines Rashbam when such a straightforward answer is available? This suggests at first glance that Tosafot do not hold that seizure must occur before the parties stand in beit din. Perhaps seizure afterward also helps. Then one can say that at the time of judgment the tortfeasor's ox was in the tortfeasor's house, and only afterward did the injured party seize it. Sumchus could then agree at the first stage that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, while the later seizure addresses Rabbah bar Natan's rule. Yet we find no authority who explicitly disagrees regarding the requirement that seizure precede the litigation. In particular, the Rosh states the requirement without qualification, and Tosafot ordinarily would not disagree with him without the Rosh mentioning their position. It therefore appears that the Rosh holds as follows. Even if the claimant later says, "I did not intend to admit anything," we disregard that subjective intention because unexpressed thoughts have no legal effect. Rather, beit din itself is uncertain whether the claimant's original formulation constitutes a complete admission that exempts the defendant from the barley or whether it does not. Because this is only a slight doubt and the language leans toward admission and waiver, the claimant must satisfy two conditions in order for seizure to prevail. First, he must have seized before the doubt arose in beit din, meaning before he made the relevant claim there. Second, the seizure must have been lawful, as the Rosh writes in Bechorot in the name of Ramban, that seizure must be with permission. That means that the possessor must have had a right to seize the very item based on the claim he was asserting, as Terumat HaDeshen explains. I discussed this principle at length above in the treatise on seizure. Therefore, the claimant must have some legitimate claim concerning both items, so that his seizure is lawful. Where, according to his own position, he has no claim at all to one of the items, seizure of that item was not lawful and is ineffective. This resolves the issue. Accordingly, Tosafot can also be understood. Since beit din itself is uncertain whether the claimant's words amounted to admission and waiver, Sumchus would require division because Sumchus applies his division rule to a doubt that exists for beit din, just as in the case of whether an intercalated month belongs to the renter or the owner, where the doubt is whether we follow the first expression or the last. The authorities write that the opinion requiring division there is Sumchus. See what I wrote above in the rules of seizure. If so, were the Mishnah according to Sumchus, as Tosafot ask, there would be no need to assume seizure at all. The difficulty from the baraita that says that without proof he takes according to the tortfeasor would disappear because in truth, according to Sumchus, he would not simply take according to the tortfeasor. He would take half of the amount admitted by the tortfeasor, since beit din is uncertain whether the claimant's earlier words constituted admission or waiver, and Sumchus requires division in such a doubt. The Mishnah could not simply say "exempt," because the claimant would collect half of what the tortfeasor admitted. One might object that if the disputed ox is in the tortfeasor's house, Sumchus does not require division. That is true only where the possessor makes a definite claim. Where the possessor himself is uncertain, Sumchus requires division even when the property is in his house, as Tosafot explicitly write in Bava Metzia 100 based on the Mishnah concerning the borrowed and rented animals. Here the tortfeasor cannot make a definite legal claim that the claimant's language constituted admission. The legal effect of that language is itself uncertain. Therefore Sumchus would always require division. Everything is properly explained. This approach, however, cannot explain the Rif and Rambam according to the Mechaber, who holds that seizure even after the doubt arose is effective. According to them, we must instead use the Tur's reason, namely that since the claimant had seized the barley, his failure to demand it does not constitute an admission. Where even the claimant agrees that there was only one loan, we must resort to the more strained explanation above, that seizure is treated as though he expressly stipulated that even if the loan is found to have been barley, he neither waives nor admits away his right. This matter still requires study.
