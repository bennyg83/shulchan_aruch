He is exempt even from the barley. Ramah explains the exemption because the defendant can say, 'I was merely jesting with you.' Since the claimant never sued for barley, the defendant's statement about barley is not a binding admission. Rosh objects from the Gemara, which asks: if the claimant does not bring proof, let him collect according to what the tortfeasor admitted. The case must involve witnesses who saw the collision, for without witnesses the defendant would be admitting a penalty and would be exempt. If witnesses saw a collision but did not know whether the larger or smaller animal caused the damage, they at least establish liability corresponding to the smaller damage. Rosh therefore explains the exemption not as jesting but because the claimant, by suing only for wheat and not barley, is treated as admitting that the defendant owes him no barley. Tosafot in Sanhedrin 5a likewise grounds the exemption in the claimant's admission. This appears to be Shulchan Aruch's position. Rama, however, appears to follow the explanation of waiver. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, holds that where witnesses establish that one of the two animals caused the damage, either the large or the small, the tortfeasor must at least pay the smaller amount according to the witnesses. He therefore understands the Gemara in chapter HaManiach as a case without witnesses, following the view that half-damages are monetary rather than penal. The Shach, with his broad understanding, develops Ramah's approach at length and concludes like Ramah that the barley claim is exempt because the defendant may say he was jesting, and the Gemara speaks without witnesses according to the view that half-damages are monetary. He further suggests that the Gemara could be speaking even according to the view that half-damages are penal if the matter had already been adjudicated: the claimant says, 'I brought you to judgment and you were held liable for the larger animal,' while the defendant says, 'The smaller animal caused it and I was adjudicated liable only for the smaller amount.' Yet the sugya itself must involve witnesses. It says that this proves the Rabbis disagree with Sumchus, who says disputed money is divided, and Sumchus says that only where there is derara demamona, a genuine objective uncertainty independent of the parties' assertions. If there were no witnesses at all, the uncertainty would arise solely from their claims and would not be a case of derara demamona. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 100a likewise explains that where the buyer and seller dispute whether a large or small slave was sold, Sumchus can say they divide only where witnesses establish the framework of the transaction but do not know which one was sold. So too here, witnesses saw the event but do not know whether the animal struck the rock or was gored by the ox, and in the later clause whether the large or small ox caused the damage. Thus the law proves that even with witnesses the barley equivalent is exempt. The Shach also says that the Maggid Mishneh and Nimukei Yosef were forced into their explanations, but this is not so. Nimukei Yosef explains the case of two damaging animals and two injured animals and asks why this is not ordinary partial admission. The claimant is demanding both large and small damages, and the defendant admits at least the smaller. Nimukei Yosef answers that we do not follow the present monetary value alone but the underlying object from which the claim arose. The claimant says the large animal caused the large damage; the defendant admits only that the small animal caused a smaller damage. It is therefore like wheat and barley, because the primary subject of the claim differs. Only where the parties already converted the underlying claim into money before litigation do we say that a claim for two hundred includes one hundred. So too, where one witness says a barrel of wine and another a barrel of oil, their testimony can combine only where the claimant had already reduced the matter to a monetary valuation. Thus in the case of forewarned animals as well, when one says the forewarned ox damaged the large animal and the other says it damaged the small animal, the claimant's formulation itself concedes there is no smaller damage from that same ox. This follows Nimukei Yosef's reasoning and is not the same as an ordinary claim for a large or small monetary sum. The early authorities already dispute whether we follow the underlying object or only the present money claim. Shulchan Aruch adopts the view that we follow the underlying subject. According to this, the Shach's central difficulty falls away. The exemption does not require jesting. It follows from the claimant's own admission regarding the object on which he bases the action. If the defendant continues to say that he owes the barley, then according to Rabbeinu Yerucham he must pay it, because the claimant did not waive it; the court simply cannot award it based on the claimant's inconsistent lawsuit. If the defendant retracts from his barley admission but knows in truth that he owes it, he remains obligated toward Heaven according to the admission rationale. This differs from the waiver rationale, under which the claimant has actually relinquished the barley even toward Heaven. The same distinction explains Tosafot at the end of chapter HaKotev concerning a woman who says she was divorced. Tosafot asks why the husband is not at least liable for her food and clothing through a migo, and answers that this is a case of wheat and barley. According to the admission rationale, if the husband continues to admit the food and clothing, he should pay. If he denies them entirely, he can assert an exempting claim such as payment or waiver, which becomes relevant because the woman's own position has admitted she has no support claim as a wife. This also resolves Tosafot's question from the last chapter of Ketubot concerning a woman who went overseas with her husband and returned saying, 'My husband divorced me.' She receives support up to the amount of her ketubah. Tosafot asks: perhaps if the husband were present he would say, 'I did not divorce you,' and then, under the wheat-barley principle, she should lose support as well. Tosafot answers that perhaps he would admit her account. The words are difficult if we say her own statement alone waived support absolutely. According to the admission approach, however, when the husband is absent beit din advances on his behalf the claim, 'I did not divorce you,' while still treating the support obligation that flows from that status as existing. They therefore pay support. If he were present, he could add an independent exempting claim regarding support, such as payment or waiver, and then her own admission would prevent beit din from awarding support. This is coherent. On the waiver approach, by contrast, once she claimed divorce she would have waived support even if the husband were absent, which does not fit. Tosafot's further statement that even according to Rabbah bar Natan, who exempts barley where wheat was claimed, Rabban Gamliel can still impose an oath despite not requiring the admission to be of the same kind, must likewise rest on the admission rationale rather than waiver. If the barley had actually been waived, there would be no admission at all. But where the defendant acknowledges a real obligation and is perhaps still liable toward Heaven, his statement can be treated as 'this is it' for oath purposes, as I wrote in siman 87, seif katan 23. See what I wrote above in seif katan 4.
As though the claimant admitted. See Shach, who asks: if the reason is that the claimant's failure to demand barley constitutes an admission that no barley is owed, how can seizure later help? A prior admission should be binding. It appears that the matter is a conflict of admissions. The claimant's words imply that barley is not owed, but the defendant himself expressly admitted that barley is owed. Once the claimant seizes, the defendant cannot recover it solely by invoking the claimant's implied admission, because his own explicit admission supports the claimant's possession. Thus seizure is effective not because the earlier admission disappears, but because the parties' opposing admissions create an unresolved monetary situation.
That I do not owe him barley. The Shulchan Aruch appears to adopt the admission rationale: by demanding wheat and not barley, the claimant is treated as having admitted that the defendant owes him no barley. This invites a further question where the claimant had previously seized the barley or where his conduct otherwise shows that he never intended to relinquish it. In such a case the silence in his pleading is less naturally understood as an admission or waiver. The effectiveness of seizure below is therefore intelligible: his possession demonstrates that his claim was not meant to concede the barley absolutely, while the defendant's own admission confirms a basis for retaining it.
As though he waived. According to the waiver rationale, one may ask how the Gemara can challenge from the case of large and small animals that, if the claimant brings no proof, he should receive what the tortfeasor admitted. The Yerushalmi in chapter Eilu Na'arot says that a penalty cannot be waived before judgment. Even according to the Bavli, which allows waiver of some penalties, Tosafot at the beginning of Eilu Na'arot explains that there the liability arises through a consensual act and is analogous to saying, 'Tear my silk garment and be exempt.' That reason does not apply here, because the damage already occurred. It appears that although other penalties cannot ordinarily be waived before judgment, this half-damages claim can be. In Bava Kamma 33a, the Gemara says one practical difference between Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Yishmael is whether the injured party can consecrate his interest. Tosafot asks how Rabbi Akiva can allow consecration when a penalty is not acquired before judgment. They answer that the verse reveals that according to Rabbi Akiva the injured party acquires from the moment of the goring, from the words 'they shall sell the live ox,' which refer to both tortfeasor and injured party. Thus, according to Rabbi Akiva, whom we follow, Scripture gives the injured party a transferable interest before formal judgment, and he can likewise sell or waive it to the tortfeasor. According to what we explain elsewhere in siman 407 in Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, however, even Rabbi Akiva does not allow sale or consecration before judgment, and the case of consecration refers to after judgment. The difficulty therefore returns. Ran at the beginning of chapter Eilu Na'arot gives another explanation for the Bavli's law concerning an orphan or seduced woman. It resembles one who writes to his betrothed, 'I have no claim or rights in your property,' and thereby removes his right to inherit because a person can waive a future inheritance coming from another source. So too here, by failing to claim the smaller penalty he can remove himself from it before judgment even if direct waiver of a presently owned penalty would not work.
And if he seized. At the end of chapter HaManiach, the Gemara says that if the claimant does not bring proof he takes according to what the tortfeasor says, then explains that he is only fit to take but has no actual right. A baraita says he collects the smaller damage from the larger animal and the larger from the smaller, and the Gemara resolves this as a case where he already seized. Tosafot in chapter HaShoel 100 explains that where there is derara demamona, seizure can be effective even before witnesses. Since the parties here dispute whether the large or small animal caused the damage, and Sumchus would divide only where derara demamona exists, why should seizure not enable the injured party to retain the amount he claims? This requires study. I later found Tumim, in Kuntres HaTefisah, raising this point, and my learned brother Rabbi Yehudah has already analyzed it at length in Kuntres HaSefekot.
The value of the barley. Tumim writes that if one claims, 'I deposited wheat with you,' and the defendant answers, 'You deposited barley,' the waiver rationale cannot apply where the barley is known to remain intact. Wherever the object is, it remains in its owner's domain. A language of waiver is therefore ineffective for a specific existing object; only language of gift could transfer it, and the claimant's words cannot be read as, 'I hereby give it to you.' This is incorrect. In chapter Chazakat HaBatim, the Gemara explains three years' chazakah by saying that an owner ordinarily does not object for up to three years but does object after that, and his silence can support an inference of relinquishment. Yet with land, ordinary verbal waiver certainly is not enough and a gift requires appropriate transfer. It follows that silence can imply an intent to give where the subject is a specific object, just as it implies waiver where waiver is legally sufficient. Only an express formula of 'I waive' is ineffective for a specific object. Where intent is inferred from silence, the law reads that silence as the appropriate form of relinquishment: gift where gift is required, waiver where waiver suffices. This is clear. If the deposit is presently in a third party's domain, however, the defendant does not acquire it merely from the claimant's silence, since he has not yet acquired the object. One should not ask why the Gemara, regarding innocuous oxen, needed to establish the baraita as a case of seizure. Why not say that even if the disputed ox stood in a meadow, the injured party's nonclaim of the smaller ox could operate as waiver? We follow Rabbi Akiva, under whom the ox is ultimately awarded to the injured party, but before judgment it is not yet legally his in the full sense. Thus a removal of rights can operate before judgment, as explained above. Even according to Tosafot, who holds that Rabbi Akiva's ownership can relate back from judgment, the claimant who does not claim the smaller ox may relinquish his prospective right before judgment. Once he has withdrawn from it, later judgment cannot revive what he already relinquished. Ran's principle regarding removing oneself from a future inheritance supports this. Examine carefully.
We do not remove it from his possession. According to the admission rationale, seizure helps because the defendant too admitted that he owes the barley. When the claimant seizes, we do not remove it because the matter resembles a two-against-two uncertainty: the claimant's admission is evidence against his own claim, but the defendant's admission is likewise strong evidence in favor of the seized amount. See seif katan 9. There remains a difficulty with half-damages, which is penal and becomes due only through witnesses. If the claimant admitted that the smaller ox caused no damage, his own admission is stronger than witnesses as against himself. The defendant's admission should not create penal liability, because penalties cannot ordinarily be imposed through self-admission. This supports Raavad and Maggid Mishneh, chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, that half-damages differs from other penalties: where the tortfeasor admits and the injured party already seized, the seizure is effective, even though seizure after admission would not work with ordinary penalties. Half-damages is a special category. This explains why seizure also works here with innocuous oxen.
