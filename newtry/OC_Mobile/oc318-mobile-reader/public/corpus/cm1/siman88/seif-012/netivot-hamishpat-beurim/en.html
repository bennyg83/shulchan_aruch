For it is considered as though he admitted. See Shach seif katan 16, through his statement that the entire sugya is discussing this case. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that, if so, there is no derara demamona, since there are no witnesses and beit din has no uncertainty except through the parties' claims. This is no difficulty. Even in the first clause, where the plaintiff says, "Not so, but rather he struck the sela," the Gemara considers it a derara demamona, although necessarily the witnesses did not see the actual goring. Since they saw the pursuit, that itself creates a derara demamona. So too in the latter clause, the case may be where witnesses saw the pursuit by the two oxen but did not see which one gored, and the sight of the pursuit creates the monetary uncertainty. Moreover, even if there are no witnesses at all to the pursuit, since both parties agree that there was a pursuit by the two oxen, there is an objective uncertainty before beit din, independent of their dispute, as to which ox gored. The Shach further writes that this also resolves the Gemara's question, because the owner of the damaging ox was not present. At first glance this is difficult, since the Shach wrote earlier that the case is one in which the parties already stood in judgment, which seemingly requires the owner to have been present. Ketzot HaChoshen raises this objection. It is not difficult, because as to a muad there is no need for an earlier adjudication of the particular damage. The injured party claims that the muad damaged the larger animal, while the owner says: you never brought me to judgment concerning damage to the larger animal, only concerning the smaller one which was damaged by the tam. When the claim is that the muad damaged the larger and the muad the smaller, the Shach's answer is that the owner did not have to respond to that point, because there had been no adjudication of it. In the case of a tam damaging the larger and a tam the smaller, however, although he had to respond, it resembles a claim of wheat and an admission of barley. The Shach needed his additional reasoning specifically for the muad because it resembles a claim for the value of a slave. Nevertheless, all of the Shach's discussion is strained, as is evident to one who studies it carefully and as Tumim writes. In my view the position of Ramah can be resolved against the Rosh's objection by explaining that there really are witnesses and that the discussion follows the view that half damages is a fine. Even so, the Gemara properly challenges Rabbah bar Natan, because the Mishnah implies that the injured party may collect from the smaller ox by virtue of the owner's admission, even if the larger ox was lost. According to Rabbah bar Natan, the owner should still be able to say that his admission was merely made in jest, and that the claimant cannot recover because of the damage itself. On the testimony of the witnesses, he may recover only when both oxen are present, as the Mishnah there states explicitly. His admission, he may say, was not intended as a binding admission. If one asks why, in truth, he may recover when both oxen are not present by virtue of the owner's admission, even though one who admits a fine is exempt, that is no difficulty. Whenever witnesses testify to the goring, although they do not know which ox gored and the owner identifies it, this is not considered an admission to a fine, as Tumim writes in seif katan 14. Maharsha goes further and writes that even witnesses merely to the pursuit suffice so that the owner's statement is not an admission to a fine. However, the Shach's attribution of Rashi, Tosafot, and Rambam to the view of Ramah is certainly incorrect, as Tumim explains. Careful study of the poskim shows that they do not accept Ramah's approach. It is also very difficult to say that the Mishnah concerning an ox that pursued another is discussing a defendant who retracts his admission and claims that he was only jesting. Ramah's view also faces the Gemara's question: why not establish the Mishnah as a case in which he remains by his admission? Tumim raises many additional objections. There is another difficulty. Tosafot and the Rosh write that even according to Rav Nachman, who imposes liability where one was sued for wheat and admitted barley, that liability is only as to an oath, while he remains exempt from the value of the barley. According to Ramah, however, if the defendant retracts his admission and claims jesting, how can he be a modeh bemiktzat? According to the approach of admission and waiver, one can say that he is nevertheless liable to swear, because the plaintiff's waiver is completed only after the defendant's admission, when the plaintiff fails to answer that he also claims the barley, as the Shach explains in seif katan 18. At the moment of the admission, therefore, the defendant was genuinely a modeh bemiktzat and became liable to swear concerning the wheat. Ramah's approach remains difficult. It therefore appears that the principal rule is as all the poskim hold, that the exemption is grounded in admission and waiver, and even Ramah can agree to this, as I will explain. Yet the statements of the poskim that this rests upon admission and waiver also require clarification. The Shach rightly asks what reason compels such a result. Ritva in Kiddushin also raises a strong question from the case of two men and a woman together with a bundle, where the woman says that the two men are her slaves and the bundle is hers, and she collects her ketubah from the bundle by a mima nafshach argument. Why is this not like wheat and barley, since by her own statement she admitted that there was no ketubah lien upon them? Ritva answers that she claims by way of mima nafshach, but if that sufficed, every wheat and barley claimant could make the same argument. There is a further difficulty. According to these poskim, if a depositor says, "I deposited or pledged a large utensil with you," and the custodian denies it and says that only a small utensil was deposited, while the depositor says that the small utensil is not his and that he deposited a different, larger utensil, the custodian should be exempt even from returning the small utensil. This is exactly analogous to wheat and barley and to the Mishnah concerning two tam oxen, because the depositor supposedly waived the small utensil and admitted that it is not his. Yet that conclusion is plainly false. Siman 87 seif 4 shows that the custodian must return the smaller item, and the Mishnah in Bava Metzia 37 expressly teaches that when two utensils are in dispute, although the depositor denies the smaller one, he nevertheless receives it. One cannot answer that there the case is bari against shema, because the sugya shows that the rule of Rabbah bar Natan, exempting the barley, applies even in a bari and shema setting. Mordechai in HaManiach asks the same question. Nor have we ever heard anyone rule that way in practice. Daily cases of disputed deposits and pledges demonstrate that if one party says, "That is not mine," while the custodian admits holding a smaller item, the smaller item is nevertheless returned. The explanation is as follows. At the end of HaManiach, the Rosh, after understanding the case of wheat and barley as referring to claims arising at different times, rejects the rationale of waiver, because a person commonly does not assert all his claims at one time. He also argues that the Mishnah is not parallel, because even if a claimant of wheat has not waived the barley, the injured party in the Mishnah is exempt for another reason: by stating that only one ox caused the damage and identifying the larger ox as the damager, the claimant thereby excludes the smaller one. Conversely, even if in the Mishnah liability were imposed because we do not follow the admission when it was made merely to gain an advantage, Rabbah bar Natan's case of wheat and barley could still be exempt because of waiver. The Rosh therefore explains that Rabbah bar Natan too concerns claims made at one time and that its rationale is also admission. Accordingly, why, once the Rosh places the claims at one time, does he abandon waiver and rely upon admission? Necessarily, waiver can be explained away by saying that the claimant did not intend to relinquish anything but framed his claim to gain an advantage. Admission is different. Even if he spoke to gain an advantage, beit din cannot adjudicate for him a right that his own pleading disclaims. This is also evident from Tosafot in Sanhedrin 6, who write that in the Mishnah waiver cannot be the explanation because the claimant stands and protests. They therefore invoke admission, in the sense explained here. This also clarifies the Shach in siman 9, who writes that because the rule rests on admission, if the defendant knows he truly owes the barley he remains personally obligated to pay it and is a robber if he withholds it, even though beit din does not extract the barley from him. Tumim objects: if the claimant's admission is not a waiver, why should beit din not extract the barley? There is also the mima nafshach argument from Kiddushin 66, where the woman with the two men and bundle collects her ketubah from the bundle. The answer is that whenever, even according to the plaintiff's own claim concerning the wheat, he also has a lien upon the barley, he can collect from the barley by mima nafshach: either the barley is itself his, or it is property subject to his claim. Since the admission here cannot be treated as a waiver, because the claims were made together and the plaintiff's purpose was to demand more, the admission likewise cannot release the property entirely when even on the plaintiff's version the property remains encumbered to him. Consequently, the exemption from both wheat and barley can occur in two distinct ways: where the claims concern different times, because of waiver, or where there is no property lien and the issue is one of admission. Thus the sugya of HaManiach properly regards the case as wheat and barley. According to the injured party's statement that the larger ox damaged, he has no lien whatever upon the smaller ox. Beit din therefore cannot turn to the smaller ox, even if the owner removes the larger ox to a marsh, because according to the plaintiff himself there is no legal nexus to the smaller ox. The Gemara can therefore challenge Rabbah bar Natan, who follows the plaintiff's own admission in wheat and barley when the claims are separated in time and treats the omission as waiver. Here too, even where the claims are simultaneous and there is no waiver, perhaps the plaintiff's admission should control to prevent beit din from proceeding against the smaller ox. The same reasoning applies to a tam and a muad. Although according to the plaintiff there may be a property lien upon the tam, siman 49, in the discussion of two persons having the same name, explains that when a person with the same name serves as guarantor for another, the creditor cannot collect from the guarantor by mima nafshach, because just as the debtor cannot be sued, neither can the guarantor. Likewise, if one such person inherited a field from another, the creditor cannot collect from the field by mima nafshach, because a person's property is treated as his guarantor. If the principal debtor cannot be sued, the property guarantor cannot be sued either. In the case of a tam and muad, when the tam gored there is no personal lien against the owner, for we rule that the ox itself is awarded to the injured party and the ox is effectively the debtor. When the muad gored there is a personal lien against the owner, and the tam can at most be subject as property collateral. If the owner denies the personal obligation and says the tam caused the damage, the personal lien is removed and the plaintiff cannot proceed against collateral by mima nafshach, just as he cannot proceed against the guarantor in the identical-name case. This also resolves the Shach's question why the Gemara treats tam and muad as wheat and barley even though a muad is comparable to a monetary value claim. It may further explain Rambam in siman 400, who rules in the case of tam and muad that it is not treated as wheat and barley. Rambam disagrees with the identical-name rule in siman 49, as appears from Hilchot Bechorot, and therefore can hold that in tam and muad the claimant recovers through mima nafshach. By contrast, in the examples brought by the Shach concerning wheat and barley, where the defendant answers that the loan consisted of barrels of grain and the claims relate to the same day and time, there is no genuine waiver. There is a personal obligation and a lien upon property by mima nafshach. It is therefore clear that he must pay at least the value of the lesser claim. The practical conclusion is that complete exemption in wheat and barley is found principally where the claims concern different times and the rule is based upon waiver, as the Shach explains in seif katan 18: because after the defendant answered barley the plaintiff did not answer that he also demands barley, he is treated as having waived it. This need not be an ordinary verbal waiver. An admission itself can operate as an effective acquisition of waiver or gift, even where the barley is not then in the defendant's courtyard and cannot be acquired by meshichah or chatzer, just as is evident in siman 209 regarding one who says, "This Levi is not my brother." This is unlike Tumim and Ketzot HaChoshen. Where wheat and barley are claimed at one time, however, the waiver rationale does not apply because, as the Rosh writes, the claimant may simply be formulating his claim so as to gain an advantage. The admission rationale may exempt him from delivering the actual barley, because as between the plaintiff's and defendant's incompatible admissions we follow the plaintiff's admission for defining the object claimed. Yet if a mima nafshach monetary claim remains, the plaintiff may still recover at least the lesser value. Thus when the Rosh states that in a simultaneous wheat and barley claim the defendant is exempt from the barley, he means exemption from delivering that actual barley, such as where it is worth more or the plaintiff insists upon the object itself because the defendant admitted it. If the claim can be satisfied monetarily through mima nafshach, the lesser value remains recoverable. Where no property lien exists, as with a tam whose liability is limited to its own body, beit din has no basis to reach the second object, and the exemption is complete. Ramah adds that even in a simultaneous claim based upon admission, one can find complete exemption from the barley if the defendant retracts and claims that his earlier statement was jesting. The Tosefta shows that such a claim may be raised when the admission was not made in response to a binding demand. In such a case all agree that he may claim jesting. In the Mishnah of tam and muad, even Ramah can agree that the exemption stems from the plaintiff's admission, because there is no mima nafshach route to the second ox. In ordinary wheat and barley, however, the defendant is exempt from the monetary value of the barley only if he retracts his own admission. If he remains by it, then although he may be exempt from delivering the very barley as such, he must at least pay the value of the lesser claim by mima nafshach. Accordingly, there is ultimately no practical dispute among the poskim. If one sued for wheat and the other admitted barley arising at different times, and after the admission the plaintiff continued to demand only the wheat, all agree that the barley may be treated as waived, as the Shach writes in seif katan 19. But the difference in times must be explicit, for example where the plaintiff says that he lent wheat in Nisan and the defendant admits barley borrowed in Tishrei. If the claim and admission are stated without any temporal distinction, we do not presume waiver, because perhaps they concern one time and the plaintiff is merely trying to gain an advantage. A doubtful waiver cannot displace a certain obligation. If they dispute what was given at one time, the defendant may be exempt from the specific barley, but the plaintiff can still recover the lesser value by mima nafshach, except in a case such as a tam where there is no general property lien and beit din has no route to the alternate property. Ramah's additional case is where the defendant retracts and credibly claims jesting. This reconciles the positions.
And if he seized. The reason seizure is effective appears to be that the plaintiff's admission is not unequivocal, as Sema writes in seif katan 25 and as Taz writes in siman 138 seif 10, that silence is not an unequivocal admission, particularly where the claimant can explain why he remained silent and did not demand the other item. Nimukei Yosef, citing Ran in the sugya of a kohen who seized, likewise holds that seizure can be effective where the prior basis was only silence. This resolves the Shach's question in siman 400 why witnesses help there, whereas here even witnesses do not. There, the claims concern one day and one time, so there was no waiver, as the Rosh writes, only an inference of admission. Since it is not an unequivocal admission, witnesses can overcome it, as explained in siman 138 seif 6 and by Taz there. Here, however, the Mechaber discusses different times, where the rationale is waiver. Witnesses do not undo a waiver, since their testimony does not contradict the fact that the claimant relinquished the claim. Seizure nevertheless helps because, once he has seized, he is believed to say that he did not waive it, as Sema writes. The Rosh's question why the Gemara does not also answer the first clause by saying that the plaintiff seized can be resolved as follows. Tosafot on Bava Kamma 100 write that where there is derara demamona, seizure is effective. One might therefore think seizure should allow collection even according to the plaintiff's larger claim. Tumim in Ketav Tokef siman 54 asks this. The answer is that in a fine witnesses are required even if the claimant is substantively correct. Since the witnesses do not know whether the larger or smaller ox caused the damage, he cannot collect more than the value of the smaller ox, because there is no testimony supporting the excess. At least to the value of the smaller ox there is definite testimony and seizure is effective. In the case of a tam and muad with two injured animals, however, seizure cannot be used to collect the full amount claimed from the muad merely because the muad requires no testimony of prior warning. When both possibilities involve a muad, Nimukei Yosef explains that the case is not wheat and barley at all but comparable to differing values of the same wheat, and seizure is unnecessary as to the muad. The Gemara's answer of seizure therefore concerns seizure of the tam. This also explains why the Gemara cannot give the same answer in the earlier case of one injured animal with a tam and a muad. There seizure could allow recovery beyond the owner's admission, because liability of a muad does not require the same testimony, and with derara demamona seizure can support the plaintiff's own claim. By contrast, where there are two damaging oxen and the plaintiff can recover through seizure only up to the owner's admission because the additional fine lacks testimony, the answer of seizure is appropriate. Ketzot HaChoshen suggests that seizure works because there are two contradictory admissions, comparable to two groups of witnesses that contradict each other, where seizure can be effective. This is incorrect. In Gittin 40, where a master says, "I made so-and-so my slave a free man," and the slave says, "He did not free me," the slave does not become free, even though he is physically in possession of himself and of his labor. If possession could resolve contradictory admissions, there could be no stronger possession than the slave's possession of his own labor. Rather, seizure does not generally resolve two contradictory admissions. Two admissions are unlike two contradictory witness groups. A presumption based merely on possession assists only one who advances a definite claim. Here the possessor himself admits that the property belongs to the party seeking to extract it, so his possession is a chazakah without a supporting claim. Instead we follow the prior ownership chazakah, which functions like an original-status chazakah in issur law. This differs from ordinary possession, whose force is not a technical chazakah but the principle that property is not removed from a possessor without proof. Hence a chazakah unsupported by a claim is ineffective. The same concept appears in siman 280 seif 4 concerning one who says, "This person is not my brother." There, those mutually acknowledged as brothers are certain heirs and the additional claimants are doubtful heirs. A doubt does not remove property from one who is certainly entitled, as in Yevamot 40 regarding the doubtful heir and the yavam. Seizure likewise does not alter that result.
We do not remove it. See Shach seif katan 22, who questions the Tur's explanation that seizure helps because the claimant can say, "Since I was already in possession of it, I did not demand it." How, then, can the Gemara answer by seizure in the case of tam and muad in HaManiach, where that explanation is unavailable? It appears that the Tur gives this reason only according to the authorities who distinguish and hold that seizure does not generally help regarding the barley, nor in the case of one injured animal. According to them, the Gemara's seizure case is only where there are two injured animals, following the Rosh. The Tur nevertheless holds that if the claimant seized before adjudication, seizure can help even in wheat and barley. Beit Yosef attributes this distinction to Tosafot. Although the Gemara does not explicitly prove it, the reasoning is that the claimant may say he did not separately demand the item because he already possessed it. The Tur's first view, however, holds that seizure is effective even for the barley and even with one injured animal, so the Gemara's statement concerning seizure can apply there as well. According to that view the reason is different, as Sema writes in seif katan 25: the plaintiff's earlier statement is not an unequivocal admission. It is effective against him only while he has not seized. Once he seizes, he may offer a reasonable explanation for why he did not demand the alternate item. Concerning barley he can say that he simply chose not to demand it at that point, and concerning tam and muad he can say that he framed his claim in that manner in order to gain an advantage. Taz in siman 138 likewise writes that an admission inferred from silence is not absolute. For that reason Sema in seif katan 25 does not use the Tur's reason, because he is commenting on the Mechaber, who follows Rambam and permits seizure even for the barley and for one injured animal. Rama, by contrast, presents the distinguishing view, and there the Tur's stated reason is appropriate. This also explains why Sema cites Hagahot Maimoniyot in seif katan 26. Although seizure can neutralize the inference of admission, all agree that where the defendant is entitled to retract an informal admission by claiming that he was jesting, seizure after the retraction does not revive the admission, unless the plaintiff has an independent migo. The Rosh disagrees with Ramah only where witnesses are present, because there the jesting rationale is unavailable, but without witnesses he too can agree that an informal admission not made in response to a binding claim may be withdrawn. Thus Sema's position is consistent and the Shach's objections in seif katan 23 and 33 fall away.
