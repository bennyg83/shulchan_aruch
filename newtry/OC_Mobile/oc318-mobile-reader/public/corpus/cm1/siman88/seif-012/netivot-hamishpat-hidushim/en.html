The Shach answers that there the witnesses do not know whether the animal was large or small, and the claimant does not contradict them. Here, however, the witnesses say definitely that the loan was barley while the claimant says it was wheat, thereby contradicting and disqualifying their testimony, so their testimony is ineffective. See the Beurim. The principal distinction is this: where wheat and barley refer to the same occasion, one says it was wheat and the other says it was barley. As I wrote in siman 400 regarding one witness who says large and another who says small, the rule is based on admission rather than waiver, as explained nearby, and therefore witnesses are effective. An unclear admission is still an admission, as explained in siman 138, seif 6, where the law likewise rests on admission and witnesses are effective. But where the wheat and barley refer to different times, so that the law is based on waiver, witnesses do not help. This is clear:
It is as though he waived it. The Shach rules like the Ramah who holds that the reason in the case of wheat and barley is not admission and waiver, but rather that the defendant may say that he was merely jesting and can retract his admission. If he maintains his admission, and certainly if there are witnesses, he is liable. Tumim agrees with the Mechaber. See the Beurim. The principal point is that there is actually no dispute among the authorities. If the claims concern different times, for example the claimant says that he lent wheat in Nisan and the defendant answers that he lent barley in Tishrei, and the claimant then says that he is claiming the wheat of Nisan, this is certainly waiver, and even witnesses do not help. The Shach also agrees. But if both refer to the same time, the claimant saying it was wheat and the defendant saying it was barley, the rationale of waiver does not apply, since the claimant intends only to gain. The rule then rests on admission. In siman 400, concerning an innocuous ox where there is no lien on property, beit din cannot act on the second claim. But where there is a property lien, such as a loan or deposit, he must at least pay the value of the barley whichever version is correct, although the claimant cannot demand the barley itself. If the barley is worth more than the wheat, he pays only the lesser amount. This applies only so long as he does not retract the admission concerning the barley. If he retracts it, he need not pay even the barley, because he can say that he was jesting when he admitted something that had never been claimed from him:
We do not remove it. He can say, "I neither admitted nor waived." This applies specifically when the claimant seized before the defendant retracted his admission. If the defendant first retracted and claimed that he was jesting, and only afterward the claimant seized, the property is removed from him, because according to all opinions the defendant may claim jesting, unless the possessor has a migo that he could have denied the seizure or claimed that he returned the item.
