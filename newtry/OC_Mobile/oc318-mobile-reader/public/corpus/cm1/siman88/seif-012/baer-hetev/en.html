Witnesses. The Shach asks that in siman 400, seif 3, the Mechaber rules without qualification that where there are witnesses the defendant is liable, and cites no dissenting opinion. Perhaps the distinction is that there the witnesses do not know which of the two things caused the damage, so they are not contradicting the defendant. The case is like one who claims wheat, while witnesses testify that the defendant owes him either wheat or barley. The defendant must at least pay barley, because the claimant can say, "I maintain that the witnesses testify to wheat, as I claim, but if you say they testify to barley, then at least give me barley." Here, however, it is as though the claimant himself admitted that he never lent the defendant barley, making the witnesses' testimony about barley ineffective because the claimant himself contradicts them, and a person's admission against his own interest is stronger than one hundred witnesses. The Shach concludes, however, that this is not the primary explanation. Rather, where witnesses testify, the defendant is liable in every case. The primary reason is the view of the Ramah, because the defendant can say that his earlier admission was merely made in jest. See there for the Shach's extended proof.
Waived. The Shach disagrees and writes that the authorities imply that the reason is not waiver. Accordingly, if the defendant knows that he owes barley, he is a robber if he fails to pay it. It is only that beit din cannot extract it from him. See there, and Responsa Maharashach, part 3, siman 25.
A person. This is also the wording of the Tur, and from it the Rama, Ir Shushan, Sema, and Bach derived their explanation that the reason is waiver. It appears, however, that the Tur's wording can be reconciled differently. He means that absent the stated reason the defendant would certainly be liable, for one cannot say that he is always exempt because the claimant waived the barley. It is obvious that a person does not waive his money in this manner. Rather, the reason is that the claimant's own admission defeats his claim, and the case is one where both statements were made at the same time. This is the meaning of the Rosh at the end of chapter HaManiach. Shach.
Seized. The Nimukei Yosef writes that if the claimant seized before the defendant retracted his admission, the seizure is effective even if made in the presence of witnesses. If he seized after the defendant retracted, we remove the property from him unless he has a migo that he could have said, "This never occurred," or, "I returned it." So writes the Sema. The Shach writes, however, that the Nimukei Yosef follows his own reasoning, that the defendant's admission is treated as jesting. According to the Mechaber and Rama, who hold that the reason is that the claimant is viewed as having admitted that the defendant does not owe him barley, or as having waived it, what benefit is there in seizure after the parties have already stood in judgment? If the claimant seized before coming to judgment, he can say that he did not claim the barley because he had already taken possession of it, not because he admitted it was not owed. After standing in judgment, however, that explanation is no longer available, because he has already made the admission. For this reason the Rama writes that, according to one opinion, seizure helps only before the claimant sued him in court. This appears to me to be the primary ruling. Nevertheless, according to what I wrote above that the primary view is that of the Ramah and those who follow him, that the reason is jesting, seizure is effective even after they stood in judgment, provided it occurred before the defendant retracted his admission.
