A house full, etc. In Shevuot 43, the Gemara says that Abaye holds that if one says, "This house was full," it is a claim involving a measurable quantity because the claim is defined. Rava asks: if so, instead of teaching in the latter clause that one says the produce reached the beam and the other says it reached the window, where the defendant is liable, the Mishnah could have distinguished within the same case and said: this exemption applies when he says merely "a house full," but if he says "this house full," the defendant is liable. Rava therefore concludes that liability exists only where the claimant asserts a measured amount and the defendant admits a measured amount. Tosafot, s.v. ela amar, ask why "this house full" is not itself considered a measured claim. Since the claimant says the particular house was full and the defendant returns it partially empty, the missing amount is visible. It should be equivalent to one saying the produce reached the roof and the other saying it reached only the beam. Tosafot answer first that a house lacking only a small amount is still called "full," so the precise difference between their claims is unknown. Alternatively, they say that the defendant must explicitly state a measure in his admission. It appears that the phrase Tosafot introduce in their second answer should not be understood as presenting an entirely independent alternative. Rather, both points are required together. The first answer alone is insufficient. If the issue were merely that a house lacking a small amount is still commonly called full, then there would nevertheless be some threshold beyond which the house is no longer called full. For example, if a house lacking one se'ah is still called full but a greater deficiency is not, the claimant's assertion would still define at least a measurable quantity from one se'ah below the roof upward. Tosafot's original question would remain. Rather, Rava did not actually need to add that the admission itself must expressly state a measure merely for the case of "this house full." He added that requirement because another difficulty would otherwise arise. The Mishnah could have taught: the claimant says the produce reached the beam, while the defendant says, "Take whatever you left there." The deficiency would still be visibly measurable. We therefore need Tosafot's second point, that the defendant's own admission must explicitly contain a measure. But that second point alone is also insufficient. Maharsha asks that the Mishnah could still have taught: the claimant says, "This house was full," and the defendant says the produce reached the window. The defendant's admission would then be explicitly measured. We therefore need Tosafot's first point as well: the claimant's phrase "the house was full" is itself not a precisely measured claim because a slightly deficient house is still described as full. The two explanations therefore operate together and require one another. Tosafot's introductory phrase should be understood as "and also," not as a completely separate alternative. This is precisely reflected in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. They rule that if the claimant says, "This house was full," and the defendant says, "There were ten kor," that does not suffice, because "full house" is not itself a measured claim. They also rule that if the claimant says, "There were ten kor," and the defendant responds, "Take whatever you left there," liability likewise does not arise, because the admission is not itself stated as a measured amount. At first glance this appears to combine two separate Tosafot answers inconsistently. According to what I have written, however, there are not two independent answers at all. Both conditions are necessary, and all the difficulties, including Maharsha's, are resolved.
For example, where they spoiled, etc. The Shach correctly writes that with money as well, one must explain that the coins became invalid or depreciated and that the bailee negligently caused the loss. The Baal HaMaor states this explicitly. See also Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia in the sugya of the borrowed and rented animals. However, one must understand why the Mechaber states this qualification regarding produce but not regarding money. It is possible that he follows what the Shach himself writes above in siman 73, that with loose money, in a place where the bailee is permitted to use it, even if the original coins remain intact and the depositor comes to demand them, the bailee may exchange them for other coins. See there. If so, even while the original coins remain intact they are not heilech, because they remain within the bailee's legal control. He may exchange them and return other coins. How then could the original coins themselves be called heilech? This is straightforward. The Baal HaMaor, who labors to establish the case as one where the money suffered a loss, evidently holds that from the time payment is demanded the bailee no longer has authority to exchange the coins, and therefore intact coins would be heilech. The Shulchan Aruch, however, follows the view cited by the Shach above, according to which the bailee retains the right to exchange them. Accordingly, intact money is not automatically heilech, and no difficulty arises. I am astonished that the Shach overlooked the implication of the very position that he himself adopts.
