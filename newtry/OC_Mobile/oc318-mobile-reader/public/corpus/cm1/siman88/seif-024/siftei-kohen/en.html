That they rotted through his negligence. Although the Mishnah, Tur, and Mechaber say, “What you left, you may take,” that language also applies where the produce rotted. It is as though the defendant says: “You claim produce filling the house to the beam. The produce remains as you left it only up to the window, and that is what you may take. If it rotted through my negligence, I will pay for the deterioration according to that measure.” The Sema explains that liability arises because the claimant says the house was filled to the beam while the defendant says only to the window. Even though he says, “What you left, take,” the case parallels the first clause, where exemption rests on the absence of a measured admission. We must also say that the produce rotted through negligence, for otherwise he would be exempt as a case of “here it is.” The measure itself, however, remains intact: he says that the amount left up to the window is there, and he will pay for what rotted. Nimukei Yosef and the Ran explain similarly. They ask how the measured case can require an oath according to the rule that “here it is” exempts, and answer that the produce deteriorated through negligence, so payment is due for the loss in value, although the original measure remains present. This also proves that the case is not one in which the produce was entirely lost.
For otherwise it would be a case of “here it is.” The Sema says that in the preceding case of money, where the defendant says, “What you left, take,” the exemption is not attributed to “here it is,” because money is ordinarily not assigned a particular place and must be returned into the claimant's hand. Produce, by contrast, is generally placed in a designated location and remains in the owner's domain. Likewise, where one claims a kor of produce and the defendant admits a half-kor, the smaller amount was presumably not assigned a place and is therefore not “here it is.” The Bach writes similarly. Their explanation is incorrect. Even in the depositary's house, the property is deemed in the owner's domain wherever it is, as I wrote above, siman 87, seif 1, seif katan 3. Baal HaTerumot likewise records that some define “here it is” even where the object is in a meadow, and answers the challenge from a house full of produce by saying that the produce rotted through negligence. The true explanation is that the form of the coins, or the produce itself, was damaged through negligence. Tosafot also indicates this. Alternatively, as Lechem Mishneh explains, the purse may concern a loan: the claimant says, “I lent you a purse full of dinars on condition that you return the same purse full,” and the defendant says that he has not spent them and that they remain as given. Yet with a loan there is no “here it is” until the money is actually delivered. Still, the simpler reading of the Mishnah and authorities is that even the money was a deposit, so one must say that the coinage was damaged through negligence. Gidulei Terumah suggests that the objects were later lost through negligence, thereby removing the “here it is” defense. This is incorrect. Had they been lost entirely, the authorities could simply have said so rather than speaking of rotting. The wording of the Ran and Nimukei Yosef expressly indicates that the measure remains present. Moreover, if the property were lost, the defendant would be one who cannot swear and therefore must pay, because his admission extends only to the smallest possible amount, one perutah, and the precise amount cannot be ascertained. This follows the Rambam, Ran, Rivash, Maggid Mishneh, and other authorities cited above, siman 72, seif 12, seif katan 50. The exemption exists only while the property remains present and its amount can be determined. Therefore the coins or produce were not lost. Their measure and number remain, but their form or quality deteriorated through his negligence. This is clear.
