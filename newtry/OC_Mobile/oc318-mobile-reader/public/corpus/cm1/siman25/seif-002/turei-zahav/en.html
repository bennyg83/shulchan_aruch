Seif 3, if he personally took and transferred the property. There are three opinions on this issue. The Rif holds that even Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for causative damage, agrees here that the judge is exempt if he did not personally take and transfer the property, because otherwise no one would agree to serve as a judge. If he did personally take and transfer it, he is liable because he is then a direct tortfeasor. Rambam holds that even without personally taking and transferring it, the judge is liable. The distinction between the two cases is different: where he personally transferred the property, the defendant has no further claim against the opposing party, the judgment remains in force, and the judge must pay. Where he did not personally transfer it, the judgment is reversed because of the litigant's loss; if reversal is impossible, the judge must pay as one who directly caused damage. Although above, regarding an error in an explicit Mishnah, Rambam rules that the judge is exempt because he did not intend to cause damage, there the status of judge does not attach to his act at all. Here, in an error of discretionary judgment, the ruling remains valid in one respect and he acted as a judge, but he corrupted the judgment, so he must pay. The "some say" cited by the Rama is the opinion of the Rosh. According to the Rosh, in an error of discretionary judgment we never find that the judgment itself is reversed. The distinction concerns only payment. Three situations create liability and four create exemption. The three liable cases each possess only one favorable factor: an ordinary layman whom the parties accepted, three laymen whom they did not accept, or an expert judge whom they did not accept. The four exempt cases have two favorable factors: an expert judge whom they accepted, three laymen whom they accepted, or three ordained experts. There is also one exempt case with only one favorable factor, namely a lay judge who received authorization from the Exilarch, provided that he is at least learned. In all the exempt cases he is exempt even if he personally took and transferred the property, and in all the liable cases he is liable even if he did not. In every case the judgment stands, even when the litigants are before us, because there is no demonstrably incorrect ruling. Rather, the judge is required to pay as a penalty.
