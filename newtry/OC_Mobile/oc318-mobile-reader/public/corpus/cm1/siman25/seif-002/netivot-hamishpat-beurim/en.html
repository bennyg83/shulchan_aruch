And he acted in accordance with one of them. See Shach, seif katan 5, through his words that this shows that the fact that he did not know is what produces the exemption, not the liability. There is a scribal error there in Shach in two places. Where it says "and he shall pay," it should read "and he does not pay." Shach's intent is that Mordechai's language, "since he did not know that another authority disagreed with him," implies that because he did not know, he is exempt. If so, this would support Shach's second explanation, namely that there was also a generally accepted course of ruling. According to Sema's explanation, by contrast, the case is a dispute in which there is no accepted course at all, and then the fact that the judge did not know of the second opinion would seem, on the contrary, to be a reason for liability, as Shach wrote at the beginning, that he should have gone and learned, for this is considered a matter subject to judicial weighing. In my humble opinion, Sema's explanation is the principal one. The judge ruled in a dispute where there was no generally accepted course, following one opinion, and he did not know of the second opinion at all. Had he known of it, he would not have ruled as he did. Nevertheless, his lack of knowledge causes exemption and not liability. The case must be one where he knew only of one authority and therefore ruled to remove money from the other party. If he had ruled merely to leave the property with the possessor, then even had he known of the second authority he would certainly have ruled the same way, because where two authorities disagree and there is not even a generally accepted course favoring one, the law follows the possessor. Rather, he knew only of the one authority and ruled to remove money, and afterward he discovered the second authority. If he had known beforehand that two authorities disagreed, and he was not of sufficient stature to decide between them, the law would favor the possessor, as stated in the gloss. Had he nevertheless ruled like one authority to remove money, without any decisive reasoning, merely because he mistakenly thought that where authorities disagree the claimant prevails, he would certainly have the law of one who erred in an explicit Mishnah, under the established rule that where authorities disagree the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. This is all the more so from one who rules to extract money in a case left unresolved by the Gemara, whom Nimukei Yosef, cited by Shach in seif katan 2, letter 2, regards as having erred in an explicit Mishnah, and according to the dissenting authorities he must pay. Mordechai therefore writes that if, at the time of ruling, he knew only of one authority, and for that reason ruled to extract money, while had he known of the second authority he certainly would not have done so because the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, he is nevertheless not considered to have erred in an explicit Mishnah. He is not even considered to have erred in a matter of judicial discretion, because he ruled correctly according to the authority known to him and was unaware of the second. He is not called an erring judge at all, nor can the error be clearly demonstrated. In such a case we say that one who follows this authority has acted validly and one who follows the other has acted validly. Thus, the fact that he did not know is what causes his exemption. Had he known of the second authority and nevertheless erred in the general rule that in a dispute among authorities the claimant bears the burden of proof, he would have erred in an explicit Mishnah and would be liable. According to this, the Rosh cited by Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot regarding Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose is also well explained, because there too there was no generally accepted course at all. The error was that he did not know Rabbi Chiyya's position.
The practice became established. See Shach, seif katan 9, through his example where an anonymous passage of the Gemara raises a difficulty, and so forth. It appears that Shach speaks of a law that is itself recorded in the Gemara as disputed and is not mentioned by the later authorities. But where the law is cited by the halachic authorities, who rule in that manner, even if they derived it from an anonymous challenge of the Gemara, once the authorities have agreed that this is the law and their works have become widely accepted as stating that law, one who rules otherwise is considered to have erred in an explicit Mishnah. This is also evident from the Rosh's response to Baal HaMaor. There is a scribal error in Shach. It should read: "He comes to exclude only a matter in which there is a dispute," and so forth.
But the litigants accepted him. See Shach, seif katan 12, who writes that when they simply come before him to be judged, that itself constitutes acceptance. Tumim asks on the Rosh. Regarding three ordinary judges, the Rosh explains that when the litigants accepted them they are exempt because, even without acceptance, their judgment would be valid, so the additional acceptance serves to exempt them from payment. Regarding an expert judge whom they accepted, however, the Rosh gives a different reason for exemption, namely that since he is an expert and should not have erred, the litigant's own bad fortune caused the loss. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. Even an expert who judges alone does not have the full status of beit din with respect to an admission made before him being a binding admission, or with respect to a litigant's ability to change his claims, unless the parties accepted him, as explained above in siman 3, seif 2. Since acceptance is needed for those consequences, it is not an extra act that can itself be used to infer an exemption from payment. Therefore the Rosh required the reason that the expert is considered compelled by circumstances. Nevertheless, it appears that Tumim's basic distinction is correct. For an expert, since the principal reason for exemption is that his error is treated as unavoidable, and when the parties did not accept him he is not regarded as unavoidable, as Shach writes, it suffices that they simply came before him for judgment. For three ordinary judges, however, the principal reason for exemption is that the express acceptance is an additional act demonstrating that the parties accepted the risk. Therefore they must actually say, "We accept you upon ourselves." This also answers Shach's question in seif katan 15 that a litigant can say, "I do not wish to be judged before you, but before another beit din." According to what I have written, an explicit verbal acceptance is required, and that is certainly an additional act showing consent to exempt them. There is no need to distinguish between claimant and defendant. With three ordinary judges, both parties must say expressly, "We accept you." With an expert, although the claimant accepted him, if he judges the defendant by compulsion he is not regarded as acting under unavoidable circumstances. When the Rosh equates three ordinary judges who were accepted with an expert who was accepted, he equates them only as to exemption from payment. The nature of the acceptance differs: ordinary judges require express verbal acceptance, whereas with an expert simple appearance before him is sufficient, as Tumim writes.
The judgment is reversed. See Rosh, who questions Rif. According to Rif's approach, Rav Huna could have answered that the Mishnah stating that monetary judgments are reversed refers to an expert who was accepted, because such a judge is exempt from payment and therefore the judgment is reversed. Tumim answers that since an expert requires acceptance, the case would not parallel capital cases, where acceptance is irrelevant, and therefore the Mishnah could not contrast monetary and capital cases in that manner. This answer is subject to two objections. First, according to Rif, in monetary cases that truly parallel capital cases, namely where a Sanhedrin adjudicates, the judges would certainly be exempt from payment, because they are superior even to ordained judges who received authorization from the Nasi, since their greatest member is himself the Nasi, and in addition they are three judges, who are certainly exempt. Second, according to Rif's version of Rav Yosef's answer, the Mishnah saying monetary judgments are reversed is established in a case of a nonexpert. Yet monetary adjudication in the Mishnah necessarily refers to experts. Thus, according to that answer too, monetary cases that parallel capital cases, meaning cases heard by experts, are not reversed. One must therefore say that the Mishnah means only that in capital cases one can never find a case where the judgment is reversed to the defendant's detriment, whereas in monetary cases such reversal can occur, for example with a nonexpert. The same answer could have been given here, and this also refutes Tumim's second answer. In my humble opinion, Rif did not establish the Mishnah here as an expert who was accepted and who is exempt from payment, because the Mishnah there states that monetary cases are heard by three, which necessarily means three ordinary judges, as the Gemara explains earlier. It also means without express acceptance, since if the parties accepted a single judge, one would suffice. The latter clause, concerning reversal, appears to refer back to the first clause. Even according to Rashi's view that Rav Yosef establishes the Mishnah here with an expert and therefore one judge suffices, one can say that three are required for purposes of an admission before them and for receiving testimony, as explained above in siman 3, seif 2. As for the Rosh's second question, from the Gemara's earlier challenge that if three ordinary judges erred perhaps they should not have to pay, to which the Gemara answers that this would all the more lock the door before borrowers, Tumim answers with difficulty. In my humble opinion, the question itself is not difficult, because Tosafot on page 3 already write that the challenge "if they erred they should not pay" does not follow Rav Chisda. The Rosh also asks from the case of Mar Zutra, arguing that a judgment should all the more remain effective where the judge was accepted. Yet the Rosh himself similarly explains that where an expert was accepted the judgment can be reversed when a greater authority is available, whereas without acceptance the greater authority does not reverse it. One can say that according to the Rosh, the continued effect of the judgment is a penalty. It is therefore reasonable to penalize a judge more where the parties did not accept him than where they did. According to Rif, however, the continued effect of the judgment follows from the law itself, not from a penalty, and therefore the Rosh properly asks that, as a matter of law, a judgment should be more readily sustained where the parties accepted the judge. The Rosh further asks on Rif, who explains the cases of Rav Yosef and Rav Nachman to mean that where a greater authority was available, even an expert must pay. Rabbi Akiva, however, was greater than Rabbi Tarfon, yet Rabbi Akiva exempted Rabbi Tarfon because he was an expert. In my humble opinion, Rif's expression "where there is a greater authority" means that at the time of the ruling, in the very place where the judge ruled, there was someone greater than him. He must then pay because he should not have ruled where a greater authority was present. Had he refrained, and the matter been presented to the greater authority, that authority would have ruled correctly and no loss would have resulted. In Rabbi Tarfon's case there was no greater authority present in the city where he ruled who would have permitted the animal. The Mishnah says that the matter was brought before the sages in Yavneh and they permitted it, indicating that in Rabbi Tarfon's city there was no one who would have permitted it. Possibly Rabbi Akiva too was not present there at the time of the ruling. Even had the case been presented to Rabbi Akiva, he may also have declared it prohibited, because only the sages of Yavneh permitted it. Rabbi Tarfon therefore did not cause the loss through the fact that he ruled, since anyone available in that city would have ruled the same way. Surely Rif could not mean that an expert is liable whenever there exists somewhere in the world a greater authority. Rather, he means a greater authority who was present at the time and place of the ruling. There is a further answer. Rif must agree that an expert whom the parties accepted is exempt from payment, as is explicit in Sanhedrin 5, where Rav Yosef told Mar Zutra, "If they accepted you, you need not pay." Since Rav Yosef establishes the Mishnah of "he judged the judgment" with an expert, there is no need for him to establish it specifically where the parties accepted him. Only according to the explanation on Sanhedrin 5 that the Mishnah concerns a nonexpert must one say that the parties accepted him. According to Rav Yosef, who understands the Mishnah as referring to an expert, it can refer to an expert whom the parties did not accept. Therefore an expert whom they did accept is exempt. This also removes the question from Rabbi Tarfon, for Rabbi Tarfon was an expert whom the parties accepted, as explained above. Tumim seeks to support Rif from the rule that a judge is believed to say, "I awarded judgment to this one," where the Gemara asks that he should simply judge the case again. Tumim says that according to Rav Sheshet and Rav Nachman one cannot answer that the judge erred in judicial discretion, but according to Rif there is no difficulty, because if the judge must pay the litigant has no concern, and if the judge does not pay the judgment is reversed. This answer is not correct, for the case can be one where the judge lacks funds with which to pay. In truth, there is no difficulty from there. According to Shach, who writes in seif katan 29 that we say the judgment stands only after the money has actually been transferred, the Gemara cannot be discussing such a case because once the money was transferred and the other party became possessor, the judge's testimony would no longer be needed. Even according to Tur, cited by Shach there, it appears that since the judge would be liable to pay, necessarily because a person is always responsible for damage and the judge is treated as one who caused loss, the judge is not believed to say that he erred and thereby caused damage to another. Even an expert who is exempt because his mistake was unavoidable cannot establish his own unavoidable circumstance merely by assertion, for a claim of unavoidable circumstances is not accepted without proof. In general, a judge is not believed to say "I erred" except where he has a migo, and then only to maintain possession. Thus, the judge is not believed there to say that he erred. Sema and Shach write that the dissenting opinion cited by Rama in the gloss to seif 3 disagrees also with the rule concerning an expert in seif 2. Tumim asks how they know this. Perhaps Rama resolved the dispute and holds that where the litigant would otherwise suffer a loss, the judgment is reversed. There is no difficulty. Rama writes that nowadays, where the community compels judges to serve and the judges therefore do not pay if they err, the judgment nevertheless remains in force. His addition that "nevertheless, they should reverse it" is explained by Shach. It is evident that Rama holds that even where the litigant suffers a loss, the judgment is not automatically reversed. See Shach, seif katan 14. I will explain his words. At the end of his first lettered discussion, where he writes that Rav Chisda too agrees in practice with Rav Sheshet, his meaning is that liability to pay exists even where the judge did not personally take and give the property. Rav Chisda requires the judge personally to transfer the property only for the judgment itself to remain effective. Rav Chisda's language could imply that even liability to pay exists only where the judge personally took and gave, because he strained to establish the case of one who declared the impure item pure as one where the judge himself mixed it with permitted items. Although in the case of one who declared the liable party exempt Rav Chisda had to establish personal transfer in order for the judgment to remain effective, in the case of declaring the impure item pure his need for personal action appears to concern payment alone. Shach therefore explains that Rav Chisda wished to make the Mishnah fit even according to the Rabbis who do not impose liability for indirect causation. Shach then writes, in another lettered section, that this is necessary so that Rif should not appear to have made a grave error. To understand Shach's question and answer one must set out Rif's words. Rif cites one of the Geonim who writes that an expert who erred in judicial discretion and did not personally take and give has a binding judgment and is exempt from payment, and the Gaon derives this from the case of the mouse found upon coins. Rif rejects that proof because the error there was not one of judicial discretion at all. Rif then quotes the Gaon as saying that both an expert and a nonexpert who erred in judicial discretion without personally taking and giving have binding judgments and are exempt from payment. Rif asks that if so, where can one find the Mishnah's case that the judgment is reversed, which Rav Chisda establishes where the judge did not personally take and give? Rif further quotes the Gaon as saying that the Mishnah of "he judged the judgment" was established where the judge personally took and gave only in order not to establish the anonymous Mishnah according to Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for indirect causation. Now that we rule like Rabbi Meir, a nonexpert who erred in judicial discretion is liable even without personally taking and giving if the property cannot practically be recovered, because he caused that person's money to be lost. Rif answers that Rabbi Meir's law of indirect causation applies in ordinary damages but not in this manner to a judge. Rif proves this by arguing that if a judge were liable simply under the rule of indirect causation, then even one who erred in an explicit Mishnah should be liable when the property cannot be recovered. The Gaon's words appear difficult because he first rules that whether expert or nonexpert, if he did not personally transfer the property the judgment stands, yet later he says that where the property can be recovered the implication is that the judgment is reversed. The essential explanation of the Gaon is as Baal HaItur indicates. The Gaon ruled like Rav Sheshet and therefore first wrote that whether expert or nonexpert, where the judge did not personally take and give, what he did remains effective. His later statement that the Mishnah is established where the judge personally took and gave merely so that the Mishnah should not become an anonymous ruling like Rabbi Meir means that the Mishnah in Bekhorot 28, which speaks of "he judged the judgment," is established where the judge personally took and gave only to avoid making it an anonymous ruling according to Rabbi Meir. As to the continued validity of the judgment, it remains valid even without personal transfer. Baal HaItur apparently did not have in the Gaon's text the words, "if the matter is not likely to be restored and clarified." This is why Baal HaItur and Shach ask Rif what his proof is from one who erred in an explicit Mishnah. According to the Gaon's ruling like Rav Sheshet, in a case of judicial discretion the judgment stands and the judge is a direct cause of the loss, whereas in an error in an explicit Mishnah his ruling is legally nothing. Shach answers that Rif follows Tosafot, who explain that the sugya in Sanhedrin saying that one who erred in an explicit Mishnah "did nothing" follows the Rabbis, while according to Rabbi Meir even such an error can constitute actionable indirect causation. Since that judge is nevertheless exempt, the reason must be that a judge is treated as having acted under unavoidable circumstances. If so, the same should apply where he erred in judicial discretion, even though the judgment stands, because according to Rabbi Meir there is no difference for liability between indirect causation and a case where the judgment stands and the judge is treated as having caused the loss. This explanation itself is difficult. If Rif follows Tosafot that the statement "he did nothing" is only according to the Rabbis, then how can Rif challenge the Gaon from an error in an explicit Mishnah? According to Rabbi Meir, such a judge would in fact be liable. According to our version of Rif, however, where the Gaon says, "if the matter is not likely to be restored," Rif's challenge is sound. According to that version, the Gaon agrees that even in judicial discretion the judgment is reversed where the money can in practice be restored, and only when restoration is impossible is the judge liable. The Gaon's position is then that Rav Chisda's requirement of personal transfer applies only according to the Rabbis, who exempt a judge from payment in the absence of personal action. Since otherwise the litigant would suffer a loss, the judgment is reversed. According to Rabbi Meir, however, who obligates the judge even without personal transfer, the judgment remains in force even without personal transfer because the litigant suffers no uncompensated loss. The passage beginning "rather, where he erred in an explicit Mishnah everyone agrees he is exempt" should be placed at the end of the previous lettered section, as I have explained. Shach later asks on Tosafot, who distinguish an expert with ordination from an expert without ordination. If so, when the Gemara challenges from the latter clause, "if he was an expert," why does it not answer that the Mishnah concerns an expert without ordination? There is no difficulty. Tosafot distinguish between an ordained expert and a nonordained expert only where the expert was not accepted by the parties. A nonordained expert was penalized so that his judgment remains effective, whereas an ordained expert was not. Where the parties accepted the expert, there is no basis for a penalty at all, because through acceptance he did not act presumptuously. The judgment is therefore reversed even for a nonordained expert. Acceptance is superior to authorization, and authorization is superior to ordination, as Shach writes in seif katan 29. Thus, when the Gemara says, "if they accepted you, you need not pay," the judgment is in fact reversed, as Tumim also writes. There is a scribal error in Shach there. It should read, "Tosafot and Rashi do not disagree," and so forth. Shach further writes that were his judgment not valid, why would the judge not have to pay, since the parties could say, "We accepted him only on condition that he judge according to Torah law." According to what I wrote above, that the main reason for exempting an expert is that his error is considered unavoidable, this argument has no place, as is further explained in Shach, seif katan 30. Regarding Shach's statement that with three ordinary judges who were accepted, even a greater authority does not reverse their judgment, this is certainly correct, as Tumim writes. Otherwise one could never find a case where the judgment of three ordinary judges remains effective, since there will always be someone greater than they. Shach's reason, that they lack the capacity to understand the greater authority's reasoning, appears to mean that the principal matter depends upon the judges themselves recognizing their error and retracting. Since they cannot comprehend the greater authority's words, they do not themselves retract. This remains somewhat strained. The better reason is that the principal basis Shach gives for exempting an expert who was accepted is that acceptance serves to exempt him. Yet with an expert, even where the parties did not expressly say, "We accept you," simple appearance before him is sufficient, as explained above. What additional acceptance is there? One must say, as Shach writes in seif katan 30, that ordinary acceptance of an expert is understood as acceptance both for a correct judgment and for an error. But if the fact that acceptance is an additional act is what implies acceptance of error for purposes of exemption from payment, then it should likewise imply that the judgment itself remains valid and cannot be reversed by a greater authority, as is evident from Sema, seif katan 24, regarding one who was accepted "for judgment and for error." Therefore, as I wrote above, with an expert the acceptance is not an additional act, because it is needed so that an admission before him counts as an admission. His exemption from payment derives instead from the Rosh's reason that his error is treated as unavoidable. With three ordinary judges, however, their acceptance is certainly an additional act, as the Rosh writes, and consequently it is understood as acceptance for both correct judgment and error. Their judgment therefore remains effective and a greater authority does not reverse it. Shach's language that they are unable to comprehend the greater authority's words also seems to imply that an expert's later realization that the general practice follows the other view does not itself constitute an effective retraction. If merely not knowing the generally accepted course initially and then learning it were itself considered an error, what role would there be for their capacity to understand the greater authority? No special understanding is needed merely to discover what the accepted practice is. Rashi's language regarding a nonexpert, that he can say, "Why do you think the later reasoning is better?" is likewise difficult, because one can ascertain how the general practice runs. Rather, the case must be that the judge initially ruled on the basis of some reasoning or proof that favored one opinion. This must be so particularly where he obligated the party who would otherwise prevail by possession. If he knew only that authorities disagreed and knew of no accepted practice, on what basis could he extract money? Necessarily he had some reason for preferring the position he followed. Later it became clear that his proof was mistaken, together with the fact that general practice followed the opposite view, or after discovering the general practice he also found a proof favoring the other view. This is called an error in judicial discretion. If the only "error" was that he had not known the accepted practice, it would not constitute an error requiring reversal, because where the law was never expressly decided one way or the other, one who follows either authority has acted validly. This is what appears correct to me, although the authorities imply that even an error caused solely by ignorance of the generally accepted course is called an error in judicial discretion. There are several scribal errors in that discussion of Shach. In the section corresponding to letter 10 it should read: "This is our ruling, that the greater authority does not reverse it." At the beginning of the earlier section it should read: "Nevertheless, regarding this point, where Baal HaMaor holds that if there is a greater authority he reverses it, the law appears to follow him," and the words "and they are exempt from payment" should be deleted. Shach later writes, based on Rashi, that although in monetary law a judgment does not become fixed merely by oral pronouncement unless money was already transferred, in matters of prohibition and permission the ruling can become fixed by speech alone. When he adds that this is also evident from the Gemara, his meaning is that if a ruling did not become fixed by speech in such matters, then even if we said that the judge could not formally retract, were the matter still before us the original ruling would have no operative effect. The Gemara therefore shows that in prohibition and permission, the ruling itself takes effect through speech. Shach's rejection of Baal HaMaor's rationale based upon negligence of the litigant has the following meaning. According to Baal HaMaor, the Gemara gives no proof that a ruling in prohibition and permission becomes fixed by speech. One could say that the ruling never becomes fixed until some act is done, just as in monetary law transfer is required. Nevertheless, if we say that an error in an explicit Mishnah is not reversed, Rabbi Tarfon's concern is understandable. If the judgment would be reversed and his declaration were legally nothing, then when the owner fed the animal to dogs he negligently fed an animal that was still legally permitted, and the judge could be exempt because the owner caused his own loss. If, however, the judge's ruling is legally effective but remains retractable only until an act occurs, then once the owner acts, the ruling becomes fixed and it is as if the animal is retroactively established as prohibited. The owner who fed it to the dogs then fed them something that, under the operative ruling, was truly prohibited. The judge who expressly declared it prohibited cannot then be exempted merely by saying that the owner was negligent, because the owner's act concerned an animal that had become legally prohibited through the judgment. Shach then cites Ran to show likewise that the ruling in prohibition and permission takes effect immediately by speech. Shach further writes that since the Mishnah in Bekhorot is established where the judge personally took and gave, this shows that it follows the Rabbis. The meaning is not that Rav Chisda would never need personal transfer according to Rabbi Meir, since he could still require it for the judgment itself to remain effective. Rather, once the first clause of the Mishnah, "what he did is done," necessarily concerns personal transfer, the latter clause, "and he must pay," must also mean that liability exists only where the judge personally took and gave. That follows the Rabbis. If the Mishnah followed Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability even without personal transfer when the property cannot be restored, the Mishnah should have taught the greater novelty that liability can exist even without personal transfer. Since both clauses describe one factual setting, the implication is that payment cannot be required without personal transfer, which is the Rabbis' view. I will now explain the words of my grandfather, the great Chacham Tzvi, who sought to resolve Shach's question on Rama in Yoreh Deah, siman 242. Rama there writes that in an error of judicial discretion a second sage may reverse the first sage's ruling. Shach asks that Rama here rules like Rav Sheshet, according to whom even where the judge did not personally transfer the money, what he did remains effective and even a greater authority does not reverse it. Chacham Tzvi answers that although the judgment is not reversed even without personal transfer, some operative act is nevertheless required. So long as no act has been done, the ruling can be reversed. Here the case is one where the money has already been transferred, and therefore Rama rules that even a greater authority cannot reverse the judgment. In Yoreh Deah the case is one where no act has yet been done, and therefore Rama rules that the greater authority may reverse it. Tumim challenges this from the Gemara's statement that Rabbi Akiva gave Rabbi Tarfon two answers, one that he had erred in an explicit Mishnah and another that even had he erred in judicial discretion, he was an expert and therefore exempt. The implication is that without his expert status he would have been liable. According to Chacham Tzvi, however, because no act had yet been done, the ruling could have been reversed and he should have been exempt since, had the matter still been before us, it would have been reversible. To resolve this, one must first reconcile apparently contradictory statements of Raavad. Rosh cites Raavad in Sanhedrin as holding that even a greater authority does not reverse a judgment. Ran, however, cites Raavad in Avodah Zarah as saying that in matters of prohibition and permission the ruling stands, whereas in monetary matters a colleague more expert than the first judge may reverse the ruling and award the money to the other party. The reconciliation is that the passage in Avodah Zarah concerns a stage before any operative act was done. Only then does Raavad distinguish: in prohibition and permission the ruling is already fixed because the object has been rendered prohibited, whereas in monetary law a more expert colleague can reverse the ruling. The sugya in Sanhedrin concerning the dispute among Rav Yosef, Rav Nachman, and Rav Sheshet likewise concerns the stage before an operative act. Rav Yosef holds that specifically where a nonexpert was accepted, he cannot retract before any act, because he had no authority to judge except through the parties' acceptance, and after he ruled, who appointed him again to reopen the matter and reverse his original decision? An expert, by contrast, who may judge alone, can reconsider his own ruling. Rav Nachman holds that specifically a greater authority may reverse it. Where there is no greater authority, the first judge can say, "Why is the later reasoning better?" A greater authority may reverse it, and the first judge acknowledges his error, for the essential point is that the original judge himself recognizes and retracts his error, as is evident from Shach's explanation that three ordinary judges cannot be reversed by a greater authority because they do not comprehend his reasoning. Rav Sheshet holds that even a greater authority cannot reverse a judgment based upon judicial discretion, even before any operative act. Once an act has occurred, however, Raavad holds on logical grounds that everyone agrees a greater authority cannot reverse the judgment, for the reason given by Rosh: since the Gemara never definitively ruled like one side or the other, possession created under the judgment cannot be disturbed merely because another authority favors the contrary view. Chacham Tzvi's resolution of Rama is therefore correct. In Yoreh Deah, siman 242, Rama follows Ran, who disagrees with Raavad's distinction between prohibition and monetary law at the preact stage, and holds that in prohibition and permission too a later sage may reverse the ruling before action. Here, where Rama states that even a greater authority cannot reverse the judgment, the case is after the money has been transferred. Before transfer, he may in fact hold that a greater authority can reverse it, while an equal authority cannot, because he follows Rav Nachman. The rationale of Rav Nachman is that an equal cannot say the later reasoning is better, but a greater authority can reverse it. In matters of prohibition and permission, however, where one witness is believed and the concern of possession does not operate in the same manner, the original sage can retract even without a greater authority. The Gemara's question concerning Rabbi Tarfon follows Rav Sheshet, who holds that even a greater authority cannot reverse a ruling based on judicial discretion before an act. According to him, the ruling takes effect by speech itself, even in prohibition and permission, and the Gemara's question is therefore sound. Ketzot HaChoshen asks on Shach, who writes that Rav Chisda's explanation involving a judge who touched the item with a creeping creature is according to the Rabbis. He asks what the additional touching accomplishes if the judgment already stands, and if the judge touched the item before ruling then he is not acting as a judge but as an ordinary tortfeasor. This is the same question Tosafot raise in Bava Kamma 99. As for Ketzot's statement that such a person is not a judge but merely a tortfeasor, I do not understand it, because the case can certainly be where he touched the item with the creeping creature immediately after his ruling, within the time of immediate retraction.
And three ordinary judges. See Shach, seif katan 15, who at length cites authorities who disagree with this ruling. In my humble opinion, the principal position is as Tumim writes, harmonizing the authorities. If the parties expressly accepted the judges and said, "You are accepted upon us," the judges are certainly exempt. The Gemara on page 3 asks even concerning three ordinary judges, "If they err, should they not have to pay?" and answers that exempting them would all the more lock the door before borrowers. If they are exempt only where the parties expressly accepted them, the concern of locking the door does not arise, because no one can compel litigants to make such an express acceptance. Where the parties merely came before them for judgment without any express statement, however, the judges are certainly liable, and the authorities who impose liability speak of such a case. The Rosh's rationale does not apply there. The proof is that the claimant always comes voluntarily to judgment. No one compelled him. If mere appearance were treated as acceptance sufficient to exempt the judges, the Gemara's answer that otherwise one would lock the door before borrowers would be difficult. To distinguish between claimant and defendant, as Tumim does, is very strained. Had the law depended upon such a distinction, the authorities should not have written their rulings without qualification.
