The judgment is reversed, etc. It is obvious that where the judge did not personally take and give, according to this opinion, which is Rif and Rambam, the ruling is reversed because we rule like Rav Chisda that where the judge did not personally transfer the property the judgment returns. But even where the judge personally took and gave, so that with an ordinary judge the ruling would generally stand under the Mishnah's rule "what he did is done," nevertheless, if the judge is a recognized expert and is exempt from payment, the judgment is reversed whenever reversal remains possible. Chazal did this to prevent loss to the litigant. Sema and Shach write that Rama's later citation in seif 3 of Tosafot and Rosh, who disagree and hold that an error in judicial discretion can remain standing even where the judge did not personally take and give, necessarily disputes this present law as well. Rama does not write "some disagree" here because he relies upon the later gloss. Shach criticizes this drafting, saying Rama should have cited the dispute here explicitly rather than state Mechaber's rule without qualification. One could defend Ir Shushan by saying the two disputes are not logically dependent. Even if Tosafot and Rosh disagree with Rif about whether Rav Chisda's rule is accepted generally, they might still accept Rif's separate policy principle that where the judge is exempt from payment, Chazal reverse the ruling if possible to avoid loss to the litigant. At first glance these are distinct questions. Therefore Rama may cite the dissent later concerning Rav Chisda while still accepting the present policy-based rule. It is true that Rosh himself connects the two. After quoting Rif's rule that because the judge is exempt the judgment is reversed to prevent loss, Rosh writes that "because of this Rif was forced" into that position by ruling like Rav Chisda. Rosh then attacks the policy reasoning and ultimately rejects Rav Chisda's rule. This indicates that in Rosh's view the policy of reversing the judgment where the judge is exempt is not an independent doctrine. It is part of Rif's broader structure. Still, one should understand why Rosh thought the two matters connected. At first glance there is no conceptual reason why rejection of Rav Chisda must entail rejection of the policy. Perhaps the link is this. What rational distinction exists between a judge who personally took and gave and one who merely ordered the losing litigant to transfer the property, if the question is whether the judgment itself stands? For liability, the distinction is clear. Personal taking is an act; mere direction can be only indirect causation. But regarding the legal force of the judgment, why should the form of transfer matter? Rif's answer is that the real determinant is financial loss. Where the judge is liable to reimburse, there is no need to reverse the judgment because the injured litigant can recover from the judge. Where the judge is exempt, Chazal reverse the ruling so that the litigant does not lose. Thus personal taking and giving matters only because it determines the judge's liability. If that policy principle is rejected, there is little reason to preserve Rav Chisda's distinction at all. Ramban, however, gives a different conceptual reason. Where the judge himself took and gave, the judgment has become strengthened through concrete implementation and therefore "stands." Where the judge merely spoke and the transfer was not judicially executed by him, the judgment is weaker and remains reversible. On Ramban's account, Rav Chisda need not depend on Rif's loss-prevention policy. So the connection is not inevitable. Why then did Rosh say Rif was "forced" into his policy? Because Rosh saw a strong difficulty from the anonymous Gemara in Bava Kamma and Bechorot. The Gemara initially seeks to infer from the Mishnah "he judged, and what he did is done, and he pays from his own property" that it follows R. Meir's dina d'garmei. Rav answers that even the Sages can agree where the judge personally took and gave. This anonymous discussion appears not to follow Rav Chisda's rule that without personal taking the judgment itself reverses, because if it did, the distinction would already be necessary simply to explain why "what he did is done." Rif therefore needed a way to reconcile the anonymous sugya with Rav Chisda and still rule like Rav Chisda. His loss-prevention theory accomplishes this: the reversal depends on whether the judge is exempt, while the payment rule can be separately established through taking and giving. Rosh attacks that reconciliation and therefore concludes that the anonymous sugya itself favors the other Amoraim over Rav Chisda. This explains why Rosh saw the matters as connected. Yet the logical implication still runs more strongly in one direction than the other. If one rejects Rif's loss-prevention policy, the basis for ruling like Rav Chisda becomes weaker. But even if one rejects Rav Chisda generally, one could still independently accept the proposition that an exempt expert judge should not cause an irreparable loss if reversal is possible. That is why Ir Shushan's defense of Rama is not impossible. Nevertheless, Rosh explicitly rejects Rif's policy and writes that "the mind cannot tolerate these words," raising several objections. Therefore according to Rosh and Tosafot the present ruling is indeed disputed. One must then ask why Rama did not say so here. The answer may simply be that he relied upon his later general citation of the contrary approach. In any event, there is a deeper point concerning the famous objections Rosh raises against Rif. I am unworthy to answer our great master Rosh. Yet out of respect for Rif, master of the entire Talmud, it is proper to examine whether his position can be defended rather than dismiss it in a few words. Rosh asks first: if the Mishnah says civil judgments are reversed in some cases, why not establish that Mishnah as a case of a recognized expert who is exempt from paying, rather than introduce Rav Chisda's distinction between taking and giving and not taking and giving? There are two answers. First, an expert judge without acceptance is not necessarily exempt. If he did not have valid acceptance, the civil case would still resemble the capital case in the Mishnah only imperfectly. Capital law contains no analogous voluntary acceptance by the litigants. Second, the capital case plainly speaks before any irreversible act was performed, because otherwise how can the judgment be reversed? The civil half should therefore be read in a parallel procedural stage, before actual implementation. If so, one cannot answer simply that the civil judge was an expert whose personal transfer already occurred, because then the two halves would not be analogous. Rav Chisda's distinction is needed. Rosh also asks from Sanhedrin's rule that three lay judges who err must pay in order to avoid "closing the door before borrowers." If according to Rif a judge who is exempt simply has his judgment reversed, what danger of closing the door exists? The creditor ultimately loses nothing. The answer is that where judges know they are personally liable if they err, they will be exceptionally careful in their initial deliberation. If they are exempt and merely know that an erroneous ruling can later be reversed, they may not examine the matter with equal precision. Who will always discover every judicial mistake? Perhaps the creditor will leave the country, the debtor will dissipate assets, or practical correction will later become impossible. A lender will hesitate to lend if judicial mistakes are common and no judge bears responsibility. Imposing payment therefore promotes careful adjudication and prevents the closing of credit. Rosh's third objection is from the Gemara's advice: "One who wishes to judge and, if he errs, not pay, should obtain authorization from the Exilarch." Rosh asks: under Rif, authorization may cause the judgment itself to be reversed. What kind of useful protection is this? But the judge's purpose in seeking authorization is precisely to avoid personal payment if he makes an error. That protection is fully achieved. Indeed, the fact that the judgment may then be corrected is a benefit, not a defect. The judge is spared personal liability and an unjust ruling does not remain in force. Perhaps Rosh means that if the judge's concern is simply to avoid an erroneous judgment, he could already refrain from personally taking and giving and thereby preserve reversibility according to Rav Chisda. Why obtain formal authorization? The answer is that a judge may value the authority and stability of his judgments. Yet financial protection remains independently worthwhile. Moreover, the authorization protects him in circumstances where the precise procedural classification may be uncertain. There is even some support for Rif from the sugya concerning a judge's credibility after judgment. The Mishnah says a judge is believed to identify whom he found liable and whom he found entitled while the parties are still before him, but later he is not believed. The Gemara asks: let him simply return and judge the case again. It answers that the case involves shuda d'dayanei. Why not answer instead that the judge had previously erred in judicial discretion? If the ruling had already been issued, it would stand; if he now rejudged the case with the error exposed, the previously prevailing party might lose. The Gemara does not give that answer. According to Rif, however, the omission is understandable. If the judge would personally have to pay for the first mistaken ruling, then the litigant suffers no ultimate loss and there is no special significance to the disagreement. If the judge is exempt, Rif says the judgment itself is reversed where possible. Thus a case of ordinary judicial error would not create the unique irreversibility needed to answer the Gemara. Shuda does. This makes Rif's approach highly coherent. I therefore do not regard Rosh's objections as sufficient to dismiss Rif's position. Although the practical halachah among the authorities remains disputed, the reasoning of Rif is powerful and worthy of full respect.
And three lay judges have the same law as one recognized expert. If the litigants accepted them, their law is like that of an expert whom the litigants accepted, according to Rambam in his approach, and according to Rosh and Tosafot in theirs. This is the view of Rosh and Tur. Shach, seif katan 15, argues at length against them and favors a responsum of Maharam, Prague edition, which holds that acceptance does not exempt three lay judges from payment when they err. I have not found among the authorities Shach cites a solid basis to overturn Tur and Rosh. Shach first infers that Rif agrees with Maharam because Rif enumerates only five categories rather than a sixth separate category of three lay judges accepted by the parties. In my view this proves the reverse. If Rif held like Maharam, he would have needed to list a distinct case: three lay judges may adjudicate even coercively, yet if they err they must pay despite acceptance. That rule is nowhere stated among Rif's existing categories. Rif explicitly rules concerning a single recognized expert who may judge coercively that if the parties accept him, the acceptance exempts him from payment. If three lay judges, who likewise can judge coercively, remained liable even after acceptance, this would require separate treatment. Rif's omission therefore indicates that he treats the two cases alike. The general principle is that anyone who can judge coercively even without acceptance becomes exempt from payment if the litigants voluntarily accept him. Acceptance adds the waiver of the judges' personal liability. Thus one recognized expert and three lay judges have the same structure. Bedek HaBayit likewise writes this. Shach's principal support comes from Baal HaMaor. Baal HaMaor describes a court lacking a full expert, for example three where one is learned but not fully reasoned and has not received authorization from the Exilarch, and writes that a person who is not sufficiently qualified can still be liable even where the litigants accepted him. Shach reads this as applying to the three-person panel. I do not think that is Baal HaMaor's meaning. His phrase "one who is not fully reasoned even though the litigants accepted him" concerns an individual judge, not a panel of three. This is clear from the immediately following alternative: "or one who is learned and reasoned but the litigants did not accept him." That certainly refers to a single expert judge. Likewise, his proof from the Mishnah "he judged the case and pays from his own property" concerns an individual judge, as the Gemara's challenge to R. Abahu makes explicit. It therefore cannot directly prove that three lay judges remain liable despite acceptance. Baal HaMaor's discussion is instead structured by analogy. Do not think that because three lay judges may adjudicate coercively they are automatically exempt if they err. We see that an individual insufficient expert may be liable despite some accepted authority, and an individual full expert may be liable absent acceptance. From these cases we learn that mere authority to judge does not automatically remove monetary responsibility. Thus, three lay judges who judge without acceptance can still owe if they err. That is Baal HaMaor's point. At the end he asks, "How do we know that even three, where one of them is learned but not fully reasoned, have the law of an unexpert beit din?" This shows that his earlier proofs concerned individuals and only afterward does he extend the principle to three. Had he already been speaking of three throughout, this later transition would make no sense. Moreover, when he finally describes the three-person panel he does not mention acceptance at all. If his great novelty were that even accepted three lay judges must pay, that essential fact should have appeared explicitly. His proofs from the rule that judges must pay to prevent closing the door before borrowers, and from how the court writes allocation of liability among judges, likewise establish liability where three judges acted without voluntary acceptance. They prove nothing about accepted judges. Baal HaItur uses these very sources only for three judges acting without acceptance. In a later section he writes explicitly that three judges, whether expert or nonexpert, whom the litigants accepted are exempt when they err in judicial discretion. He does not report Baal HaMaor as disagreeing, though elsewhere he regularly notes disputes with Baal HaMaor. This strongly supports my reading. Ramban in Milchamot also argues against Baal HaMaor's imposition of liability as dina d'garmei by saying: the judge does not act on his own. Either the parties accepted him, or, if they did not, he is a qualified judge, or three judges include one who is sufficiently learned, and the claimant is crying before them to adjudicate. They are performing a great mitzvah and are compelled by the Torah's command to judge. Notice that regarding the expert individual and the panel of three, Ramban emphasizes the claimant's demand for judgment, not a situation where both litigants voluntarily accepted the panel. If Baal HaMaor held that even full voluntary acceptance of three lay judges leaves them liable, Ramban had an even stronger objection available: how can their conduct be actionable causation when both parties freely asked them to adjudicate? Ramban does not say this because Baal HaMaor was not discussing accepted three-person courts. Thus Baal HaMaor provides no support for Shach's reading. Shach also cites the Yerushalmi, which explains an individual judge's liability by saying that he arrogantly undertook to judge alone. This implies that where three judges sit, and the litigants accept them, the rationale of arrogance does not apply and they should be exempt. Shach tries to avoid the inference by saying the Yerushalmi treats dina d'garmei as a penalty and therefore follows a special punitive theory not accepted by us. But Shach himself below in siman 388 rules like Ritzba that dina d'garmei is indeed grounded in a form of penalty. If so, the Yerushalmi's logic cannot simply be dismissed. In any event, the natural rule is symmetrical. Either an accepted court falls into the category of exemption like an accepted expert, or, if not, one would need a clear source for imposing payment on all such courts. Shach seeks additional proof from Rashi in Sanhedrin 3. Rashi infers that three lay judges who err must pay because the Gemara says recognized experts are exempt. Shach says the passage itself concerns an expert accepted by the parties, implying that by contrast three lay judges remain liable even with acceptance. This inference is not sound. If three lay judges who adjudicate without acceptance were exempt, the Mishnah or baraita should have stated the greater novelty: three judges are exempt even without voluntary acceptance. Then certainly an individual expert who both possesses compulsory authority and was affirmatively accepted would be exempt. But if three lay judges are exempt only when accepted, there is no reason the source had to choose them rather than the single expert. The two are parallel cases. Indeed it is natural to formulate the ruling around an individual expert because the opening clause "he judged the case" itself discusses an individual judge. The source thereby teaches that even an individual expert does not incur liability merely because he judged alone, despite the general advice not to sit in judgment alone. This explains the choice of wording without implying any distinction from three accepted lay judges. There is even a positive inference in favor of Rosh and Tur. The Gemara in Sanhedrin 6 challenges R. Abahu's view that two who judged do not produce a valid judgment from the Mishnah "he judged the case and pays from his own property." The Gemara assumes the Mishnah concerns an individual because it uses singular language, though singular formulations are common in Mishnah and need not always exclude a panel. A better conceptual basis is that the Gemara treats three lay judges and one recognized expert as one legal class with respect to authority and liability. If the Mishnah's latter clause exempts an accepted expert, the corresponding case of three accepted lay judges is likewise exempt. Thus the first clause of liability must concern a truly unqualified individual. The Gemara's structure is coherent precisely because one expert and three lay judges share the same general rule. I therefore regard the practical law as clear in accordance with Rif, Ramban, Baal HaItur, Rosh, and Tur, and, as explained, Baal HaMaor need not disagree. Three lay judges who may ordinarily adjudicate coercively but who are voluntarily accepted by the parties are exempt from payment for a mistake in the same manner as an accepted recognized expert, subject to the different broader systems of Rambam versus Tosafot and Rosh concerning whether the judgment itself stands or is reversed. There remains, however, another question: what counts as "acceptance"? Is it enough that the litigants came voluntarily before the judges without coercion, or must they explicitly say, "We accept upon ourselves whatever you rule"? Shach, seif katan 12, cites several authorities that voluntary appearance itself counts as acceptance. The Rif text "if they called you, go and do not pay" likewise suggests mere summons or voluntary resort is enough. The incident of R. Tarfon's cow also seems so. The owner simply came to ask him the halachic status of the animal. No formal verbal waiver is recorded, yet this is treated as acceptance of an expert's ruling. At first glance, however, Rosh's reasoning concerning three lay judges suggests a more explicit acceptance. Rosh writes that acceptance benefits them and exempts them from payment, because even without acceptance their judgment would be valid, and the added acceptance operates to free them from liability. This sounds like a distinct additional act. If "acceptance" merely means that the litigants came voluntarily instead of waiting for the court to coerce them, what significant additional waiver exists? Why should a person be penalized because he behaved properly, came willingly to a Jewish beit din, and did not wait until officers compelled him? It seems unreasonable that by honoring the court and appearing voluntarily he automatically forfeits his right to reimbursement if the judges err. This suggests that for three lay judges there must be an explicit verbal acceptance of their ruling. Initially I considered distinguishing an individual recognized expert from three lay judges. For an expert, ordinary voluntary resort can count as acceptance. Rif's wording and the case of R. Tarfon support this. Since an expert is not ordinarily expected to err, Rosh explains the exemption not primarily as waiver but by saying the litigant's own mazal caused the unusual mistake. For three lay judges, however, error is more foreseeable. Rosh therefore relies upon the parties' actual acceptance, and perhaps that requires explicit language. This would also reconcile Maharam and Rosh. Maharam's case could involve only ordinary voluntary appearance. In that situation three lay judges remain liable. Rosh's exemption could involve full verbal acceptance of whatever they decide. Each authority would then address a different case. Still, the distinction is difficult because Rosh explicitly compares three accepted lay judges to a single accepted expert. If the very meaning of acceptance differs, the analogy is imperfect. Another explanation of Rosh is therefore preferable. With three lay judges, the Gemara itself suggests that their liability is not the natural baseline. The Gemara asks why three lay judges who err should pay. Rashi says one might have treated them like experts. Tosafot say otherwise lenders may be unable to find judges willing to adjudicate. The Gemara answers that payment is imposed to prevent closing the door before borrowers. Thus absent the special policy of protecting credit, there is substantial reason to exempt them. When the litigants appear voluntarily, the "closing the door" concern is weaker. A debtor who fears judicial error need not voluntarily accept this particular panel. He can insist on ordinary compulsory process. Once both sides willingly submit, the special policy reason for imposing liability recedes, and the underlying exemption returns. In that sense, even ordinary voluntary acceptance can be sufficient without a formal verbal waiver. This does not apply the same way to a single expert. A single expert who judges without acceptance is liable under the ordinary rule because he chose to sit alone, and the Gemara never suggests an institutional reason to exempt him simply to preserve access to courts. Hence Rosh needs the separate explanation that where the expert was voluntarily accepted and nevertheless made an unusual error, the litigant's mazal caused it. This resolves the differing rationales while preserving one ordinary meaning of acceptance. Even so, a distinction between plaintiff and defendant remains necessary. For the defendant, voluntary appearance before the judges can readily count as acceptance. No one forced him to come before receiving a summons and compulsory order. If he feared their lack of expertise, he could have waited for proper judicial compulsion or requested another valid process. For the plaintiff, however, I do not understand how merely initiating suit before beit din can itself waive the judges' liability. A plaintiff has no alternative but to come to beit din to seek judgment. Without his complaint the court will never summon the defendant. He has done nothing extra merely by filing his claim. Why should the defendant's voluntary appearance diminish the plaintiff's separate right to reimbursement if the judges later err against him? It is strained to say that because the defendant voluntarily accepted the court and would therefore be unable to recover if the judges erred against him, equality requires the same rule against the plaintiff. The plaintiff has no benefit from the judge's exemption toward the defendant. Why should he be deemed to waive his own rights because of another party's choice? Therefore I am inclined to distinguish. For the defendant, ordinary voluntary appearance can function as acceptance sufficient to exempt three lay judges from payment. For the plaintiff, who necessarily had to initiate the proceeding, one should require a more explicit acceptance if the judges seek exemption from liability. This may be how Maharam's case should be understood: the plaintiff merely brought the matter before them in the ordinary way, and that alone did not amount to a waiver. This distinction requires further consideration, but it appears the most coherent practical understanding.
Practice it as prohibited. Shach cites Bach that the same applies where a city had a practice to be stringent based on the ruling of one sage. After that sage dies, another sage who rules leniently may permit the practice. In my view this requires study. Tosafot, Rosh, and Ran at the beginning of Perek Makom Shenahagu imply that when a restrictive practice was established under the direction of a sage, it cannot simply be permitted in the presence of those who already accepted it. They have taken the prohibition upon themselves through a practice founded upon rabbinic authority. See Pri Chadash to Orach Chaim siman 468, who discusses this at length, and see what I write there. Bach proves his ruling from Perek Keitzad Mishtatfin. R. Yosi forbids a suspended partition on Shabbat. The Gemara asks how the people of Tzippori permitted carrying by means of such a suspended partition when R. Yosi had been the leading authority there. It answers that the incident occurred after R. Yosi's death, under R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi, who permitted it. Bach infers that although they had previously followed R. Yosi's stringency, after his death another sage could permit the matter. But there is no proof. During R. Yosi's lifetime no actual case involving carrying by a suspended partition had occurred in Tzippori. There had therefore been no established practice at all. The Gemara's initial difficulty was simply: how could such an incident of leniency have occurred in R. Yosi's lifetime when he himself ruled stringently? The answer is that the practical question did not arise until after R. Yosi died. At that time the matter was first presented to R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi, who was among those who permitted. That is entirely different from a community that already practiced stringency by the ruling of its sage. Where the restrictive practice has actually been established, a later sage should not simply undo it, unless the original ruling rested on an obvious and demonstrable error in law or similar clear mistake. Then the original acceptance was itself mistaken, as Tosafot, Ran, and Rashba explain in Perek Makom Shenahagu. Therefore Bach's general ruling requires study.
