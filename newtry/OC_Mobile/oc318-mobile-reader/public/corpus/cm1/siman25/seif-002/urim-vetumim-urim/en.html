Or Amoraim. The same applies to a dispute among the poskim in our own times. Shach.
And he did not know that the practice had already become settled, etc. He knew of the underlying dispute, but did not know that the law had already been accepted in accordance with one side. If, however, he did not know at all that a second authority disagreed and knew only one opinion, and he ruled in accordance with that opinion, the Sema, citing Hagahos Maimoniyos and the Rosh, holds that what he did stands and he need not pay from his own funds. The Shach cites those who disagree and inclines to their view, because an error arising from insufficient study is treated as culpable. It appears that where he could have known, it is certainly an error. Rabbi Tarfon's error concerning an animal whose uterus had been removed likewise arose from a lack of knowledge, as do most errors mentioned in the Gemara. But where he had no reasonable way to know, such as where an earlier work had not become available in his time or city, this is plainly an unavoidable circumstance. What he did stands, but he is exempt from payment. Where there is no formal dispute and the law is not explicit, but common judicial practice follows one approach based on reasoning or proof from a Gemara passage, and he rules the opposite, the Shach concludes that this is an error in an explicit rule. This is correct.
Throughout the world. This does not literally mean throughout the entire world, but throughout the relevant region. For example, in the Rhine region they treat Rabbeinu Yoel's view concerning eating the rumen as controlling, while in Egypt they rule generally in accordance with Rambam.
But they accepted him, etc. No kinyan is required for this acceptance. If the parties voluntarily, without compulsion, came before him to adjudicate and he judged them, that constitutes acceptance for this purpose. Shach. See above siman 22, where I discussed the views of Rashba and Ran that with otherwise qualified judges, once the litigants begin presenting their arguments this itself constitutes acceptance.
Since he is an expert, etc. The status of expert exists even today where a person is deeply accomplished in Gemara and poskim, reasons correctly, is experienced in adjudication, and has not been found to err. Although Rama wrote above, siman 3 seif 2, that today we do not apply the law of a publicly recognized expert, that was said regarding his authority to sit as a sole judge. With respect to liability for an erroneous judgment, expert status applies even today. He must, however, be known and publicly recognized as an expert, as the phrase "expert for the public" indicates. His own assertion, "I am an expert," is not enough. Shach, and this is correct.
The judgment is reversed. This is obvious where the judge did not personally take and transfer the property, because the Mechaber, following Rif and Rambam, holds that even a nonexpert's judgment is reversed in that case, as stated below in seif 3. Rather, even where he personally took and transferred it, because this expert judge is exempt from payment the judgment is voided to prevent loss to the litigant. Below in seif 3 Rama cites a dissenting opinion that even without personal transfer the judgment stands and the judge pays. Whether that dissenting view also disagrees here, where the judge is an expert and is exempt from payment, so that the judgment would nevertheless stand and the litigant suffer the loss, is discussed in Tumim. Rama's language is not decisive. The Levush therefore did not cite a dissent here. Nevertheless, the approach of the Shach and Sema is reasonable, namely that Rama understands the dissent below to disagree here as well. See Tumim, where I explained that the Rif's reasoning here has substance and cannot lightly be dismissed. The Levush therefore states this rule without qualification as practical halachah.
And three lay judges, etc. Their acceptance has the same effect as acceptance of a single expert, each authority following his own approach: Rif and Rambam according to theirs, Tosafos and the Rosh according to theirs. The Rosh and Tur therefore treat the cases alike. The Shach argues at length against them, but in Tumim I wrote that the primary view is that of Tosafos and the Rosh. I remain uncertain, however, regarding the precise form of acceptance: whether it suffices that both litigants come before them voluntarily without summons or compulsion, or whether they must expressly say, "We accept you upon ourselves to judge us according to Torah law." In Tumim I distinguished in this regard between claimant and defendant. These "three lay judges" means that at least one among them has learned the law but lacks refined judicial reasoning, for such a panel can adjudicate by compulsion. Their acceptance therefore serves to exempt them from payment if they err. If none among them is even learned, they cannot adjudicate at all without acceptance. Acceptance gives them authority to judge, but they remain liable if they err. Shach and Bach.
A person should not say, "I will rule according to whichever opinion I choose," etc. This concerns a matter where common practice has not adopted one side. If common practice follows one side, ruling against it is plainly an error in discretionary judgment. Shach.
Wherever there is a doubt, etc. The laws of seizure in cases of doubt are numerous, with many competing views and distinctions. The Shach wrote an entire work on the subject, Tekfo Kohen, and I composed Kuntres HaTefisos. Go study it at the end of this sefer, "and seek His dwelling place."
And if it concerns a ruling of issur, etc. See what I wrote at length in Yoreh Deah siman 242 in the rules of halachic decision.
We follow the majority everywhere. This refers to issur and heter. In monetary law, see Kuntres HaTefisos regarding whether a majority is followed to extract property from one in possession.
They agree for one reason, etc. The Shach questioned this rule in Yoreh Deah. See there and what I wrote there. Nevertheless, the Shach himself concludes that the concern applies when one attempts to infer the numerical majority from books and decide according to the majority of authors. If sages actually sit together to deliberate whether to permit or prohibit, and the majority agree to permit, or the reverse, we do not care whether they reach the same result for one reason or for two different reasons. Ultimately, the majority permits or prohibits. If one opinion takes one extreme, a second takes the opposite extreme, and the third takes an intermediate position, the intermediate view may represent the majority on each component. For example, one says the item is permitted for eating, one says it is prohibited even for benefit, and the third says it is prohibited only for eating. The law follows the third. Two agree that benefit is permitted, for the one who permits eating certainly permits benefit, and two agree that eating is prohibited, for the one who prohibits benefit certainly prohibits eating. This is also implied by the Rif in the chapter Elu Tereifos concerning a skinned animal. See the Rosh, Rashba, and Ran there, and Beit Yosef's discussion. They all indicate as I have written. See also below, siman 103, Sema seif katan 5.
Practice it as prohibited. The converse also applies. If a community practiced a prohibition based on a sage's ruling, he died, and another sage later permitted the matter, they may rely on the latter and practice permissively. The Shach writes this in the name of the Bach. In Tumim I wrote that this requires study.
We follow the later authorities, etc. This is where the later authority is recognized as exceptionally learned and capable of weighing and disputing the earlier view. If he is not established at that level, one certainly should not follow him when he disputes the earlier authorities whose wisdom has been our eyes and our protective wall. Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 39, cited by the Shach, seif katan 21, explains that Maharik cannot invoke the rule that the law follows later authorities when he disagrees with Tosafos, because the difference between their levels is comparable to that between early and later Amoraim. From this one can infer all the more for our generations, when, through our many sins, the strength of wisdom has diminished. May the Omnipresent arouse upon us a spirit from above. The Sema cites the Mordechai that if Tosafos does not disagree with Rif, one should rule like Rif. This requires study, for one cannot decide such matters by sweeping rules. A judge has only the considered judgment appropriate to the particular case and the views of the authorities addressing it. The author of Responsa Chavos Yair writes in his addenda, folio 262b, that where Beit Yosef ruled without qualification in Shulchan Aruch, Rama did not gloss otherwise, and neither Sema nor Shach disagrees, a litigant cannot say "I rely on another opinion," because the halachah has become fixed accordingly. If Rama writes "some say," a litigant may rely on that view. A judge who nevertheless rules to extract money against it commits an error in an explicit rule. (See what I wrote in seif katan 2 in Urim VeTumim.) Where Rama expressly decides in favor of one view, a judge in these lands, where Rama is followed, who rules against him commits an error in an explicit rule. The same is true where Rama did not decide but Sema and Shach both decide for one side. Where Sema and Shach disagree, it appears that one who rules against the Shach likewise commits an error in an explicit rule because the Shach is later, although I have heard that it is very difficult to extract money contrary to the Sema's reasoning, and one might therefore say that a judge following Sema has not erred so clearly. Nevertheless, it appears to me as stated, unless the judge knows and recognizes that on that point the Shach departed from the proper path in his criticism of the Sema. These are Chavos Yair's words. With the help of Heaven, throughout this work I have tried to clarify in each place where the Torah's reasoning inclines and where the Divine spirit directs us.
