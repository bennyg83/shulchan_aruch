And he did not know. This means that he knew the substance of the dispute but did not know a particular authority's view. If he was entirely unaware that there was a dispute and ruled according to the one opinion he knew, and later discovered the disagreement, then if accepted practice follows the opinion he had not known, the Shach agrees that his ruling is treated as an error of discretionary judgment. If there is no accepted practice at all, see Beurim. If his ruling resulted from ignorance of the dissenting opinion, what he did remains effective and he is exempt from payment. If he erred and extracted money, this is treated like an error in an explicit Mishnah. And three ordinary judges. Acceptance is effective to exempt them from liability, and this is the primary rule. See Beurim, seif katan 9. Although with three ordinary judges express acceptance is required, meaning the parties must say, "We accept you to adjudicate for us," whereas with an expert voluntary appearance itself constitutes acceptance, this distinction concerns exemption from payment. According to the dissenting opinion cited by the Rama in seif 3, where no greater authority reverses the ruling, the judgment stands. The Shach holds that if among them there was another scholar who was learned and capable, but the litigants did not accept the judges, a greater authority may reverse the ruling. If reversal is impossible and the judges did not themselves transfer the money, they are exempt because the litigant caused his own loss by failing to seek review by the greater authority. If they themselves transferred the money before the greater authority reversed the judgment, they must pay where recovery is impossible, provided the parties had not accepted them and they were acting coercively. But if the parties voluntarily came before them and one of the judges was an expert, they are exempt, because with an expert voluntary appearance counts as acceptance for purposes of exempting the judge from payment. Acceptance exempts three ordinary judges only if at least one of them is sufficiently learned to be independently eligible to adjudicate. In that case the acceptance is an additional act that can exempt them. If none is sufficiently learned and they would not be permitted to adjudicate without acceptance, the acceptance does not exempt them from payment, Shach. Three judges among whom one is an expert remain liable for an erroneous ruling if the parties did not accept them, unlike the Baal HaMaor:

They agree for one reason. The Shach rules that in a Torah prohibition we do not follow a lenient majority where the authorities reach the same conclusion for different reasons, if we are deciding solely from written works. But where the authorities themselves are present before us, we follow the majority, since members of the Sanhedrin and other judges are not required to give identical reasons. Two judges who derive one ruling from two different verses are counted only as one view, because certainly one of those verses was not written for that teaching, since two verses are not ordinarily written for the same point. But with the Mishnah, Gemara, and decisors, even if two authorities agree while each derives the result from a different Mishnah or passage, they are counted as two. In monetary law, if authorities agree on the result for different reasons, the possessor may say, "I accept the first authority regarding this point, but not his other reasoning, and there I rely on the other authority who disputes him," Shach:
They practiced it as prohibited. The same is true in reverse. If a community practiced a prohibition on the ruling of one scholar and another scholar later permitted it, they may rely on the latter and act permissibly, Shach:
After the later authorities. This applies only where the later authority is outstanding in wisdom. See the Responsa of the Alshich, who writes that Maharik cannot invoke the principle that the law follows the later authorities when he disagrees with Tosafos, because their stature is not comparable. From this, one may infer the rule for our own generations. The Sema writes that the law follows the Rif where Tosafos does not dispute him. One should not mechanically infer legal conclusions from such general rules, and a judge must decide according to his reasoned assessment of the particular case. Where the Beit Yosef rules without qualification, the Rama does not disagree, and neither the Sema nor the Shach disputes him, a litigant cannot assert kim li against that ruling. If the Rama writes "some say," the litigant may assert kim li. If a judge nevertheless extracts money contrary to that position, it is treated as an error in an explicit Mishnah. Where the Rama expressly decides between the views, one who rules against him likewise errs in an explicit Mishnah. Where the Sema and Shach agree, one who rules against them likewise errs in an explicit Mishnah.
