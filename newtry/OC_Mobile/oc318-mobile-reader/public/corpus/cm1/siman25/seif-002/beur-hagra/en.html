The Rif writes in his explanation of Rabbeinu Chananel that the reason a case is reversed where the judge did not personally transfer the property is because otherwise the litigant would suffer loss while the judge is exempt, even though Rabbeinu Chananel disagrees with the Rif in seif 3 on this point.
(Collected note) Seif 1. "Every judge, etc." And seif 2, "if he erred in a matter of judgment, etc." The approach of the Rosh and Tur is as follows. A single nonexpert judge whom the litigants accepted, or a single expert whom they did not accept, or three lay judges who are like a single expert whom they did not accept, their ruling stands but they must pay from their own property. A single expert whom they accepted, three lay judges whom they accepted, or three experts even if not accepted, their ruling stands and they are exempt. If one received authorization from the Exilarch, even a single nonexpert not accepted by the litigants, his ruling stands and he is exempt. A single nonexpert who was not accepted has no valid judgment at all even if he did not err; but if he received authorization from the Exilarch, even if he was not accepted and erred, his ruling stands and he is exempt, as above. This applies specifically where he has learned the material but lacks mature analytical judgment. One who has neither learning nor judgment is disqualified from judging, and authorization does not help. All this concerns an error in discretionary judgment. An error in an explicit Mishnah is always reversed, even by three experts whom the litigants accepted. If reversal is impossible, they must pay. In all these matters there is no distinction whether the judge personally transferred the property or not, nor whether there is a greater authority than he or not. The Rosh initially holds that for a single expert who was accepted, if there is a greater authority the ruling is reversed. He later cites the Ra'avad that even in such a case the ruling is not reversed, and so he writes in his abridgment and in the Tur. The Rosh refutes the Rif's rule that whenever the judge is exempt the judgment is reversed. The latter clause of the Mishnah in Bechorot, "if he was an expert, he is exempt," refers back to the first clause, which says "what he did is done"; the difference is only that in the first clause he is liable and in the latter he is exempt. Furthermore, if the Rif were correct, why did Rabbeinu Chananel have to establish one source as a case where the judge personally transferred property? He could have answered that the Mishnah in Sanhedrin concerns an expert. Further, Sanhedrin 3a says, "if so, etc.; all the more so you, etc." If the judgment were reversed whenever the judge is exempt, that argument would fail. There are many further objections; see there. The Ra'avad rules only like Rav Sheshet: judgment is reversed only for an error in an explicit Mishnah. For an error in discretionary judgment, the ruling always stands. A single nonexpert must pay, as in the Mishnah in Bechorot, which is established above as a case where the litigants accepted him. The same applies to an expert whom they did not accept, as in the case of Mar Zutra: "if they accepted you, etc." Three lay judges have the status of one expert, as stated on 3a and 32b, and there 31 concerning how to write, etc. A single expert whom they accepted is exempt, as stated in Bechorot, which concerns acceptance as above, and in the case of Mar Zutra. Likewise three lay judges whom they accepted are exempt. The Tur adds that the same applies to three experts even if not accepted. Proof is Rabbi Akiva's statement to Rabbi Tarfon, "you are exempt, etc.," although they had not explicitly accepted him, according to those who require explicit acceptance, as stated in the Yerushalmi cited by Nimukei Yosef and other authorities. One must say that because halachic instruction requires only one authority and he is obligated to rule, the same applies to three experts; so writes Nimukei Yosef. The Rosh also initially rules like Rav Yosef and Rav Nachman, with Rav Nachman explaining Rav Yosef: the Mishnah in Sanhedrin concerns an expert where there is a greater authority than he. Nevertheless, regarding exemption, such as where reversal is impossible, there is no distinction, and an expert is always exempt. For a single nonexpert, there is no distinction whether a greater authority exists, since Rav Nachman comments on Rav Yosef's statement. Nimukei Yosef writes that even a nonexpert's judgment is reversed if there is a greater authority, following Rav Nachman's resolution alone, and Rav Yosef's words may also be read this way, calling one without a greater authority an expert. The Rosh later returns to the Ra'avad's position that the halacha does not follow Rav Nachman, from Rav Yosef's statement to Mar Zutra, "if they accepted you, you need not pay, etc.," and he did not reverse the ruling. This proof is not conclusive, because perhaps reversal was impossible. Baal HaMaor, Nimukei Yosef, and other authorities follow the Rosh's initial view that the halacha also follows Rav Nachman. Nimukei Yosef and Ra'ah hold that all three explanations of Rav Nachman, Rav Sheshet, and Rabbeinu Chananel are accepted. Rav Hai Gaon, Baal HaMaor, and the Rosh hold that the halacha does not follow Rabbeinu Chananel because in Bechorot that interpretation follows the Sages, whereas we rule like Rabbi Meir. Therefore there is never a distinction whether the judge personally transferred the property, for liability, exemption, or reversal. There are other approaches to these issues; see Nimukei Yosef. Authorization from the Exilarch is effective even for a nonexpert, as the Gemara says, "one who wishes to judge, etc.," implying even a nonexpert. This applies only where he has learned but lacks mature analytical judgment. If he has neither learning nor judgment, it is ineffective, as Tosafot 3a show from the statement "there is not one, etc." See also 25b, "why specifically three cattle herders, etc.," and 7b concerning the household of the Nasi, etc.; authorization does not help. In summary, according to the Ra'avad, in an error of discretionary judgment the ruling always stands. With one favorable factor, such as expertise or acceptance, the judge must pay. With two favorable factors, such as expertise and acceptance, or three experts even if not accepted, they are exempt. The same applies to one with authorization from the Exilarch. A nonexpert who was not accepted and has no authorization from the Exilarch, or one who has authorization but is not learned, has no valid judgment even if he did not err. Three lay judges have the status of one expert, but two have the status of one ordinary judge. All this concerns an error in discretionary judgment. An error in an explicit Mishnah is always reversed, even by three experts whom the litigants accepted and who received authorization from the Exilarch. If reversal is impossible, they must pay. Although the Gemara asks, "let him say it is an error in an explicit Mishnah, etc.," that is according to the Sages. According to Rabbi Meir they are liable even if they did not personally transfer the property, as Tosafot explain there, s.v. "Hashta, etc." The Ra'avad's approach is the clearest of all the approaches, for the straightforward sugya indicates that Rav Nachman, Rav Sheshet, and Rabbeinu Chananel disagree, and the halacha follows Rav Sheshet, since the flow of the Talmud in all places follows his view. Thus on 6a it asks what kind of error is involved, etc., and does not answer by distinguishing whether he personally transferred the property, nor whether there is a greater authority. Several sugyot likewise support his view. (End of collected note.)
