They accepted him. The Shach writes that every acceptance mentioned in this siman requires no formal kinyan. It is enough that the parties simply came before the judge and litigated before him, even without an express acceptance. In my view this depends upon the underlying rationale. According to Baal HaMaor, an expert who was accepted is exempt because his error is treated as unavoidable accident, like an expert money changer. If he forced the parties to appear, his act was not unavoidable in the relevant sense. Therefore if they came before him voluntarily, even without express language of acceptance, he can be exempt because he acted under the circumstances of unavoidable error. But according to Rosh, the reason an expert who was accepted is exempt is that the parties' acceptance itself must be understood as waiving their right to reimbursement if he errs. Otherwise the acceptance would add nothing, since an expert can already judge them. Under that rationale, mere voluntary appearance does not prove that they intended to exempt him from payment. Express acceptance is therefore needed.
And he is an expert. The Shach rules that the judge must prove his expertise and cannot merely say, "I am an expert and therefore exempt," just as a money changer or butcher must prove expertise. The Mishnah therefore says specifically, "If he was an expert for the public." According to Sefer Tamim De'im, however, the judge's liability itself depends upon his admission that he erred. Unlike the litigant, who may refuse to pay until shown that the ruling was wrong, a God fearing judge admits the mistake. If he says, "It is true that I erred, but I am also an expert and therefore my mistake was unavoidable," perhaps he should be believed through a migo, since the entire liability arises only through his own mouth. A money changer or butcher differs because their error can be independently proven. In a judge's case the mouth that creates the liability should also be the mouth that limits it. According to Rosh, however, exemption of an expert who was accepted rests on the parties' presumed intention to waive reimbursement. If the litigants did not know he was an expert, their acceptance does not prove such a waiver; they may simply have accepted him as a single judge because they did not know his technical status. Therefore even witnesses proving his actual expertise would not necessarily help unless it was known to the litigants. This explains Rambam's formulation in his commentary to the Mishnah that one whose wisdom is publicly known is called an expert for the public. If his wisdom is publicly known, the litigants presumably knew it and their acceptance can be understood as exempting him. Independently of Tamim De'im, however, it appears that the judge can be compelled to pay even if he refuses to admit the error. A party ordinarily cannot say, "I rely upon such and such minority authority," when prevailing legal reasoning is against him. The victorious litigant's ability to say, "Show me clearly that the judge ruled improperly," rests on his possession under an actual judgment. The judge himself has no such possession except through his own ruling. Perhaps his ruling can support himself too, but it is difficult to reject the ancient authority. Thus one may say that the judge's own ruling helps him until, as a God fearing person, he concedes his error.
The judgment is reversed. The Shach concludes that even an expert accepted by the parties ordinarily has his mistaken discretionary ruling stand, but if there is a greater authority the case is reversed. He writes that even where the judge declared pure food impure, the greater authority can reverse it if the first judge was an accepted expert, or if there were three lay judges including one expert. This requires analysis. In monetary law the reason an erroneous discretionary judgment can stand is that the party in possession may say, "We have only what those judges ruled," and what is called reversal by a greater authority is not really rejudging by ordinary laymen who may not understand the matter. But in prohibition law there is no opposing litigant who can insist upon the first court's ruling. If the first judge himself wishes to reconsider, why should his status as a layman prevent him, especially according to the Shach who treats acceptance as making him like an expert? The matter turns on the nature of the rule that a mistaken prohibition ruling stands. Raavad and Rashba hold that once a sage prohibits something in a matter of discretion, the object becomes forbidden to the questioner through the principle that he has made it forbidden upon himself. Ran disagrees and holds that even according to Rav Chisda, who requires an operative act in monetary cases, prohibition does not become final merely through speech. The Shach rejects Ran's proof from the Gemara's need to establish, according to Rav Chisda, that the judge touched the food with a sheretz. He says that case was needed only to create the judge's payment liability under the Sages who reject direct causation. But if the judge's speech had already made the food forbidden, touching it afterward would add nothing. Once it had already become prohibited by his ruling, the sheretz would be superfluous. Tosafot and Rosh can answer because they treat the original speech itself as direct damage and explain the sheretz only incidentally alongside the other clauses. But the Shach rejects that model. There is a further difficulty. If prohibition law differs so sharply because the questioner has made the item forbidden upon himself, then even according to Rav Sheshet, who reverses monetary errors in explicit law, why not say that in prohibition the ruling should remain because of self prohibition? Yet the Gemara's challenge from Rabbi Tarfon assumes that if an explicit legal error is reversible, his declaration is nothing. The better explanation is the one reflected in Rama in Yoreh Deah siman 242: a sage may debate with the first decisor until the first decisor himself retracts. A second sage may not simply prohibit after the first has permitted based only on his own discretion. Thus the first decisor can retract his own permission or prohibition when convinced, and another cannot impose a contrary discretionary ruling independently. In a case where the first decisor admits his mistake, his own retraction can alter the status. This also explains "he declared pure what was impure and must pay." If he himself admits he erred, the prohibition status is restored by his own retraction, and the loss can trigger payment. Where he does not admit error, the second sage cannot simply override him based on competing discretion. Therefore the rule that a greater authority reverses is consistent with the first judge's own retraction or with authoritative review. The Shach's formulation still needs care, but the central principle is that the ruling of prohibition does not mechanically become immutable merely because the first judge spoke.
And three lay judges. This is the view of Rosh and Tur. Their reason is that an accepted expert is exempt because, since he can judge alone even without their acceptance, the acceptance must have been intended to exempt him from reimbursement. The same reasoning applies to three lay judges who were accepted. Baal HaMaor, however, explains the expert's exemption as arising from unavoidable error. According to him, lay judges remain liable even if accepted, because they lack that expertise. Conversely, under Baal HaMaor's approach, even an expert not expressly accepted can be exempt if he did not force the parties and his error was unavoidable. This also resolves Tosafot's question in Sanhedrin why the Gemara, when challenging the view that two judges have a valid judgment, did not answer that the first clause of the Mishnah involved an expert who was not accepted and the last clause an expert who was accepted. According to Baal HaMaor that distinction cannot work in the prohibition clauses such as declaring pure food impure, where there is no coercive litigation and an expert's mistake is unavoidable even without express acceptance. Tosafot's question therefore reflects Rosh's model, where express acceptance is what demonstrates waiver. Rashi's brief statement that one cannot establish the first clause with an expert because the Mishnah later says, "if he was an expert, he is exempt," fits Baal HaMaor especially well. Mordechai likewise writes that the inclusion of the prohibition cases proves the first clause cannot concern an expert, because in prohibition rulings an expert is exempt even without acceptance. Nimukei Yosef expressly rules that in prohibition matters any expert who is obligated to rule is exempt even without acceptance. The Shach attacks this, but his proofs can be answered if one recognizes that under Nimukei Yosef even ordained judges who are obligated to judge can be liable when they personally transfer the property, because actual handling creates stronger liability. The Shach's own view that formally ordained judges are exempt even after handling rests on treating their Torah authority like prior acceptance. But if so, in prohibition matters a single expert has a Torah mandate to rule, and the same logic should exempt him. It therefore appears that Nimukei Yosef is correct. Finally, in our own era the custom has spread that judges receive compensation for issuing rulings. According to Baal HaMaor, whose exemption of an expert rests on unavoidable error, receiving compensation should make the judge liable just as an expert butcher or money changer is liable when paid, even though expertise otherwise makes the error unavoidable. Even payment merely for lost time can have the status of paid custodial responsibility. This point deserves practical attention.
