An expert of beit din. This is Rambam's wording. It appears to mean one who is both learned and analytically competent, fit to judge independently, and need not be formally ordained. The Tur cites Rambam but writes simply "expert," because in the Tur's terminology an ordinary "expert" means one who is both learned and analytically competent. Rambam, however, sometimes uses "expert" for one who is learned but not analytically competent, and therefore calls the higher level "expert of beit din" or "expert for the public." See Perishah above in siman 3 and Sema below, seif katan 26. According to this, the Mechaber's rule in seif 2, that if he is an expert and the parties accepted him he is exempt where reversal is impossible, may even refer to one who is learned but not analytically competent. That would differ from the Tur, who exempts only a true expert who is both learned and analytically competent and whom the parties accepted. This requires study.
But the litigants accepted him. The text does not add, "or he received authorization from the Exilarch," because authorization is ineffective for one who is not an expert at all, neither learned nor analytically competent, as stated in the following seif. The author did not wish to lengthen this clause by distinguishing among levels of qualification.

And the judgment is not reversed. Meaning even where reversal is technically possible. The reason is that he nevertheless ruled according to one of the tannaim or amoraim who disputed this point, so it is not within his power to undo the ruling. If money was already taken from the defendant and delivered to the claimant, they certainly do not remove it back from him. The Tur, based on the Rosh, implies further that even if collection has not yet occurred, the judgment stands and the defendant must pay the claimant, and afterward the defendant seeks reimbursement from the judge who caused the loss. Likewise, where the judge is an expert whom the parties accepted or who had valid authorization from the Exilarch, the Rosh and Tur hold that the judgment stands and is not reversed even if reversal would be possible. Their further position there is that the judge remains exempt from payment, unlike Rambam. See the Tur.
Nevertheless, they should reverse if they erred. The reason is that the public appointed and accepted them with that understanding. But if the public expressly accepted them whether they ruled correctly or incorrectly, and money has already been transferred from the defendant to the claimant, perhaps they cannot reverse the result and take it back. Mordechai's discussion shows that Rama's clause applies specifically to the situation he has just described, judges whom the community compels people to use nowadays, and where they were accepted generally for both correct and mistaken rulings. The Tur in siman 3 writes that such judges are exempt from payment. See there.
And they followed the two among them. The Rosh and Tur distinguish. This rule applies where neither of the two was an expert who could have judged alone. If one of the two was such an expert, then the two must pay the entire loss. They cannot say, "Without the third judge we would not have been fit to adjudicate," because the expert was fit to judge alone. The nonexpert must nevertheless pay his share equally with the expert, because since the panel was sitting as three, the litigants would not have followed the expert alone without the second judge's concurrence, and that concurrence created the majority. See Perishah for the reasoning. Rama may have omitted this because nowadays the practice is not to adjudicate alone even where one is technically an expert, as he wrote in siman 3.
