If the judge who erred was an expert of beit din. Tumim writes that acceptance helps only where he is publicly recognized as an expert, meaning that his expertise has become known. If his expertise was not publicly known, acceptance does not help and he must pay. Urim writes the same. In my humble opinion this is difficult, because he can later prove his expertise. The reason an expert is exempt is that he is regarded as having acted under unavoidable circumstances. When he proves that he is an expert, he has thereby proved the circumstance that exempts him. Likewise, where one showed a coin to a money changer, it is effective if the money changer later proves that he was an expert. Why should a judge be worse? Ketzot HaChoshen writes that the judge need not prove that he is an expert where he has a migo that he could have said he did not err. This is incorrect, for his ruling requires a substantive rationale. Moreover, this is not a valid migo, because he fears that his error may be established by others. In Shach, seif katan 22, there is a scribal error. It should read: "It speaks even where he learned the law but lacks sound judicial reasoning."
Or he was not an expert, but they accepted him, and so forth. See Shach, seif katan 24, who responds to Ramban. Ramban writes that where the judge has not learned the law, even if the parties accepted him, and even if he personally took and gave the property, the judgment can be reversed. Shach asks, first, that according to Rabbi Abahu any panel of two judges is disqualified even if both are learned, yet acceptance is effective. See Tumim, who answers with difficulty. Shach also writes above in siman 22, seif katan 9, that if it becomes known that such judges erred, then where the money was already transferred their judgment remains effective. He seeks proof from Nimukei Yosef, who writes concerning people who accepted unqualified marketplace judges that there is a dispute whether the judges must pay, implying that the judgment itself remains valid. In my humble opinion this is difficult. Teshuvot Maimoniyot and Mordechai, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 23, state explicitly that if litigants accepted three cattle herders and it later became known that they erred, their judgment is not a judgment. They explain that such acceptance is no stronger than a compromise, which requires a formal act of acquisition. They must necessarily speak of a kind of error where, had qualified judges made the same mistake, their judgment would remain effective. Otherwise there would have been no need for the reason that the acceptance resembles a compromise. It therefore appears, on the contrary, that Nimukei Yosef cited by Shach proves that the judgment is void. Nimukei Yosef writes that with three cattle herders one cannot say the litigants accepted them with the understanding that they would judge according to Torah law, because everyone knows that such people do not know Torah law. Since they were not accepted at all to judge according to Torah law, Mordechai and Teshuvot Maimoniyot correctly say that their acceptance resembles a compromise, which requires a kinyan, and without a kinyan it has no effect. With qualified judges who were accepted both for correct judgment and for error, however, it appears that no kinyan is required, since the authorities do not mention one. This will be explained below, with Hashem's help. The reason is that an error in judicial discretion is itself still a ruling within Torah law. The qualified judges intended to rule according to Torah law and relied upon a recognized halachic position. The cattle herders, by contrast, did not enter the matter to decide Torah law at all, but only to rule according to their own understanding. One cannot say that the litigants became possessors by virtue of a particular halachic opinion, since the judges did not know that opinion and did not rely upon it. Nimukei Yosef therefore speaks where there was a kinyan, unlike the ordinary case of qualified judges. Accordingly, specifically with cattle herders who have no learning at all, an erroneous judgment, even an error of judicial discretion, has no effect without a kinyan, just as with a compromise. But if one accepted a relative or other person disqualified to judge who is nevertheless an expert, there would seemingly be no difference between him and other qualified judges. In this respect Shach's argument is certainly correct: according to Rabbi Abahu, every single judge is disqualified to adjudicate alone, yet when the parties accept him his judgment can remain valid even where he errs in judicial discretion. Why should an expert relative or otherwise disqualified person be different? However, the Mechaber writes without qualification in siman 22 that whenever one accepts a relative or disqualified judge and it is later discovered that he erred, the judgment does not stand. This implies that even where an ordinary qualified judge's judgment would remain effective despite an error in judicial discretion, the judgment of the relative or disqualified judge is void. One must say that the Mechaber holds that the principle that what the judge did remains effective is part of a rabbinic enactment and penalty, as Rosh writes, and the Sages enacted it only for one who is fit to judge. A relative or disqualified person is an uncommon case, and the Sages did not make that enactment for an uncommon circumstance. As a practical ruling, it therefore appears proper to distinguish as I wrote above. Acceptance of cattle herders is ineffective without a kinyan, while acceptance of an expert relative or disqualified person is effective without a kinyan, as Shach writes. In Shach, seif katan 4, there is a scribal error. It should read in substance that where an expert was accepted and personally took and gave the property, the judgment is not reversed.
They accepted him. See Sema, seif katan 1. Sema's words make sense if we say that one who has learned the law but lacks sound judicial reasoning, and who received authorization, is not thereby exempt from payment. There is a scribal error there. It should read: "to distinguish between one who has learned but lacks sound reasoning, and one who has not learned but does possess sound reasoning." The intent is that he should have distinguished as follows. Where one has learned the law but lacks sound reasoning, authorization is effective and what he did remains effective. Where one has not learned, authorization is ineffective, and even if he possesses reasoning ability, his ruling has no status of a judgment at all if he erred.
He personally took and gave. See the words of Shach, seif katan 45, who writes that if the judge compelled the litigant to make payment, this is considered as though the judge personally took and gave. Tumim likewise writes this, and it is obvious.
He shall pay from his own property. See Shach, seif katan 26, and all that he asks on Rambam. A further difficulty occurs to me. Since Rambam does not accept Rif's reasoning that the judgment is reversed in order to prevent a loss to the litigant, from where does Rambam derive that with an expert whom the parties accepted the judgment is reversed? Rif established that rule only because he understood Rav Chisda's distinction, that without personal taking and giving the judgment is reversed, as protecting the litigant from uncompensated loss, as Rosh explains Rif. Since Rambam does not accept that rationale, what is his source for reversal in the case of an accepted expert? It appears that Rambam was troubled by Rif's position itself. According to Rif, one must say that as a matter of strict law the judgment stands, but because otherwise the litigant would suffer a loss the judgment is reversed, as Rosh explains. The Yerushalmi, however, indicates that the reason what the judge did remains effective is a penalty, because he presumptuously undertook to judge alone. The Yerushalmi's reason must refer to the clause that "what he did is done." It cannot refer to the next clause, "and he must pay from his own property," because payment is not a penalty but follows from the law. Once the judgment stands, he necessarily must pay from his own property, since we rule like Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for indirect causation. Indeed, once the judgment stands, the judge is more than an indirect cause, for he is a direct cause of the loss. Thus, the Yerushalmi's rationale concerns only why the judgment itself remains effective. Since the continued validity of the judgment is merely a penalty, Rambam understood that this is the basis for Rav Chisda's distinction between a case where the judge personally took and gave and a case where he did not. The Sages imposed the penalty only where the judge personally acted and removed the money from its prior possession. They therefore penalized him by allowing the result to stand. Where he did no act but merely issued an oral ruling, they did not penalize him merely for his speech. Accordingly, Rambam correctly rules that with an expert whom the parties accepted, where the concern of presumptuous judging does not apply, there is no reason for the penalty, and the judgment is reversed. Rambam's further ruling that even without personal taking and giving, if restoration is impossible the judge must pay, also disagrees with Rif. Rif holds that ordinary liability for indirect causation is not applied to a judge in this manner, whereas Rambam imposes liability.
And the judgment is not reversed. See Shach, seif katan 29, who rejects Tur's position. Tur holds that even before the successful litigant has actually received the money, once judgment has been rendered it is not reversed. Shach holds that the money must actually have been transferred. At first glance, Shach's position is very difficult. If so, why must the judge pay from his own property? Had the matter still been before us, the ruling would have been reversed. Shach himself labors earlier, in seif katan 5, to resolve this very difficulty regarding one who declared an impure item pure, yet according to his position the same difficulty remains where a judge obligated the party who was actually exempt. Tumim also asks this. In my humble opinion, there is a distinction. Where a judge erred in an explicit Mishnah and obligated the party who was actually exempt, one can say that the judge legally did nothing, even though his words directly led to payment and the litigant certainly had to pay in obedience to beit din. The judge's statement itself did not create an irreversible loss, because even after payment the money could be recovered, since the judgment is reversed. If it later became impossible to recover it, that later development was not certain at the time of the ruling, and therefore the judge's speech was not itself a certain cause of loss. With an error of judicial discretion, by contrast, the moment the money is paid, the judgment becomes effective because beit din placed the recipient in possession. Without that judicial possession, the money would be removed from him in accordance with the generally accepted halachic view. Since the judgment remains effective precisely because of the judge's ruling, the judge's speech is itself what causes the loss. It cannot be said that he legally did nothing. In an error of judicial discretion the speech is therefore a causative act of damage. Although he is not a direct physical tortfeasor because he performed no act with his hands, it is certainly actionable indirect causation, since his words cause the loss, and according to Rabbi Meir he is liable. With an error in an explicit Mishnah, by contrast, the judge's words do not themselves cause the legal loss, because the ruling is void. This explains why one may say that he legally did nothing. But in judicial discretion he is liable. The case where the judge declared an impure item pure and the owner then mixed it with permitted items is different, for there the judge's speech itself did nothing legally and the owner performed the act of mixing. Shach therefore had to explain that case separately in seif katan 5.
Whether for a correct judgment or for an error. It appears that acceptance alone is sufficient and no kinyan is required. This is unlike a compromise, which requires a kinyan. Here the case concerns an error in judicial discretion, which is still a form of Torah law, as Shach writes in seif katan 30. Although Beit Yosef in siman 22 cites an opinion comparing an erroneous ruling to compromise, that case has a different reason, as I explained above. Shach further writes in order to resolve Rashi's words, but in my humble opinion his explanation is difficult. He should still have proved the rule directly from the Mishnah of "he judged the judgment," which according to Rashi concerns a judge whom the litigants accepted without further specification. The Mishnah teaches that if the judge was an expert he is exempt and his judgment remains effective. This is exactly like the case in which Mar Zutra was told, "If they accepted you, you need not pay." It is an explicit Mishnah, so why did Rashi need to derive the point from statements of the Amoraim? Tumim asks the same question. In my humble opinion, Shach's explanation may be defended as follows. From the Mishnah alone one could say that an expert is exempt for the reason given by Rosh, namely that his mistake is treated as unavoidable. If so, even if the parties' acceptance served only to authorize him to judge, he would still be exempt because he acted under unavoidable circumstances. The case of Mar Zutra, however, supplies a clearer proof. Rav Yosef said, "If they did not accept you, go pay." Rashi explains this as a case where they said to him, "Judge for us according to Torah law," and so forth. If the exemption of an expert were solely because his error is unavoidable, then even in such a case he should be exempt. Rather, according to Rav Yosef, who accepts the rule known as the benefit of Rabbi Yosef, liability extends even to a form of unavoidable loss comparable to theft, for the reason I wrote above in seif katan 3. Rashi's explanation in Sanhedrin 33 that the expert is exempt because his error is unavoidable follows Rav Nachman, who agrees with Rabbah and does not accept that rule, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 56. According to Rav Yosef, by contrast, the expert's exemption must stem from the fact that acceptance is an additional act, and therefore it is understood to include acceptance of error as well. Rashi did not explain Rav Yosef's phrase "if they did not accept you" in the simple sense that the expert judged by compulsion without any acceptance at all, because in such a case the judgment itself would also be reversed. Rav Yosef establishes the Mishnah in Sanhedrin 32, which teaches reversal, with an expert. Only with an ordinary judge, or with an expert whom the parties accepted so that he has the status of beit din, does the judgment remain effective. The reason appears to be that the principal basis for a judgment to remain effective after an error in judicial discretion is possession, as Rosh writes. Although ordinarily even property in the hand of a possessor can be removed when the general halachic practice follows the other opinion, once possession was created by beit din the property is not removed, as I wrote above. An expert who judges by compulsion, however, has no status of beit din at all, as explained in siman 3, seif 2. Consequently, his erroneous ruling does not remain effective, because the successful litigant was not placed in possession by beit din. If the parties accepted him so that he has the status of beit din, but specifically said, "Judge for us according to Torah law," then an error of judicial discretion is itself within Torah law, as Shach writes. The judgment therefore stands because possession was created through beit din, but the judge must pay. Where they accepted him without qualification, however, we say that they accepted him for both correct judgment and error. One cannot say that the acceptance was meant only to make an erroneous judgment stand but not to exempt the judge from payment. Once it is clear that the acceptance included error, and they did not expressly limit him to a correct Torah ruling, their acceptance extends to all consequences, including exemption from payment. The Gemara in Sanhedrin 33 asks from the latter clause, "if he was an expert," against the Mishnah saying monetary judgments are reversed. Why does it not answer that the Mishnah concerning reversal speaks where they did not accept him, while the Mishnah of "he judged the judgment" speaks where they did accept him, and ordinary acceptance of an expert includes both correct judgment and error? The answer is that Rav Nachman himself was the questioner there, as is stated in the rules of the Gemara. Rav Nachman holds on page 5 like Shmuel that even one ordinary judge can render a valid judgment. Therefore, the Mishnah of "he judged the judgment" must speak without acceptance, because according to Shmuel and Rav Nachman, even with an ordinary judge, acceptance without qualification would be understood as acceptance for correct judgment and error, since his judgment is valid even without acceptance, just as with an expert. Since the Mishnah must concern a case without acceptance, Rav Nachman's question is valid and he was compelled to give a different answer. Rav Yosef, who follows Rabbi Abahu, was not troubled by the question raised there from the clause "if he was an expert," because he can establish that Mishnah where the parties accepted him, and there is no difficulty.
For what else could they have done? See Shach, seif katan 32, who asks that in any event they should be exempt because the situation is equivalent to acceptance. One can answer that the practical difference is where the parties said to them, "We accept you on condition that you judge according to Torah law." According to Rashi, such judges are liable, as Shach writes in seif katan 31. See there. Shach's statement in seif katan 3 that coercion through a financial penalty is not considered compulsion is also difficult. In such a case even financial coercion is certainly considered coercion. Only with respect to one who causes another person's property to be delivered to violent authorities do we distinguish between bodily coercion and financial coercion. Tumim writes the same. Likewise, Shach's statement in seif katan 35 that if the judges personally took and gave they are liable is difficult, because they are compelled to carry out the ruling through the agent of beit din, and that is equivalent to personal taking and giving. Tumim likewise writes this.
And he loses the third share. Ketzot HaChoshen asks that we rule like Rav Nachman that whenever payment cannot be obtained from one liable party, it is collected from the other. I see no difficulty. Rambam himself rules in Hilkhot Nizkei Mamon, regarding consecrated peace offerings that caused damage, that the injured party does not collect from the meat an amount corresponding to the sacrificial portions. The Gemara in Bava Kamma explains that even according to Rabban Gamliel one can say: did the meat cause the damage while the sacrificial portions did not? The same reasoning applies here. Ketzot suggests that here the judgment was completed through the two judges because they could have completed it without the third. I do not understand this. In fact, the final judgment was rendered through all of them, for all said, "So and so, you are liable." I also proved above that all the judges must be present at the time the judgment is completed. See Shach, seif katan 39, who asks that since Rama did not decide between Rif and Rosh, the judgment should be reversed with respect to the third share, because otherwise the litigant suffers a loss. According to what I wrote above in seif katan 13, there is no difficulty. In seif 3, Rama decided like Rambam and not like Rif, holding that even without personal taking and giving the judge must pay from his own property where payment is due. The reason a judgment remains effective is therefore not the avoidance of loss to the litigant at all. With an expert whom the parties accepted, the judgment is reversed because the penalty for presumptuously judging does not apply to him. With three ordinary judges, by contrast, who should not have judged by compulsion, as Shach writes in seif katan 14, letter 9, the judgment remains effective. It remains effective also for the additional reason Shach gives there, letter 11, that the judges lack the capacity to comprehend the reasoning of the greater authority.
