If the mistaken judge was an expert of the beit din, etc. Sema, seif katan 20, understands this to mean one who is both learned and capable of independent reasoning. He explains that Rambam adds the words "of the beit din" because in Rambam's terminology a plain "expert" can sometimes mean one who is learned but not fully capable of analysis. According to Sema, above in seif 2, where an accepted expert who errs in judicial discretion is exempt if the loss cannot be reversed, even one who is merely learned but not fully analytical could qualify. Shach, seif katan 22, correctly rejects this. No authority grants the legal status of an expert judge to one who is learned but incapable of reasoned adjudication. For all relevant rules the judge must be a complete expert, both learned and capable of independent reasoning. The phrase "expert of the beit din" in Rambam is therefore either a textual corruption, an explanatory gloss that entered the text, or a scribal expansion. It does not create a lower category of expertise. That is correct as practical law. A separate issue concerns one who is learned but not fully capable of independent reasoning and who received authorization from the Exilarch. Shach cites Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah and other authorities that such authorization does not help. Without acceptance by the litigants, his judgment has no validity. Baal HaMaor likewise holds so. Tur and Rosh disagree and hold that authorization can be effective for one who is learned even if not fully reasoned. Mordechai in Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot lists a case of one who is learned but not fully reasoned, whom the litigants accepted or who obtained authorization from the Exilarch. If he erred in judicial discretion and personally took and gave, the judgment stands and he pays from his own property. This seems to imply that authorization can give jurisdiction even to one who is not fully reasoned, agreeing with Rosh. Shach explains that Mordechai follows Rosh's view. But this does not fully resolve the matter, because according to Rosh and Tur, authorization that validly empowers such a judge should also exempt him from payment when he errs. Where does Mordechai obtain a middle position that authorization validates the judgment but does not exempt the judge from liability? To resolve this we must understand Rambam more carefully. Rambam writes in Hilchot Sanhedrin 2:11 that "one who is an expert to the public, or one who received authority from beit din, may judge alone." At first glance the word "or" is difficult. If one who is not expert cannot be helped by authorization, while an expert can judge alone even without authorization, what new category does authorization add? Similarly, in chapter 4 Rambam writes that any judge who receives authorization from the Exilarch may judge litigants coercively even if they do not wish to appear before him. Again, if authorization helps only one who is already an expert and an expert already has full compulsory jurisdiction, what function remains for the authorization? Lechem Mishneh infers from this that Rambam holds an individual expert can judge only where parties accept him, and requires authorization for compulsory jurisdiction. He therefore criticizes Tur for counting Rambam among those who permit an individual expert to compel litigation. But Lechem Mishneh's reading is difficult. First, the Gemara itself strongly implies, as Tosafot and Rosh explain, that an individual expert can adjudicate coercively. Otherwise what is the difference between an expert and a nonexpert once the parties accept him? Likewise the phrase "if they called you, go and judge, but if not, go and pay" implies that even without express acceptance the expert's judgment is legally a judgment. Otherwise, if they never accepted him, why would he have to pay for a judgment they are not required to obey? It is strained to invent multiple forms of acceptance to solve this. Second, Rambam himself in chapter 2 places "an expert to the public" and "one authorized by beit din" in one sentence as parallel grounds for judging alone. If one gave only voluntary jurisdiction and the other compulsory jurisdiction, it would be misleading to formulate them as equivalent alternatives. The better explanation comes from Rambam's own Commentary to the Mishnah at the beginning of Zeh Borer. He writes that laymen whose jurisdiction the people accept have valid judgments. If they are not appointed by the parties but act on their own, or are appointed by a king, governmental authority, or communal elders, then their judgments are valid only if they are genuine experts. Rambam then defines an expert as one who is learned and can reason independently and derive one matter from another. He adds a further requirement for what he calls "an expert to the public": the person's expertise must be publicly known and recognized. A person with that public reputation may judge alone even without authorization from the Exilarch. But one who is learned and capable of reasoning yet whose expertise has not become publicly established is an expert in substance without being an "expert to the public." Such a judge does not possess full compulsory authority merely from his own status. Authorization from the Exilarch supplies that missing public authority. Thus there are three categories. One who is learned but not capable of independent reasoning is not an expert at all. Authorization does not help him. One who is learned and reasoned but not publicly recognized is an expert, yet not an expert to the public. Authorization enables him to exercise compulsory jurisdiction. One who is both fully expert and publicly recognized is an expert to the public and can judge coercively even without authorization. This resolves Rambam's wording perfectly. When he says "one who is an expert to the public, or one who received authorization," he means either a publicly established expert or a substantively qualified expert whose public authority comes from formal authorization. Likewise chapter 4's rule concerning authorized judges refers to a true expert whose expertise has not otherwise acquired public recognition. Kesef Mishneh's comments appear to point in this direction. Tur in siman 13, speaking of an individual expert who is ordained and publicly recognized, correctly treats Rambam as allowing compulsory adjudication. Such a person is the highest category and requires no further authorization. Lechem Mishneh's objection therefore falls away. Now Mordechai can also be understood. When he speaks of one who is "learned but not fully reasoned," his terminology may not mean utterly incapable of analysis. It can mean someone not established as a public expert, though he has enough learning and reasoning to count as a judge. Acceptance or authorization validates his jurisdiction. But exemption from payment is a separate rule. The Mishnah says specifically that "an expert to the public" is exempt from payment where accepted. That phrase is deliberate. An ordinary substantive expert who lacks public recognition can have jurisdiction through acceptance or authorization and nevertheless remain personally liable for error. Authorization from the Exilarch is no stronger for monetary exemption than the litigants' own voluntary acceptance, as Rivash and other authorities explain. If direct acceptance by the parties does not exempt one who lacks public expert status, authorization alone should not do so either. This is Mordechai's case. The judge is qualified enough that acceptance or authorization makes the judgment valid, but he is not an expert to the public and therefore, if he errs, he must pay. Accordingly Rambam's wording here is also exact. Where he speaks of an expert judge who is exempt from payment, he means an expert recognized by beit din and the public, not merely someone with minimal technical learning. That is the force of the phrase Sema noticed. The correct reading therefore does not require deleting Rambam's words. They are meaningful once the levels of expertise are properly distinguished.
Or if he was not an expert, but they accepted him, etc. Here Baal HaMaor and Ramban disagree. Baal HaMaor holds that acceptance helps where the judge is learned but not fully expert. It does not help a person who has no Torah learning at all. Such a person is an asherah, an unfit object, and acceptance cannot transform him into a judge. Ramban disagrees. He asks: why should this differ from the Mishnah's case where the parties accept three cattle herders? R. Yonah explains that their dispute depends upon the law above in siman 22 concerning acceptance where there are two independent defects. A person who is both a single judge and wholly unlearned has two defects. If so, according to Rama above, who rules that ordinary acceptance does not cure two defects without a kinyan, the same should apply here. But this explanation is not necessary. A single recognized expert is permitted to judge alone. Therefore the numerical fact that there is only one judge is not inherently a defect in itself. The only true defect here is lack of expertise. Shach himself uses similar reasoning above in siman 22 concerning an expert judge who is also a relative. See what I wrote there. More fundamentally, the case of three cattle herders is conceptually different. When the parties accept cattle herders, they are obviously not asking them to rule according to precise Torah law. These men do not know Torah law. The acceptance means: "Decide according to your own judgment and assessment, and we accept that outcome." That kind of arbitration can be binding after final judgment because the parties knowingly chose lay decision-makers. But the present siman concerns a person accepted specifically to judge according to Torah law, as is clear from the Gemara and poskim. If the man is entirely devoid of Torah knowledge, there is no greater mistaken acceptance than appointing him for that task. It is like saying to wood, "You are my father," or to stone, "You shall instruct me." A person cannot meaningfully accept an ignoramus to issue Torah judgment that he is incapable of knowing. Thus Baal HaMaor's ruling is entirely understandable. Ramban, however, holds that even then acceptance can create an arbitral authority of its own. Still, according to Ramban, if such a person errs, the judgment is reversed even if he personally took and gave. Shach asks: if a learned but nonexpert single judge is accepted, an error in judicial discretion can stand according to the relevant views. Why should acceptance of a wholly unlearned person be treated differently? There is no difficulty. In the first case Chazal protected the dignity and stability of a person who has at least the legal title of judge. Reversing every discretionary ruling would expose judicial authority to contempt. Here, the person has no status of judge at all. His only power comes from the parties' private acceptance, and that acceptance itself is defective if it was made on the assumption he would render Torah law. There is therefore no basis to preserve an erroneous ruling merely for judicial dignity. Rama above in siman 22 follows Ramban on this point. However, I would limit the rule. If the parties explicitly said, "Judge us according to Torah law," then an entirely unlearned person cannot meaningfully fulfill that charge and his erroneous ruling should be reversible as Ramban says. But if they simply said, "We accept this person to decide between us," without specifying Torah law, then Shach's approach is correct. We assume they accepted his personal judgment and assessment, not precise Torah adjudication. Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Sanhedrin similarly writes that where parties accept ordinary street-corner judges, their errors can stand and they can be exempt from payment, because the very acceptance shows the parties were not demanding expert Torah judgment. It would be impossible to say they chose such men for their mastery of Torah law. Shach asks from that source against Ramban. According to the distinction above, there is no contradiction. Ramban addresses a case where the acceptance was to judge according to Torah law. Nimukei Yosef addresses ordinary unrestricted acceptance of lay arbitrators. This is the correct practical distinction.
If he personally took and gave, etc. It appears that this does not require the judge himself physically to take from one litigant and hand the property to the other. If, in a case where the law authorizes seizure of assets from one who refuses to comply with a judgment, the beit din's agent takes the defendant's property at the judge's direction and gives it to the plaintiff in accordance with the ruling, that too is considered "the judge took and gave." The court agent acts by the judge's command and as his agent. Throughout Torah law, a person's agent is like himself. There is therefore no difference between the judge carrying out the transfer personally and the beit din's agent doing so on his instructions. Rif's exclusion applies only where the litigant did not resist at all and voluntarily paid merely because of the judge's ruling. In that case Rif treats the judge's role as indirect causation, and because the judge is exempt from payment, the judgment can be reversed. But where the judge orders the beit din's agent to descend upon the litigant's assets, seize them forcibly, and transfer them to the opposing party, this is fully within the category of taking and giving. This also resolves Nimukei Yosef's difficulty against Rif. The Gemara says that one who wishes to judge and, if he errs, not be required to pay, should obtain authorization from the Exilarch. Nimukei Yosef asks: why is authorization needed? The judge could simply avoid personally taking and giving. That would be strained if "taking and giving" required the judge's own hands. Why should a judge ordinarily undertake such a burdensome act? But according to what I have explained, the matter is straightforward. A judge is obligated to enforce his ruling against one who refuses to comply. If necessary, he must order the beit din's agent to descend upon the litigant's assets. Once he does so, that is considered the judge's own taking and giving. Thus a judge who wishes to be protected from payment if he errs cannot simply assume he will avoid the category. Proper enforcement may require him to enter it. Hence the advice to obtain authorization from the Exilarch. The same resolves the Gemara's concern that if judges who err were exempt, one would "close the door before borrowers." If the borrower refuses to obey the judgment, the court will have to enforce it by means of a beit din agent. That enforcement is taking and giving, and the judges may then become liable for a mistaken ruling. Therefore the lender knows the court cannot simply issue an erroneous decree without responsibility. Nimukei Yosef was driven by his difficulty to say that "taking and giving" is not literal at all, but means simply that the judgment became effective through the judge. That is unnecessary. The term should retain its ordinary force. The beit din agent's coercive transfer is legally the judge's own act because the agent acts as the court's emissary. This is the correct interpretation.
If reversal is impossible, he must pay from his own property. This is Rambam's view. Shach and other authorities ask from the Gemara, which seems to say that wherever the judgment itself is reversible the judge is exempt from payment even if practical restoration has become impossible. They therefore ask what source Rambam has for this ruling. Kesef Mishneh labors to bring proof, but Shach correctly notes that the proof is not decisive. In my view Rambam's reasoning is straightforward. Tosafot ask concerning the first stage of the Gemara in Bechorot and Bava Kamma, where the Mishnah is established according to R. Meir without requiring the judge personally to take and give. How does one explain the case of "he declared the impure pure"? Tosafot answer that according to R. Meir, who imposes liability for dina d'garmei, even though the judgment is reversible in law, if the practical loss cannot be undone the judge must pay. This creates a question from the Gemara's challenge concerning R. Tarfon. If his ruling was an error in an explicit Mishnah and therefore reversible, why should he have thought he must pay? Why not simply say the case follows R. Meir, who is the halachic position and who imposes liability for irreversible loss even where the ruling is theoretically reversible? Rambam answers by distinguishing between an error in an explicit Mishnah and an error in judicial discretion. Shach already asks elsewhere how Rosh can describe judicial liability as a penalty when Rosh holds that dina d'garmei is substantive law rather than a penalty. The answer is that a judge is different from an ordinary person who causes damage. The judge entered the matter with lawful authority and did not intend to harm. Therefore ordinary dina d'garmei does not naturally apply to him in the same way. His liability is a rabbinic penalty imposed upon certain judicial mistakes. This must also be Rambam's understanding. Rambam says the judge is exempt in certain situations because although he caused loss, he did not intend damage. Ordinarily lack of intent would not exempt a tortfeasor. A person is always forewarned, and one who mistakenly tells another that a counterfeit coin is good may be liable through dina d'garmei even though the mistake was innocent. Thus Rambam must mean that with a judge, because he acts under judicial authority and not as a tortfeasor, the liability functions as a special penalty. Now a known principle is that Chazal enacted penalties primarily for common situations. They did not ordinarily create penalties for rare cases. An error in an explicit Mishnah is unusual. A competent judge is not expected to make such a mistake. Therefore Chazal did not impose this special judicial penalty where the mistake was in an explicit Mishnah. If the ruling is reversible, he is simply exempt. An error in judicial discretion, however, is common. Even learned judges can err where authorities and reasoned positions conflict. In that common category Chazal imposed liability under the framework of dina d'garmei where reversal has become impossible. Accordingly, the Mishnah's case "he declared the impure pure," which concerns an error in judicial discretion, can obligate the judge according to R. Meir even where the judgment would otherwise be reversible, once practical reversal is impossible. The R. Tarfon incident is different because his mistake was in an explicit Mishnah. Had the judgment been reversible, even R. Meir would not impose the special payment penalty in such an uncommon category. This resolves the Gemara exactly and provides a clear source for Rambam. Rambam therefore rules that an error in an explicit Mishnah is reversed and the judge is exempt, whereas an error in judicial discretion, where the judge did not personally take and give but reversal has become impossible, can obligate him to pay. There is support for this from Tosafot in Avodah Zarah 58b. They say that Reish Lakish should not have to pay merely because of mistaken instruction unless he personally took and gave, referring to the Sanhedrin discussion. At first glance this is difficult. That episode involved an error in an explicit Mishnah. If the loss was reversible, then everyone agrees the judgment should simply be reversed. If it was no longer reversible, then according to Tosafot's general approach to R. Meir's dina d'garmei one might think he should pay even without taking and giving. Why then do Tosafot invoke the need for taking and giving? According to Rambam's distinction it is clear. Tosafot can agree that where an error is in an explicit Mishnah, the special judicial liability does not apply unless the judge actually performs the harmful transfer. Their broader statement that R. Meir can impose payment despite legal reversibility concerns the common category of error in judicial discretion. Thus the Avodah Zarah Tosafot align naturally with Rambam. This also resolves the apparent contradiction whereby Tosafot elsewhere rule unlike Rav Chisda concerning whether reversal depends on taking and giving, while here they still require taking and giving. The categories are different. Rambam's ruling is therefore well founded.
Some say that even if he did not personally take and give, what he did is done, etc. Bach inclines toward Rif, who rules like Rav Chisda that if the judge did not personally take and give, the judgment is reversed. Shach, seif katan 28, correctly objects that Bach's ruling cannot simply override major authorities such as Baal HaMaor, Tosafot, and Rosh. But Shach himself wishes to decide in favor of Tosafot and Rosh, and that conclusion is no more decisive against Rif, Ramban, Rambam, and even one approach in Tosafot themselves which reads the sugyot of Bechorot and Bava Kamma according to Rav Chisda. Tosafot in Avodah Zarah also appear to follow Rav Chisda, requiring actual taking and giving before the result becomes fixed. Therefore the matter remains a genuine doubt of law, exactly as Rama writes by presenting the second opinion as "some disagree." Within the authorities who do not rule like Rav Chisda but follow Rav Nachman, there are further major uncertainties requiring careful clarification. R. Yosef explains the contradiction between the Mishnah saying civil judgments are reversed and the Mishnah in Bechorot saying "what he did is done" by distinguishing between an expert judge and a nonexpert. Rav Nachman then says that the distinction is whether there is a greater scholar available to reverse the ruling. Most authorities understand Rav Nachman not as disputing R. Yosef but as explaining him. Raah is exceptional in understanding Rav Nachman to disagree and to make the existence of a greater scholar decisive regardless of whether the first judge was expert. R. Chaim Yonah Teomim favors Raah and argues that if a greater scholar can identify the error, why should the power to reverse depend upon the first judge's expertise? But this does not overturn the earlier authorities. Rosh already explains that where an unqualified judge arrogantly undertook to judge, Chazal penalized him by allowing his erroneous judgment to stand while making him pay. The Yerushalmi expresses this idea directly. Even where an unusual circumstance later prevents actual collection from the judge, rabbinic enactments were made for ordinary cases and were not restructured for every rare exception. Baal HaMaor gives a related reason. Where the unqualified judge must pay, the litigant suffers no ultimate loss. Chazal therefore had less reason to undermine the stability of judicial decisions. One cannot simply answer that the ruling should be reversed and the judge thereby exempted. There is an independent concern for the dignity and stability of beit din. Constantly reversing judgments creates ridicule, as though today the law is one way and tomorrow another. We already find that some authorities resist reversing a judicial act even after witnesses establish the error because of degradation of beit din. Thus, where the litigant can be compensated by the mistaken judge, Chazal can reasonably leave the ruling standing and impose payment rather than repeatedly nullify judicial acts. Accordingly, the first judge's lack of expertise is highly relevant, and Raah's broad equation is not compelled.
However, Raah's own position creates a serious practical difficulty. If whenever any greater scholar exists the ruling of even a nonexpert judge can be reversed, how can the Mishnah ever say that a nonexpert's mistaken judgment stands? Israel is not bereft of learned sages. Once an error in discretion becomes apparent, surely some greater scholar can be found. One cannot say the reversing scholar and the original judge must live in the same city. Raah can answer only by combining his approach with his separate view, cited by Nimukei Yosef, that even Rav Nachman accepts Rav Chisda's distinction: a greater scholar can reverse only where the judge did not personally take and give. Where the transfer was judicially executed, the judgment stands. Thus the Bechorot Mishnah can concern actual taking and giving. But most authorities do not accept this hybrid. Once that position is rejected, Rav Nachman and R. Yosef should be read together: a greater scholar reverses specifically the ruling of an expert judge, while the ruling of a nonexpert is preserved under the penalty and stability considerations described above. Rashi's wording does not prove otherwise. When Rashi explains R. Yosef's rule that a nonexpert judge cannot retract because the litigant can say, "Who says your present reasoning is more correct than your first reasoning?" the point is that the parties accepted this judge's own understanding. They can say: "We accepted you to decide according to your reasoned judgment. We did not accept the changing opinions of other sages." If the judge himself is nonexpert, even his later change of mind can be challenged: perhaps his first reasoning was better than his second. An expert judge is different. His considered retraction carries greater authority. Rav Nachman adds that even for an expert, an external reversal requires a greater scholar whose authority is superior enough to displace the accepted ruling. This harmonizes the two statements without forcing them into contradiction. There is then a well-known difficulty in Rosh. At the beginning of his discussion he repeatedly treats Rav Nahman's rule as practical law, meaning that a greater scholar can reverse an expert's error. Yet later he cites Raavad, who proves from R. Yosef's statement to Mar Zutra, "if the litigants accepted you, do not pay; otherwise pay," that even a greater scholar does not reverse the accepted expert's judgment. R. Yosef was greater than Mar Zutra, yet he did not reverse the case. Rosh concludes, "and this appears correct." Bach tries to say the words "this appears correct" belong only to Raavad's quotation and not to Rosh's own conclusion. Shach rightly finds that difficult. Others distinguish between a judge whom the parties expressly accepted and one who judged without such acceptance. Where the judge was expressly accepted, the litigants can say they chose his judgment and not that of another scholar, however great. This also explains why R. Akiva did not simply reverse R. Tarfon's ruling. The owner had voluntarily brought the question to R. Tarfon, and that consultation itself amounted to acceptance. Yet this distinction is not entirely satisfactory because Rosh's earlier discussion of the Mishnah itself already concerns accepted judges. A better explanation is found by examining the meaning of "expert" in Tosafot. Tosafot ask why R. Yosef did not reverse Mar Zutra's ruling and answer that the "expert" whose error can be reversed by a greater scholar refers to one who is ordained. This should not be understood as requiring that the original mistaken judge be ordained. There is little reason why an expert's mistake should become more reversible merely because he received semikhah. Rather, the greater scholar who reverses the judgment must himself possess the superior judicial authority of semikhah. The litigants never accepted that greater scholar. Why should his personal wisdom alone allow him to override the judgment of the court they did accept? If he is ordained by the supreme beit din, however, he carries formal public authority equivalent to acceptance. Thus Rav Nachman's "greater scholar" means a greater and formally authorized judge. R. Yosef and Mar Zutra lacked that semikhah in their era. R. Yosef therefore could not simply reverse Mar Zutra's accepted judgment despite being greater in learning. This is likely Raavad's practical point for our generations as well. Rav Nachman's rule is true in the era of semikhah. But after semikhah ceased, no external scholar has the formal authority necessary to reverse the accepted expert merely because he is greater in wisdom. This harmonizes Rosh's entire discussion. He first explains the Talmudic law according to R. Yosef and Rav Nachman. Then, when he states the practical rule for his own era, he cites Raavad that because we no longer have ordained judges, even a greater scholar cannot reverse the accepted expert. This also aligns with Tur and with the abbreviated rulings, which state that in our time even a greater scholar does not reverse the ruling. Thus there is no need to say Rosh retracted his earlier analysis. He distinguishes the law of the Talmudic era from practical law after the loss of semikhah.
And through this Tur's presentation becomes precise. He rules that even a greater scholar cannot reverse the judgment in our time because, through our sins, semikhah has been lost. Why then did Rosh spend so much effort recording Rav Nachman's rule if it has no direct contemporary application? First, he was explaining the Gemara correctly and showing that R. Yosef and Rav Nachman stand together against Rav Chisda. Second, important consequences still emerge from the analysis, including the rule that mere general expertise is enough to exempt an accepted judge from payment, even if the authority needed to reverse another court would require the higher status of semikhah. Many derivative questions depend upon this distinction. Raavad's statement in Avodah Zarah can also now be reconciled. There he distinguishes civil law from issur v'heter and says that even where a greater judge could reverse a civil ruling, one sage cannot simply permit what another sage prohibited in matters of issur v'heter unless the first ruling involved an explicit error. That baraita speaks in the era where ordained judges existed. Raavad therefore had to explain why the civil-law power of a greater ordained scholar did not translate directly into issur v'heter. For our generations, however, where no one has semikhah, the external reversal power is already absent in civil cases as well. This explains why Rosh in Avodah Zarah can rule broadly that a second sage does not undo another's ruling merely through discretionary disagreement, without contradicting his Talmudic analysis in Sanhedrin.
Indeed, Ran asks strongly against Raavad from Rav Chisda's explanation of the Mishnah "a sage who declared the pure impure." Rav Chisda says the case must involve actual contact with impurity. This seems to imply that without such an act the ruling itself would be reversible even in issur v'heter. Several approaches are possible. One is to distinguish between a nonexpert and a genuine expert. The Mishnah's "one who declared the pure impure" can concern a nonexpert. His halachic pronouncement is weak enough that, before an irreversible act, another sage can correct it. Raavad's rule that a second sage cannot overturn the first concerns a recognized expert whose prohibition has acquired full halachic force. Another approach is that Rav Chisda's proof from actual contact with impurity is not the source for the rule about a second sage at all. According to Rav Nachman, the inability of another sage to overturn an issur ruling can arise from the special finality of issur v'heter, while Rav Chisda's technical establishment concerns a different framework. Ran, who rejects that distinction, therefore rules like Rif and Rav Chisda more broadly. Moreover, there is also support for Raavad from the episode in Chullin where R. Abba instructed Rava to pay for an ox. Rashba explains that the mistake was in judicial discretion and the ruling stood. Yet R. Abba was greater than Rava. Why did he not simply reverse the decision? The natural answer is that in issur v'heter a greater sage does not automatically reverse the ruling of the first sage. Thus the distinction has strong Talmudic support.
The second answer to Ran's difficulty is that the rule may arise specifically according to Rav Nachman. Rav Nahman holds that in civil law a greater authority can reverse an expert's discretionary ruling. That creates Maharsha's question in the R. Tarfon case: why did R. Akiva reassure him based on expertise rather than simply reverse him as the greater scholar? The answer can be that the R. Tarfon case concerned issur v'heter, where even a greater sage does not reverse another sage's discretionary ruling. Rav Chisda, however, does not accept the greater-scholar reversal framework at all. For him, the question is simply whether the ruling has been implemented. He therefore can establish "declared the pure impure" as a case where contact with impurity occurred. Thus the distinction between civil law and issur v'heter is a consequence specifically of Rav Nahman's approach and need not be read back into Rav Chisda.
This explains why Ran, who rules like Rav Chisda, attacks Raavad, while Raavad and Rosh, who prefer Rav Nahman's framework, distinguish the fields. The same reasoning explains the Chullin incident. R. Abba could not reverse Rava merely because he was greater, since the matter was one of issur v'heter. If Rava's ruling had caused irreversible loss under the applicable liability rules, payment could be required without judicial reversal.
Returning to our main point, Raavad, Rosh, Tur, and Rama therefore hold that in our time a greater scholar does not reverse an expert judge's discretionary ruling merely by superior learning, because we lack ordained judges with formal authority. Semag adopts a third position. He rules that an ordained expert can have his ruling reversed, whereas an unordained expert cannot. The latter can nevertheless be exempt from payment if the parties accepted him, as R. Yosef told Mar Zutra. Semag then records the Mishnah and Rav Chisda's establishment involving acceptance and taking and giving. Shach finds Semag's formulation confused, but it can be explained. Semag combines R. Yosef and Rav Chisda. A nonexpert's error is not reversible merely because another scholar disagrees. An ordained expert's error can be reversed by a greater authorized scholar where the judge did not personally take and give. But once the court itself performed the transfer, even an ordained expert's decision acquires sufficient implementation that it is not reversed. Why then does the Gemara challenge R. Yosef from the Mishnah's clause that an expert is exempt? Because in the era of the Mishnah a true publicly recognized expert ordinarily would have received semikhah. If his ruling were always reversible, there would have been little need for a broad rabbinic payment exemption directed specifically at unordained experts. Rav Chisda's taking-and-giving distinction creates a meaningful setting even for ordained experts where the ruling stands and the exemption from payment is necessary. Thus Semag can rule with both R. Yosef and Rav Chisda. For our practical era the outcome largely converges with Rosh and Tur: an unordained expert's discretionary ruling is not externally reversed merely because a greater scholar appears, yet if accepted he can be exempt from payment under the rules discussed above.
But Shach cites many authorities who disagree with Raavad and Rosh and hold that even today a greater scholar can reverse the error, at least where the judge did not personally take and give, and perhaps even after such a transfer according to those who rule like Rav Nachman against Rav Chisda. This remains a major dispute. Shach himself suggests that R. Yosef did not reverse Mar Zutra because practical reversal had become impossible, perhaps because one party had gone overseas. He then explains that R. Yosef's statement "if they accepted you, do not pay; otherwise pay" can still prove that a single expert can judge coercively, because the very fact that acceptance affects payment shows that his judgment had legal force even without acceptance. Shach uses this to defend Rashi's interpretation that where the parties accepted an expert "both for judgment and for error" he is exempt, whereas if they said only "judge us according to Torah law" he must pay for a mistake. Shach says that ordinary unqualified acceptance implies acceptance even of error, because otherwise the added act of acceptance would accomplish nothing. I do not find this satisfactory. As Shach himself says elsewhere, "acceptance" can mean nothing more than the parties' voluntary appearance before the judge. If so, there is no extra verbal waiver from which one can infer that they accepted even an erroneous ruling. A litigant who voluntarily comes before a judge rather than waiting for coercion has not thereby said, "I waive all protection against judicial mistakes." Moreover, if the Gemara meant to prove coercive authority from the fact that acceptance otherwise would add nothing, the same proof should have been brought directly from the Mishnah's rule that an accepted expert is exempt from payment. There would be no need for the Mar Zutra episode. The real difficulty against Rashi is Tosafot's question. Why interpret R. Yosef's words "if they did not accept you" as meaning that they said, "judge us according to Torah law," rather than simply that they never accepted him at all? The latter would give a direct and obvious proof that an expert can judge coercively. The better explanation is that Rashi was addressing Raavad's difficulty: if R. Yosef was greater than Mar Zutra, why did he not reverse the decision? Rashi therefore understands the case as one of explicit acceptance to judge according to Torah law. That formal acceptance made Mar Zutra's tribunal the chosen forum and prevented simple external displacement by another unappointed scholar. But because the acceptance was limited to Torah law, if Mar Zutra in fact erred he could still owe payment. If the acceptance had been broader, embracing his personal judgment even if mistaken, he would be exempt. This preserves the legal force of the acceptance without requiring the problematic inference that every voluntary appearance equals acceptance of error. And the Gemara can still infer that a recognized expert possesses coercive authority, because otherwise an acceptance limited to Torah law would itself have been mistaken and ineffective once he erred. The judgment's continued legal force therefore shows that the judge had independent authority. This also explains why the Gemara uses the Mar Zutra case rather than the Mishnah. The episode demonstrates not merely that acceptance can exempt, but that limited acceptance to Torah adjudication does not destroy the judge's underlying authority when he errs. It thus reveals the coercive status of the recognized expert more directly. Rashi's explanation then fits with his separate reason why an accepted expert can be exempt from payment: an expert is not expected to err, and where an unusual mistake occurs the litigant's own mazal is treated as the cause. That rationale concerns ordinary acceptance, not an express contractual waiver of every possible error. There is therefore no contradiction in Rashi. Now we can also understand why the Gemara proves the coercive authority of a single expert from R. Yosef and Mar Zutra rather than from the similar episode of Rabbah bar Chana and R. Chiya, who was substantially older and greater. In the latter episode one could say the parties merely asked the judge to rule according to Torah law. If the judge's discretionary error were not considered an "error" at all according to Rav Sheshet, because such rulings are never reversed, then there would be no mistaken acceptance. The inference to compulsory authority would be weaker. R. Yosef, however, is associated with the position that discretionary error can be subject to reversal through a greater authority. Thus if the parties' acceptance were the sole source of Mar Zutra's jurisdiction and it was expressly limited to Torah law, an erroneous decision would undermine that acceptance. The fact that the judgment nevertheless possessed force proves that an expert has independent judicial authority. This also clarifies R. Yerucham's statement that Rashi and Tosafot disagree with Raavad's rule that even a greater scholar cannot reverse. Rashi's entire interpretation is structured to answer the very question why R. Yosef did not reverse Mar Zutra. It therefore assumes that in an appropriate setting a greater authorized scholar does possess that power, unlike Raavad's broad practical limitation. As for Rashi's further implication that even an expert may have to pay where the parties expressly accepted him only to rule according to Torah law, Shach notes that Rambam in his Commentary to the Mishnah appears to disagree. The Yerushalmi also supports Rambam by establishing the Mishnah in such a case and nevertheless saying a publicly recognized expert is exempt. The broad language of the poskim likewise does not generally distinguish between types of acceptance in this manner. The better practical halachah therefore follows Rambam and the general authorities: an accepted recognized expert is exempt from payment even if the parties understood that he was to judge according to Torah law, unless there was some explicit contrary condition. Shach further concludes that according to authorities who allow a greater scholar to reverse, this applies only where the litigants accepted the original judge. If he judged without acceptance, Shach says even a greater scholar cannot reverse him, citing Baal HaMaor. As I explained earlier in discussing Mordechai, that reading is difficult unless the "expert" in Rav Nahman's statement means specifically an ordained judge. If a nonordained expert judged without acceptance and another greater scholar appears, why should the lack of acceptance make the judgment less reversible rather than more? The Mishnah's civil-law reversal rule is naturally parallel to capital law, where litigant acceptance is irrelevant. The more coherent structure remains that formal authority of the reversing judge, rather than the presence or absence of voluntary acceptance by the litigants, is central. In practice, because our era lacks semikhah and because major authorities disagree, one cannot simply reverse a settled discretionary judgment on the strength of later scholarly disagreement. The matter is governed by the established positions and by possession. Three lay judges present another case. Shach concludes that where three ordinary judges sat, whether or not the parties accepted them, even a greater scholar cannot reverse their discretionary ruling. His reason is sound in outcome even if his explanation is not. Otherwise errors of ordinary three-judge courts would never achieve finality. There will always be learned sages somewhere in Israel who can disagree. The litigant can say: "I accepted this beit din. I do not care what other scholars think. Perhaps the first court is correct and the later one mistaken." The accepted tribunal, not later intellectual disagreement, controls the case unless a true superior judicial authority exists. Where three judges include one fully qualified expert and the parties did not accept them, Baal HaMaor and Rosh dispute both payment and reversal. Baal HaMaor tends to exempt and allow reversal by a greater authority because the panel possesses sufficient judicial quality. Rosh tends to impose payment and preserve the judgment. Shach suggests Rosh's no-reversal position follows only because Rosh accepts Raavad that no greater scholar can reverse even a single accepted expert. I do not see why that follows. According to Baal HaMaor's own reasoning, reversal is tied to the absence of litigant loss because the judge can or cannot pay. If Rosh says these judges must pay, the very reason Baal HaMaor gives for reversal disappears. Thus even someone who otherwise allows reversal by a greater scholar can accept Rosh's conclusion in this specific panel because the compensation structure is different.
And now Ramban's objections to Baal HaMaor can be understood more carefully. In Perek HaKotev the Gemara says judges ruled like R. Tarfon and the decision was not reversed as an ordinary discretionary error. R. Chaim Yonah argues this is no difficulty because the Gemara says only "the ruling inclines toward R. Akiva," which is weaker than a fixed halachic rule. But Ramban can follow Ritva, who explains that even "the halachah follows R. Akiva against his colleague" was not always an explicitly fixed statement but can still define the error as one of judicial discretion rather than an explicit Mishnah. The point of Ramban's question is then powerful. If discretionary error can be reversed by a greater authority under the circumstances Baal HaMaor describes, why did that judgment remain fixed? Shach answers that the case may have involved three lay judges with no expert among them, where everyone agrees a greater scholar cannot reverse. R. Chaim Yonah objects that the Gemara's phrase "that judge" sounds like a fixed, qualified judge, especially in the environment of R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish. The language indeed leans that way. But one can defend Shach in a dilemma. If "judge" means a recognized expert, then according to Baal HaMaor he may have judged without acceptance and become personally liable; the statement that the earlier ruling stood can be understood through that payment structure. If "judge" does not necessarily imply full expertise, then the case can be a three-judge lay court. Either way the passage does not conclusively refute Baal HaMaor. Ramban also asks from Chullin's ruling that Rava must pay the value of the ox. If a three-judge context can be exempt under Baal HaMaor, why was payment required? Shach says Rava himself may have personally taken and given. But Shach elsewhere reasons that even a recognized expert can be exempt despite taking and giving under some readings, so this does not fully solve the case.
The better answer is that Ramban's challenge assumes a halachic teacher in issur v'heter is equivalent to a three-judge court in monetary law. Baal HaMaor need not accept that equivalence. In civil law, three judges receive jurisdiction because the claimant properly brings his dispute before them and Torah obligates them to adjudicate. In issur v'heter, where an expert gives unsolicited instruction without the owner having come to ask him, there is no comparable litigant acceptance or judicial obligation. If he voluntarily inserts himself and causes loss by an erroneous ruling, liability can remain. Thus Rava's case need not undermine Baal HaMaor. After all this analysis, the practical summary is as follows. Where the judge did not personally take and give, a possessor can certainly say kim li according to Rif and those who follow him that the judgment is reversible, because great authorities support that position. Even where Shach would say a greater scholar cannot reverse an unordained judge in our era, possession and the Rif line prevent extracting money solely on the basis of the contrary view. Where the judge personally took and gave, however, the balance shifts strongly toward finality. Rif and those who follow Rav Chisda say that after personal taking and giving even an expert's ruling is not reversed. Raavad and Rosh also deny ordinary contemporary reversal by a greater scholar, and Tosafot, Semag, Ramban, and Nimukei Yosef provide substantial support that in our era such reversal is unavailable. Therefore one cannot ordinarily claim kim li for reversal against this broad group. Likewise, where three judges included one genuine expert and the litigants did not accept them, the practical rule appears closer to Rosh and Ramban that if liability falls upon the judges, the judgment is not externally reversed merely because later scholars disagree. This is the safer practical conclusion.
And the judgment is not reversed. If that is the law in civil matters, then all the more so in issur v'heter one might think a second sage cannot reverse the ruling of the first sage who declared something impure or prohibited. This creates an apparent contradiction in Rama. Here Rama cites Rosh and Raavad that in our time even a greater scholar does not reverse an error in judicial discretion. Yet in Yoreh Deah siman 242, seif 31, Rama rules that in an error of judicial discretion a second sage may debate the first until the first retracts his own ruling. Those are Ran's words at the beginning of Perek Ein Ma'amidin, where Ran disagrees with Raavad and argues from Rif, who rules like Rav Chisda that where the judge did not personally take and give the matter can be reversed. But Rama here follows Rosh and Raavad against Rav Chisda. How then can Rama adopt Ran's leniency in issur v'heter? R. Tzvi Ashkenazi, responsum 53, answers that even according to the view that a judgment is not reversed merely because another scholar disagrees, some concrete implementation is still needed before the ruling becomes fixed. So long as no act has yet been done, the first sage can retract. I do not find this satisfactory. The Gemara says to R. Tarfon that two points apply: first, his mistake was in an explicit Mishnah and therefore the judgment is reversed; second, even if the error were in judicial discretion and therefore the judgment stood, he was a recognized expert and exempt. This implies that without his expert status he could have been liable in judicial discretion. According to R. Tzvi Ashkenazi, however, before any act was done the ruling would still be reversible. If so, even after the animal was later fed to the dogs by its owner, why should the judge become liable? The damage occurred only after a ruling that could still have been withdrawn. The same problem appears in Tosafot and Rosh's rule regarding one who declared the pure impure. They say that even according to the Sages the judge can be liable because his speech functions like an act, comparable to kohanim who disqualify sacrificial service through piggul. But if the ruling remains freely retractable until some later act occurs, the speech itself has not yet created irreversible damage and cannot resemble piggul, which immediately invalidates. Moreover, Tosafot and Rosh establish the case of "declared the pure impure" as one where the object was later touched with a source of impurity only because Rav Chisda requires actual taking and giving. If the owner's later act itself were enough to create finality, the precise role of the judge's own act would disappear. Therefore the distinction cannot simply be between before and after practical implementation.
What appears correct is to distinguish between external reversal and the first authority's own retraction. Rosh in Avodah Zarah writes concerning a sage who prohibited something and a second sage who wishes to permit it: the second should debate the first. If he can demonstrate an explicit error from Mishnah or Gemara, he may correct him. If the disagreement is only in judicial discretion, he should say, "I hold differently, but I do not permit what you prohibited, since the prohibition has already issued from your mouth." If the second sage's debate can never change the practical result unless he produces an explicit Mishnah, what purpose is there in encouraging the debate in a discretionary dispute? The answer is that if the first sage himself becomes convinced and admits that he erred, he may retract his own ruling. A beit din cannot ordinarily annul the act of another beit din unless it has superior authority. But the same beit din can certainly reconsider its own ruling when it recognizes its own error. Rambam in Hilchot Mamrim is precise: one beit din cannot annul the words of another without superior stature. The same court can revisit its own determination. This explains Rashi as well. Where the first judge himself says, "I erred," the problem in civil litigation is that the litigant may respond, "I do not believe you. Perhaps your first reasoning was correct and your current reasoning is mistaken." Rashi writes explicitly that the party need not obey the judge's attempted retraction. In issur v'heter, however, that credibility problem is absent. If the sage who originally prohibited the item now says, "I erred and the item is permitted," why would he be suspected of lying in order to permit a prohibition? Even an ordinary single witness is trusted in matters of prohibition, all the more so the very sage who gave the original ruling. Thus, where the first sage himself retracts after reconsideration, everyone can agree that the prohibition falls. This is different from a second sage unilaterally overruling the first. Now the positions align. Raavad and Rashba hold that a second sage cannot independently permit what the first sage prohibited merely through a stronger discretionary argument, even if he is greater. Ran is more willing to allow the greater sage to reverse. Rama does not need to decide that broader dispute in Yoreh Deah. He writes specifically that the second sage should debate the first "until he retracts." Once the original sage himself retracts, the item can be permitted according to everyone. This is exactly why Rosh says the second sage should argue with the first. The debate has a practical purpose: to bring the original authority to recognize and withdraw his own error. If he remains convinced of his original ruling, the second sage does not permit it merely on discretionary grounds. This also explains the expression that the questioner "made it a forbidden piece for himself." The owner who asked the first sage accepted that sage's ruling. Another sage cannot simply undo the owner's accepted prohibition. But once the first sage himself withdraws his ruling, the original acceptance falls with it because its basis has been removed. The owner accepted the first sage's halachic determination, not a result the sage himself now declares mistaken. Therefore the contradiction in Rama disappears. His Choshen Mishpat ruling concerns an external greater authority reversing a final discretionary civil judgment in our era. His Yoreh Deah ruling concerns persuading the original sage to retract his own ruling. There is no disagreement. The Ran's statement that he did not have the courage to act against Raavad concerns the broader case where the first sage does not retract and the second wishes to permit on his own authority. Rama does not adopt that leniency. This also explains Rosh's wording precisely and avoids the forced distinction between different kinds of judicial discretion suggested by later writers. The same applies to the incident of R. Tarfon. R. Tarfon accepted R. Akiva's explanation and recognized his error. R. Akiva's statement that R. Tarfon was an expert therefore covers the relevant consequence of liability, while the permissive or prohibitive ruling itself can be corrected through the first sage's own admission.
However, another question remains from Rama in Yoreh Deah siman 242, seif 32. Rama writes that where one sage permitted something, another sage cannot prohibit it after the first permissive ruling took effect if the dispute is merely one of judicial discretion. But our Mishnah says that one who "declared the impure pure" can be required to pay. Tosafot and Rosh explain the Mishnah in two branches. If the impure item was mixed into a majority of permitted produce and thereby became permitted, "what he did is done." If it was mixed in a manner that caused the larger mixture to become prohibited, the judge pays from his own property. According to Rama, once the first sage's permissive ruling took effect, why should the second sage's view matter at all? The produce should remain permitted. What monetary loss is there for the judge to pay? Even according to Taz's explanation that the first ruling must have been acted upon before it becomes fixed, here the owner acted upon it by mixing the produce with other permitted produce. That is precisely practical reliance on the first ruling. One could answer that the Mishnah speaks of a nonexpert sage. Rama's protection of an implemented permissive ruling may concern a genuine recognized expert whose halachic authority is stronger. A nonexpert's mistaken permission does not acquire the same finality, and a later sage can prohibit the resulting mixture, producing monetary loss. This is plausible. The Mishnah itself appears to concern a nonexpert in its first clause, since the later clause separately says, "if he was an expert to the public, he is exempt." It is therefore natural that the cases of declaring pure and impure speak primarily of the nonexpert. Alternatively, one can combine two factors. Where the first sage is a recognized expert and he does not admit error, his implemented permissive ruling is not displaced by another sage's discretionary disagreement. Where he himself admits the mistake, or where he lacks true expert status, the later prohibition can take effect. Thus the Mishnah's payment case remains possible. This matter still requires further study, and see what I have written at greater length in my Yoreh Deah novellae.
The judgment is reversed. Rosh writes that even if the opposing litigant is present and has sufficient property to repay what he received improperly, the judge remains exempt and what the judge wrongfully transferred can nevertheless be recovered through reversal of the judgment. The rule is "what he did is done" only in the category where the law leaves the result standing. Rosh rejects Baal HaItur's citation in the name of Rav Hai Gaon. Rav Hai is reported to hold that where a single nonexpert judge was accepted by the parties, so long as it remains possible to recover the property from the litigant who received it, the court recovers from that litigant even if the judge personally took and gave. Only where recovery from the litigant is impossible does the judge become liable for the personal transfer. Rosh understood Rav Hai as speaking about an error in judicial discretion. On that reading one would have to say that in this responsum Rav Hai followed Rif and Rav Chisda: without personal taking and giving the judgment is reversed. If the judge personally transferred the property, the judgment ordinarily stands and the judge pays only when recovery from the recipient is impossible. This would differ from another ruling quoted in Rav Hai's name, where he appears to follow Rav Nahman and Rav Sheshet and to impose liability through dina d'garmei even where the judgment is legally reversible but practical recovery is impossible. Such changes of position are not unprecedented in the writings of the Geonim. One statement can reflect an earlier view and another a later view. Shach refuses this explanation. He understands Rav Hai's present ruling to concern an error in an explicit Mishnah, where everyone agrees the judgment itself is reversed. Accordingly, so long as recovery from the recipient is possible, the judge certainly pays nothing because the erroneous transfer is undone. If the judge personally took and gave and the recipient can no longer return the property, the judge is liable as a direct tortfeasor. This interpretation is more plausible. At first glance one might still ask that other sources attribute to Rav Hai the view that R. Meir imposes dina d'garmei even without personal taking and giving. But there is no contradiction. That broader liability can concern a judgment which remains standing. In an error of explicit Mishnah, where the judgment itself is reversed, Rav Hai can agree that ordinary indirect judicial causation does not create payment liability unless the judge personally committed the damaging transfer. This accords with the authorities who distinguish errors in explicit Mishnah from the more common errors of judicial discretion. Shach's reading is therefore the stronger one.
The judgment is not reversed. Tur cites Ramah that if a judge ruled the innocent party liable, and before payment was made it became clear that the ruling was mistaken, even if the error was one of judicial discretion, we do not extract payment from the party who has not yet paid. His proof is from the incident in Zeh Borer concerning the person called "the mouse," where R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi held that a person does not ordinarily make a declaration merely so that his sons should not appear wealthy. On the basis of his ruling the heirs paid half. R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi died, and R. Chiya ruled that a person may indeed speak so as not to enrich the appearance of his sons. He exempted them from paying the remaining half, but he did not require return of the half already paid, saying, in effect, that an elder had already ruled. Ramah understood that case as an error in judicial discretion. If such an error always fixes the judgment immediately, then why did R. Chiya exempt the unpaid half? Necessarily, even though the prior ruling remains standing with respect to money already transferred, one does not compel payment that has not yet left the possessor's hands. Tur notes, however, that Rosh explains the incident differently. Rosh writes that R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi's ruling was not a true error in judicial discretion, because there had been no prior dispute among Tannaim or Amoraim on that precise point. He ruled by his own reasoning, and R. Chiya disagreed by a different reasoning. Tur therefore infers that according to Rosh, if there had been a genuine error in judicial discretion, the original judgment would have taken effect immediately even before payment, and the unpaid party would still be obligated to pay. Shach and Shaar Ephraim object to Tur. If the mistake is now clearly known, why should a person be forced to pay money that remains in his possession merely because an erroneous judgment had earlier been issued? They also reinterpret Rosh. According to them, Rosh was explaining why R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi himself, or his estate, was not liable as a mistaken judge. Since R. Chiya could not demonstrate that the earlier ruling was definitively wrong, there was no established judicial error for purposes of payment. That explanation is not satisfactory. Why assume R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi had not been accepted by the parties? The very fact that they came before him can itself constitute acceptance. Moreover, he may have sat with two others. And as a great sage, he certainly had authority from Rebbi, the Nasi, which in Eretz Yisrael functions like judicial authorization and can exempt from payment. There is no need for Rosh to introduce an elaborate explanation merely to exempt him or his heirs. Another answer offered is that Rosh quoted Rif's formulation because Rif rules like Rav Chisda that where the judge did not personally take and give, the judgment is reversed. Therefore Rosh had to explain why the earlier ruling was not simply reversed. This can also resolve an apparent contradiction in Rosh. In Zeh Borer he says this was not a true error in judicial discretion, while in Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot he gives this very incident as an example of such an error. But it is strained to say he used the phrase one way only to fit Rif and another way according to his own final halachah. The better explanation is found in Rosh's own words. Rosh writes that if a later scholar introduces a new reasoning not explicitly stated in Mishnah or Gemara, and the first judge, upon hearing it, acknowledges that it is correct, then the first ruling is treated like an error in an explicit Mishnah and is reversed. But if the first judge remains convinced of his original reasoning and there is merely a dispute of reasoning, then the matter is one of judicial discretion. That is exactly what occurred here. R. Chiya introduced a new logical distinction: just as a person may say something so as not to appear wealthy himself, he may say it so that his sons not appear wealthy. Had R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi been alive and admitted the force of R. Chiya's reasoning, his first ruling would have become an error in an explicit matter and would be reversed. Had he remained firm in his own reasoning, the case would be one of judicial discretion because the accepted direction of halachic reasoning followed R. Chiya. But R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi had already died. R. Chiya therefore could not know which of those two possibilities would have occurred. As to the half already paid, the recipients could say: perhaps R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi would have maintained his original reasoning. Then it was merely an error in discretion, and the judgment stood. We do not remove the money from them. As to the unpaid half, the heirs could say: perhaps had he heard R. Chiya's reasoning, he would have admitted the mistake. Then the original ruling would have been reversible as an error in an explicit matter. Since the money remains in our possession, the burden of proof lies on the party seeking payment. This explains Rosh precisely. His statement that the two sages merely differed in reasoning means there was no way to prove retrospectively that the first judge would have admitted error. And his description elsewhere of the incident as an example of judicial discretion refers to the possibility that R. Yishmael son of R. Yosi would have continued to disagree. There is therefore no contradiction.
And I found further support for Ramah's practical conclusion from Rosh's discussion in Perek Eizehu Neshekh concerning a case where Rav Pappa divided wine without his partner and the partner protested. Rosh and Tosafot ask why Rav Pappa needed to explain his reasoning only because the other party complained that the division was improper. Elsewhere, when a litigant asks for the reasons for a judgment, the court supplies them. At first glance there is no question. A clear error in an explicit Mishnah is uncommon and need not be suspected. If the judge erred only in judicial discretion, what would be gained by discovering the mistake if the judgment already stands? And Rav Pappa was a recognized expert whom the parties accepted, so he would ordinarily be exempt from payment. Therefore, absent the complaint, there would seemingly be no reason to provide an explanation. The fact that Rosh still finds the issue difficult indicates that identifying an error in judicial discretion can have practical effect even after a ruling where payment or transfer has not yet fully occurred. The possessor may retain what is still in his hands, as Ramah holds. One should not object from Tosafot and Rosh's discussion in Bava Kamma and Bechorot that the Mishnah "he ruled the innocent liable" is attributed to R. Meir because the judge's speech alone did not directly perform an act. If a ruling obligates payment even after the mistake is later identified, perhaps the words themselves should count as the damaging act. That is not so. Even if the litigant is legally obligated to pay until the ruling is reversed or corrected, an additional act of payment is still required. At the moment the judge speaks, no property has yet moved. This resembles false witnesses who obligate a litigant to pay. Their testimony can create an inescapable obligation, because they cannot retract their testimony, yet according to the Sages they can still be exempt from payment as indirect causation when no direct act occurred. The same is true of the judge. Thus the sugyot remain consistent. For practical law, Ramah's rule appears correct, especially where the money remains in the possessor's hands. One should not extract it when a serious error in judicial discretion has become apparent before payment. This also makes Shach's own position difficult. In seif 1 he argues against Tosafot, who say that according to R. Meir a judge can owe dina d'garmei even where the ruling is legally reversible, and Shach offers various novel answers. But if Shach accepts Ramah here, then where the ruling was discovered to be mistaken before payment, the possessor is exempt. If payment was later made, why should the judge suddenly become liable if the same judgment could have been resisted before payment? The case resembles R. Tarfon's cow, where we say that had the animal not been fed to the dogs the ruling could have been corrected. Thus Ramah's view strongly supports Tosafot and Rosh that according to R. Meir practical payment liability can remain even where the formal ruling was reversible. Shach's positions therefore sit uneasily with one another. Tur also writes that three experts, even without explicit acceptance, have a binding judgment and are exempt from payment. Shach claims this is not found in Rosh. But if, as Shach himself writes, three ordained judges are exempt because they are Torah judges, Rosh's rule follows naturally. Rosh writes in the first chapter of Sanhedrin that Chazal did not distinguish between ordained and unordained judges once the panel is genuinely expert. Tur therefore states his father's approach correctly. Shach further asks against Nimukei Yosef, who says that three ordained judges who personally take and give can become liable: why should they be worse than judges who received authorization from the Exilarch? The same question applies to Semag, who says an ordained judge without litigant acceptance can be liable. The answer is that semikhah and governmental authorization serve different primary purposes. Authorization from the Exilarch is given specifically to empower the judge to act coercively and to protect him from personal liability if he errs. That is the central function of the grant. Semikhah, however, primarily authorizes the judge to adjudicate penalties and to administer forms of corporal punishment and other judicial powers that require ordination. Its purpose is not chiefly to exempt the judge from monetary liability for ordinary judicial mistakes. Therefore semikhah does not automatically carry the same payment immunity as formal authorization from the Exilarch. This cannot be answered by saying the ordination lacked the Nasi's approval, because Rambam writes explicitly that semikhah requires the Nasi's authorization. Nor can the case be restricted to outside Eretz Yisrael, because the authorities discuss R. Tarfon, who was ordained and the incident occurred in Eretz Yisrael. Accordingly, Nimukei Yosef can say that three ordained judges who personally take and give remain liable when the parties did not accept them. But where the litigants voluntarily accepted them, the ordinary principle that the litigant's own choice caused the loss applies, and they are exempt even after taking and giving. This resolves the question of R. Tarfon as well. The owner came to him and accepted his ruling, so his expertise plus acceptance exempted him. Nimukei Yosef speaks where there was no such acceptance. The distinction is sound.
By force of the ban. The Ir Shushan writes that the same applies if they would have to pay a penalty. The Shach, seif katan 33, objects that a monetary penalty is not considered compulsion, as explained below in siman 388. But the comparison is not sound. There, where the case concerns an informer who acts wickedly, he certainly has no excuse because of monetary compulsion, for one should despise all wealth rather than do evil; therefore he is exempt only under threat to life. Here, however, he is being required to adjudicate between one person and another. Is he obligated to pay a penalty and diminish his own property merely to avoid judging? We have never heard such a thing. If so, each time he would have to pay a penalty, his wealth would not suffice. The Ir Shushan therefore spoke well, and the Gaon R. Yonah Teomim likewise agreed in his kuntres.
They should not pay, for what else could they have done? Shach cites Raavan that three lay judges chosen by the community may adjudicate, and if they err they need not pay. Shach limits this to a case where they did not personally take and give. If they did, he says they are liable. I am surprised. Raavan himself rules in the first chapter of Sanhedrin that a mistaken judge pays only where he personally takes and gives, following Rif, and that without such an act even an ordinary judge is exempt from payment. Therefore Raavan's separate exemption for judges selected by the community must necessarily include even a case where they did personally take and give. Otherwise it would add nothing at all. R. Chaim Yonah Teomim already made this objection to Shach. Shach asks: if the judges personally take and give, why say "what else could they have done?" Who compelled them to perform such an act? The answer follows what I wrote above. Where a litigant refuses to obey the ruling, the judges are obligated to enforce it. If they direct the beit din's agent to descend upon the litigant's assets and seize property, that is legally considered the judges' own taking and giving because their agent acts on their behalf. They cannot simply refuse enforcement. Thus "what else could they have done?" applies even to taking and giving in this legal sense. Raavan's exemption therefore includes that case. The matter is clear.
