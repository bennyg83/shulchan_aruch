An expert of beit din, etc. The Sema explains this as one who is both learned and possessed of sound judicial reasoning. The ordinary "expert" mentioned by the Mechaber in seif 2, who is exempt from payment when the parties accepted him, means according to Rambam one who is learned but does not possess that level of reasoning, and is therefore called merely an expert rather than an expert of beit din. The Tur, however, requires even there both learning and sound reasoning. The Shach objects that according to all views expert status always requires both learning and reasoning, and "of beit din" here is not precise. He also cites Rambam and Baal HaMaor as holding that for one who is learned but lacks sound judicial reasoning, even authority from the Exilarch is ineffective, whereas according to the Rosh and Tur it is effective. In Tumim I discussed this at length and concluded that Rambam recognizes three levels. One who is neither learned nor competent receives no authority even from the Exilarch. One who is learned and competent but has not become publicly recognized as an expert cannot adjudicate coercively unless he receives authority from the Exilarch, and if he errs he must pay even if the parties accepted him. One whose expertise has become publicly recognized may adjudicate coercively even without such authority or acceptance, but if he errs he must pay. If the parties accepted him, and all the more if he received authority, he need not pay. This explains the precise expression "expert of beit din." See there.
But they accepted, etc. See Tumim, where I concluded like Ramban that even accepting one who is not learned and competent is effective acceptance, contrary to Baal HaMaor. Still, if he errs, even where he personally took and transferred the property, the judgment is reversed, contrary to the Shach who disagrees. It appears that this is specifically where the litigant said, "Judge me according to Torah law." If he accepted him without qualification, the presumption is that he accepted him even if he would not judge accurately according to Torah law, and therefore the judgment is not reversed merely because he erred. See there.
The litigants accepted him, etc. The Mechaber did not alternatively say, "or he received authority from the Exilarch," because such authority is effective only for one who is learned. For one wholly unlearned it is ineffective, and a lengthy distinction would have been needed. He therefore stated acceptance by the litigants, which according to Ramban is effective even for one wholly unlearned. Sema.
And if he did not personally take and transfer. See Tumim, where I concluded that even according to this view, if by order of beit din the court officer descended to the litigant's property and paid the opposing party, this too is treated as though the judges personally took and transferred it, since the officer acts at their command and a person's agent is like himself.
He shall pay from his own funds. This is Rambam's view, disagreeing with Rif on this point. Rif holds that since the judgment is reversed the judge does not pay. See Tumim, where I explained Rambam's reasoning at length.
And some say that even if he did not personally take and transfer, what he did stands, etc. In Tumim I discussed at length the reason the Tur and Rama omitted the rule that where there is an expert greater than the judge the judgment is reversed, and explained that this is because we lack formally ordained judges today. I concluded that although many poskim disagree and hold that even today, if an expert errs and there is a greater authority, the judgment may be reversed, nevertheless where the judge personally took and transferred the property, the Mechaber, following Rif, Rambam, and their supporters, also holds that the judgment is not reversed. The possessor therefore cannot say "I rely on" the authorities who allow reversal against all these major authorities. Where the judge did not personally take and transfer, however, not only where a greater expert exists can one rely on the authorities who hold that even without formal ordination a greater authority can reverse the judgment, but even where there is neither an expert nor a greater authority one may say, "I rely on Rif and his supporters," who always reverse the judgment so long as the judge did not personally take and transfer, without distinguishing expert from nonexpert. The Shach's ruling against Rif and his supporters is not sufficiently conclusive to prevent such a claim. If reversal is impossible, Rambam has already ruled that the judge must pay. Although Rif and his supporters disagree, it is difficult to allow the possessor to rely on them, since Tosafos and their supporters independently require payment under the law of dina d'garmi, Rambam rules similarly, and neither Mechaber nor Rama cites the opposing view. If three judges sat, one of them being an expert, without the parties accepting them, I likewise concluded that although Baal HaMaor exempts them from payment, the primary law follows Ramban and Rosh that they must pay. This is the more reasonable view. See Tumim, where I discussed it at length. I also concluded there that in monetary law, even if a greater authority cannot reverse the judgment, and even if the expert judge himself later says, "I erred," his statement does not void the ruling, because the successful litigant may answer, "You have no authority to undo it. I do not believe you, and I continue to follow your original ruling." In issur and heter, however, if an established decisor prohibited something and afterward says that he erred, he is believed and the item becomes permitted according to all. If he does not admit error but other authorities, even greater ones, say he erred, their view does not undo his ruling, just as in monetary law, and the item remains prohibited. If the original decisor was not an expert, then even his own later admission that he erred has no legal effect and the prohibition remains. With this I resolved Rama's words in Yoreh Deah siman 242, seif 32. See Tumim and my Yoreh Deah novellae at length. If an expert sage permitted something through an error in discretionary judgment and does not retract his view, Rama in Yoreh Deah holds that another sage has no power to prohibit it. See Tumim, where I explained that this reconciles the Rosh and Rama with the Mishnah, "he judged the case, declared the impure pure," etc.
And he shall pay from his own funds, etc. Even if the successful litigant remains present and possesses funds with which to repay, that litigant is exempt because the judgment stands. The judge must pay from his own pocket. The Rosh concludes this, contrary to Rav Hai Gaon. The Shach writes that even Rav Hai Gaon agrees. See Tumim.
And the judgment is not reversed, since he erred in discretionary judgment. If authorities disagree in reasoning and common practice follows the other view, this too is an error in discretionary judgment. But if the judge himself agrees to the other reasoning, and he is an expert whose admission has legal force, it is treated as an error in an explicit rule. If he issued a ruling and died, and only afterward another scholar developed the opposing reasoning, one certainly cannot extract money by saying that had the first lived he might have admitted the point. The possessor may say that perhaps even had he lived he would have continued to disagree, in which case it would remain an error in discretionary judgment and the judgment would stand. I concluded this in Tumim in explanation of the Rosh. If the judge obligated the party who was truly entitled to prevail, the party has not yet paid, and it is then established that the judge erred in discretionary judgment, Ramah and the Tur disagree. Ramah holds that payment cannot now be extracted because the judgment was erroneous, while the Tur holds that since the judgment stands the losing party must pay. The Sema rules like the Tur, but the Shach and Shaar Ephraim decide like Ramah, and I too concluded so in Tumim, although I supplied support for the Tur and explained his words as far as possible. If three experts sat, even today when we lack formal ordination, and the parties did not accept them, the Tur writes that the judgment stands and they are exempt from payment. See Tumim, where I asked for the Tur's source.
But if they accepted him, etc. If a litigant expressly said, "Judge me according to Torah law," see Tumim above, where I wrote that according to Rashi it appears that even an expert who errs must pay, since he acted contrary to the condition upon which he was accepted. The Shach cites proof from the Yerushalmi and from Rambam that even in that case he is exempt. This is unquestionably the correct view.
In these times, when they compel, etc. The Shach questions this, because most poskim do not agree with the rule in that form. Everything depends on the time and the nature of the communal enactment.
By force of the cherem. The same applies where they must pay a fine if they refuse to serve as judges. The Shach objects, but see Tumim, where I concluded that the Levush is correct.
Against their will, etc. The Shach holds that the community may appoint only a panel of three, but lacks authority to appoint a single judge, or a relative or otherwise disqualified person. Only the king may appoint a sole judge, as stated at the end of siman 3. He therefore asks why Rama needs the reason that the appointees had no choice. Since the case is necessarily one of three lay judges whom the public accepted, such judges are already exempt if they err. The Shach answers with difficulty. But according to the Bach above, who holds that the community may also appoint a single judge, the law is necessary. An unqualified sole judge remains liable if he errs even when accepted by the parties. Rama therefore teaches that in this special case he is nevertheless exempt, because he had no reasonable alternative.
They may retract, etc. The Sema explains that even after money has already been extracted pursuant to their ruling, although an error in discretionary judgment ordinarily leaves the judgment standing, where these judges were appointed by the community the ruling may be reversed afterward, because the community did not appoint them on the understanding that an error be perpetuated. If so, this must refer only to judges appointed by the community. In the earlier case, where the parties expressly accepted a judge both for correct judgment and for error, he is certainly no worse than any other judge, and once the ruling has been executed an error in discretionary judgment leaves it standing. The Shach objects that judges appointed by the community are likewise no worse after execution than ordinary judges who err in discretionary judgment, whose ruling stands. He therefore explains that Rama speaks before money has actually been extracted. At that stage they may retract when they discover the error, because the community never authorized them to maintain an acknowledged mistake before executing it. If they refuse to retract and nevertheless cause the money to be extracted, they must pay as tortfeasors, since they should have withdrawn the ruling. This explanation works well according to Ramah, who holds that before execution we do not say that an erroneous judgment nevertheless stands. According to the Tur, however, who holds that the judgment stands even before actual payment, the Shach has not answered the difficulty, and one must use the Sema's explanation. It appears that reversal is possible only when authorities greater than these judges determine that they erred. The judges cannot reverse merely because they themselves now think their reasoning mistaken, for since they are not ordained, who says that the later reasoning is better, and who establishes the first ruling as erroneous? Thus a greater beit din must determine their error. This is also implied by Responsa Maharam, where the greater court is the body that establishes the mistake. This is clear.
And the third one's share is lost. The Shach cites Kesef Mishneh that according to Rif, the judgment is reversed as to the third share, because Rif holds that where the judge does not pay the judgment is reversed to avoid loss to the litigant. It is difficult to say that part of one judgment is reversed while part remains in force. One should not ask how the third judge could ever have personally transferred the property so that an exemption from payment would be relevant, since Rif imposes liability only where the judge personally took and transferred, while the third judge did not agree with the decision and no one forced him to handle the property. This supports what I wrote in several places: if a judge orders the court officer to descend to the litigant's property, that is considered personal taking and transfer, because the officer acts as the judge's agent. Thus the case can readily arise where beit din issued an order to the officer to seize assets, as courts are obligated to direct their officer to execute the judgment, even though the third judge did not agree. The Sema writes that Rama omitted the rule that if one of the two judges who erred was an expert he alone must pay the entire amount, since the third judge was unnecessary because a single expert can adjudicate. The reason for the omission is that the Rosh holds that today an expert does not adjudicate alone. The Shach disagrees and holds that although an expert may not initially sit alone, if he did so his judgment is valid and he bears the resulting liability.
