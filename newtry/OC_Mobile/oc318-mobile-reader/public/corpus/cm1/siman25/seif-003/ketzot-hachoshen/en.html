Or he was not an expert. The Shach writes that even a total ignoramus, if accepted by the parties, has a valid judgment. Baal HaMaor disagrees and holds that acceptance cannot validate one who knows nothing at all. Such a person is called an idolatrous tree placed beside the altar, and it is forbidden to judge before him even by consent. Ramban disagrees with Baal HaMaor in another direction. He holds that if such a completely ignorant person happened to rule correctly, the judgment can stand; but if he erred, then even if he personally transferred the property the judgment is reversed. The Shach objects that once a discretionary ruling ordinarily stands, why should ignorance matter? The answer is that a discretionary ruling stands only because it was issued through a legally recognized act of adjudication. If one is completely unlearned, he does not have the status of a judge at all. His discretionary error is therefore like an error in an explicit Mishnah, which is automatically reversed. As I wrote in siman 22, ordinary acceptance can cure a single deficiency by substituting a layman for an expert, and a formal kinyan certainly strengthens the acceptance. Baal HaMaor's contrary view rests on the separate prohibition against appointing one entirely unfit to judge. According to him, that statutory defect cannot be cured even by consent.
If he personally handled the property. This is the view of Rif and Rambam following Rav Chisda. Rif explains that the sugya in Bekhorot, where Rabbi Ilai in Rav's name establishes the Mishnah as a case in which the judge personally transferred the property, follows Rav Chisda. Ramban in Milchamot responds that Rabbi Ilai can agree with Rav Sheshet. The need to establish personal handling there concerns only the clause, "he shall pay from his own funds," because the Gemara wishes to establish the Mishnah according to the Sages who reject liability for direct causation. The fact that the judgment itself stands need not depend upon personal handling. In defense of Rif we should first note a difficulty in the sugya. To establish payment under the Sages, Rabbi Ilai says that where the judge wrongly exempts a liable party, he had a pledge in hand and returned it. But damaging a creditor's pledge is ordinarily only damage to a lien. In Bava Kamma concerning an innocuous ox, if the creditor can be paid money instead, slaughtering the ox is described as damaging another's lien. Even according to our law that such damage can create liability, that is itself because we adjudicate direct causation. How then does personal return of collateral help establish liability according to the Sages, who reject direct causation? The fact that a creditor acquires some interest in collateral does not make him full owner for all purposes, as shown by his exemption for unavoidable accidents. Responsa Maimoniyot similarly treats handing over pledged property to a gentile as damaging the creditor's lien. The answer is that according to Tosafot and Rosh, who treat operative judicial speech as direct damage, wrongly exempting the debtor already directly destroys the debt, and the Gemara's references to returning collateral or touching pure food are merely parallel illustrations. Rif, however, does not treat speech alone as direct damage. He must explain the priests who create piggul by saying that the speech works together with an actual sacrificial service, such as slaughtering or sprinkling. Accordingly, under Rav Chisda, the judge's personal act upon the collateral combines with his legal ruling to make the erroneous judgment effective. Once the judgment is thereby finalized, the debtor is actually discharged from the debt. The judge has not merely damaged the lien on the pledge; through the act and ruling together he has caused the body of the debt itself to be lost. Destroying the substantive debt is direct damage. This is analogous to piggul, where words take effect through a simultaneous sacrificial act. By contrast, if Rav Sheshet were correct and the erroneous judgment already stood through speech alone, the later handling of the collateral would add nothing except damage to a lien, which according to the Sages would be exempt. The Bekhorot sugya therefore supports Rif: Rabbi Ilai's establishment of personal handling is meaningful precisely because the operative act is what causes the judgment itself to stand under Rav Chisda. This is a strong defense of Rif's position.
And if he did not personally handle it. It remains uncertain whether the judge must handle the entire amount or whether an act concerning only part causes the whole judgment to stand. Ramban says that the judge's power is not strong enough for the judgment to stand through speech alone until he performs an act upon his ruling. This implies that any substantive act may suffice to transform the matter from mere speech into an operative judgment. If the case concerns collateral securing part of the debt, and the judge takes that collateral and returns it to the debtor, this act demonstrates that in the judge's ruling the creditor is entitled to nothing. Had even a perutah remained due, he would not have returned the pledge. Therefore the entire judgment certainly stands. In a case where the judge wrongly obligates an exempt person and personally transfers only part of the money, the matter is less clear. Still, it appears more likely that even handling part counts as the required operative act and causes the whole ruling to stand. This also explains Rav Chisda's establishment of "he declared pure food impure" as a case where a sheretz touched it. If the sheretz touched only some of the produce, the judge's operative act can cause his ruling concerning the whole mixture to stand. Rashi's language, that he cast it upon the produce, can readily refer to part of them. The untouched produce can then be rendered impure through the legal ruling, even though direct contact would not independently transmit indefinitely. A similar principle applies in the episodes of Rabbi Chiya and Rabbi Yishmael: where the litigant already changed possession based on the judge's ruling, the possessor can say he follows the prevailing legal view. But if the judge personally acted upon even part, the ruling can become fixed more broadly.
Whether for a correct ruling or for an error. It appears that even if the parties did not add the phrase, "and you are exempt," the judge is exempt from payment. Once they explicitly accepted him "whether for a correct ruling or for an error," this resembles saying, "Tear my garment," where according to the accepted law the owner can exempt the actor even without expressly adding "and you are exempt," provided the object did not come into the actor's hand as a custodian. According to Baal HaMaor, however, one who says, "Tear my garment," remains liable unless the owner explicitly says, "and you are exempt." On that approach, the judge too might remain liable unless the parties expressly add that exemption. The Bavli in Sanhedrin asks, according to the view that two judges have no valid judgment, from the Mishnah saying that one who judged improperly nevertheless has his act stand and pays. It answers that the parties accepted him. The Gemara then asks why he pays if they accepted him, and answers that they said, "Judge us according to Torah law." The Jerusalem Talmud asks similarly and answers that they expressly said, "You are accepted by us on condition that you judge according to Torah law." This suggests a disagreement. The Bavli assumes that ordinary acceptance is itself conditioned upon Torah law unless the parties say otherwise. The Jerusalem Talmud assumes that ordinary acceptance means acceptance even for error, unless they expressly condition Torah law. If Baal HaMaor is correct that an instruction to damage does not exempt without express release, then even acceptance "for error" would not necessarily exempt payment. This leaves the Yerushalmi difficult. The matter requires study.
Whom the community compels. Even here it appears that today, now that the custom has spread for judges to take payment for their rulings, they should be held to a higher standard. Although communal coercion under ban can make their service involuntary, a paid expert butcher is liable despite the fact that his underlying mistake is treated as unavoidable. A judge receiving compensation should similarly be expected to exercise greater care. See what I wrote above in seif katan 6.
And they followed the two. Rosh rules that if two judges ruled one way and the third dissented, and the majority later prove mistaken, the dissenting judge does not share their liability. He can say, "Had you agreed with me and exempted him, there would have been no loss." The two majority judges likewise do not pay the dissenting judge's share, because they can say, "Without you the judgment would not have been completed." This requires analysis under Rabbi Natan's rule that when two causes jointly inflict damage and one cannot be collected from, the other sometimes pays the deficiency. The classic case is an ox that pushes another into a pit, or a person and ox that jointly damage. Tosafot explains that each can be regarded as having caused the entire damage. If one party is exempt and cannot be collected from, the other can therefore bear the deficiency. Why should the two majority judges not similarly pay the dissenting judge's share, since no collection can be had from him? Maharshal, following Hagahot Maimoniyot, explains that Rabbi Natan's rule operates where each actor can be considered to have produced the entire damage. In the ox and pit case, there are versions of the sugya. According to one, each cause is viewed as doing the whole damage. According to another, each does only half, but the owner of the pit is charged with the remaining loss because the victim was found in his pit and the final completion of the damage was through the pit. In cases where neither cause can be viewed as having done the entire damage and there is no comparable "I found my ox in your pit" rationale, the deficiency does not shift. Accordingly, in the three judge case, without the third judge the two could not have finalized the judgment. They cannot be said to have independently caused the whole damage, and Rabbi Natan's rule would not apply. Yet Tosafot in Bava Kamma often treats causes as each producing the whole damage precisely because without one another there would have been no damage. That seems to revive the difficulty. Moreover, even under Maharshal's more limited formulation, the two majority judges did produce the final legal act. Once a majority of the panel reached a ruling, the third's dissent did not itself cause the operative outcome. This resembles the pit owner who completes the damage. Why then should the two not bear the entire loss when the dissenting judge is exempt? I have not found a satisfactory resolution.
