And there are those who disagree. The Sema writes that even three experts accepted by the parties must pay, because according to this view Rabbi Meir applies dina degarmi even in such a case. The Bach attributes this to the Rosh and Tur. This is plainly mistaken. According to the Rosh and Tur, where an accepted expert errs in discretionary judgment, his ruling stands and he is exempt even when reversal is possible. All the more so is he exempt when reversal is impossible, and certainly for an error in an explicit law. The Mishnah in Bekhorot likewise says that an expert is exempt where restoration is impossible. Even where an ordinary judge is liable as a damager, expertise exempts him. The moneychanger case similarly shows that an expert is exempt under Rabbi Meir. The Rosh’s own discussion also implies that an expert remains exempt where reversal is impossible. The Bach’s distinction between governmental authorization and acceptance by the parties is untenable, because acceptance by the litigants themselves should be at least as effective. The Tur’s reference to an “expert” here must therefore mean one who was not accepted, and Rabbeinu Yerucham can be understood the same way. They did not restate the qualification because they had already established that an accepted expert is exempt even for discretionary error, all the more so for an explicit error.
As to practical law, the “disagreeing opinion,” namely the Rosh and Tur following Tosafot and Mordechai, is not convincing. They hold that the sugya follows the Sages, but that Rabbi Meir, who applies dina degarmi, imposes liability in every case. The wording does not support this. The Baal HaMaor observes that the Talmud says, “You did nothing,” not merely, “You caused only indirect harm,” indicating that even one who applies dina degarmi would exempt him. Otherwise Rav Hamnuna could not have challenged Rav Sheshet, since Rabbi Akiva might follow Rabbi Meir. The forced interpretations required by Tosafot and the Rosh are substantial. The primary view is that of the Rambam, Rif, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Baal HaIttur, Rashba, and Shiltei Giborim: even according to Rabbi Meir, an error in an explicit law is not actionable where the judge did not physically cause the irreversible loss. Tosafot elsewhere also imply this. The comparison to showing a coin to a moneychanger is distinguishable. Once the coin was shown, there was no reason to show it to another expert, and liability arises only where the customer expressly or evidently relied upon the moneychanger. That reliance is effectively an undertaking to bear the loss unless the expert says not to rely upon him. A judge, by contrast, may be compelled by conscience to state what he believes the law requires and cannot safely tell the litigants not to rely upon him, lest they act unlawfully. His silence therefore does not imply acceptance of liability.
Tosafot further ask from the case where a judge declared impure produce pure and the owner mixed it with his produce. They infer that Rabbi Meir must impose liability even though, had the produce remained before us, the ruling could have been reversed. With respect, their analysis is strained. They first ask why the ruling stands and why the judge pays, before the Gemara ultimately explains that the owner mixed the produce. Their answer distinguishes whether it was mixed into a majority of pure produce or a minority, but this is highly forced. Moreover, even an intentional physical act of rendering something impure is an unrecognizable form of damage for which one acting inadvertently is exempt, and no error is more inadvertent than a sincere judicial mistake. Their answer that the judge is treated as intentional because he should have anticipated that the owner would mix the produce is likewise difficult.
The sugya can be explained differently, removing these difficulties. Initially, the case of “declared the impure pure” does not involve the judge physically transferring or mixing anything. It parallels “obligated the exempt party”: a buyer asks a sage whether he may purchase the seller’s produce as pure; the sage incorrectly declares it pure; the buyer relies upon him and purchases it; later the error becomes known. The ruling stands insofar as the seller sold it under a presumption of purity and need not return the price, while the sage compensates the buyer, like a moneychanger who misidentifies a coin. Only when the Gemara later insists upon a case of personal transfer must “declared the impure pure” be interpreted as the judge himself mixing it with other produce. This explanation yields a new practical rule: if a sage merely declares impure produce pure and the owner then mixes it, the sage is exempt even for an error in discretionary judgment. He did nothing actionable, and the case is no worse than one who inadvertently causes unrecognizable ritual damage. Thus, if a sage today declares another’s wine permitted, the owner mixes it with other wine, and the entire mixture is later found prohibited, the sage is exempt whether the error concerned an explicit law or discretionary judgment. This appears correct.
Tosafot, the Rosh, and Mordechai also ask from the Mishnah: one who is not an expert examined a firstborn animal, and it was slaughtered at his direction; the animal must be buried and he pays. Why not say that had it not been slaughtered, the mistake could have been corrected and he accomplished nothing? One could answer, as Tosafot do, that “slaughtered at his direction” is imprecise and means he slaughtered it himself, but unnecessary strained readings should be avoided. Rather, the phrase is exact: the slaughterer told him, “I will slaughter it in reliance upon you,” and he instructed him to proceed. He is therefore liable because the animal was slaughtered expressly on his instruction, as though he slaughtered it with his own hands. This also explains why the Gemara does not prove from this clause alone that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. Even the Sages impose liability in such a case, because once slaughter occurs at his express direction there is no remedy. This differs from the moneychanger case, where the Sages exempt despite express reliance, because there the coin could still have been returned had it remained available; the expert did not necessarily accept liability. Here the instruction immediately creates irreversible loss.
Finally, Tosafot, the Rosh, and Mordechai compare the case to priests who intentionally render sacrificial service invalid through a disqualifying intention, who are liable even according to the Sages. They infer that where a judicial ruling stands, such as an error in discretionary judgment, the judge should likewise be liable. I do not understand the comparison. A priest who introduces a disqualifying intention causes damage at the moment of slaughter; his thought operates through the physical act of slaughter and is equivalent to direct action. A judge who merely rules does not compel the litigant to act and performs no physical act. The Rashba similarly distinguishes a disqualifying intention during slaughter from a mere mental intention unaccompanied by action. We have therefore established that the primary law is exemption, contrary to the opinion cited by the Rama.
