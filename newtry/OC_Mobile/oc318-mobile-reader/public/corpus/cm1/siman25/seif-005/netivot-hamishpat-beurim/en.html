And specifically. See Shach citing Behag that even though property later became revealed, the compromise remains. This cannot refer to property that proved the original denial false in a way that would have changed the compromise. Siman 12:14 states that if one denied and compromised, and afterward witnesses establish the claim, he may retract from the compromise. Therefore Behag must mean that property later became known which neither side knew about at the time, so the original negotiation itself was not mistaken. But if the compromise payment was given solely to buy off an oath and afterward part of the very claimed money is established, the compromise was made in error and must be adjusted. For example, if one claimed a deposit of one thousand gold coins and compromised the custodian's oath for five hundred, and the thief is later found with five hundred, the depositor must return the amount paid for compromise to the extent appropriate. It becomes clear that the oath had concerned only the remaining amount, and had the judges known this they would have compromised for less.
