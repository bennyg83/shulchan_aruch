Throughout the world. Baer Heitev cites Nimukei Yosef that where the authorities are divided and no accepted practice resolves the issue, a judge may rule as his own understanding inclines. Chut HaShani, siman 60, discusses two judges who jointly rendered a judgment. One litigant later protested that they had erred, and one judge came to agree, but the other refused even to hear reconsideration, arguing that because the written judgment had already been issued it would be demeaning to beit din to retract. The first wished to submit the matter to a leading sage. Chut HaShani responds that if the second judge admits he erred and is merely embarrassed to retract, he must be forcefully compelled to correct the judgment, as Shevuot teaches that a judge must not become an advocate for his own prior words. If he denies error, one distinguishes. If the error concerns an explicit rule stated plainly by the poskim, or a matter of judicial discretion where the general course of halachic decision clearly runs against him, he is compelled to reverse the ruling or must pay from his own funds. But if it is a genuine dispute of discretion, such as two poskim disagreeing with no established consensus, the judge may say, 'I stand by my original reasoning,' since Sema in the name of Nimukei Yosef permits a judge in such a case to follow the view that appears correct to him. He may therefore prevent the other judge from sending the parties' claims and responses to another decisor, for this resembles shuda deDayanei. If the incident occurred in a place without scholars capable of determining whether the error is explicit, a clear error of discretion, or merely an unresolved discretionary issue, the communal leaders should compel submission of the pleadings to a great beit din acceptable to them or to the litigants, and that authority's ruling should control.
