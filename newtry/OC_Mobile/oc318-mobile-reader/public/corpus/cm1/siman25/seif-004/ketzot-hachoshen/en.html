The Sema disagrees with Kesef Mishneh and holds that even where the judge did not personally transfer the property, the judges must first pay the injured litigant and then recover from the person who received the money. In my view Kesef Mishneh is correct. If one shows a coin to a money changer, the appraisal is wrong, but the coin can still be returned, the money changer is certainly exempt because no loss has yet occurred. Here too, if the judge did not personally transfer the property but only caused the litigant to pay, he is at most a causer. So long as the recipient can return the money, there is no completed damage. Personal handling is different because the judge then directly moved the property from one domain to another and can be treated as the immediate damager, so the injured party can look only to him. If the recipient went overseas and recovery now requires expenses, the case resembles one who pushes another's coin into the sea and it can be recovered only by hiring a diver. According to the Shulchan Aruch in siman 386, the damager must pay the recovery expense. According to Rama, he is exempt because the body of the coin was not damaged. So too, according to Rama, where the judge did not personally transfer the money and merely caused the payment, he should be exempt from the travel or recovery expenses. If the judge did personally handle it, he is more like one who physically takes another's coin and throws it away, and liability is stronger. Even according to the Shulchan Aruch, which imposes the diver's expense under direct causation, our judge who did not physically handle the property may be only a cause of a cause, so there is room to exempt him from expenses. Where no expense is necessary and the recipient can simply return the money, the judge is certainly exempt.
