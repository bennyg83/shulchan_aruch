He must pay like any person who causes damage, etc. Kesef Mishneh asks why Rambam calls this merely causative damage when the judge is directly damaging, since he took and transferred the property himself. He also asks why Rambam repeats "he shall pay," after already saying that he must pay from his own pocket even where reversal is possible. Kesef Mishneh therefore proposes correcting the Rambam's text so that the cases divide as follows: if he ruled improperly and did not personally take and transfer the property, the judgment is reversed; if reversal is impossible, etc. According to that reading, where the judge personally took and transferred the property, the litigant first looks to the judge, and the judge may then recover from the recipient. Where the judge did not personally transfer it and the defendant himself paid based on the ruling, the defendant first seeks recovery from the recipient, and only if recovery is impossible does the judge pay, because his mistaken ruling caused the loss. Kesef Mishneh says that even without changing the text it must be interpreted this way. At the end of siman 3 the Tur cites Rambam according to the text before us. In Derishah I wrote that it is difficult to emend all copies of Rambam and the Tur, and Rambam's plain wording does not support Kesef Mishneh's interpretation. In my view Rambam does not distinguish here between whether the judge personally transferred the money. This parallels his view concerning error in an explicit mishnah. There both cases are exempt because he did not intend damage; here both are liable because his conduct counts as intended damage. When Rambam says, "if he took and gave," he may mean simply that the property actually left the defendant's hand and reached the claimant. In that event the judges must reimburse the defendant even if the defendant himself physically paid, and the judges may then recover from the claimant if they wish. Earlier, where the defendant has not yet transferred anything, there is no payment obligation; the parties simply return and litigate before another beit din. Rambam repeats "he shall pay" to teach that where recovery from the claimant is impossible, the judge bears the loss. This explanation preserves the received text. It also explains why the Tur cites Rambam without objection. Although Rif's text in the first chapter of Sanhedrin appears to support Kesef Mishneh, Rambam and the Tur's received wording seems better explained as I have written. The Mechaber too apparently remained uncertain and therefore copied Rambam's words without alteration. Beit Yosef likewise did not mention the proposed emendation.
For this is treated as one who intended to cause damage. Rambam writes this consistently with Rif. Rif holds that Rabbi Meir's rule making one liable for causative damage even where the act was inadvertent, mistaken, or compelled applies to ordinary torts because a person is always forewarned, but not to a judge who intended to adjudicate and erred. Therefore Rambam explains that a person who is neither learned nor analytically competent and nevertheless enters to judge is different. Such conduct is treated as intentional damage and he is liable. This also explains why the Tur, when citing Rambam at the end of siman 3, omitted the phrase "for this is one who intended to damage," because the Tur follows the Rosh, who holds that Rabbi Meir's rule concerning causative damage applies to judicial causation as well.
