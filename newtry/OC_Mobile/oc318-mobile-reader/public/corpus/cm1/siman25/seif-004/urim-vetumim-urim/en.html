Before beit din. This means before another beit din, because these persons are not legally a beit din. Practical differences include the thirty-day judicial payment period, entitlement to profits, *shuda deDayanei*, and many other procedural consequences. Shach.
And if he has no means to restore, etc. Kesef Mishneh adds an omitted clause to Rambam and reads: if the judge did not physically transfer the property, the ruling is reversed; if it cannot be restored, the judge pays. Shach agrees, and this reading is correct. Sema labors to preserve the older text without amendment, but his explanation is not convincing. The proper structure is this: if the judge physically transferred the property, even if the successful litigant is present and able to repay, the losing party's claim lies against the judge, who may then recover from the litigant who improperly received the money. If the judge did not physically transfer it, then as long as recovery from the litigant remains possible, the litigant himself must restore it and there is no claim against the judge. If restoration from the litigant is impossible, the judge must pay as one who directly caused the loss. This is clear.
He pays as one who caused damage. Shach considers limiting this to a case where the judge used coercion. Without coercion, perhaps even Rif would exempt him because he was not a real judge and his words alone were meaningless. See Shach's lengthy discussion. The substance is that where such an unauthorized person actually compels execution, even Baal HaMaor, who is more lenient than Rif, can agree that he must pay because his coercive act caused the loss.
