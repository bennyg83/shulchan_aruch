He did not intend to cause damage. The Sema writes as follows.
1. See Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh, who infer from Rambam's language, "although he caused damage," that Rambam discusses only a case where the judge did not himself render the item impure or feed it to dogs with his own hands. If he acted directly, he is an actual damager, and in that case Rif and Rambam agree that he must pay even where he erred concerning an explicit Mishnah. In Derishah, however, the Sema says he proved that Rif and Rambam hold that even an act done with the judge's own hands is still classified here as causing damage. The Bach likewise infers from Rambam's unqualified language that he includes a case where the judge personally took the property and transferred it, as the Tur supposedly understood Rambam. In my humble opinion this is not the primary view. Neither Rif nor Rambam holds so, and the Tur did not derive this from Rambam, as I will explain. First, I already proved above from the Gemara that even Rav Chisda obligates a judge who personally took and transferred the property, even when he erred regarding an explicit Mishnah, where restoration is impossible. Certainly Rav Nachman and Rav Sheshet agree. Moreover, Ramban writes in Sefer HaMilchamot, chapter Echad Dinei Mamonot: "Certainly, if the judge personally took and transferred the property and the money can no longer be restored, I concede that even one who erred regarding an explicit Mishnah must pay, like one who erred in discretionary judgment. So too appears to be the view of our great master," meaning the Rif. This indeed appears to be Rif's position. As I demonstrated above, Rif holds that whenever liability arises under dina d'garmi, the judge is liable even for an error regarding an explicit Mishnah. When the judge personally takes and transfers the property, he has at least caused the loss, because he had no right to place his own hands upon it. This is also evident from Rif himself. Tosafot and Mordechai in chapter Echad Dinei Mamonot likewise write that one who personally takes and transfers the property is liable even when he erred regarding an explicit Mishnah, because he performed a substantial act. The same is explicit from Tosafot in chapter Elu Treifot, which I cite below in seif katan 5, letter 2. It also appears explicitly from Rashi in chapter Echad Dinei Mamonot and in chapter Ad Kamah. Nimukei Yosef writes: "Everything we have said applies when the judge did not personally take and transfer the property. If he performed the act with his own hands, he is liable, even if there were three ordained judges and even if he erred concerning an explicit Mishnah and has no means to pay, for he is a damager. So I found written, and Ramban likewise explained." Rambam's view also appears to be this, as Beit Yosef writes. His use of the phrase "caused damage" implies that he did not personally take and transfer the property, for if he did so he is an actual damager. Beit Yosef, Kesef Mishneh, and Tosafot Yom Tov at the beginning of chapter Zeh Borer, however, say that the Tur understood Rambam to exempt even where the judge personally took and transferred, and the Bach says likewise. In my humble opinion they did not understand the Tur correctly. On the contrary, the Tur adds to Rambam's language the words, "and they fed it to the dogs at his direction." Why add "at his direction" unless the Tur means that Rambam did not expressly distinguish the cases, whereas Ramah did distinguish and state explicitly that if the judge personally took and transferred the property he is liable? Shiltei Giborim rules so, and Ir Shushan writes likewise. This is the primary law.
2. This applies, however, specifically to an expert judge whom the parties did not expressly accept, or to a nonexpert judge whom they did accept. But where an expert judge was accepted by the litigants, he is exempt even if he personally took and transferred the property. Rav Chisda apparently understood the incident of Rabbi Tarfon as one where Rabbi Tarfon personally took and transferred the item, as I proved above, yet Rabbi Akiva exempted him because he was an expert accepted by the parties. Rav Nachman and Rav Sheshet certainly do not disagree with that result; their discussion concerns a case where the judge did not personally take and transfer. This also follows from Rav Nachman and Rav Sheshet themselves. What difference should it make that he personally transferred the property and thereby performed a substantial act? Even when he errs in discretionary judgment, his judgment stands and he has done a substantial act, yet Rabbi Akiva exempted Rabbi Tarfon. Tosafot and Mordechai in chapter Echad Dinei Mamonot likewise write that where he personally transferred the property he performed a substantial act. If an accepted expert were liable for that reason, they should simply have asked why Rabbi Akiva exempted Rabbi Tarfon because he was an expert to beit din, given that he had personally transferred the property. Clearly, their argument applies only to the attempt to exempt him as one who erred regarding an explicit Mishnah without regard to his expert status. An expert judge accepted by the litigants is plainly exempt even if he personally took and transferred the property and restoration is impossible. The Tur explicitly writes this at the end of seif 3 in the name of Ramah. The same rule applies even if he erred in discretionary judgment, as is evident from the incident of Rabbi Tarfon and as I write below in seif 2, seif katan 13, letter 1, in the name of the Rosh and Tur. Certainly it applies to an error concerning an explicit Mishnah. Likewise, three ordained expert judges are exempt even if they personally took and transferred the property, even where the parties did not expressly accept them, as I wrote above in seif katan 29, letter 10.
