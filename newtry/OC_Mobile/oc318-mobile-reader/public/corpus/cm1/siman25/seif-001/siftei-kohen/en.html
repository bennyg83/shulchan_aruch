Every judge. The Rambam carefully writes “every judge who adjudicated,” not “everyone who adjudicated,” teaching that this rule applies only to one legally called a judge, namely an expert or one accepted by the parties. An ordinary person who was not accepted is no judge at all; his ruling is void even if he did not err. Even without personally transferring the property, he is liable, because he intended to cause harm by adjudicating coercively without authority. This explains why here the Rambam says that although the judge caused loss, he did not intend harm, while below he says that the unauthorized adjudicator intended harm. The Rif likewise indicates that where liability rests upon dina degarmi, the adjudicator is liable even for an error in an explicit law. Yet he separately states that one who is neither expert nor accepted has no valid ruling, whether or not he erred, and if the successful party cannot restore the money, the unauthorized adjudicator must pay because he caused the loss. The Ramban explains that such a person acted unlawfully and resembles an informer who independently points out another’s property. Thus the Rif, Rambam, and Ramban regard him as liable under dina degarmi.
However, this is certainly so according to the Rif and those who hold that whenever liability exists under dina degarmi it applies even to an error in an explicit law, and all the more according to Tosafot, the Rosh, and Mordechai, who hold that Rabbi Meir imposes liability in every case, even upon an unaccepted expert or an accepted layman. Yet according to the Baal HaMaor and Baal HaIttur, who understand the sugya as following Rabbi Meir and nevertheless exempt an error in an explicit law because the judge accomplished nothing, the same exemption should apply here. This is also the principal reading of the sugya and the view of most authorities. The Nimukei Yosef cites such an opinion, and the Rashba’s responsum clearly indicates it. One might argue that an unauthorized layman is an actual damager, but the Rif and Rambam describe him only as one who causes damage. Moreover, the Baal HaMaor and Rashba explain that an error in an explicit law is partly the litigant’s own negligence, because the mistake was obvious and he should have inquired rather than rely upon the ruling. They explicitly apply this reason even where the adjudicator was neither expert nor accepted. See further below.
The Baal HaMaor and Rashba’s further statement that even one who personally transferred the property is exempt for an error in an explicit law appears incorrect. Even Rav Chisda, who is more lenient than Rav Nachman and Rav Sheshet and imposes liability for an error in discretionary judgment only where the judge personally transferred the property, agrees that personal transfer creates liability even for an error in an explicit law. Rav Chisda’s explanation of the Mishnah of Bekhorot would otherwise leave Rav Hamnuna’s original question unanswered. It is strained to say that “personally transferred” means that the judge forcibly seized the item and fed it to dogs. The Baal HaMaor himself appears to understand the phrase ordinarily and merely rules unlike Rav Chisda. The principal view is therefore that of the majority cited below: where the judge personally transferred the property and restoration is impossible, he is liable even for an error in an explicit law, because he performed a substantial act. The exemption for an explicit error applies where the litigant himself caused the irreversible loss, such as feeding the item to dogs, allowing the recipient to travel abroad, or dealing with an insolvent opponent. Where the judge physically transfers the property, however, he has performed the damaging act.
At first glance, one might distinguish between a judge who physically takes an item and feeds it to dogs, who is liable, and one who merely transfers money to the successful litigant, who later travels abroad or becomes insolvent. Since an explicit error can always be reversed, perhaps the judge should not be responsible for the recipient’s later disappearance or insolvency. The authorities, however, do not distinguish; the Ramban, Nimukei Yosef, Shiltei Giborim, and others state that whenever the judge personally transferred the property he is liable. Their reasoning appears to be that the exemption based on reversibility applies only while the property remains under the litigant’s control. Once the judge himself transfers it, he has done a substantial act and should have anticipated that the recipient might depart or spend the funds. More fundamentally, by taking and transferring the property the judge steps into the recipient’s place. He becomes as though he borrowed from the losing party, whose claim is thereafter against the judge, while the recipient’s later use or disappearance concerns the judge’s money. This is the primary explanation.
