A judge who adjudicated. See Shach, who rules that where the judge physically transferred property, he is liable even for an error in an explicit matter of law. Urim VeTumim seeks to prove that according to Rif he is exempt even then, reasoning that an expert judge is treated as acting under compulsion. In my view this is very difficult. If actual physical transfer by mistake were itself exempt because of compulsion, there would be no reason why adding the factor that the judgment stands should create liability. The stronger basis is as follows. Rif's proof from the sugya in Bekhorot shows that the discussion there likewise assumes Rav Chisda's distinction: not only payment liability but the continuing validity of the judgment depends on whether the judge physically transferred the property. If speech alone already fixed the result, then transferring a pledge afterward would add nothing. Ketzot HaChoshen's argument based on damage to a lien is likewise unsound, because an after-loan pledge can be fully acquired for relevant purposes and its destruction is direct damage. Even where the judge unwittingly hands another person's property to the wrong person, he is at least a direct cause of loss, comparable to throwing an object into the sea or handing it to a thief. The law of inadvertent tort applies to such direct causation. The distinction from an innocent agent who becomes liable as a robber rests on liability for accidental loss after acquisition, not on direct damage actually caused by his own transfer.
The judgment is reversed. See Shach. In an unresolved talmudic question, if a judge ruled for extraction according to authorities who validate seizure in such a doubt, the Shach does not mean the judgment stands before actual transfer. His point is that once beit din itself transferred the property, the recipient became the possessor and may retain it. This is also the meaning of Tosafot: if the judge had already ruled and afterward became uncertain, possession controls. Urim VeTumim says the judge must nevertheless reimburse from his own pocket. I do not agree. In a pure legal doubt where any possessor could say, 'I am certain like this authority,' the judge himself should be no worse than any other person. He too can say, 'I am certain that I ruled lawfully.' This differs from an error in judicial discretion where the accepted course of practical rulings points the other way even against an existing possessor. There the judge cannot defend himself by saying his ruling was lawful. Here the issue remains genuinely unresolved. Nor is this like one who stole disputed property and became certainly obligated in restitution. The judge never intended to steal for himself and it may be that the recipient was legally entitled all along. Therefore the judge is exempt.
And some disagree. See Shach, who challenges Sema's rule that even experts accepted by the parties can be liable in this situation. The Shach argues that an expert accepted by the parties is exempt even for an error in judicial discretion where the judgment stands and thus all the more for a direct error that merely causes loss. Sema can be defended. Tosafot compare an irrecoverable error in an explicit law to a money changer who incorrectly validates a coin. A paid money changer, even if expert, is liable. A judge performs a mitzvah and can have the halachic status of a paid custodian because of the small benefit attached to doing a mitzvah. Thus where the error is direct and irrecoverable he can be liable like a compensated expert. By contrast, in an error of discretion where the judgment stands, the judge's liability rests largely on the penalty for presuming to judge alone, and an accepted expert is exempt because that presumption is absent. This also explains why merely saying the judge was 'expert' does not automatically make him like a money changer who still needs instruction. Once qualified to judge, he has a duty to adjudicate and is not comparable to one who unnecessarily gives professional advice while still needing training. The various difficulties concerning ritual rulings are likewise resolved by distinguishing where the first ruling already took legal effect. If the judge himself later admits error, the party who relied on him may say, 'I accept your later admission that you damaged me,' even though other courts cannot reverse the earlier effective ruling. This is why liability can remain although the ritual status stays in place. The Shach's broader discussion is difficult, but this is the coherent basis of Sema's position.
And regarding ritual rulings. See Shach. His distinction can be understood as follows. In monetary law, the reason an expert who was not accepted may be liable for an error in discretion is that he presumptuously judged alone. This does not apply in ritual law, where one sage may properly issue a ruling alone. Therefore even an expert who was not formally accepted is exempt there. But for an error in an explicit matter of law, where liability depends on direct causation and an avoidable professional mistake, the same accepted-versus-unaccepted distinction can apply in ritual law as in money. Hence the Shach's point is that Nimukei Yosef's distinction belongs primarily to errors of discretion. The later authorities imply there is ultimately no fundamental difference between monetary and ritual rulings regarding the general structure of liability.
