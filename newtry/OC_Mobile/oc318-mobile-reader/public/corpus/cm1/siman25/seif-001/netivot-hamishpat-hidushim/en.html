Every judge. The wording is precise. This means someone authorized to judge: a sole expert, a nonexpert accepted by the parties, or three lay judges even without formal acceptance. One layperson or two laypeople not accepted by the parties have no valid judgment at all, even if they did not err and even if they received authorization. If the judgment cannot be reversed because the recipient went overseas, they must pay from their own funds even if they did not personally handle the property, while the judgment is reversed as against the litigant. This is the Rif's view. Baal HaMaor exempts them because the litigant was negligent in allowing them to handle his property. If they personally handled the property through coercion, everyone agrees they are robbers and liable.

And some disagree. According to the Sema, even an expert accepted by the parties is liable for an error in an explicit Mishnah, because regarding such an error there is no distinction between an expert accepted by the parties and a nonexpert. The Shach disagrees and holds that an accepted expert is exempt in every case according to everyone. See Netivot, seif katan 3. The Shach also rules like the Mechaber. If one asks a sage whether to buy certain produce or meat, and the sage says it is permitted but it is actually tereifah, then if the ruling was an error in an explicit law the sale is void. Whether the judge must reimburse depends upon the dispute between the Mechaber and Rama here. For an accepted expert it depends upon the Sema and Shach. If the error was one of judgment, see Netivot: according to Rama in Yoreh Deah siman 242:31, another sage cannot prohibit it, so the sale remains valid, and according to Rama's dissenting view here the judge pays from his own pocket if he admits that he erred and it was tereifah. According to the Mechaber he is exempt unless he personally transferred the money from buyer to seller, even without coercion. If he was an accepted expert he is exempt. According to the authorities cited by the Shach in Yoreh Deah, another sage can prohibit it, so the sale is void and reverses. If reversal is impossible, the law is like an error of judgment in monetary law. If the buyer had already taken the produce and then came before the judge to void the sale because it was tereifah, and the judge ruled it kosher and upheld the sale, the case is like any other monetary error. If he ruled that ordinary wine was permitted although it was prohibited wine, causing the owner to mix it with his own and thereby prohibit everything, the Shach rules unlike Tosafot that the judge is exempt because this is no worse than one who renders wine prohibited through an imperceptible injury. But if he mistakenly ruled permitted wine prohibited, the Shach agrees that the judge is liable wherever a judge is liable for an explicit legal error. See Netivot that according to the Shach, if he ruled a tereifah kosher and the owner said before mixing it, "See, I am mixing it based on your ruling," and the sage told him to proceed, the sage is liable like one who personally handled the property.
Although he is not ordained. The Sema asks why Rama placed this rule under an error in an explicit law, since according to the Sema even an expert accepted by the parties is liable for such an error. See Netivot, seif katan 5: even according to the Shach, who exempts an accepted expert even for an explicit error, this rule does not fit there. In monetary law an expert who was not accepted is certainly liable according to the dissenting authorities. The rule properly belongs only to an error of judgment, where in monetary law he is liable because he presumptuously judged alone. In forbidden and permitted matters, however, where one may issue a ruling alone, he is exempt. Even regarding an error of judgment, the Shach cites many authorities who disagree with Nimukei Yosef and hold that forbidden and permitted rulings are like monetary law, requiring specifically an expert accepted by the parties.
