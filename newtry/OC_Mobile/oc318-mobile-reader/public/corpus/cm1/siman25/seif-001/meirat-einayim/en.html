Every judge who adjudicated and erred, etc. The wording 'every judge' means an expert judge, or three ordinary judges not accepted by the parties, or one ordinary judge whom the parties accepted. Such judges were authorized to adjudicate, so their ruling is valid if they did not err, and if they erred the distinctions that follow apply between an error in an explicit Mishnah and an error in discretionary judgment. This excludes one or even two ordinary judges whom the parties did not accept, whose judgment is no judgment even if they were correct, and even if they received authorization from the Reish Galuta.
Nevertheless one should not be lenient, etc. This wording refers to a matter of prohibition, where 'leniency' is meaningful. In monetary law what is lenient for one party is stringent for the other, so the rule is expressed as leniency to the defendant because the burden of proof rests on the claimant.
Or he declared something pure to be impure, etc. This is the Mishnah's and Rambam's wording. The Gemara explains that after the judge ruled it impure, those relying on him allowed it to become actually contaminated by contact with a creeping creature. Otherwise the judge could simply retract the mistaken ruling.
And they fed it to dogs. This wording implies that the owners fed it to dogs because of his ruling that it was a tereifah, and the Tur states this explicitly. The baraita and Rambam, however, use the singular form that can imply the judge himself fed it to the dogs to implement his ruling. That reading is not certain, as Rashi and Tosafot do not explain it that way.
Although he caused damage. We rule like Rabbi Meir that dina d'garmi can create liability.
He did not intend to cause damage. Rif distinguishes ordinary dina d'garmi, for which a person can be liable even without subjective intent because a person is always responsible for his acts, from loss caused through an authorized judicial ruling. If judges were always liable for good-faith judicial mistakes no one would judge. A person authorized to judge therefore is not considered one who intended damage. An ordinary individual or two ordinary persons who judged without authority and erred are liable because their unauthorized intervention is treated as intentional damage.
He did not intend to damage. Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh infer from Rambam's language, 'although he caused damage,' that the judge himself did not physically render the item impure or feed it to dogs. If he personally did so, they understand it as direct damage and hold that even Rif and Rambam would require payment. In the Derishah I proved that Rif and Rambam can still regard even those physical acts as judicially caused damage because his purpose was to implement a legal ruling, not to harm. I also explained why an error in an explicit Mishnah can be treated differently from an error in discretionary judgment.
Some disagree. This refers to the case where the result can no longer be reversed. They hold that even three experts accepted by the parties must pay because Rabbi Meir imposes liability for dina d'garmi even here. Regarding the opening case, where the mistake can be corrected, everyone agrees it is reversed, because an error in an explicit law never remains in force.
Regarding rulings in matters of prohibition and permission, etc. Nimukei Yosef did not write this regarding an error in an explicit Mishnah, but regarding an error in discretionary judgment, where exemption from payment in monetary law ordinarily requires both expertise and valid authorization or acceptance. In matters of prohibition, however, a scholar who is learned and skilled is exempt even without formal semichah, because he is independently obligated by Torah law to issue halachic rulings when qualified. Therefore Rama's placement of this gloss here is somewhat misleading; its natural place is among the following seifim dealing with discretionary error.
