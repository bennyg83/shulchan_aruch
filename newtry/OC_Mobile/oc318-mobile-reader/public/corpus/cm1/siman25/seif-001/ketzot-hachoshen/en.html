By physically transferring it he did a substantial act and effectively entered in place of the party who received it. The litigant whose money was taken then has no further claim against the opposing party, only against the judge. If the recipient later went overseas or spent the money, it is as though he spent the judge's money, and the judge owes the injured litigant. This still requires study. The Gemara's rule that a person is always responsible, whether acting intentionally or unintentionally, was stated specifically regarding a tortfeasor and is derived from the extra language of "wound for wound." With theft and robbery we do not find that an unintentional taker is treated like an intentional robber. Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef imply that if one tells another, "Take for me the ox in such and such a house, for it is mine," and it turns out he intended theft, the sender is exempt because there is no agency for a transgression, while the unwitting taker is also exempt because he did not know. Even if one calls that closer to unavoidable accident, it is not obvious that it is complete accident, because there is a presumption that what is in a person's possession belongs to him and he should have been cautious before removing it. More fundamentally, the special equation of inadvertence with intent appears limited to tort damage because Scripture specifically revealed it there. Here the judge thought the property belonged to the other litigant and transferred it while mistaken. He did not destroy the property. At the time of the transfer it was still possible to recover it. His act is therefore closer to mistaken taking than to direct physical damage, and it requires further study whether he can be made liable as a robber for such an inadvertent transfer.
Some disagree. The roots of this dispute are explained thoroughly by the Shach, Rabbi Chaim Yonah, and later authorities. I will nevertheless set down what appears to me. In Sanhedrin the Gemara challenges from Rabbi Tarfon's ruling about a cow whose uterus had been removed. Rabbi Tarfon had declared it forbidden and it was fed to dogs. The sages at Yavneh later permitted such cows on the testimony of Todos the physician that every cow and pig leaving Alexandria had its uterus cut so that it could not reproduce. Rabbi Tarfon lamented his loss, and Rabbi Akiva told him he was exempt because an expert accepted by the public is exempt from payment. The Gemara says: if an erroneous judgment stands, we understand Rabbi Tarfon's fear. But if one who errs in an explicit Mishnah can simply reverse the ruling, then had the cow still existed his ruling would have been nothing, so why should he now be liable merely because it is gone? The Gemara answers that Rabbi Akiva gave two reasons: first, Rabbi Tarfon had erred in an explicit Mishnah and was exempt; and even if it were an error in judgment, he was a public expert and exempt. Rif and Rambam derive from this that one who errs in an explicit Mishnah is exempt. Tosafot and Rosh ask why this should differ from one who shows a coin to a money changer and relies on a mistaken appraisal. Even if the coin still exists and could be returned, the expert can be liable under the law of direct causation. They therefore explain that the Sanhedrin passage follows the Sages who do not impose liability for direct causation. An error in judgment nevertheless creates liability because once the judgment stands, the judge has caused actual loss through his ruling. According to Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for direct causation, even one who erred in an explicit Mishnah can be liable. Tosafot further says that speech itself can count as direct damage, as with priests who render offerings piggul. Rabbi Chaim Yonah asks from one who sells a debt instrument and then waives the debt. The Gemara says liability for that waiver exists only according to the view that judges direct causation. If speech itself is direct damage, why should waiver need the doctrine of direct causation? The answer is that a purchaser of a debt instrument does not acquire the body of the debt itself. He acquires only the lien on the debtor's property. Therefore when the seller waives the personal debt, he has damaged only the purchaser's lien, and damage to another's lien is itself merely causative. By contrast, burning another's debt instrument can destroy the creditor's own substantive debt right and therefore can be direct damage. This distinction is already reflected in Mordechai: the purchaser of a debt note acquires only its liens, and the seller's later waiver is therefore causative rather than direct damage. This also explains why false witnesses are liable only as causers according to the Rosh, while a judge who wrongly renders a party liable or exempt can be a direct damager under Tosafot's approach. Witnesses do not directly alter the legal right through their words. Their testimony is false, and beit din mistakenly acts upon it. The causal chain runs through beit din. By contrast, a judge's operative ruling itself changes the practical legal posture. Similarly, a priest's piggul declaration takes legal effect through the sacrificial service being performed. Even so, upon further reflection, a judge who wrongly exempts a debtor may also be only a causer. Sefer Tamim De'im cites an early authority who asks why the judge cannot say, like the victorious litigant, "Show me that you judged me wrongly and I will pay." He answers that the judge is assumed to be God fearing and admits his error. If the judge's own ruling stood with complete intrinsic force, his speech would directly destroy the debt. But in truth an erroneous discretionary ruling stands only because the party who benefited can say, "I relied upon the judge. Show me clearly that he ruled improperly." Heaven, however, knows whether the ruling was wrong. Thus the judge's words do not themselves annihilate the debt; our inability to prove the error is what prevents recovery. This resembles false testimony. Therefore even wrongly exempting the liable party may be causative damage rather than direct damage. In the case of wrongly declaring pure food impure, however, the result can be different. When the judge's ruling of prohibition itself gives the food a binding prohibited status, his words act like piggul and can be direct damage. This also explains the Sanhedrin passage according to those who deny liability for direct causation. If an erroneous ruling in an explicit Mishnah stands, then Rabbi Tarfon's declaration itself would have rendered the cow forbidden and caused direct damage. If such an error is automatically reversed, the declaration itself did nothing, so without direct causation there would be no liability. Rabbi Akiva therefore says he is exempt both because it was an explicit Mishnah and, alternatively, because he was a public expert. The Shach's interpretation of "he declared impure what was pure" as a case where buyer and seller came to a sage, the buyer relied upon the sage's ruling that the produce was pure, purchased it, and was later harmed when the ruling was shown wrong, is extremely difficult. That is an obvious mistaken sale. If the buyer had known the produce would later be prohibited, he would not have bought it. Even in an unresolved legal doubt, the seller must return the purchase money where the item is prohibited because of the doubt, as Ran and Rama rule. All the more so here, where the judge plainly erred in legal discretion. Thus the Shach's explanation cannot be maintained.
