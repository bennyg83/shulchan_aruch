Seif 3. In the gloss: “Some say that in every case, what he did stands and the judgment is not reversed.” Note: See the Shach, who disagrees with the Tur and holds that where the judgment has not yet been acted upon, it is reversed according to all opinions. I find this very difficult. If so, what is the Gemara’s question in the chapter Echad Dinei Mamonot, where it says that monetary judgments are reversed and challenges this from the teaching, “He judged the case,” etc.? The Gemara could simply answer that “monetary judgments are reversed” refers to a case in which no act has yet been done, just as the statement regarding capital law necessarily concerns a case before execution, while “what he did is done” refers to a case where the ruling has already been implemented. Why is any contradiction perceived? It appears rather, as the Tur holds, that even before implementation we may say that the judgment stands. A case where the judgment has been pronounced but not yet implemented is easily found, for example where a lender holds collateral and the judge ruled that the borrower is exempt, but the collateral has not yet been returned. No practical transfer has yet occurred. According to the Shach, the judgment should then be reversible, yet the Gemara did not resolve the contradiction in this obvious way. This requires study. There is also a separate issue where three authorities dispute a matter and a fourth adopts a middle position. See Shach, Choshen Mishpat siman 34, seif katan 5, who writes in the name of Rivash that the halachah follows the one who mediates between the views, and adds that everywhere the halachah follows the compromiser. I previously questioned this, because the rule that the halachah follows a mediating opinion applies only where the original disputants themselves articulated the respective possibilities and the third view truly resolves between their stated positions, as explained at the end of the first chapter of Pesachim. I later reconsidered his words in a responsum. Now I see that the language of the Rif and Rosh in the first chapter of Kiddushin expressly indicates that the halachah follows a mediating opinion only in a Mishnah, not in a baraita. If so, certainly a later dispute among halachic authorities does not automatically create such a rule. A dispute among poskim is no stronger than a baraita for this purpose. This appears correct.
