Every judge who judged, etc. The Sema writes that the wording specifically says "judge," meaning one who is authorized to adjudicate: either a single expert, or a nonexpert whom the parties accepted, or three lay judges even if the parties did not expressly accept them. But one or two laymen whom the parties did not accept render no legally effective judgment at all, even if they received authority from the Exilarch, and even if they did not err. This rule appears in the Rif's fifth category: the litigant may retract even where there was no error. If reversal is impossible because the recipient went overseas or the like, the judge must pay from his own funds even if he did not personally take and transfer the property. If he personally took and transferred it, then even where recovery can be had from the party who received it, the judge must first pay the one whom he damaged and then recover from the litigant who received money unlawfully. Baal HaMaor, however, in his fourth category writes that the very stringency of their lack of authority produces leniency: since their judgment has no legal substance, even if they personally took and transferred the property they are exempt, because the litigant was negligent in permitting such a judge to handle his property and should never have relied on his ruling. If the judge coerced the litigant and forcibly took and transferred the property, he is a robber and must pay. The Rashba appears to agree with this. The Shach rules, in a case of personally taking and transferring, like the Rif. See Tumim for my discussion.
In the words of the poskim. Nimukei Yosef writes that where the Gemara leaves a monetary question unresolved and we therefore apply the rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant, if a judge mistakenly ruled to extract property from the possessor, this is an error in an explicit rule. The Shach writes that this depends on the dispute whether seizure is effective in an unresolved Gemara question. In Tumim I wrote that all may agree to Nimukei Yosef's practical rule. At the least it is an error in discretionary judgment. If the judge has already caused the property to be placed in the other litigant's hands, then according to the view that seizure is effective in an unresolved question it is not removed from the new possessor. Rather, the injured party turns to the judge for payment. This then depends on the dispute between the Mechaber and Rama, reflecting the Rif's view and the Tosafos view that disagrees with it in this seif.

Nevertheless one should not be lenient. This refers to a matter of issur and heter, because in monetary matters the expression "lenient" is inapplicable. There the burden of proof always rests on the claimant. Sema.
Although he caused damage, he did not intend to damage. Meaning, ordinarily he should be liable under dina d'garmi, because we rule like Rabbi Meir that indirect causation of this kind creates liability. Nevertheless, the judge is exempt because he did not intend to cause damage. Although in other cases of dina d'garmi intent is irrelevant, since a person is always responsible for his acts, a judge is treated differently. Otherwise no one would agree to judge for fear that any error would require payment. If he personally took and transferred the property, Beit Yosef and the Shach hold that the judge is liable even for an error in an explicit rule. The Sema and Bach disagree and hold that since he did not intend to cause damage, he is exempt even if he personally took and transferred the property when the error was in an explicit rule. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length. This is the dispute between Ramban and Rashba in their understanding of the Rif and Rambam. Money cannot be extracted from the possessor, who may say, "I rely on the Rashba," as Sema and Bach write, for the Rashba's reasoning is persuasive. If the judge is an expert and the parties accepted him, all agree, even according to Ramban and Beit Yosef, that he is exempt even if he personally took and transferred the property. The Shach writes so, and it is clear.
And some disagree. They hold that if reversal is impossible, since we rule like Rabbi Meir that dina d'garmi is actionable, the judge must pay because he caused the loss. In Tumim I discussed at length how to answer, according to this view, all the objections raised against it by the Shach and Rabbi Yonah Teomim. If he is a publicly recognized expert and the parties accepted him, the Shach concludes that even according to this view he is exempt and dina d'garmi does not apply, contrary to the Sema and Bach who hold him liable. The Shach's position is correct. See Tumim, where I explained Tosafos in the chapter Elu Tereifos and concluded that if the judge personally took and transferred the property, then even though he is an expert and legally exempt, he remains morally obligated beyond the strict letter of the law. By contrast, if he did not personally transfer the property, erred in an explicit rule, and is publicly recognized as an expert, he is exempt even beyond the strict law, as we find that Rabbi Akiva exempted Rabbi Tarfon and did not tell him that he should pay beyond the strict law. If a purchaser asked a sage whether he should buy certain produce, and the sage advised him to buy because it was pure, and afterward it was discovered that the sage erred and the produce was impure, the Shach holds that if the error was in an explicit rule the sale is void, while if it was an error in discretionary judgment the sale stands and the purchaser has no claim against the seller; he may claim only from the judge if the judge was not a publicly recognized expert accepted by the parties. Rabbi Yonah Teomim already objected, and I agreed in Tumim, that this is not a matter of judicial liability at all. The sale is plainly a mistaken transaction and is void. The seller must return the purchase money and take back his produce. The Shach further holds that if a sage ruled that prohibited gentile wine was permitted wine, and the owner therefore mixed it with his other permitted wine, and later the sage's error was discovered so that the entire mixture became prohibited, the decisor is nevertheless exempt even if not an expert. This too is incorrect according to the present dissenting view. Since we rule like Rabbi Meir that dina d'garmi is actionable, even if the judgment itself can be reversed, this judge must pay whether he erred in an explicit rule or in discretionary judgment. See Tumim. One should not object that according to this view liability should require the language stated below in siman 306 regarding one who shows a coin to a money changer, "See, I rely upon you," and otherwise the expert is exempt. Here, those who come before judges for adjudication are inherently saying that they rely on them. They asked them to judge and it is uncommon to go afterward to other judges merely to verify whether the first judgment was correct. With a coin, by contrast, it is common not to rely upon one money changer but to pass from one money changer to another. Therefore explicit reliance must be stated there to create liability.
Although he is not ordained, his law is like that of an expert. This is because one who has reached the level of issuing Torah rulings is obligated to rule, as the verse is expounded, "and mighty are all her slain," referring to one who is qualified to rule and does not rule. The Sema asks why Rama placed this law here, in the discussion of an error in an explicit rule. Either reversal remains possible, in which case the ruling is reversed in every event, or reversal is impossible, in which case according to the dissenting view brought by Rama even an expert must pay. The Sema follows his own position that where an explicit rule was violated and reversal is impossible even an expert must pay. But according to what I concluded above in the name of the Shach, that an expert remains exempt, as is clear from Rabbi Tarfon's case, this rule fits perfectly here. In a ruling of issur and heter, although he is not fully ordained, he is exempt from payment where reversal is impossible because he is treated as an expert. There is no difficulty. I am therefore surprised that the Shach himself, in seif katan 7, agrees with the Sema's objection, although he himself disagreed with the Sema and held that an expert is always exempt, even for an error in an explicit rule. His words require study. The Shach brings many proofs against this law of Nimukei Yosef, but all his proofs show only that in issur and heter too the parties must have accepted the decisor. In my view there is no contradiction. Nimukei Yosef also agrees that acceptance is required. So long as people have not come to ask his ruling in issur and heter, which according to most authors constitutes acceptance, why should he be obligated to rule? Nimukei Yosef's point is only that monetary adjudication differs from issur and heter. In monetary law, even if the parties accepted him, he must still be a distinctly qualified expert. In issur and heter, once he is learned and competent he is called an expert because he is obligated to rule. Acceptance is nevertheless required, and without it he is not exempt. Rabbi Akiva's statement to Rabbi Tarfon that he was a publicly recognized expert accurately described the facts, but mere learning and competence would have sufficed for exemption. Thus none of the authors cited by the Shach disproves this rule. Still, Nimukei Yosef follows his own view that an "expert" for monetary adjudication means one formally ordained. Therefore he writes that for issur and heter ordination is unnecessary and that reaching the level of ruling, meaning being learned and competent, is enough. According to our ruling that even a contemporary expert is exempt from payment and ordination is not required, what distinction remains? One must answer as I wrote above: in monetary law one needs a publicly recognized expert, proven and tested, while for issur and heter mere learning and competence suffice. Rama used the word "ordained" because he followed Nimukei Yosef's wording, but his intent is as I have explained. Even so, this requires study. The Mishnah states, "He judged the judgment, declared the pure impure," and requires him to pay from his own pocket. How can the case arise? If he had not reached the level of ruling, why should his ruling have any standing? He is among those of whom it is said that many casualties have fallen through them. If he had reached that level, he is an expert and should be exempt from payment. It is strained to say that the Mishnah presents separate cases: in monetary judgment he was not an expert but was accepted by the parties, while in declaring pure food impure he was an expert but was not accepted. This remains difficult. See what I wrote concerning the Ran.
