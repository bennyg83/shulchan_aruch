Exempt. The Shach writes that the case of "declaring the impure pure" means, for example, that a seller and buyer came before a sage to ask whether the buyer might purchase the seller's produce because it was pure. The sage erroneously declared impure produce pure, the buyer purchased it on that ruling, and later it became known that the ruling was incorrect. The sage wishes the seller to refund the money and take back the produce. The teaching that "what he did is done" means that the seller sold the produce under its presumed status as pure and need not return the money; rather, the sage must reimburse the buyer from his own funds, like one who shows a coin to a moneychanger and relies on his evaluation. From this follows a new rule: where the sage declared an impure item pure and the owner then mixed it with other items, the sage is exempt even if his error was one of judgment. A present-day example is a sage who declared another's wine permitted, the owner mixed it with other wine, and it was later discovered that the ruling was incorrect and all the wine became prohibited as libation wine. Whether the sage erred in an explicit Mishnah or in judgment, he is exempt from payment, for he is like one who inadvertently renders another's wine prohibited, who is exempt. If he erred on a question left unresolved in the Talmud, then if the parties accepted him or he is an expert, even an error in judgment is not treated as a definite error because it cannot be established with certainty; what he did therefore remains done and he is exempt from payment, contrary to Nimukei Yosef. Likewise, one who ruled that a bird was non-kosher because its signs of kashrut had not been established, and it was later learned that this species is eaten on the basis of a received tradition, has erred in an explicit matter and the ruling is reversed. The same applies in analogous cases.

This follows the Rif, who distinguishes between actionable indirect damage, for which a person may be liable even without intending harm because a person is always deemed forewarned, as stated in siman 378, and loss caused through a judge's ruling. If judges were held liable for every such loss, no one would ever agree to judge. Since an authorized judge entered the case lawfully, he is not regarded as having intended to cause damage. By contrast, one or two laymen who presume to judge may be liable, for they are treated as having intended the damage. Beit Yosef understands the Rif and Rambam to hold that if the halachic authority personally rendered the item impure or fed it to another with his own hands, this is direct damage and he must pay even for an error in an explicit Mishnah. I demonstrated, however, that according to the Rif and Rambam even this is classed as causing damage rather than direct damage, since his intention was to judge and issue a practical halachic ruling, not to cause harm. And matters of prohibition and permission. The Sema writes that this law comes from Nimukei Yosef, who did not write it regarding an error in an explicit Mishnah, but only regarding an error in judgment. Ordinarily, a judge who errs in judgment is exempt from payment only if he is an expert who received ordination from a beit din of Eretz Yisrael or authorization from the Exilarch. Nimukei Yosef says that in ritual-law rulings it is different: if the authority is simply an expert who is learned and analytically competent, that suffices to exempt him from payment. The Rama therefore should have placed this gloss in the next seif, concerning an error in judgment. The Shach challenges the underlying distinction in Nimukei Yosef and sees no basis to distinguish between monetary rulings and rulings of prohibition and permission. After a lengthy discussion he concludes that the primary rule is that there is no distinction between monetary law and ritual-law rulings, contrary to Nimukei Yosef and the Rama. The Levush likewise omits this gloss.
