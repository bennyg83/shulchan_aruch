Every judge who adjudicated, etc. See what I wrote in Urim concerning Rif, Baal HaMaor, and Shach's conclusion. Even according to Baal HaMaor, who writes that if the judge coerced the litigant and through that coercion the litigant transferred his own property, the judge is liable as a robber, this does not mean that the judge himself physically took and transferred the property. Rather, the judge's coercion forced the litigant, against his will, to hand over the property. That is enough to treat the judge as responsible for the wrongful taking, because the litigant acted under compulsion created by the judge's command. What else could he have done? This also explains Baal HaMaor's later statement that in all these cases there is no distinction between whether the judge personally "took and gave" and whether he did not. At first glance there would be a major difference: in one case the ordinary judge would have to pay, and in the other he would not. The answer is that where coercion occurred, the judge need not physically take anything. The coerced transfer by the litigant himself is sufficient. Where there was no coercion, even if the judge physically handled the property, Baal HaMaor does not impose liability on that basis alone. Thus there is no relevant distinction. Mahari writes that Baal HaMaor's phrase "took and gave" is not literal and merely describes the ordinary circumstance. That is not the best explanation. Rather, the phrase means that the litigant himself transferred the property because of the judge's coercion. This resolves the passage without multiplying disputes.
In a matter disputed by the poskim. See what I wrote in Urim. Shach disagrees with Nimukei Yosef, who rules that where a Talmudic question remains unresolved as teiku and a judge nevertheless orders money removed from the current possessor, the judge is considered to have erred in a matter of Mishnah. Shach follows his broader view that seizure is ineffective in a teiku. He argues that according to the poskim who hold seizure is effective even in a teiku, a judge who rules to transfer the property should not be considered mistaken. His proof is from the principle that "one who acted like R. Elazar acted validly," for which reason we say that if one party seized the property we do not remove it from him. See below, siman 108. This proof has no foundation. The opposite logic applies there. Because one who rules like R. Elazar has a legitimate halachic position, a person who seizes in accordance with that position is not dispossessed. This fits the principle that a person may sometimes enforce his own legal right. See Baal HaTerumot and what I wrote in the treatise on seizure. But to say that merely because possession acquired through seizure is sometimes protected in an unresolved doubt, a judge who affirmatively removes money from the established possessor in a case of doubt is not considered mistaken, is something entirely different. The foundational rule of monetary law is that the claimant bears the burden of proof. What greater error could there be than a judge knowingly extracting money from a possessor where the law itself remains unresolved? The passage Shach cites concerning "one who acted like R. Elazar acted validly" is not analogous. There, the substantive halachah is actually against Rav and Shmuel. We rule like R. Abba that where a person is obligated to swear but cannot, he must pay. This undermines Rav and Shmuel's separate principle that one cannot bequeath an oath obligation to heirs, as Tosafot and Rosh explain. Nevertheless, because the view of Rav and Shmuel was stated by such great authorities, Chazal say not to extend their rule beyond its precise context. Accordingly, one who ruled like R. Elazar did not simply decide an unresolved teiku against the possessor. He ruled according to a recognized halachic position that could legitimately be followed. Of course such a judgment is not reversed. Our case is different. Shach also brings proof from Tosafot in Bava Batra 62. Tosafot write that where no halachah was stated either like one authority or the other, and the judge's own understanding favors one side and he rules accordingly, what he did stands. In my view this source proves the opposite. Tosafot are precise in saying that the judge himself holds like one of the authorities. Meaning, he has reached a genuine halachic conclusion through his own reasoning and intellectual judgment. If so, who can prevent him from ruling in accordance with the position his understanding establishes? But if the judge has no basis to decide between the authorities and himself remains uncertain, yet nevertheless chooses one side and extracts money from the current possessor, that is plainly an error. Tosafot therefore write specifically, "and the judge holds like one of them." They do not simply say, "the judge acted like one of them." This distinction is decisive. In a teiku of the Gemara, who can stand and say, "My reasoning is pure and my power is great enough to resolve what the Gemara itself left unresolved"? One who says such a thing is foolish and arrogant. The judge himself necessarily remains in doubt. If despite that doubt he orders money removed from a possessor, he has violated the elementary rule that the claimant bears the burden of proof. That is a clear judicial error. Tosafot's language yields another important distinction. They first say that where no halachah was fixed and the judge holds like one opinion and rules accordingly, what he did stands. This implies only validity after the fact. It does not say that ideally he should extract money based solely upon his own preference. If that were the correct initial rule, Tosafot should have said directly that he may rule that way. Tosafot in Bava Batra 32 likewise imply that the preservation of such a ruling is an after-the-fact rule. This creates an apparent question from Tosafot's continuation. They say that if the judge himself is uncertain, then the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Why specify that he is uncertain? Even where he personally inclines toward one view, if initially he should not extract money from the possessor, the same burden-of-proof rule should apply. The answer is that Tosafot's entire passage can be discussing what happens after the judge already acted. If the judge had a reasoned halachic position favoring one authority, then after the fact his judgment stands even though ideally he should have been cautious about extracting from a possessor amid unresolved dispute. But if the judge himself was uncertain and nevertheless ruled for the claimant, then even after the fact the ordinary burden-of-proof rule controls. His ruling is treated as an error and the property is restored. This is the correct reading of Tosafot. Accordingly, in a Talmudic teiku the judge is certainly in the category of uncertainty. If he orders money removed from the possessor, the judgment should be reversed because the claimant bears the burden of proof. At most, one might disagree with Nimukei Yosef's classification of the mistake as an error in a matter of Mishnah, because the underlying issue itself is not explicitly resolved and the judge's mistake is therefore not identical to contradicting a clear Mishnah. But it is certainly an error in judicial discretion and settled practice. The accepted course of courts in unresolved monetary doubt is to leave the property with its possessor. With respect, Shach goes too far in saying the ruling is not even an error of that kind. All of this concerns a case where the judge has not yet caused the claimant to take physical possession. If, however, the judge already placed the property into the claimant's possession, then according to those who hold that seizure is effective in a teiku, the court does not remove it again. All the more so where the transfer occurred through beit din itself. In that event the dispossessed party may have a claim against the judge personally, and according to Tosafot the judge may be required to pay from his own property, depending on the rules discussed above. See what I wrote there.
Although he caused damage, he did not intend to cause damage. Sema and Bach explain that, according to Rif and Rambam who follow his approach, even if the judge personally took property from one litigant and handed it to the other, if his error was in an explicit Mishnah he is exempt from payment. Shach, however, argues at length in seif katan 4 that according to everyone, once the judge personally took and transferred the property, even an error in a matter of Mishnah obligates him to pay. In my view, Shach's proof is not decisive. He cites Tosafot and Mordechai, who indeed write that where the judge personally takes and gives, he must pay even for an error in an explicit Mishnah. But those authorities follow the view that according to R. Meir, who imposes liability for dina d'garmei, a judge can be liable even where the judgment itself is reversible. If so, it follows naturally that according to the Sages, when the judge does an actual physical act rather than merely causing loss indirectly, he is certainly liable. Rif and those who follow him reason differently. They hold that even R. Meir, though generally imposing liability for indirect causation, does not impose it here where the judge erred innocently, because he did not intend harm and acted under the compulsion of his judicial role. If that is the basis of the exemption, one can reasonably say that even where he personally took and transferred the property, the same excuse applies. Why should the absence of malicious intent disappear merely because the judge himself handled the money? Ramban in Milchamot Hashem does appear to hold that under Rif, once the judge personally took and gave in a matter of Mishnah he is liable. But Rashba, in his novellae to Chullin in the chapter Eilu Tereifot, writes explicitly that according to Rif even where the judge personally took and gave, if the error was in a matter of Mishnah he is exempt. The matter is therefore a dispute between great authorities, Ramban and Rashba, and who can decide between such mountains? Nevertheless, the words of Rif themselves appear to incline more toward Rashba. Ramban himself explains that R. Elazar in the name of Rav, who interprets the Mishnah in Bechorot and Bava Kamma as a case where the judge personally took and gave, is not giving the same explanation as Rav Chisda. Rav's statement comes to explain why the judge must pay, so that one should not infer that the Mishnah follows only R. Meir's law of indirect causation. Rav Chisda, by contrast, uses the distinction between taking and giving and not taking and giving to explain why the judgment remains in force rather than being reversed. If these two statements were one and the same, the Gemara should have said, "and so said R. Elazar in the name of Rav," as it ordinarily does. According to Ramban, therefore, Rav's rule of liability and Rav Chisda's rule concerning whether the judgment stands are conceptually separate. This creates a difficulty. The Gemara challenges Rav Sheshet, who holds that an error in an explicit Mishnah is always reversible, from the incident where R. Tarfon mistakenly permitted a cow and R. Akiva said to him, "Your donkey has gone, Tarfon," meaning he thought he would have to pay. If R. Tarfon did not personally take and transfer anything, then according to Rif even R. Meir would exempt him from payment, since this is merely indirect damage by a mistaken judge. The question against Rav Sheshet would therefore be meaningless. If R. Tarfon did personally take and give, then according to Ramban's understanding of Rif he would have to pay even though the ruling was reversible. Again, there is no question against Rav Sheshet. The only way to sustain the challenge is to say, as Ramban apparently must, that according to the initial assumption, before the Gemara accepts Rav Chisda's distinction, a mistaken ruling that remains standing can create liability under R. Meir even without personal taking and giving. Once the judgment is reversible, however, the causation is incomplete and there is no liability. Thus the challenge to Rav Sheshet works because if the judgment should have been reversed, R. Tarfon should not have thought he had to pay. But this itself requires several strained assumptions. Ramban had to answer Gaon and Baal HaMaor, who argued that from the Gemara's initial establishment of the Mishnah according to R. Meir and Rav's later answer of taking and giving, it follows that according to R. Meir even without taking and giving the judge should pay. Ramban gives a lengthy answer rather than simply saying that the initial sugya did not follow Rav Chisda, because there remains the case of "he declared the impure pure." There the judgment is inherently reversible once the mistake is discovered, yet the Mishnah still says the judge pays. This appears to show that R. Meir can impose liability even where the judgment itself is reversible. Thus Ramban is forced into complexity. According to Rif's own rationale, however, it is more natural to say that a judge is different from an ordinary tortfeasor because the judge did not intend damage and was compelled by the judicial obligation to decide. If so, even where the judge's ruling happens to remain standing, mere indirect causation should not create liability. This is exactly the logic Rashba applies. Rif further argues that even according to R. Meir, payment is imposed only where the judge personally took and gave. He supports this from Rav Chisda's establishment of the Mishnah as a case of taking and giving. If the distinction merely concerned whether the judgment remains standing, but R. Meir still imposed payment without taking and giving whenever the judgment stood, Rav Chisda's formulation would not prove the payment rule. Rather, Rif appears to understand that with judges, liability itself is tied to the judge's own concrete act. One can also see this from the very structure of Rif's explanation. He says that a judge is unlike an ordinary person who causes loss indirectly, because if judges were liable for every mistaken act of causation no person would ever agree to judge. That policy rationale applies whether the judgment remains in force or is later reversed. The judge's lack of intent and the necessity of judicial activity remain the same. Therefore, if the judge did not personally take and transfer the property, Rif's rationale supports exemption even if the mistaken judgment stood for a time. This strengthens Rashba's reading. Shach also argues from the language of the Gemara that Rav Chisda's answer of taking and giving applies to the challenge from R. Tarfon. But Rashi himself says Rav Chisda is returning to the earlier Mishnah and not answering Rav Sheshet's episode. This supports the view that the two discussions are independent. If Rav Chisda's answer really supplied the decisive resolution of the R. Tarfon incident, Rif should have quoted the challenge and answer because it would yield a major practical distinction. His omission suggests otherwise. Ramban himself does not state with absolute certainty that Rif imposes liability in an error of Mishnah when the judge personally took and gave. He writes only that this appears to be Rif's view. Where Rashba reads Rif in the opposite way, and Rif's own reasoning seems to fit Rashba, one certainly cannot extract money from a possessor on Shach's certainty. A litigant can say kim li like Rashba's understanding of Rif and Rambam. Therefore, where a judge erred in an explicit Mishnah, even if he personally took property from one party and transferred it to the other, there is substantial basis to exempt him from payment according to Rif and Rambam. At minimum, the doubt cannot justify extracting money from the judge.
And some disagree. This is the opinion of Tosafot, Rosh, and those who follow them. They hold that according to R. Meir, who imposes liability for dina d'garmei, if a judge erred in an explicit Mishnah and the loss can no longer be reversed, the judge must pay even though the judgment itself would ordinarily be reversed. Likewise, according to those who rule like Rav Chisda, if the judge erred in judicial discretion and did not personally take and give, so that the judgment should be reversed, but reversal has become impossible, the judge is liable through dina d'garmei. Their principal proof is from the Mishnah in Bava Kamma and Bechorot concerning one who "declared the impure pure." At the first stage the Gemara wishes to establish the Mishnah according to R. Meir without yet requiring personal taking and giving. But if the judge declared impure produce pure, once the error is discovered the ruling itself is plainly reversible. Why then does the Mishnah say that he must pay? Necessarily, according to R. Meir, even where the ruling is reversible in principle, if practical reversal is no longer possible and the judge's mistake caused the loss, he must pay as dina d'garmei. According to the Sages, Rav later establishes liability only where the judge personally took and gave. Shach, following R. Chaim Yonah Teomim, raises several objections to Tosafot and Rosh. I do not find any of them sufficient to reject the great masters of Tosafot and Rosh. Their principal question is from the Gemara's challenge to Rav Sheshet from the incident of R. Tarfon's cow. The Mishnah nearby also contains the rule that a mistaken judge pays, and that Mishnah can be interpreted either according to R. Meir or according to the Sages where he personally took and gave. Why not interpret the R. Tarfon incident in the same manner and avoid any challenge to Rav Sheshet? If the case follows R. Meir, R. Tarfon could owe even though the judgment was reversible because the loss could no longer be undone. If he personally took and gave, then even according to the Sages payment could follow. Moreover, the answer to one side resolves the other. Ramban already writes that it is strained to say R. Tarfon himself actually fed the cow to the dogs. There is no natural basis to characterize the episode as the judge personally taking and giving. Thus the answer that he physically performed the loss is not compelling. One should not object that Rav Chisda, who distinguishes taking and giving, would then have to say R. Tarfon actually fed the cow to the dogs. Ramban himself can understand Rav Sheshet's statement differently: first, R. Tarfon did not personally take and give; and even if one were to classify the matter as analogous to an error of discretion, he was a recognized expert and therefore exempt. The more important point is that the incident was a famous actual event. It was known in the academy and not merely a hypothetical baraita. It is unreasonable to say the historical facts themselves vary depending upon whether one follows R. Meir or the Sages. The same is evident in Chullin, where Tosafot and Rashba challenge other positions from the story of R. Tarfon. They do not treat the facts of that episode as a Tannaitic dispute. Therefore the Gemara can properly challenge Rav Sheshet from the actual case without saying, "perhaps it followed R. Meir" or "perhaps he personally took and gave." Shach and R. Chaim Yonah also attack Tosafot's conceptual claim that according to R. Meir a judge can owe even though the judgment itself is reversible. But Rif himself accepts the underlying either-or. Rif argues that if one says R. Meir imposes dina d'garmei upon a judge whenever the judgment stands, then the same should logically apply even to an error in an explicit Mishnah despite the judgment's being reversible. Conversely, if judicial causation is exempt, it should be exempt across both categories unless there is personal taking and giving. Ramban in Milchamot strongly defends Rif's conceptual structure. Thus Rif, Ramban, Tosafot, and Rosh all agree on the principle that for R. Meir there is no inherent reason to distinguish liability merely because the legal judgment is formally reversible or irreversible. Either the judge's causation is treated as actionable, or it is not. Where such giants agree on the basic reasoning, who can casually overturn it? A separate objection compares this to one who waives a promissory note sold to another person. The Sages exempt that person because they do not impose dina d'garmei. If a judge whose mistaken ruling deprives another of money is liable even according to the Sages in some circumstances, why is the waiving creditor exempt? There are several answers. First, as Rosh writes in the first chapter of Bava Metzia in the name of a Gaon, a purchaser of a note bears some responsibility because he could have protected himself by arranging the documentation in his own name. Chazal may therefore have declined to penalize the original creditor in the same way. Second, Tosafot explain that the creditor who waives the debt acts upon a right that remains his own. Even after sale, the underlying personal obligation remains attached to the original creditor, while only the property lien was sold. When he waives his own retained right, the purchaser's lien falls away indirectly. Thus he acts within his own legal sphere and only secondarily damages the purchaser. That is classic indirect causation, for which the Sages exempt him. A judge who wrongfully exempts a debtor or transfers another person's property has no comparable proprietary right. His ruling directly interferes with another person's money. It can therefore be treated more strictly even by the Sages where the judge himself physically acts. This also explains why Tosafot compare the judge to one who burns another's document rather than to the creditor who waives his own retained right. Shach cites Tosafot in Chullin 43 as apparently contradicting Tosafot elsewhere. There, regarding the instruction to Rava, "Go tell him to pay the owner the value of the ox," Tosafot say that although Rava erred in an explicit Mishnah, the case involved his personally taking and giving. Shach argues that if Rava follows R. Meir and R. Meir imposes liability even where the judgment is reversible, why was the taking-and-giving element necessary? That is indeed a strong textual difficulty, and the answer should preserve harmony among Tosafot rather than invent a disagreement among schools of Tosafot where no early authority reports one. Ramban already asks another difficulty in that case: Rava was a recognized expert, and an accepted expert judge is normally exempt from payment. Rashba's answer that Rava was not accepted is strained, because the parties plainly came to him for a ruling in matters of issur v'heter, and such voluntary consultation itself ordinarily counts as acceptance. I therefore suggest that Rava was legally exempt, but R. Abba told him to pay beyond the strict law. We find a similar case with R. Chiya, who was a recognized expert and nevertheless paid after mistakenly validating a coin, as an act beyond the strict law. One might object that if so, R. Akiva's reassurance to R. Tarfon that he was a recognized expert and exempt would have been empty comfort, since R. Tarfon, a wealthy sage, would surely still have paid beyond the strict law. The answer is that judicial error is different. Where a judge is legally exempt for a nonphysical judicial mistake, Chazal did not impose even an extra-legal moral expectation to pay, because doing so would undermine the entire willingness of qualified sages to serve as judges. This is precisely Rif's institutional concern. But where the judge personally took and gave, that policy concern is much weaker. Who required him to physically insert himself and cause the loss with his own hands? In such a case, even though legal exemption remains because he is a recognized expert, an expectation beyond the strict law can remain. This explains Tosafot in Chullin. Rava was a recognized expert and therefore not strictly liable. Yet because he personally took and gave, R. Abba told him to pay as proper conduct beyond the strict law. R. Tarfon had not personally taken and given, so R. Akiva's statement that he was exempt was complete. This also fits Nimukei Yosef, who cites an explanation that "go pay" was said rhetorically, meaning that it would be appropriate to pay. Shach, however, also proposes a new interpretation of "he declared the impure pure." Before Rav Chisda's answer, Shach says the case need not involve mixing the impure produce into other produce. Rather, a buyer and seller ask the sage whether the produce is pure. The sage says it is pure, the buyer purchases it, and only afterward the error becomes known. The sale stands, and the judge must reimburse the buyer. R. Chaim Yonah correctly objects to this. Why should the buyer be forced to keep a purchase he made based upon an erroneous representation that the produce was pure? This is no ordinary judicial ruling between litigants. It is a mistaken piece of advice. Shach himself rules below in siman 306 that where an expert tells a creditor a coin is valid and on that basis the creditor accepts it, only to discover it was counterfeit, the waiver is mistaken and the creditor may return to the debtor. Why should produce be different? If the buyer bought in reliance on the sage's ruling that it was pure, the sale should be void or subject to rescission as a mistaken transaction. The suggested analogy to Ran's doubt concerning a possible tereifah also does not help. We do not rule like that approach in a manner that preserves a sale where the merchandise carries a serious halachic defect. Below, siman 232, the sale of an item with such a defect is treated as a mistaken sale. Conversely, where the animal is only of doubtful tereifah status, the butcher may be exempt because the claimant cannot prove the animal was certainly defective. The two principles actually run in opposite directions from Shach's interpretation. The sale can be rescinded because the buyer never agreed to acquire such doubtful or forbidden merchandise, while the expert may still be exempt absent proof that he caused a definite monetary loss. The Mishnah, however, says the act stands and the judge pays. That is much more naturally understood as Tosafot explain it: the owner, relying on the sage's ruling, mixed the produce into other produce so that practical reversal became impossible. Shach also complains that Tosafot's explanation requires different cases: where the impure item was mixed into a majority of pure produce, one aspect follows one rule; where it was mixed into a minority, another result follows. But once Rav and Rav Chisda already require the Mishnah to involve taking and giving or actual mixing, this division is unavoidable under their explanations too. There is no reason to reject the straightforward Tosafot reading merely to avoid a division that other views also require. It would have been possible to soften Shach's interpretation by imagining that the buyer had already acquired the produce but had not yet paid, and the sage mistakenly ordered him to pay because he ruled the produce pure. Even then, if the sale itself was mistaken the seller's right to payment is questionable. And there is no need to invent a novel reading of the Gemara that none of the early authorities gives us. Blessed is He who chose the early sages and their teachings. Therefore I retain Tosafot's straightforward interpretation. If a sage mistakenly rules that wine or produce is permitted, and the owner relies on the ruling and mixes it with other permitted produce, the sage can be liable where his mistaken ruling caused an irreversible loss. This is not analogous to one who accidentally renders produce meduma, because here "an error of a Torah scholar rises to the level of intentional conduct" in the sense that the owner relied on authoritative instruction and acted normally upon it. The same principle applies to wine. That is the correct practical consequence according to Tosafot and Rosh.
