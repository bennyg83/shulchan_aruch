A deaf-mute and a person of sound mind who lifted it. I was uncertain about a deaf-mute and a competent person who were both riding an animal or leading it. Is this like the case where both lift a found object, so that neither acquires, or does the defect arise only in hagbahah because one person must lift the entire object off the ground? There, when the deaf-mute holds part of it, it is as though half remains on the ground. Riding or leading, however, is an acquisition because the animal moves through his force. Perhaps one participant's ineffective act does not nullify the other's acquisition, and the competent person acquires the whole.
The first chapter of Bava Metzia 8 appears to indicate that even where two people ride, each would nullify the other's acquisition were it not for the rule that one who lifts a found object for another acquires it for the other. The Gemara initially seeks to prove that rule from the Mishnah where one says, 'It is entirely mine,' and the other says, 'Half is mine.' Why is this additional case needed? The redundant Mishnah teaches that one who lifts a found object for his fellow acquires for him. Rashi explains that they divide because we treat both as having lifted the object jointly, so each is lifting for himself and his fellow. If the division rested merely on uncertainty which party lies, the first clause would already teach it. The Gemara rejects that proof and later brings the case of two people riding an animal as the redundant Mishnah teaching the same rule. This implies that even with two riders we need the doctrine that one acquires for his fellow. Without it, each one's acquisition would interfere with the other's and neither would acquire. Therefore, if the pair are a deaf-mute and a competent person, where the doctrine of acquiring for one's fellow cannot operate in the ordinary manner, perhaps neither acquires.
Shitah Mekubetzet, however, cites Ritva explaining that the proof from two riders is only from the redundancy of the case. The Mishnah teaches that just as the two riders divide because they each acquired simultaneously through riding, so too the opening case of two people holding a garment divides because they acquired simultaneously through lifting. This implies that two riders acquire even without the doctrine that one lifts for his fellow. An acquisition based on causing the animal to move does not have one participant nullify the other's act. According to this, where a deaf-mute and a competent person ride or lead together, the competent person acquires the whole.
Rashi appears to hold otherwise, treating two riders as dependent upon the doctrine of acquiring for one's fellow. In Bava Metzia 9, s.v. 'rather, one riding where another leads,' Rashi explains the question as a case where one party rides and one leads, each saying the entire animal is his, and Shmuel says one of them acquires. Rashi's wording suggests that the uncertainty is not which act is legally capable of acquisition in the abstract, but which party is considered the stronger possessor. Tosafos explain the question as one of acquisition itself, asking which mode is stronger.
Rashi's approach is understandable in light of his view that where two people ride, each one's movement-causing acquisition would nullify the other's were it not for the doctrine that one acquires for his fellow. In the rider-versus-leader case, the possibility that riding is superior because the rider also holds the reins cannot mean that the reins independently acquire. The rider's acquisition remains based on the animal's moving through his force, just like the leader's. If the leader's act had no acquisition at all, then according to Rashi the rider's movement-based acquisition would likewise fail in the leader's presence, just as a competent person's acquisition fails when coupled with a deaf-mute whose ineffective participation prevents joint acquisition. The rider cannot be saved through the doctrine of acquiring for his fellow if we say the leader himself acquired nothing. Thus Rashi frames the question as which one has the stronger possession for purposes of adjudicating competing claims, while for pure acquisition both must stand equally. Tosafos, who frame the question as which acquisition is legally stronger, appear to follow Ritva's view that simultaneous riding does not require the doctrine of acquiring for another. An act that causes movement can coexist with another's similar act. It is therefore possible that riding acquires and leading does not, with the rider's acquisition remaining effective even though the leader also caused movement.
This also resolves Rashi's difficult wording on Bava Metzia 8, s.v. 'learn from here that riding acquires': 'Since he acquires when there is no leader, where there is a leader they disagree.' Commentators wondered, because the Gemara originally understood Shmuel's statement to ask whether one mode is stronger even in the presence of the other. According to what I wrote, Rashi means that the dispute concerning acquisition itself can exist only insofar as both modes are otherwise equal. If one said that the rider alone acquires and the leader does not, then according to Rashi the rider would also fail in the leader's presence, because each movement-based act would interfere with the other and the doctrine of acquiring for one's fellow could not rescue the rider when the leader himself has no acquisition. Hence for acquisition they must be treated alike, and the Gemara's question concerns their relative status as possessors. This is precise.
