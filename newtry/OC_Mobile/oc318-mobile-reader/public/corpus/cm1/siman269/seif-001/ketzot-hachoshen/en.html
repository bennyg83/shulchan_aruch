One who lifts a found object for another. Sema explains that Reuven must say while lifting, "I lift this found object in order to acquire it for Shimon." Shach disagrees and says that even without speech, if he admits that this was his intention, Shimon acquires. Sema's view is more persuasive. Matters in the heart do not ordinarily have legal force. Bare inner intention should therefore not suffice to acquire for another. There is support from the law where a person bought land intending eventually to dedicate it to sacred use. Mere intention does not create the dedication. If acquisition for another could be effected by thought alone, we could say the person acquired the land immediately for consecrated purposes through the same rule as one who lifts a found object for another. The fact that intention alone does not suffice indicates that acquisition for another likewise requires expressed intent. The sources concerning acquiring ownerless property for consecrated purposes also use an actual statement directing one's four cubits or one's acquisition to the consecrated purpose. Thus acquisition for another ordinarily requires words, not merely private intention.
There is an apparent difficulty from Bava Kamma concerning clothing and dye that a husband arranged for his wife. The Gemara asks, "Who informed the dyer that his dye should be acquired for the woman?" This suggests the husband can acquire the clothing for her merely through his own intention, without any verbal declaration. Perhaps the case is one where he had already stated before witnesses that he was buying for her, but merely failed to inform the dyer. That reading may be necessary for an additional reason: if his later statement that he had intended the purchase for her could remove property from the power of consecration, why should he be believed merely on his own later assertion? The earlier case concerning land intended for consecration can also be distinguished. There the purchaser did not mean that consecrated property should acquire immediately. He bought the land for himself with the intention that he would later dedicate it. His inner plan was therefore only a vow in the heart and had no legal effect. Furthermore, where one buys with his own money for another, Rabbeinu Yerucham says the other person does not acquire even if the buyer announces before witnesses that he buys for the other, because the seller intends to transfer to the owner of the money unless informed otherwise. The same would apply to buying for consecrated purposes with one's own money. Still, if someone expressly tells another, "Lift this found object for Reuven," and the person silently lifts it with that obvious purpose, Reuven acquires. This is not a private unexpressed intention. The circumstances themselves make the intent universally evident. Such "matters in everyone's heart" are legally effective.
