Reuven said to Shimon. One person spoke with another who was negotiating to buy an animal from a gentile and told him that if he acquired it he should sell it to him for a specified profit. The negotiator then bought it and later wished to retract. At first glance this resembles saying "acquire it for me," so that a general acquisition on the principal's behalf should be effective. Yet there are several grounds for doubt. In places where local enactments fix a standard brokerage fee even if a larger fee was promised, perhaps the negotiator's purchase cannot automatically be treated as an acquisition for the other. If it were, he would be only a broker and could not receive the larger profit they expressly discussed. Their conversation may therefore disclose that he intended to buy for himself and then resell at the agreed profit, which would leave him able to retract before the resale. Perhaps, however, the fixed brokerage rule applies only where he functioned solely as a broker and the buyer himself legally purchased from the original seller. Where the intermediary actually purchases from the gentile, even if he intends the acquisition for the other person, he may be entitled to the expressly agreed fee because he could have purchased for himself. This resembles the rule allowing substantial compensation for medical assistance and may fall outside the brokerage enactment. This is especially plausible if it is known that the intermediary had his own funds available. If he actually used his own money, the case is still more difficult to classify as "acquire for me." According to the authorities who hold that a gentile transfers movables through money rather than meshichah, the intermediary's later pulling of the animal does not effect acquisition for the principal, while his own money cannot simply leave his ownership and become the principal's through an act of agency. On the other hand, acquisition by payment can sometimes operate by the model of an eved Kena'ani, where one person's money effects acquisition for another without the recipient acquiring the money itself. Still, one may invoke the Talmudic question, "who informed the owner of the wheat" that the acquisition should be for the third party. This requires further study. Moreover, Tosafot in Bava Metzia 73 write that when the acquisition is directly from a gentile, one Jew does not necessarily acquire on behalf of another in the ordinary manner. It may be possible to distinguish our case. Separate from the law of agency, the principle that where another expended effort and purchased in reliance on a definite arrangement he may acquire rights from that effort requires a firm and sufficiently definite agreement. A mere statement without any act of acquisition is not always enough. Perhaps the rule applies especially where the contemplated item is not readily available elsewhere and the negotiations had progressed close to an actual sale. Even if Tosafot's reasoning prevents ordinary agency, one can perhaps say that once the gentile received the money the item became momentarily ownerless for purposes of Jewish title and the intermediary then acquired it from that status for the principal. In any event, some prior act such as money, meshichah, kinyan sudar, or situmta appears necessary before the principal can insist that the intermediary may not retract.
