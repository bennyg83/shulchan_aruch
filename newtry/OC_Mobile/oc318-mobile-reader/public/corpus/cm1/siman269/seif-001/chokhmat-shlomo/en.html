Seif 1: One who lifts a found object for another acquires it for him, etc. If a found object lies within a person's four cubits or courtyard, and he says, 'Let my courtyard or my four cubits acquire this found object for so-and-so,' does the other person acquire it? See the Ran's commentary to Nedarim 34b, s.v. 'there was a loaf of ownerless bread before him,' from which it is evident that in such a case the other person acquires. I explained the reason in my work there. If a person says that his courtyard should acquire for another an object already owned by himself, it is ineffective. Here it is stronger, because he himself has not yet acquired the object and could acquire it now if he wished. Therefore, through the principle that since he could acquire for himself he can acquire for another, it works. This principle is stronger than ordinary agency. We rule that one who seizes for a creditor where he thereby disadvantages others does not acquire, even if the creditor appointed him an agent; yet one who lifts a found object for another does acquire it because he could have acquired it for himself. The same applies to courtyard acquisition and four cubits. Although courtyard ordinarily operates through agency, and another person's courtyard cannot serve as his agent, where the object is not yet owned and the person standing there could acquire it now, we say that since he could acquire for himself he can acquire for another. This is also implied by the Ran. Other commentators there, however, imply that initially he should intend to acquire it for himself and only afterward allow the consecration to take effect, which suggests that one cannot immediately direct his four cubits to acquire for hekdesh. Nevertheless, one can say that as practical law they agree with the Ran regarding acquisition for another person. Their special rule regarding hekdesh may be because four cubits acquire by the law of courtyard, and we rule that courtyard operates through the law of a person's hand, while hekdesh has no 'hand,' as Magen Avraham, Orach Chayyim siman 154, writes in the name of Agudah that one who finds an object in a synagogue courtyard acquires it. How, then, could four cubits acquire for hekdesh, when hekdesh has neither courtyard nor hand? If those four cubits were literally a courtyard belonging to hekdesh, it would not acquire. But where one acquires for another person, and if that person himself were standing there and the courtyard belonged to him he would acquire, then even when the present person stands there he may say, 'Let my courtyard or four cubits acquire for so-and-so,' because he could acquire for himself and therefore can acquire for the other. All authorities can agree with the Ran on this point, for we minimize disputes. Indeed, there is a difficulty on the Ran himself: how can he explain the intent as acquiring the four cubits for hekdesh when hekdesh has no courtyard? This would imply that the Ran holds, contrary to Agudah, that hekdesh does have a courtyard. I have no time to elaborate. Perhaps Hashem will merit me at another time to explain it more fully.
