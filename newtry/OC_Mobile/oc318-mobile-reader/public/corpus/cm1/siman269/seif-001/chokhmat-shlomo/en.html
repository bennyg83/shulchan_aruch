Seif 1. One who lifts a found object for another acquires it for him. Note: If a found object lies within a person's four cubits or in his courtyard, and he says, "Let my courtyard or my four cubits acquire this object for so-and-so," does the other person acquire it? Ran to Nedarim 34 implies that he does. Although a courtyard cannot ordinarily transfer to another person property already owned by the courtyard owner, here the object has not yet become his. Since he could acquire it for himself, we apply the principle that because he could acquire it for himself, he can also acquire it for his fellow. This is stronger than ordinary agency, just as one may lift a found object for another even where ordinary seizure for a creditor would fail because it harms third parties. Other commentators on Nedarim appear to require him first to intend to acquire the object himself and only afterward consecrate it. They may nevertheless agree with Ran regarding acquisition for another person, while disagreeing only about consecration because a courtyard is treated as an extension of the hand and consecrated property has no "hand." Magen Avraham, citing Agudah, similarly rules that one who finds something in a synagogue courtyard may acquire it. Thus all may agree that for an ordinary person one can use one's own courtyard or four cubits to acquire a found object for him, because the owner of those four cubits could have acquired it personally. Ran's treatment of consecration might, however, imply that he holds consecrated property does have acquisition through a courtyard, contrary to Agudah. I have no time to expand. Seif 4.
