Once he lifted it. See Shach, seif katan 3. His intent is as Sema writes in seif katan 9, and as I will explain shortly.
The one who lifted it is not believed. See Shach, seif katan 4. Even though the Tur's words are not internally contradictory, it remains difficult to know the Tur's source for this rule. I saw in Responsa Geonim Batrai, siman 23, that Ulla and Rabbi Yochanan disagree about this point. Ulla holds that the person who lifted the item is believed even where the other said "acquire it for me," whereas Rabbi Yochanan holds that he is not believed in such a case. Therefore Rabbi Yochanan had to answer that "give it to me" is not equivalent to "acquire it for me." Had "give" meant "acquire," it would not help even if the person lifting it first stated otherwise. See what I wrote above in siman 183 so that the Ran's need, in the case of "buy a field for me," to explain that the agent is dishonest rather than simply saying he is not believed to claim that he withdrew from the agency, should not be difficult. There the case concerns money that had been given to him. It appears that only here, where witnesses heard the first person tell his fellow "give it to me," and then saw the latter lift it, is the latter not believed. If there are no witnesses, it is reasonable that he is believed to say that he lifted it for himself, through a migo that he could have denied receiving any instruction. Where do we find in the Gemara that the presumption that an agent did not deviate from his agency is so strong that one cannot be believed against it through a migo, especially where possession supports the migo? On the contrary, the presumption that an agent performs his mission is generally relied upon only stringently. Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 183 regarding shopkeepers' practice, states that even if a person expressly said he was purchasing for all of them, he can still claim that he was jesting. All the more so we should not create a presumption strong enough to defeat a migo. Since the Tur's case involves witnesses, and the person was told in their presence and remained silent, his silence resembles an admission. The Tur therefore reasonably holds that he is not believed, because had he wished to withdraw he should have said before the witnesses, at the time of lifting, that he was retracting from the agency. This resembles the statement in Gittin 55 that had he truly nullified the agency he would have informed the witnesses. Moreover, the case concerns an object seen only by the rider and not by the one who lifted it, as Responsa Geonim Batrai, siman 23, explains. In that setting the Tur's rule is readily understood.
He is believed to say. See Sema, seif katan 9, who writes that where one spontaneously lifts an item for his fellow, even if he later said that he lifted it for his fellow, he is believed to say that he had merely been jesting. Shach explains that Sema was driven to this because the Tur says he is believed when he says, "I lifted it for myself," which otherwise seems obvious since he lifted it without any statement. In my humble opinion, it is impossible to explain the Tur as discussing an admission made only after the lifting and then a retraction from that admission. This is not the implication of the Tur's language at all. Moreover, why would he have reason to jest after he already lifted it, when his fellow had been the one jesting with him? It therefore appears that there is a scribal error in the Sema, and it should read: "even if before he lifted it he said." Then the claim of jesting is readily understood, since he may have said it only to prevent his fellow from taking possession, while intending for himself at the moment of lifting. This can be read into the Tur's words. Shach also notes that the Tur stated this rule only because of the concluding case.
If he said, "give it to me." See Shach, seif katan 5, who writes that even if the person admits that he initially lifted it for the other person's benefit, he may retract and acquire it for himself. See Shitah Mekubetzet, which asks in Rashba's name that one who lifts a found object for his fellow acquires it for him even without the fellow's authorization. It answers that here he certainly intended only to carry out the specific instruction of his fellow, who said "give it," not "acquire it." According to Sema, who requires an express statement by the person lifting it, the matter is simpler: since he was told only "give," his fellow does not acquire merely because he lifted it for him, as he made no acquisition statement. Shach's further discussion of Tosafot Yom Tov is a separate matter. Tosafot Yom Tov asks against Sema's requirement of an express statement that the Gemara's question from the Mishnah in Pe'ah against the Mishnah in Bava Metzia could have been answered by saying that Pe'ah concerns an express statement while Bava Metzia concerns silent lifting. Shach answers that at that stage the Gemara assumed "give it to me" is equivalent to "acquire it for me," so no additional statement was required.
Once he gave it to him. See Sema, seif katan 11, who explains that once he gives it to the other person, and so forth. This follows Rashi's first explanation of the Mishnah. In the Gemara, however, Rashi explains that the reason is that the person thereby reveals that he originally lifted it with the other person in mind. Rashi needed this because if he had initially lifted it for himself, for example after expressly saying so before witnesses, later delivery would be attributed to a deposit rather than a gift. Only because the later delivery reveals that the original lifting was for the other person does the recipient acquire. Shitah Mekubetzet likewise writes this. According to this, even for us, where witnesses testify that he lifted it for himself, later delivery does not transfer title on this theory.
That he acquired it for himself. See what I wrote in siman 183 concerning this.
