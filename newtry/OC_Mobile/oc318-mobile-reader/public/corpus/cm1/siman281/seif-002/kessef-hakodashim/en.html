And he must fulfill his condition. A man, while gravely ill, divided his property among his widow and his orphaned son and daughter, assigning each a specified sum. He further said that his son in law should receive nothing, notwithstanding a written obligation for a specified amount, because the son in law had already received approximately a year's additional support beyond what the later marriage conditions required, and that amount should be deducted from the written obligation. He did not mention the son in law's shtar chatzi zachar. Afterward, a shtar chatzi zachar was found entitling the son in law to 400 Polish gold coins. According to the will the son himself was to receive only 600 gold coins. There was movable property not mentioned in the will. That property belongs to the son under the ordinary law of inheritance. Regarding a daughter's dowry, although we follow the Taz that where one daughter was married during the father's lifetime one may use that amount as an estimate even to add beyond one tenth of the estate, this does not contradict Maharik, root 78, who dealt with daughters whose marriage provisions were not equal. Even if most authorities generally do not add beyond one tenth, they may agree where the father himself established a clear measure by marrying off another daughter. Here, however, the father expressly directed that the younger daughter receive a fixed sum, and that express will is stronger than the ordinary estimate. Similar reasoning appears in the Nachalat Shivah and Beit Shmuel concerning marriage conditions. The surplus property therefore belongs to the son, and his share will exceed the stated amount. The son in law's shtar chatzi zachar should not be reduced merely because the testator said that there was "no inheritance" here, or even, according to the widow's account, because she reminded him of the shtar chatzi zachar and he still said that the son in law should not take under it. The Beit Shmuel's statement that an amount added beyond one tenth is not deducted from a shtar chatzi zachar concerns an addition reached by compromise with the heir rather than an express deathbed gift. Nevertheless, this will still does not necessarily defeat the shtar chatzi zachar. A deathbed gift to outsiders can indeed reduce the estate and thereby reduce what later remains for the shtar chatzi zachar, because that instrument becomes payable only after death and follows the estate then available for inheritance. The clause customarily stating that payment is due one hour before death does not change the essential structure, for the instrument gives a half share of an ordinary son's inheritance and is realized when the heirs descend to the estate after death. Thus an effective gift to outsiders can diminish the estate before the shtar chatzi zachar is measured. A gift to the testator's own son is different. We rule that a gift by a dying person to one who is an heir does not cease to have the legal character of inheritance merely because the language of gift was used. The Rama in siman 250 seif 12, the Beit Yosef, Nimukei Yosef, and Rif all indicate that with respect to an heir, such a deathbed disposition remains inheritance in substance. Therefore the 600 gold coins given to the son are also part of the estate in the sense relevant to the shtar chatzi zachar, and certainly the unmentioned surplus property remains ordinary inheritance. The son in law's half share should therefore be calculated against those assets as well. Even if the deceased expressly stated that his arrangement was not inheritance and, according to the widow, expressly intended to deny the shtar chatzi zachar, the accepted form of that instrument includes an undertaking not to evade or undermine the daughter's right. Although here only a kinyan sudar and signature had yet been made, that is treated as though the instrument had been written in the customary form. We therefore prefer to interpret the deceased's statement "there is no inheritance" as resting on his mistaken assumption that the marriage conditions themselves effected a transfer, for he expressly said that he was merely completing those conditions. It is not proper to invent a novel form of will that defeats the accepted shtar chatzi zachar contrary to the rulings of the authorities. Since he was not a learned man, there is even less basis to assume that he intended a sophisticated restructuring of the son's share into a gift taking effect after death solely in order to defeat the inheritance character. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek emphasizes careful reading of the wording of a shtar chatzi zachar. The usual language grants the daughter one half of the share of an ordinary son. Thus, even if the father's gift to the son were expressly described as noninheritance, so long as it is a deathbed transfer taking effect only after death, there is strong reason to include whatever the son receives within the practical measure of an ordinary son's share. At minimum, in the present case the phrase "there is no inheritance" appears to have resulted from the father's mistake concerning the marriage conditions, not from an intent to destroy the shtar chatzi zachar. The Beit Yosef in siman 250, before the discussion of partial retraction, cites a responsum of the Rashba that also leaves room for this approach. In practice we arranged a mutually accepted compromise.
