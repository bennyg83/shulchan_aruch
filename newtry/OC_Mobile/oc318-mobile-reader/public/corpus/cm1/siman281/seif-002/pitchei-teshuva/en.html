A certain man, my brother, shall inherit me. Netivot HaMishpat writes that although inheritance passes first through the father, it follows that where there are many sons of a brother one may bequeath the entire estate to one of them. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, responsum 192, concerning a person on his deathbed who gave his estate to one of his brother's sons. The questioner argued that according to Baal HaTerumot, who does not say with respect to other heirs that they come by force of their father's father, the deceased brother in the grave is the true heir rather than this nephew, and therefore Rabbi Yochanan ben Beroka's rule should not apply. He further wondered whether, even according to the authorities who generally recognize representation, all the nephews are treated as one unit so that one cannot separate among them. Rabbi Akiva Eiger answered that according to the authorities who reject Baal HaTerumot there is no doubt that the disposition is effective. He infers this from the Rambam's formulation, 'or to one of his son's sons,' showing that even where several descendants are considered together, one who is entitled to some share may be given the whole. He adds that even according to Baal HaTerumot one can derive from the Rambam that where the inheritance comes by representation it may still be assigned to one of the representatives. He discusses Tosafot, Bava Batra 108b, at length, initially seeking support and later rejecting that proof and deriving the opposite. As to Netivot's assumption that a brother inherits through representation from the father, Rabbi Akiva Eiger disagrees. In responsum 192 he writes that this would have been true had the Torah not expressly listed brothers, but once brothers are written in the verse, a brother inherits directly. In responsum 138 he notes that Beit Yosef and Rama in Darkei Moshe, siman 253, disagree on this point: Beit Yosef regards a brother's inheritance as direct, while Darkei Moshe treats it as passing by representation through the father.
