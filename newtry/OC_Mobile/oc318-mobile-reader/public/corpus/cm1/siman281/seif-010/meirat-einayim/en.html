We do not follow an assessment of presumed intent, etc. This refers to the preceding case, where the heirs of three deceased persons take even though it was evident that their intention had been to keep the brother from inheriting. The Rosh and Tur explain the reason. Whenever we follow presumed intent, we ordinarily annul an act that was performed and restore matters to their original legal state. Thus, if one heard that his son died and wrote all his property to others, and afterward the son appeared alive, the gift is void because we assess that had he known his son was alive he would never have made it. The act is therefore cancelled and the property returns to its prior status, so the son inherits. Here, however, although it is known that they intended to keep the brother from inheriting, they did not actually perform a legally effective act that accomplishes such exclusion. An unexpressed intention cannot uproot a Torah inheritance.
Whichever preceded returns and acquires. Rama abbreviates here. The matter is explained fully in the responsum of the Rosh and in the Tur. The brother inherits whatever remained in the possession of the sisters who died. Sometimes he inherits everything, namely where the three who gave first died and the surviving sister was the one who gave last. Each sister gave her own share and whatever she had received from the others, so that when the final sister made her transfer nothing remained with her. The brother therefore inherits the entire property. If one of the sisters who did not give last remains alive, the brother inherits everything except whatever remains in that surviving sister's hands according to the calculation. Rama abbreviated because the Rosh and Tur discussed a concrete case in which all four sisters were partners with equal property, allowing the estate to be divided mathematically into four equal parts. Rama states the rule generally concerning four sisters who were financially separate and whose property would not necessarily be equal.
