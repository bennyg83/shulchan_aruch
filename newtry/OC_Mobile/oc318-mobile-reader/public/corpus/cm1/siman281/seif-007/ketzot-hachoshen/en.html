But if he used language of gift. Sema says there is little novelty for an ordinary son, because even inheritance language can increase or reduce his share unless the father expressly says he shall not inherit. Shach asks why the earlier rule says that if the father tells the firstborn, "he shall not take two shares," the words are ineffective, since "take" is ordinarily language of gift. There is no real doubt. Positive language such as "he shall take," "he shall acquire," or "he shall possess" can be language of gift. But saying "he shall not take" is merely denying a gift. An ordinary son still inherits automatically despite being denied a gift. A firstborn is different because Scripture itself calls the double portion a gift and places it within the firstborn's power to refuse. Therefore the Mishnah uses "he shall not take" regarding the firstborn and "he shall not inherit" regarding an ordinary son. If the father says, "Shimon shall not take, rather Reuven shall take everything," the positive words granting to Reuven are effective gift language even though the exclusionary clause concerning Shimon is ineffective as such. The same applies in the firstborn context. The recipients named positively can acquire through valid gift language.
And he wrote to her and to the descendants of her body, daughters and sons, that they shall take equally. Mahari Weil rules that where a deed says "to my daughter and to her descendants," and the daughter dies before the father, her male and female descendants share equally. He relies on the broad meaning of "descendants," which includes males and females. His wording implies that this equality applies specifically where the daughter died before the donor. If the father died first, the daughter herself already acquired, and upon her later death ordinary inheritance should favor sons over daughters. Rama states the rule without this limitation, which is difficult. There is a deeper problem. The donor cannot presently transfer to descendants not yet in existence. If the deed's essential transfer is only to the daughter, with "and her descendants" merely describing who will take through her later, then those descendants should inherit from their mother under ordinary inheritance law, and daughters should not share equally with sons. Rosh's responsum concerning attempts to create a perpetual primogeniture confirms that conditions cannot grant property to future generations not yet in existence. The only secure method is to transfer presently to someone already alive and then, when a later heir is born, create a new valid transfer. Therefore Mahari Weil's broad equality among unborn male and female descendants requires study. It may be that the particular deed was structured as a present obligation payable to the daughter or, if she predeceased the donor, to whoever then constituted her descendants, with the amount held as an obligation rather than a direct present gift to unborn persons. Yet even then the legal mechanism must be analyzed carefully. In ordinary inheritance, once the daughter herself is the source of title, sons and daughters do not share equally.
One who lent on books, the creditor does not acquire the pledge. Mahari Weil wrote that even a pledge taken outside the time of the loan is not fully acquired except for limited purposes, such as kiddushin. Sema left this difficult because elsewhere a creditor is said to acquire a pledge. Shach concludes that where the lender took the pledge outside the time of the loan, the heirs' possession should protect them against a daughter's claim. In my view, even where a creditor acquires a pledge in a significant sense, the underlying debt remains. The debtor can ordinarily redeem the pledge by paying money, and the creditor cannot force the debtor to satisfy the debt through the pledged object itself. Therefore a daughter whose deed grants her a share even in expectant claims has rights in the underlying debt, even if she has no direct share in the physical books as pledged property. If the debtor has no cash and eventually satisfies the debt by surrendering the pledge itself, that is later payment at its appraised value. The daughter's share in the debt therefore gives her a corresponding share in the value received. The phrase that the creditor does not acquire the pledge can thus be regarded as an additional reason, but even if he does acquire it, the daughter still has a claim in the debt itself.
