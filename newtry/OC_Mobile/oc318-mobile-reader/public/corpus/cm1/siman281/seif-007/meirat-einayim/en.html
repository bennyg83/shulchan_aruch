He increased one son's share, reduced another's, and made the firstborn equal to them. This is also Rambam's wording in chapter 6 of Hilchot Nachalot. The clause "increased one and reduced another" adds no novelty regarding ordinary sons, for it has already been explained that even with language of inheritance his words are effective unless he says in negative form, "So-and-so my son shall not inherit except such and such, and my other son shall inherit such and such." In that form his words do not take effect, as explained above in seif 3.
Even though he mentioned language of inheritance at the beginning and end. Although Rashbam, cited by the Tur, holds that where one combines gift and inheritance language it is actually stronger to mention inheritance both at the beginning and end than to omit it, that is where he gives two fields to two different people. In that situation, just as the repeated inheritance language clearly applies to both, the gift language is likewise read as applying to both. Here, however, the case concerns one recipient, and therefore saying inheritance language at both beginning and end is the greater novelty.
The language of gift must be interwoven with all three. Meaning, he says, "So-and-so, so-and-so, and so-and-so shall inherit field A, field B, and field C, which I have given them as a gift, and they shall inherit." This works according to everyone, because each person is included in both the inheritance language and the gift language. According to Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, even if he omits either the first or last inheritance expression and uses one inheritance phrase together with interwoven gift language, since he groups them together and applies gift language to all, the gift language overcomes the inheritance formulation. See the Tur.

Its law is like ordinary inheritance. Although a creditor and ketubah claimant ordinarily do not collect from movable property that the debtor gave to others, and an oral creditor likewise does not collect from land already gifted, this case differs. It is not a complete lifetime gift; rather, she takes his property after his death like one half of a son. Its law is therefore that of inheritance.
He must give her from everything he owns. Nevertheless, it has the law of inheritance in the other direction as well: the creditor and ketubah are first collected from the estate, and only the remainder is divided according to the half-male-share clause.
A creditor does not acquire the collateral. We rule that a creditor acquires a pledge only where he takes it after the loan, not where the loan itself was made on the pledge. Here the case is the latter. Mahariv, siman 109, writes that even where the pledge was taken after the loan, the creditor acquires it only for certain purposes, such as using it to betroth a woman, not for every legal effect. See there and Hagahot Darkei Moshe. The Ir Shushan challenges Rama here, saying, "We already wrote in siman 278:7 that we rule a creditor acquires a pledge." He did not examine the matter carefully. I wrote there in siman 278:6 that although Rashbam uses the expression "acquires the pledge," he does not mean that a pledge taken at the time of the loan is literally acquired. Rather, since a pledge taken after the loan is acquired, even one taken at the time of the loan, though not owned by the creditor, is in his hand and is considered presently held rather than merely expected property. The firstborn therefore takes a double portion in it. But for a daughter to receive a share under a gift formula requiring the property to be fully acquired by her father, mere possession is insufficient. For this reason the Tur and Mechaber carefully state there that the creditor is "in possession" of the pledge, not that he acquires it. This is clear.
