But if he used language of gift. See Shach, seif katan 5, who cites Responsa Mabit. His work is not presently available to me, but Shach's quotation indicates that a father can remove the firstborn from his firstborn share when the firstborn also renounces it, as in that case, where the father gave him a house during his lifetime in exchange for his firstborn share and the firstborn accepted it willingly. This supports what I wrote in siman 278, that with the firstborn's consent he can be removed from his firstborn share; see there.
Language of gift. See Shach, seif katan 6, who writes that the same is true if the father says of an ordinary son, "he shall not take," because "take" is language of gift. This is exceedingly difficult. The Beit Yosef's entire reason that language of gift is effective is that a person can give his own property to another as a gift, leaving nothing for the firstborn to inherit. If he merely says, "he shall not take," what does it help that the word has the form of gift language? Ketzot HaChoshen likewise questions this. Shach's further statement that if he says of the firstborn, "he shall take like an ordinary son," it is effective, is also very difficult. Even if language of gift grants the firstborn part of the estate, by what means is his inheritance right in the remainder removed? This is also indicated by seif 10; examine it carefully. It appears clear that the ruling is effective only if the father gives the remaining property to the brothers through language of gift. In my humble opinion, Sema, seif katan 9, means that the novelty concerns cases where one clause depends upon another. With language of inheritance, if he says, "Shimon shall not inherit, rather Reuven," he has said nothing. But if he says, "Reuven shall not inherit, rather it shall be given to Shimon," the entire statement is effective.
And he increased one person's share. I am astonished that the ruling cited in Darkei Moshe in the name of Nimukei Yosef was omitted, namely that if one says an ordinary son shall take a firstborn share, whether he has the law of a firstborn with respect to property merely due; see there.
And the descendants of her body, her daughters and sons. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this because those descendants do not yet exist and one cannot transfer property to someone not yet born. He discusses the matter at length and concludes against Rama. In truth, Rama's words are clear and the principle is correct. The standard half male share document operates by having the father obligate himself in a sum of one thousand gold coins, while stipulating that if the heirs choose to give the child who will later be born a specified amount, they are released from the debt. Once they fulfill the condition and give a half male share, half to a son and half to a daughter according to the obligor's condition, they are released from the debt. Certainly, if one obligates himself in a maneh and stipulates that giving something to a person not yet born will release him, the condition is effective, because a person may make a condition concerning something not yet in existence. If, however, the inheritance share exceeds the debt and the heirs wish merely to discharge the debt itself, the money belongs to the sons by inheritance and the daughters have no right to it. According to this, the wording in the standard half male share document printed in Nachalat Shivah, where the principal debt is stated as owed "to her and to the descendants of her body," is a scribal error. The reference to descendants need be written only in the condition, not in the principal debt.
A creditor does not acquire a pledge. See Sema, Shach, and Ketzot HaChoshen. In my humble opinion, the proper distinction is as follows. If the debtor did not pay with money and the heirs ultimately collected the debt from the pledge itself, then even if the pledge was given at the time of the loan, all agree that the daughter has no share in the books. According to what I wrote above in siman 72 and siman 113, with a pledge, even according to Rava, collection works retroactively, as Rashi writes in Pesachim 31 regarding the Mishnah of a gentile who lent money. Consequently, if the creditor had consecrated or sold his interest, the act remains effective when he ultimately collects, because he is deemed to have acquired retroactively. Since the books thus become the father's retroactively, the daughter has no right in them. But if the debtor paid with money, Ketzot HaChoshen is correct and the daughter has a share in the debt.
