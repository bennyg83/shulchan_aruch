See there, and also Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 186, and above siman 209, seif 8, seif katan 14. Shevut Yaakov III, 174, discusses a man who died leaving a firstborn son and a daughter holding a standard half-male-share instrument that entitled her to a stated sum or, at the heirs' option, half the share of an ordinary son. The firstborn argued that the estate should be divided into five units, four going to him and one to the daughter. One dayan argued that the daughter should receive one third, contending from Bava Batra 127 that the Torah's double portion for a firstborn applies only in relation to another son. Shevut Yaakov rejects this as an error. If the father had given the daughter a full ordinary-son share, she plainly would receive one third against a firstborn brother, since her contractual share cannot exceed the Torah share of an ordinary son. Thus, with a half ordinary-son share, the estate is divided into five parts: the firstborn receives four and the daughter one. This applies to property presently held; with property deemed prospective, where the firstborn has no double portion, the daughter receives one third. R. David Oppenheim and Kiryat Chana 31 agreed. By contrast, Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat end of 19, ruled that where the only male child is the firstborn, the daughter receives one third because the firstborn takes the whole estate by his status as a son and not by an additional firstborn right. He understood the standard wording 'half an ordinary son's share' to address the case where ordinary sons exist, and held that otherwise the daughter takes half the firstborn's effective share. Pitchei Teshuva therefore records a dispute among the great authorities. Shev Yaakov 20 further cites She'erit Yosef that where a father undertook by kinyan to give a half-male-share instrument, the debt written must be large enough to ensure that the daughter in fact receives the intended half share. Shev Yaakov adds that if the father later became wealthy so that the original fixed debt no longer assures the promised half share, a replacement instrument may be required to fulfill the original undertaking, though this point requires study. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 51, discusses a father who first gave his daughter a standard half-male-share note for one thousand and years later, to appease her after giving another daughter a larger dowry, issued a second such note for two thousand. The heirs argued that the second note canceled the first. Chatam Sofer rules that this principle does not apply to loan instruments: according to Rif and Rosh, even two notes for the same amount and date may both be collected. Since the half-male-share instrument is fundamentally structured as a debt, both notes may be collected, though because the result is somewhat contrary to ordinary expectation he recommends compromise.
