Several years later a creditor of Reuven sought to collect his debt from the son-in-law because the property inherited by Reuven's son had been lost. If the son-in-law had left in the heir's hands enough property to pay the debt, the creditor has no claim against him. Maharam of Lublin, siman 4, cited in Chukei Mishpat, letter 10, seif 3. If a man wrote his daughter a customary deed granting a half-share of a male child for a fixed amount and later died leaving no sons, only daughters, while he wrote such a deed only to that one daughter, Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 95, rules that she has no priority and all the daughters divide equally. We say that the father's intent in writing the half-male-share deed was only to protect her against sons, if he would have sons, not against his other daughters. A pledge. We rule that a creditor acquires a pledge only when it is taken other than at the time of the loan, whereas here the case is that he lent money on the pledge. Mahari Weil there writes that even a pledge taken other than at the time of the loan is acquired only for purposes such as betrothing a woman with it, not for every legal consequence. See there. The Levush challenges Rama here and writes: in siman 278, seif 7, it was stated that a creditor acquires a pledge, and this requires study. But this is not precise. There it was written that although Rashbam uses the expression 'acquires a pledge,' he refers to a pledge taken other than at the time of the loan. His intent is that since a creditor acquires such a pledge, even when the pledge was given at the time of the loan, although he does not acquire it fully, because it is in his possession it is considered presently available rather than merely prospective, and therefore a firstborn takes a double share from it. In the present law, however, the issue is not whether the property is presently available, but whether the father had complete ownership of it, and therefore the daughter does not take a share in the pledge. For this reason the Tur and Shulchan Aruch carefully did not say there that the reason is because the creditor acquired the pledge, but because he is in possession of it. This is clear. Sema. The Shach writes that the Sema's words are correct in explanation, but as a matter of law it appears that a pledge taken other than at the time of the loan is acquired completely. I concluded in siman 72, seif 2, that the principal ruling follows Rashi and those who agree with him, unlike Mahari Weil, who followed the Rosh and those who agree with him. Even if one were to say that the matter is a doubt in law, the heirs are in possession and the daughters would bear the burden of proof. See Migdal David and Responsa Rama, siman 77.
