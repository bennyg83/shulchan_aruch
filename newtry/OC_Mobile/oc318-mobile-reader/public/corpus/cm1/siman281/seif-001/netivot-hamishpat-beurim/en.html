My brother’s wife, and even her father’s wife, is a sign of her father’s death, and she is given to her father’s father from his father’s office that he will be able to let him go of his father’s womb and so on, at the end of the day, he will bring his son to his father, and that he will be the heir to his father, and to his father’s house
His words are effective. Beit Yosef cites Nimukei Yosef in the name of Ritva that this applies only when he distributes the entire estate, but not a part. Otherwise we would find a partial gift effective through inheritance language, which is not found throughout the Talmud. This implies that Torah gave a father power to designate inheritance only when he distributes all his property among his sons. On that reading, even a kinyan would not help for a partial designation in inheritance language, because kinyan does not validate defective inheritance language. It is surprising Mechaber omitted such a rule. At first glance Nimukei Yosef himself is also difficult. Bava Batra 131 seeks to prove that even a healthy person can designate inheritance from the enactment of ketubat benin dichrin, which Chazal formulated with "they shall inherit." Yet that institution is only a partial transfer. A dinar must remain, and from the outset only the amount of the ketubah is being assigned. It is also difficult to say Nimukei Yosef created such a major rule with no clear Talmudic source. In my view Nimukei Yosef holds, like most authorities, that a healthy person cannot use inheritance language. His intent concerns a shechiv mera. One might think that since a shechiv mera who dies from that illness is in the period called "the day he causes his sons to inherit," inheritance language should work even for a partial gift. But Chazal themselves gave a partial shechiv mera gift the legal status of a healthy-person gift, because we infer he expects to live. Therefore, for inheritance designation too, he is treated as a healthy person with respect to a partial disposition. When he distributes the whole estate, however, that very conduct shows his mind is on death, and then inheritance language works. Accordingly, if he explicitly commands because of impending death, even a partial inheritance designation should be effective, because the requirement of "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" is satisfied. This also resolves the attempted proof from ketubat benin dichrin. Once the Gemara seeks to prove inheritance language works even for a healthy person, partial transfer would pose no problem. Nimukei Yosef's limitation exists only because a partial shechiv mera disposition ordinarily receives healthy-person status. This is why he says we never find a partial disposition effective without kinyan: in all other laws a partial shechiv mera gift is treated as the act of a healthy person.
Whether he said it orally, his words are effective. I was uncertain whether, if a father designates an ordinary son to receive a double share, that son also acquires property that comes into the estate afterward. One might argue that once he has been made an heir, he inherits like any other heir, including a davar shelo ba la'olam. We say only that one cannot designate a person not yet in existence as heir, just as one cannot give him property. In my view this is not so. The designation does not reach a davar shelo ba la'olam or property not presently in the father's control. Proof appears from Bava Batra 133, which asks what practical difference there is between acquisition as inheritance and as gift. If inheritance designation reached things a gift cannot reach, the Gemara could have answered simply that inheritance works for a davar shelo ba la'olam and intangible rights. A further proof comes from Bava Batra 134, where Rashbam explains that the verse of recognition applies where it is known the claimant is his son. If one could simply designate a double inheritance share in after-acquired property, then the Gemara in Bava Batra 156, which needs "he shall recognize" for property acquired afterward, becomes difficult. Why would Scripture be needed? The father would have a migo that he could simply designate that son for a double share, including later-acquired property. Necessarily, just as he cannot transfer such property, he cannot designate it through inheritance language. One cannot answer that he might thereby impair the true firstborn's share, because where firstborn status is disputed, the other claimant would need proof of firstborn status to displace the designated heir. If he brings proof, even the father's recognition would not help against witnesses. Accordingly, the same applies to an oral loan, which is not presently in the father's possession. Even a documented loan, according to authorities who treat sale of debt instruments as rabbinic, cannot be designated d'oraisa through inheritance if it cannot be transferred d'oraisa. Proof appears from Bava Batra 151 concerning the mother of Rav Amram Chasida, who had a bundle of notes and said they should belong to her son Amram. The Gemara says he acquired through shechiv mera gift. It does not say he acquired through inheritance designation, even though he was fit to inherit. This shows that anything the father cannot transfer d'oraisa cannot be given greater effect merely by inheritance language. Therefore, when a shechiv mera says, "My son so-and-so shall inherit a double share," property capable of d'oraisa transfer can be acquired through inheritance d'oraisa. Things not transferable d'oraisa can be acquired only through the rabbinic law of a shechiv mera gift. Inheritance language does not weaken that rabbinic gift where the recipient is fit to inherit. The general rule is that there is no category of property acquired more powerfully through inheritance designation than through shechiv mera gift. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that once a father says, "My son so-and-so shall inherit," the son becomes a d'oraisa heir and the father can no longer reduce that right by later saying, "He shall not inherit." Nimukei Yosef explicitly says the donor can retract, implying complete retraction. Bava Batra 131 likewise does not list retraction as a practical distinction between inheritance and gift. Ketzot's view therefore cannot stand.
