A firstborn shall not take a double portion. Ramban counts as a Torah prohibition the father's attempt to deprive the firstborn of his double share. The verse says that he may not prefer the younger son over the firstborn, and Ramban explains that this is not merely a rule that the father's words are legally ineffective. It is an actual prohibition. He further understands the command to recognize the firstborn and give him two shares as a positive commandment. Thus one who attempts to transfer away the firstborn portion violates both a positive and a negative command. The Gemara, however, derives from the phrase "he cannot make firstborn" that the father can alter ordinary inheritance shares while he cannot alter the firstborn share. If that phrase merely created a prohibition, why would it prove that ordinary shares can be changed? Perhaps both attempted changes are legally ineffective, with the firstborn case alone carrying an additional prohibition. The answer lies in Ramban's own reading of the verse. Had Scripture merely said, "he shall not make firstborn," we might read only an obligation. By saying, "he cannot make firstborn," the Torah teaches both prohibition and legal incapacity. The special legal incapacity is confined to firstborn status, and from that restriction we infer that ordinary inheritance can indeed be redirected where the Torah permits. This also fits Sifrei's distinction: the father is not authorized to make another son firstborn, and if he attempts it, the act is ineffective.
My brother so and so shall inherit me among my brothers. This is difficult because the father precedes all of his descendants in the inheritance order. If the father is deceased, he inherits in the grave and transmits equally to his sons. How can the decedent designate one brother to inherit more than the others when they all come through their father? Rambam likely derives the rule from the Tosefta, which implies that designation of one brother among brothers can be effective. The reason is that although the brothers formally inherit through their father, the father himself inherits from the decedent. Since the inheritance comes from the decedent's estate, the decedent may use the Torah's power of designation among eligible heirs to choose one brother. The same should apply where a son predeceased the father and left several children. The grandfather can designate one grandson among that son's children, even though they inherit through their deceased father, because their father's right ultimately derives from the grandfather.
