Seif 7, gloss. Nevertheless, regarding a document granting a daughter half the share of a son, etc. Note: I was asked about a man who had two sons and one daughter. Before his death he instructed that his sons were not to divide his estate until his wife died, and that during her lifetime she would control all the property. In the interim much of the estate was lost. At the time of his death, the daughter's half-son share exceeded the amount of the debt alternative provided in the document, but by the time the wife died the assets had diminished and the half-son share was less than the debt. In the meantime one son also died without viable descendants, and the surviving brother inherited him. I was asked whether the daughter receives the debt amount or her present half-son share, and whether her fraction is one fifth or one third. I answered that certainly here, where the heirs could not immediately fulfill the alternative condition of paying the debt because the father expressly directed that there be no division until after his wife's death, the relevant valuation is at the time of division. Indeed, even if the father had not so instructed and the heirs could have paid immediately, as long as the daughter had not demanded one alternative, the heirs had not selected which alternative to give her, and they continued to transact with the assets until they diminished in the ordinary course of business without negligence, they may give her the half-son share as it now stands. We follow the time of division, not the time of death, and the brothers retain the right to choose the applicable alternative until they actually select one. A proof is the rule in Even HaEzer 113 that heirs may satisfy creditors with movables and retain the land, thereby reducing or eliminating the daughter's half-son share. Even though the creditors' principal lien is on the land, and at the moment of death the heirs acquired the movables so that the daughter then had a valuable half-son share, the heirs may later reverse the allocation and give the movables to the creditors. The Beit Shmuel explains there that they may arrange matters as they wish because the determining moment is division, not death. The same applies here. However, once they have selected one path, I believe a verbal selection alone can bind them, though a distinction is necessary. If they selected the path of paying the debt, they become obligated to pay, like one who acknowledges before witnesses, 'I owe you one hundred under a document,' where the obligation arises through speech. But if they said they would give the half-son share, that is a transfer in the property itself and requires an act of acquisition. Without one, they may retract and say they were merely jesting or delaying her, comparable to the rules in Choshen Mishpat 166 and 335 concerning 'take what you produced as your wages.' As to the second question, the daughter receives only one fifth. The share of the son who died without descendants was not inherited by the surviving brother from their father but from his deceased brother. Just as descendants of that deceased son would have inherited his share, so too the brother stands in their place and inherits from him, not from the father. Even according to the implication of the Gemara in Yesh Nochalin that the inheritance is notionally returned to the father and then passes from him to the surviving son according to the order of inheritance, it is as though the father acquired those assets after his own death. His undertaking extended only to what remained from him at the time of death, not to property notionally acquired later. Therefore the daughter has only one fifth, with four fifths going to her brother. This is what I ruled.
