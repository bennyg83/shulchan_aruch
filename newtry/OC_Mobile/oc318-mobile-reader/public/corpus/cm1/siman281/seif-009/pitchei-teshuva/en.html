It is the debt. Baer Heitev cites Maharam Lublin concerning a half-male-share instrument and states that a creditor has no claim against the relevant property. Ateret Tzvi questions that ruling; see also Taz. Baer Heitev refers to Maharshal 49 and Rama 3. Their case concerned Reuven, who married off his daughter while his father Yaakov was alive and gave the daughter and son-in-law a standard half-male-share instrument: a fixed debt of one thousand gold coins, with the heirs having the option to discharge the debt by giving the son-in-law half the share of an ordinary son in all property, both presently held and prospective. Reuven then died during Yaakov's lifetime, and before Reuven's heirs divided his estate, Yaakov died leaving substantial property. The son-in-law claimed a contractual half share even in Yaakov's estate because that property was prospective with respect to Reuven and the instrument expressly included prospective assets. Maharshal ruled for the son-in-law. Reuven's sons ultimately inherit through Reuven, and the fact that Reuven died before his father and never possessed the property does not diminish the daughter's contractual right. Even if an ordinary creditor cannot collect prospective property, the half-share instrument is written as a debt coupled with a condition: the heirs must either pay the fixed debt or, if they choose, give the half share in both present and prospective property. A person may validly condition on such prospective property. Rama agreed, and Maharam Padua approved the ruling. Maharam Lublin 14 likewise preferred Maharshal to She'erit Yosef, but limited the rule. The son-in-law can claim the grandfather's estate only if the grandfather died before the heirs had divided with him. If they divided Reuven's estate while Yaakov was still alive, the later estate of Yaakov is not included. Moreover, this option presupposes that Reuven left enough estate to satisfy the fixed debt. If he left only enough to pay part of the debt, or left nothing, the son-in-law cannot reach Yaakov's later estate through the half-share clause, because the heirs have no personal duty to pay their father's debt beyond inherited assets, and the half-share alternative falls with the enforceable debt. The Shach, above at the end of siman 104, accepts Maharshal and Rama against She'erit Yosef. Avodat HaGershuni 75 considers a related case in which Yaakov and Leah each gave their daughter Dinah a half-male-share instrument, while Leah herself held such an instrument from her father Lavan. After Yaakov and Leah died and later Lavan died, Leah's sons sought their contractual half share in Lavan's estate, while Dinah claimed that her own instrument from Leah entitled her to share in that very right. The sons argued that this was an especially remote form of prospective property. Avodat HaGershuni rules for Dinah. He rejects the sons' first argument that they come directly from their grandfather, because here their right derives through their mother. He also rejects the claim that the general phrase 'prospective property' excludes a grandparent's estate. Maharshal and Rama already refused such a distinction, and the wording 'all property' in the instrument is itself expansive. Tzemach Tzedek 114 reached the same result in essentially the same case. He explains that the heirs' argument that they come directly from a grandparent can apply to a paternal grandfather, where grandchildren may stand as children, but not in the same way to a maternal grandfather where the sons' right is through their mother. He further notes several contractual formulations that expressly broaden the term 'prospective,' including the word 'all' and provisions that doubtful language is construed for the daughter's benefit. Tzemach Tzedek also addresses Maharam Lublin's limitation where the deceased parent left insufficient assets to satisfy the fixed debt. In the maternal-grandfather case, that limitation does not necessarily defeat the daughter because the sons themselves are asserting a contractual half-share right inherited through their mother, and the daughter's instrument can attach to that right. The responsa discuss additional cases involving daughters' maintenance and dowry rights, the timing of division, and whether property later reverting to the brothers was ever acquired by daughters who died before marriage. Where a daughter died before marriage, later authorities reason that her statutory dowry share may never have vested, so the property remains in the father's estate and may fall within the half-share instrument. The literature also considers a father who left only daughters and one daughter with a half-male-share instrument. Tzemach Tzedek holds that the instrument does not give her priority because the instrument was intended to protect a daughter from male heirs, and where there are no sons she inherits as a daughter together with her sisters. Avodat HaGershuni questions this where her ordinary inheritance is less than the contractual benefit, but Shev Yaakov defends Tzemach Tzedek on the ground of clear presumed intent: such instruments are written because sons would otherwise exclude daughters. If there were no sons at the father's death, the instrument was never intended to operate. If sons survived the father and only later died before division, Shev Yaakov discusses whether the daughter first acquired her contractual half share at the father's death and then shares equally in what later comes from the deceased brothers; he leaves aspects of that case requiring study. Tzemach Tzedek further distinguishes the case where a daughter died in her father's lifetime leaving descendants. The father of those descendants can inherit a contractual right through them only where the daughter herself would have taken by virtue of the half-share note because sons or their descendants survived. Where she would instead have inherited directly as a daughter, there is no contractual right for her husband to inherit through the child in the grave. See the cited responsa for the many detailed applications.
