And he made the firstborn equal to them. Avodat HaGershuni, responsum 61, discusses a will directing lawful division of cash and movables, allowing the wife to remain in the house, and providing that the house should never be sold but should remain associated with the sons and their descendants, 'all equal for the good,' and that they should possess the house forever. A son of the deceased firstborn claimed a double right of use in the house because he stands in his father's place. The other heirs argued that the testator had equalized the firstborn with the ordinary sons regarding use of the house. Avodat HaGershuni writes that the phrase 'all equal for the good' by itself is not decisive, because in context it may refer only to the naming and continuation of the house. Nevertheless, he rules for the other heirs. The additional phrase that they 'shall possess the house forever' is superfluous if the testator meant only that it should not be sold, and the word 'possess' is gift terminology. If the testator intended the firstborn to have a double share of use, there would have been no reason to employ gift language. Rather, he intended to equalize the firstborn with the ordinary sons in use of the house. He cites Rashdam, Choshen Mishpat 375, for a comparable approach. He adds that even if the clause were inheritance language, the Ramban's Torah commentary would support its effectiveness where the firstborn son had already died during the father's lifetime. Accordingly, the son of the firstborn does not receive a double right in that house.
