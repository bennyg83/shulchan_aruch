To one person, etc.; and some say only, etc. See Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 145, concerning a will that divided the estate equally among the firstborn, ordinary sons, a daughter, and the wife. In the paragraph dealing with the house it said, 'I leave the above house in five shares,' followed by the beneficiaries. The questioner feared that 'I leave' is inheritance terminology, based on a statement cited by the Shach in siman 281. Chatam Sofer notes, however, that the Shach himself refers to siman 253, where 'I leave' is treated as gift language based on a responsum of the Rosh, and a review of Divrei Rivot does not establish that the expression is inheritance language. Even if it were, the will would still be effective because another paragraph dealing with cash and movables begins with 'there shall be given,' and then directs that the remainder 'shall be divided' into shares for the same beneficiaries. The initial gift expression can govern the subsequent clauses, and 'shall be divided' itself is gift terminology. Since the same persons receive a share in the house and a share in the movables, this is analogous to two fields given to one person, where valid gift language in one disposition validates inheritance language in the other. There is room to question this under the Nimukei Yosef's view cited by Rama that the clauses must be linked with the conjunctive 'and,' especially since another paragraph intervenes. But that view is cited only as an alternative opinion, whereas the Rosh, Tur, Shulchan Aruch, Sema, Shach, and Rivash support treating 'I leave' as gift language. Chatam Sofer therefore upholds the will. In responsum 112 he states more strongly that the Shach's wording calling 'I leave' inheritance language is a scribal error and should read that it is the language of a deathbed gift, as is evident from Divrei Rivot itself.
