If a will directs that a particular daughter receive a specified sum smaller than what she is entitled to under her shtar chatzi zachar, and she did not consent, the will does not by itself reduce the shtar chatzi zachar. It is true that during his lifetime the father could have arranged his estate in a manner that left less inheritance, for example by valid gifts to his sons, but the fact that he had such power does not create a migo or equivalent argument once the ordinary inheritance remains. If under the actual estate the shtar chatzi zachar produces a larger debt, the will alone does not erase it. It may, however, be argued that the amount she receives under the will should be credited against the shtar chatzi zachar. Perhaps the father forgot that she already held such an instrument and therefore gave her the stated sum as what he intended her to receive from the estate. Alternatively, he may have believed that his direction would itself defeat the instrument, either because he thought he had legal power to do so or because he assumed she would agree. If the legal meaning remains doubtful, the ordinary rule of possession may govern, so that if she is in possession she may perhaps retain both amounts. If the father ordered that his wife manage all of his affairs until a specified time and only then should the estate be divided, it is possible that the shtar chatzi zachar does not become payable until the time of division, depending on its wording that payment is due when the heirs "agree to descend to the inheritance." Even if they would personally wish to divide immediately but are prevented only by the will, the phrase may refer to the actual time of division. Since the holder of an instrument bears the burden of unfavorable ambiguity, the clause can be read to mean that so long as division is delayed, even by the heirs, the shtar chatzi zachar is not yet collectible. On the other hand, if a deathbed gift ordinarily cannot reduce the shtar chatzi zachar, perhaps a mere direction delaying division should not delay a debt said to arise an hour before death. Still, the phrase "half the share of an ordinary son" may indicate that the parties tied payment to the actual distribution whenever it occurs. This requires further study. Where there is doubt regarding the conditions written into a shtar chatzi zachar, one might initially say that the obligor is in the posture of "I do not know whether I paid," because the instrument creates a large admitted debt and the conditions operate like a release or repayment. Yet the Taz's reasoning concerning a counterfeit coin suggests that the decisive factor is the legal status established between the parties at the relevant stage. In a sale, even if money was first received and goods of doubtful validity were later delivered, the seller is not always treated as one who says "I do not know whether I paid." Likewise, ambiguities in the conditions of a shtar chatzi zachar may normally be construed against the holder of the instrument, unless possession shifts the burden. Where there are two separate instruments not created in one legal transaction, the analysis may differ. Perhaps this is one reason the customary kinyan sudar recites that "afterward the following was stipulated," placing obligation and condition in a single structured transaction so that the original owner's possession is not weakened by later uncertainty.
