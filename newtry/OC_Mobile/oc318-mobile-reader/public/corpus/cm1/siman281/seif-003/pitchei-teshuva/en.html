His words are valid. Netivot HaMishpat raises the question whether, when a father grants an ordinary son a double portion, the son also acquires property that comes to the father only afterward. He concludes that the rule cannot operate on property not yet in existence. Accordingly, he writes that the same applies to an oral loan, which is considered outside the owner's possession, and even to a loan evidenced by a document according to the view that assignment of notes is only rabbinic. Since the father cannot transfer such property by ordinary Torah conveyance, he cannot bestow it by the special law of inheritance either. He cites Bava Batra 151, where the mother of Rav Amram Chasida had a bundle of notes and said they should go to her son Amram, and the Gemara explains his acquisition through the law of a deathbed gift rather than through inheritance. Thus, when a dying person says, 'My son shall inherit a double portion,' property that can be transferred by Torah law is acquired through inheritance, while property that cannot be so transferred is acquired only, if at all, under the rabbinic institution of a deathbed gift, because inheritance terminology does not impair a deathbed gift to one who is an heir. This distinction can matter, for example, where there is an 'after you, to another' clause. The basic proposition that one cannot bestow by inheritance property not yet in existence is clear. Rabbi Akiva Eiger likewise cites Bnei Aharon in the name of Maharit that one cannot give by inheritance property that will later fall to him. Although Rabbi Akiva Eiger questions the proof offered from Bava Batra 127b, he notes that the ruling is explicit in Rashba's novellae to Kiddushin, that one cannot bestow by inheritance property not presently held. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 150, also takes this as obvious and compares a fetus, who cannot yet inherit in this manner, to future property. Chatam Sofer challenges Netivot's proof from Bava Batra 133, but offers a different proof. As to Netivot's second proposition, that a loan likewise cannot be bestowed by inheritance because it is not transferable by Torah law, Chatam Sofer records that his father-in-law Rabbi Akiva Eiger entertained the same view and sought support from Tosafot, Bava Batra 141b. He notes that Avodat HaGershuni, end of responsum 11, had ruled without proof that even a gentile's oral debt may be bestowed by inheritance. Chatam Sofer inclines toward Avodat HaGershuni, reasoning that the special law of bestowing inheritance is not identical with an inter vivos conveyance. The inability to bestow future property derives, in his view, from the fact that such property is not subject to ordinary automatic inheritance at that time, rather than from the technical limitations of conveyance. He rejects both the proof from Tosafot and Netivot's proof from the bundle of notes. The matter therefore requires further study.
