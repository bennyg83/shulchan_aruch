Therefore, one who divides his property, etc. See Responsa Panim Meirot, II, 133, concerning a will which, after distributing houses and vaults, stated in Yiddish that the firstborn son should demand no more than the share received by the other male children. The question was whether this language merely declares that the firstborn shall inherit like an ordinary son, which would be ineffective against the Torah law of the firstborn. Although the will later stated that everything written in it was given to each beneficiary as a complete gift, Panim Meirot reasons that this closing gift language may apply only to the affirmative distributions, not to the clause saying that the firstborn should not claim an additional share. If part of an estate is given by gift and the testator says only that the firstborn shall not receive a double portion in the balance, the gift language used elsewhere does not validate the attempted deprivation. Nor can one necessarily say that because the land was given as a gift, gift terminology automatically carries over to the movables. The rule that gift language used for one beneficiary can validate inheritance language used for another applies where the testator actually used inheritance terminology. Here he said merely that the children would 'divide,' and in context that may mean that they would divide automatically according to Torah law, not that he was directing a gift. Therefore the attempted deprivation of the firstborn is ineffective, and the firstborn receives a double portion in the remaining estate. Panim Meirot concludes that this is clear and without doubt.
