If a creditor collected his debt from houses belonging to Shimon pursuant to an order of the municipal authority after a public announcement was made for one day, and afterwards an earlier creditor came to dispossess him, while the later creditor argues, "Because you did not protest when the announcement was made, you lost your rights," then if the proclamation expressly said, "Anyone who has a right or lien in this land must come and object, and anyone who does not object will lose his rights," and the established local practice gives such a proclamation this force, then dina d'malchusa applies and the earlier creditor's rights are extinguished. Rama: The same applies if creditors were disputing and the authorities ordered the debtor's property to be mortgaged or attached, and we do not know which creditor came first, while the official testifies in favor of one of them. If local custom recognizes the official's testimony, he is believed by virtue of dina d'malchusa. Likewise, if one creditor already collected and the other claims that he collected unlawfully because no debt was actually owed to him, where the collection was carried out pursuant to dina d'malchusa, what was collected remains collected. But if the proclamation merely said generally, "Anyone who has a right should come and object," without stating that failure to object would forfeit rights, the creditor's rights are not extinguished merely because he remained silent. Rama: Even if it was proclaimed that anyone who failed to appear would lose his rights, if one person claims that he did not hear the proclamation, that claim is effective. We do not apply the presumption that one friend has another friend who would have informed him.
