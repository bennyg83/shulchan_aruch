He is believed by force of dina d'malchuta. The Sema, seif katan 5, understands this even where the collateral was taken from a Jew, because the rule of the government gives credibility to the official. I am astonished. Even if we assume that the officer is believed by virtue of royal law, as explained above in siman 68 where a royal rule can give credibility to an official scribe, that is only where the official is not known to accept bribes. But what legal right does the first creditor acquire under Torah law merely because he was first to place an attachment upon the property? Even if the officer physically took possession of the collateral, the Jewish creditor has not thereby acquired it. The officer cannot seize for one creditor where doing so harms others, and a non Jew has no agency to acquire on behalf of a Jew. If one says that the king's law itself creates the acquisition, then Torah monetary law would be abolished, because all foreign civil law is ultimately enacted under royal authority. Ramban and the other authorities explain at length that dina d'malchuta is not a license to replace Torah civil law wholesale. It applies to matters connected to the sovereign's authority, such as taxes, currency, and comparable royal interests, not to private legal rules that contradict the law given by the King of the world to Israel. The correct reading is therefore like the Ir Shushan: the case concerned collateral taken from a non Jew, and the borrower himself was a non Jew. In that setting the Jew who lent him money did so with knowledge of the governing royal law and accepted the local legal structure of the transaction. The applicable commercial rule therefore follows that agreed framework. Between Jew and Jew, however, the law is plainly Torah law, and the priority of a foreign attachment or an officer's seizure has no independent force against it.
Who collected contrary to law, etc. The same difficulty applies here. Above, siman 97, seif 24, concerning a woman who collected her ketubah through a non Jewish court, the ruling is that a Jewish creditor may still claim against her because under Torah law her collection was improper. It is forced to distinguish that case from the present one if both concern Jews. Therefore the explanation given in the preceding seif katan is clearly correct: the present responsum concerns a debt against a non Jew. The parties entered the transaction subject to the legal customs governing that non Jewish borrower, so collection is judged through that framework. But where one Jew collected from another through a foreign court, if the result is contrary to Torah law, beit din removes the property from him. See Responsa Beit Avraham, siman 26.
