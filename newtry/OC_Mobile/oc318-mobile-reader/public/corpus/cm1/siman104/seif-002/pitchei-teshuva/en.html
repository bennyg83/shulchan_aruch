And if they announced it. See Sema and Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 64. A man wished to borrow from another, who refused despite the borrower's ownership of a house because he feared there might be earlier debt instruments against it. The borrower agreed to have his house publicly announced to show that he owed nothing. In truth he owed a large sum to Levi, his cousin. Levi, wishing not to harm his relative, did not object during the announcement, but later sought to collect from the house under a debt instrument predating the loan, explaining that he had not objected because he knew the house would not then be sold. Chavot Yair writes that under strict law Levi is correct, because the custom is to announce houses only at the time of sale. Yet if the local formula regularly calls upon anyone with a lien or objection to come forward and concludes that the announcement is pursuant to communal regulation, it is possible that this case is included. One must investigate the local enactments and customs; if doubt remains, the enactment is construed narrowly and the holder of the instrument retains the stronger claim. Chavot Yair then discusses another case. A seller sold his house but stipulated, for his own reasons, that it not be publicly announced as usual. The purchaser suspected that the seller might owe money to two residents, Reuven and Shimon, and asked them. Reuven denied any debt. Shimon showed the purchaser a handwritten acknowledgment for one hundred Reichsthaler. The purchaser bought the house, relying on Reuven's denial and not fearing Shimon because Shimon held only a handwritten note without a formal document and kinyan. Both later sought to foreclose: Reuven on the basis of a formal debt instrument, and Shimon arguing that although his claim rested only on handwriting, he had shown it to the purchaser, who therefore should not have bought the house, especially since liens are Torah law and the reason an oral loan does not collect from purchasers is protection of purchasers who had no knowledge, which does not apply here. Still later, holders of debt instruments dated after the sale also sought foreclosure, arguing that had the purchaser announced the house as customary they would have refrained from lending to the seller. Chavot Yair rules that Reuven may foreclose under his debt instrument and did not lose his right by initially denying it. Although Reuven acted improperly, this remains so even if the purchaser had told him explicitly, 'See, I am relying upon you,' because the purchaser should have suspected that Reuven might deny the debt for his own benefit, hoping later to collect from the sale proceeds. Moreover, a person may deny wealth so as not to appear wealthy. The purchaser's foolish reliance does not destroy Reuven's documentary right, though Reuven is subject to a serious grievance and is liable before Heaven, as the Sema writes in siman 146. Shimon, however, cannot foreclose merely because he showed the purchaser his handwritten claim. Even if an oral creditor, or one holding a handwritten obligation containing a credibility clause and other strong language, appears during the house announcement, objects, and warns the purchaser, and the purchaser nevertheless buys, that creditor has no claim against the purchaser. Once the Sages ruled that an oral loan does not collect from purchasers, they made no distinction. Chavot Yair repeats this briefly elsewhere. Likewise, the holders of debt instruments written after the sale cannot foreclose on the theory that the purchaser should have announced the property. Announcements of houses and estates are instituted for the purchasers' benefit. If purchasers choose not to insist upon them, they have no duty to protect future lenders. See there.
