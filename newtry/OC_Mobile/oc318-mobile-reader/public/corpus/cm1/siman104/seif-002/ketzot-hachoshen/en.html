Likewise, if the creditors ordered a gentile official to seize or attach property. The cited responsum from Chazeh HaTenufah concerns Reuven and Shimon, each of whom held a claim against the same gentile debtor. Each sent the royal enforcement officer to seize property, and they disputed which creditor had caused the first seizure. The gentile officer's statement was accepted because he and the accompanying royal scribes were officially appointed, and the law of the kingdom governs the evidentiary procedure where no Jewish witnesses can establish the order. I do not understand this responsum if applied to a Jewish debtor. A gentile ordinarily has no halachic agency for a Jew. Even a Jewish agent who seized for one creditor at the expense of others may fail under the rule that one cannot seize for a creditor where doing so harms other creditors. Why should the officer's earlier seizure give priority? One cannot simply answer that the law of the kingdom itself creates priority based on the first enforcement act. The responsum appears to invoke the law of the kingdom chiefly regarding the official's credibility, just as secular records may be evidentially recognized. We do not generally use secular law to replace Torah law in disputes between Jews who are commanded to litigate under Torah law. Therefore the better limitation is that the debtor there was a gentile. Jewish creditors who voluntarily lend to a gentile do so within the commercial and legal framework governing that gentile's property. The royal enforcement procedure can therefore determine which creditor obtained priority. Where the debtor is Jewish, however, the mere fact that a gentile officer first attached property for one creditor does not override Torah rules of agency and seizure. Rama's wording includes both seizure and attachment. Attachment itself does not constitute halachic collection at all. This further shows that the rule depends on the governmental legal system of the gentile debtor, not on ordinary Torah seizure principles. Ir Shushan's limitation to a gentile debtor is therefore correct.
