Whether land or movables, etc. The Sema writes at length and states that he concluded in a responsum that movables are not subject to the law of priority at all, even initially, before one creditor has collected. We have not merited to see that responsum or the Derishah on this siman, but his conclusion is nevertheless correct, for most authorities indeed hold this way. The Baal HaTerumot, citing the Rif in Sha'arim, sha'ar 13, writes that movables are called "property without responsibility" precisely because a creditor has no enduring responsibility or lien upon them. Therefore there is no law of earlier and later priority with respect to them. The same implication appears in the Rif in Perek HaKotev. He writes that whether a promissory note and a ketubah bear the same date, whether both cases are analogous to two loans followed by later acquisition, or whether the property is movables in our time, since the liens of both creditors arise together neither has a right of priority over the other. Regarding a ketubah, under Talmudic law movables were not collected at all. The Ran likewise writes explicitly in Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui that with movables, even where an earlier creditor holds a promissory note and a later creditor has only an oral loan, if both come to collect they divide, because there is no law of priority in movables. The Ritva writes similarly in Ketubot, Perek HaKotev, concerning the rule "give it to the one whose proof is weakest." The Raah at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui also writes that with movables the creditors divide and the earlier and later creditors are equal. The Mordekhai in Perek HaKotev, siman 220, implies the same in the name of the Raavyah. Tosafot in Ketubot 86, s.v. la'ishah, likewise write that because the debtor can sell movables, the creditor's lien does not take effect upon them at all. They are therefore treated as though the claims became due at the same time in every respect. There is no difficulty from Rabbenu Tam at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui. The Gemara infers from the Mishnah's wording "the first precedes the second," rather than "if the second collected, we remove it from her," that a later creditor who collected first retains what he collected. Rabbenu Tam explains that the Mishnah concerns movables and follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that the movables of orphans are subject to creditors. One might ask: if so, why does the Mishnah say that the first creditor precedes the second? This appears to show that, at least initially, there is priority even with movables. The same question arises according to the Ri, who establishes the Mishnah as involving movables held by third parties, following Rabbi Tarfon, who holds that movables held by others may be collected by creditors even from orphans. Why then does the Mishnah say that the first precedes the second? The questions of the Maharsha and Maharit do not create a problem. They ask that according to Rabbi Tarfon, property is given to the claimant whose proof is weakest. But Rabbi Tarfon said this where there are creditors with other encumbered property available, so that the earlier creditor can collect from encumbered property while the later creditor cannot. The movables are therefore given to the later creditor, because the earlier creditor suffers no loss. Where there is no other place from which the earlier creditor can collect, it would be unreasonable to give the movables to the later creditor and cause the earlier creditor to lose precisely because his legal position is stronger. The Raah and Ritva in Perek HaKotev explicitly write this. The Mishnah in Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui can therefore refer to a case where the earlier creditor has no other source of collection, and he accordingly receives the property first. Nevertheless, this does not prove that ordinary movables are generally subject to priority. The entire reason the movables of orphans are not subject to creditors is that no asset lien attaches to them. The creditor does not rely upon them at all, and they are excluded from the lien. For the same reason, even during the debtor's lifetime there is no earlier or later priority among creditors with respect to ordinary movables, because no lien ever attached to them. This is precisely the Rif's and Tosafot's reasoning. But according to an opinion that the movables of orphans are subject to creditors, a lien must necessarily have attached to the movables. Otherwise, what basis would there be to diminish the orphans' rights? Once a lien truly attaches, priority naturally follows, and initially the earlier creditor receives the movables. Thus Rabbenu Tam, who establishes the Mishnah according to the opinion that movables of orphans are subject to creditors, presents no difficulty. There is a parenthetical difficulty from Tosafot in Kiddushin 29 and similarly in Bekhorot regarding the case where a person's son requires redemption. The Gemara says that the kohen is satisfied from unencumbered property for the son's redemption while the debtor's own redemption can be collected from encumbered property. Tosafot ask that the purchasers should tell the kohen to collect from the unencumbered property that another kohen already collected, or the purchasers should return against that property. One might ask: those unencumbered assets are movables, and if so, once collected they should not be removed at all. The Ritva indeed writes that the case involves movables. One must say that the question follows Rabbenu Tam, who holds in the conclusion that with movables what a later creditor collected does not remain with him, because his position is weaker than that of a purchaser. On that view Tosafot's question is sound. This might imply that Rabbenu Tam says so even not according to Rabbi Meir. One can reject this, however, by saying that Tosafot follow authorities who understand that Rabbi Meir generally holds movables subject to creditors. See the Ran and Ritva. If so, it remains difficult how the first Tanna's reason is proved to be that the obligation is not like a written loan. Perhaps his reason is simply that he follows Rabbi Meir, under whom prior collection by the wrong creditor can be reversed. This requires study. Even according to the Ri, who establishes the Mishnah according to Rabbi Tarfon, the matter is clear. Rabbi Tarfon must hold that those movables are subject to the creditor's lien. Otherwise, why should it matter whether they are in the hands of third parties or in the public domain? Why should the orphans' rights be any weaker merely because another person holds the property? It follows that the movables are legally encumbered, but when they are in the orphans' own hands it is difficult to extract them. Once the movables are in the debtor's hands and creditors come to collect, the earlier creditor therefore receives them, because his lien has attached. According to the law we follow, however, the movables of orphans are not subject to creditors even when held by third parties. This proves that no lien attaches to ordinary movables in any form. There is therefore no law of priority in them even initially at the time of collection. The responsum attributed to the Ramban, siman 61, cited by the Beit Yosef, states that initially beit din gives movables to the earlier creditor. The Sema writes that the Ramban did not mean this as a legal ruling but merely as theoretical reasoning. This is strained, because the responsum's wording does not suggest such a limitation. The responsum itself must be examined carefully. It states: "Even with movables there is priority so long as they remain in the debtor's hands. They said there is no priority only as against purchasers, or where a later creditor has already collected them. The reason is that movables have no publicity, as we say concerning one who made his ox an apotiki: why can the creditor not collect it after sale? Because this one has publicity and that one has none." If the responsum were discussing ordinary movables, the rationale based on lack of publicity would be puzzling. With heirs, publicity is irrelevant, yet we rule under the original law that the movables of orphans are not subject to creditors at all. The reason is not lack of publicity but that the creditor never relied upon the movables and no lien attached to them. The Rashba makes this distinction explicitly at the end of Kiddushin in the Kuntres Acharon, discussing the rule in the first chapter of Bava Kamma that if one makes his ox an apotiki and sells it, the creditor does not collect because the arrangement lacks publicity. The Rashba writes that if lack of publicity were the only issue, then where a creditor expressly specified movables as security, he should collect them from heirs, since publicity is unnecessary against heirs. Ordinary movables are nevertheless not collected from heirs because the creditor did not rely upon them. The Rashba says the same in responsum siman 914, cited by the Beit Yosef. Thus ordinary movables are not excluded merely because of lack of publicity. How then could the responsum attributed to the Ramban prove from the apotiki case, where the movables were expressly specified? The answer is clear to anyone who examines the responsum itself. It first writes that where the debtor did not write a clause encumbering movables together with land and then sold the movables, the creditor cannot collect from purchasers even if he made those movables an apotiki. This is the case of expressly designated movables. It then says: "I see no doubt in this law. But I hold that if both creditors come to collect, each from the debtor's movables, the earlier one receives them," etc. We rule that liens are of Torah origin, and even with movables there is priority so long as they remain in the debtor's hands and he has not sold or given them to a later creditor, because movables lack publicity. It is therefore obvious that the later statement refers back to the earlier case of movables expressly designated as an apotiki. Those movables were specifically included in the lien. The responsum rules that if they are sold the creditor cannot collect because the lien lacks publicity, but if they remain in the debtor's hands there is priority. Since the movables were expressly designated, one cannot say the creditor failed to rely upon them. The lien truly attached; only sale to outsiders defeats it for lack of publicity. Thus the proof from the ox made an apotiki is exact, because it is the very case under discussion. The later authorities did not examine the full responsum and relied only upon the Beit Yosef's shortened citation, and therefore they did not reach its true meaning. But there is no doubt that all of the Ramban's words concern expressly designated movables. There is therefore no dispute at all. All authorities agree on the law, and peace is restored among the judges of Israel. We can now resolve the Sema's difficulty in the Mechaber's wording. At the beginning the Mechaber says that the earlier creditor has priority even in movables. Necessarily this refers to movables expressly included in the document, because the Mechaber himself rules in seif 10 that ordinary movables have no law of priority. How then can he conclude that if the later creditor first collected land, the property is removed from him, apparently implying that only with land do we remove it, not with movables? The Sema was forced into a very strained answer. The matter is straightforward. The Mechaber speaks of a promissory note that expressly includes movables and land, though not with a clause encumbering the movables together with land. He writes without elaboration because this was the customary formula of promissory notes. The Rambam in chapter 16 of Hilkhot Ishut, law 8, writes that in all places people had become accustomed to write liens upon both movables and land, and that this was a very beneficial enactment. Accordingly, initially beit din gives the movables to the earlier creditor, because these movables are encumbered and can even be collected from heirs. But if the later creditor already collected them, we do not remove them from him, because the earlier creditor's lien on movables lacks publicity. This is exactly the law of the responsum attributed to the Ramban. Gidulei Terumah, page 195, likewise writes that in such a case, if the later creditor collected first, the property is not removed from him. This approach could also resolve all of Tosafot's difficulties at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui. Tosafot ask how the Gemara could initially assume that a later creditor who collected first retains what he collected, since an earlier creditor can even remove property from purchasers. Tosafot answer by discussing movables and are forced to establish the matter according to Rabbi Tarfon. Instead, one could say that the Mishnah concerns a document that expressly encumbered movables and land without an agav clause. Such movables are subject to creditors from heirs under the strict law but not from purchasers, and if a later creditor collected them first the earlier creditor does not remove them because of lack of publicity. All of Tosafot's questions would then disappear. Still, this is somewhat strained, because the Mishnah speaks without qualification, and in the period of the Mishnah it was not customary to write such clauses in ketubot. The Rambam indicates that this custom arose only from the era of the Geonim onward.
