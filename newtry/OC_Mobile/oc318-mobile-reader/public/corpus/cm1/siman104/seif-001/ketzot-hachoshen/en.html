The earlier creditor has priority. Sema infers from the Shulchan Aruch that there is no priority among creditors in movables, even against the debtor himself, contrary to a responsum attributed to Ramban. Bach likewise says the accepted practical law follows Rif and Rosh that movables have no priority. Tosafot, however, appears to recognize priority even in movables. In Arakhin the Gemara discusses one who undertakes two valuations while holding five sela. If he gives four to the later valuation and one to the earlier, he fulfills both, because what the later claim already collected remains collected. But if he gives four first to the earlier claim and only one to the later, the later valuation is not satisfied in full because the assets were already subject to the earlier claim. Tosafot establishes the case with movables. This seems to show an underlying priority in movables, even though a later creditor who already collected may retain what he collected. Another Tosafot answer establishes the case as an oral debt and likewise implies priority can exist even in oral obligations. Ran, by contrast, holds an oral loan has no priority. The Arakhin sugya is therefore difficult for his position. One can reconcile Sema by distinguishing private debts from consecrated obligations. On the Torah level, if liens are biblical, even movables can carry priority. The Sages later removed ordinary movable liens for private commerce. They need not have uprooted consecrated liens, and they may have lacked power to do so in the same manner. Thus Arakhin can preserve Torah priority in movables while ordinary private creditors have no such priority. The same may explain the unresolved question in Arakhin when a person undertook two valuations at once. If each claim simultaneously reaches the same assets, perhaps half must be assigned to each, or perhaps all five sela are potentially subject to either claim until actual allocation. The sugya's structure assumes priority concepts that are strongest in the consecrated setting.
But when he comes to collect movables. Beit Yosef cites a Responsa Maimoniyot ruling that where a debtor has many creditors and insufficient assets to pay all, beit din should not satisfy one movable creditor fully while the others are absent. If the creditor himself seized movables, the court may leave them with him, but the judges should not actively seize for one claimant at the expense of the others. Beit Yosef finds this astonishing. If one creditor is overseas, why should all others lose their present right to payment until he arrives? He concludes that if only one creditor presently demands payment, beit din can compel the debtor to pay him. Bach disagrees. Since movables have no priority, all creditors should share equally. Creditors who are present take their proportional shares, while the absent creditor's share should be held by beit din until he comes. Tzemach Tzedek likewise cites Maharam Recanati that there is no distinction between creditors who presently appear and those who do not. I think Beit Yosef's position can be defended. If there is no movable lien or priority, the debtor's obligation is fundamentally the personal mitzvah of repaying a creditor. A debt that has not been demanded is not presently enforceable in the same way. Even one who swore to pay generally does not violate the oath until the creditor demands payment, as explained in siman 73. Therefore the creditor who appears now has a presently matured mitzvah claim, whereas the absent creditor has not yet presented his demand. This resembles Menachot's question whether the frequent offering or the more sanctified offering takes precedence. One interpretation calls the offering whose time has already arrived "more sanctified" because its mitzvah is presently before us. A future mitzvah does not displace a present mitzvah merely because it may later be weightier. Accordingly, if the absent creditor had already made a demand and the debtor's duty toward him had already matured, Beit Yosef could agree that his share must be protected even though he is not physically present before the court. Beit Yosef speaks chiefly where no one but the present claimant has yet demanded payment. This also helps reconcile Maharam Recanati. His case may involve several creditors who had all already asserted their claims, even though only one stood presently before the court. Once the duties toward all have matured, their equal shares must be preserved. Radvaz uses a related principle regarding one imprisoned and allowed to perform only one mitzvah outside prison in a year. He rules that one should perform the first mitzvah that becomes available rather than wait for a later one. Chacham Tzvi objects from the Menachot sugya, but under the interpretation that present timing itself gives a mitzvah special priority, the two approaches can be reconciled. Thus the present creditor's claim can properly be paid first when the others have not yet demanded their debts.
But an oral loan, if the later creditor collected first, what he collected remains collected. This is taken from Ran at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHaya Nasui. Yet Ran himself holds that oral loans have no priority at all, so his conclusion follows naturally from that premise. According to Tur and the Mechaber, who rule that even an earlier oral loan can have priority over a later debt, it is doubtful whether a later oral creditor who collects first should retain what he collected. The Gemara states generally that a later creditor who collects before an earlier one does not retain the collection. Movables are more receptive to seizure and can differ because physical possession creates a stronger right. Land, however, is not ordinarily acquired by mere seizure. Therefore where oral loans nevertheless have priority, an earlier oral creditor may be able to undo the later creditor's land collection. Rif, Rambam, and Maggid Mishneh also suggest this. In Arakhin they explain that a later consecrated claim retains what it collected because consecrated property is special. Since valuation debts are treated as oral obligations, their reliance on the special status of consecration implies that in an ordinary private oral debt a later creditor would not necessarily retain collection. Tosafot has one answer allowing a later oral creditor to retain collection, but the dominant direction of Rif and Rambam is otherwise. Accordingly, because there is substantial uncertainty and seizure of land does not itself create an independent presumption, one should not confidently allow a later oral creditor to retain land against an earlier oral creditor.
And he left inferior land. Rashba explains that where the later creditor collected land and the debtor still left other land available for the earlier creditor, the later creditor can sometimes retain what he collected. This does not prove that a later creditor has purchaser status. Even an heir who receives one parcel can sometimes say that another available parcel should be used first. The rule follows the general principle that where sufficient free property remains, one does not disturb property already taken.
