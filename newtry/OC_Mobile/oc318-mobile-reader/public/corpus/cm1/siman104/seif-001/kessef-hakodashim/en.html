Seif 5. (Movable property together with land.) Under certain regional enactments, the seller whose merchandise remains unpaid may have priority in that very merchandise over other creditors. Tzemach Tzedek explains that this priority rests on enactment rather than on the ordinary halachic law, and applies specifically to the merchandise itself. One might therefore have thought that a wife with a ketubah should take priority over the unpaid seller, because a ketubah can contain a lien on movable property together with land, and among creditors a lien of movable property together with land can create priority, as explained in simanim 60 and 104. However, the published regional enactments expressly annulled the priority created by a lien on movable property together with land among competing creditors. Combining the two enactments, the unpaid seller therefore retains priority even against the ketubah. This is also consistent with the broader principle that an ordinary creditor is favored over a ketubah creditor in order not to close the door to lending. Although many other strict provisions of those enactments are no longer practiced, that may be because governmental arrangements changed and later authorities refrained from enforcing every historic provision with the same force. The specific rules giving priority to the unpaid seller in his merchandise and eliminating priority from a movable property together with land clause may nevertheless remain in force. I later heard, however, that my revered father in law did not generally apply those enactments because many of them had fallen out of accepted practice. A particularly strong case for applying the unpaid seller protection exists when an administrator sold orphan property on credit. If the purchase price is not fully paid, it may become clear retroactively that the sale was not for the orphans' benefit, in which event the administrator's sale can be void retroactively. The same may be true even where beit din approved the sale, if the orphans could have received the same amount immediately from another buyer for cash. Similar reasoning can apply to land. Although land is generally not subject to ordinary price fraud rules, a judicial agent may not initially disadvantage the orphans even slightly unless he was expressly empowered to do so. A buyer who wishes to purchase from minors without any possibility of later cancellation can protect himself by stipulating expressly before beit din that the sale shall remain valid regardless of subsequent events. The burden would be on him to prove such an explicit condition. If no such condition is established and later events reveal that the transaction was not for the orphans' benefit, the sale may be void. Perhaps depreciation of currency occurring only later at the time of payment is different, particularly where beit din could not reasonably have anticipated it. Yet where the buyer simply fails to comply with beit din's conditions, even because of an unusual accident such as death followed by the attachment of a ketubah lien, the special force of a judicial act may not prevail over the orphans' demonstrated loss. If a person took the orphans' movable property away and sold it, and it is claimed that this was done with the administrator's consent, he cannot necessarily rely merely on the presumption that property in a person's possession is his. This requires further study, especially where the administrator and the intermediary both admit that the sale was only on behalf of the orphans. Just as an unpaid seller can have priority in the merchandise he supplied and in proceeds directly derived from it, a landlord who rented a house on credit can have priority in rent generated by that house over other creditors. If the first tenant sublets the house, the original landlord ordinarily cannot collect directly from the subtenant merely because of the sublease, but if rent remains due from the first tenant, the rental value can still be relevant to the original landlord's claim. If part of the payment also covered firewood or other items unrelated to the premises, the original landlord has no special priority in that portion.
