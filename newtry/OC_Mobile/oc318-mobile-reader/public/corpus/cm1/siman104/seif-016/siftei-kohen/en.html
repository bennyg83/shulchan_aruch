For a creditor does not take prospective property, etc. The Tur gives this reason, but the ruling can be explained differently. In the Mishna in the chapter Mi Shemet, the heir may say, “I come directly from my grandfather,” so the property never passed through the debtor's estate. The practical difference is a case where that argument cannot be made, such as property descending after death from the debtor's maternal grandfather. There, collection depends upon the unresolved question whether a creditor may take prospective property as he takes property already possessed. Hagahot Ashri permits collection if that broader rule is accepted. Maharshal, the Rama, She'erit Yosef, Baal HaTerumot, Ramban, Rabbenu Ephraim, and Rabbenu Chananel discuss the issue without a uniform conclusion. Were I not hesitant, I would rule that a creditor does collect prospective property. The Gemara classifies increase as prospective property, yet we rule that a creditor collects increase. The distinctions suggested between ordinary prospective property and increase are weak, and the scriptural exclusions found regarding firstborn and levirate inheritance imply that without a specific exclusion a creditor may collect. The Rama's phrase here therefore need not express his own final ruling. It explains the Mechaber according to the Mechaber's premise. Since the Mechaber describes the sons as claiming that their father first inherited in the grave and then transmitted the property to them, he does not rely on the direct-grandfather argument. Their success must therefore rest on the premise that a creditor does not collect prospective property.
The Sema's discussion here is inaccurate. The Mordechai's statement that wages are prospective property concerns a husband's rights and a wife's ketubah, not an ordinary creditor. The word “creditor” there is a scribal error for “husband,” as shown by the Krakow edition, the Agudah, and the Rama's ruling in Even HaEzer. The Sema also misstates Maharshal and the Rama concerning a half-male inheritance deed. Neither resolved the general question whether a creditor collects prospective property. They ruled that the son-in-law may receive prospective property under the express condition of the deed, because one may contract regarding property not yet possessed, and refusal to honor the condition triggers payment of the full stated debt. This assumes that the father-in-law left an estate sufficient to cover that debt. She'erit Yosef disagrees and applies the rule that the holder of a document bears the disadvantage of ambiguity, even where the deed mentions both prospective and possessed property. The primary ruling follows Maharshal and the Rama. Maharam of Lublin also agrees in practical law, though he mistakenly identified the Rosh cited by Baal HaTerumot as the Rosh of Toledo. Baal HaTerumot lived long before that Rosh and was referring to the Rosh of Lunel. Maharam adds that the son-in-law acquires a share in the prospective inheritance only if the wife's grandfather died before division of the father-in-law's estate, so that both estates are before the parties at the time of division. If they divided during the grandfather's lifetime, the later inheritance is excluded. He further requires some meaningful estate from the father-in-law. If nothing, or only an amount already tendered in satisfaction of the debt, remains from him, the son-in-law has no claim upon the grandfather's estate.
