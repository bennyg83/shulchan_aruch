For a creditor does not collect property merely expected to come to the debtor. See the Shach, who notes that although the Tur states this reason, it is not essential. Tosafot in the chapter Mi SheMet and Hagahot Ashri there explain the rule through the heir's ability to say, "I come by inheritance from my grandfather," and not through his father. The practical difference is that where the heir cannot make that claim, the father's creditor may collect even property that came only after the father's death. Hagahot Ashri gives the example of property that came after death from the son's maternal grandfather, where the paternal brothers could inherit only through the deceased son, in which case the creditor may collect. Dagul MeRevavah observes that this language is difficult because a son does not inherit his mother in the grave in order to transmit her estate to his paternal brothers, as Bava Batra 114b states explicitly. He suggests that the case concerns a mother who was a convert and had no other heirs, so that the son is deemed to inherit in the grave in order to prevent the property from becoming ownerless, analogous to the rule that a female convert may inherit her son to prevent his estate from becoming ownerless. See also the discussion in Tosafot there, the Shach below siman 280, seif katan 3, and siman 423, seif 2. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat discuss the matter further. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 138, cites Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 48, part 3, that the doctrine "I come from my grandfather" applies specifically where one inherits his father's father. If one inherits the father's brother, however, he must pay the father's debt, because the innovation is limited to its exact case. Rabbi Akiva Eiger expresses surprise that Beit Yosef and the later authorities did not cite this ruling. Yet Beit Yosef may not be difficult, since elsewhere he appears to accept that a creditor does not collect expected property at all. The Baal HaTerumot's distinction would then have practical significance mainly for the residual mitzvah upon heirs to pay their father's debts, which may remain where the heir cannot say he comes directly from the prior ancestor. Rabbi Akiva Eiger was also asked how a divorced father might prevent the eventual inheritance of property from his children, who live with their mother, from passing into his family. He suggests at first that the father could obligate himself to a designated member of the mother's family in a very large sum, payable when he or his representative comes to inherit his children, similar to the form of a half-male inheritance note. Yet this does not solve every case. If the father dies during the children's lifetime and they later die, his brothers might inherit them and say that they inherit through the children, not through their deceased brother, and thus are not liable for his debt. Even according to Baal HaTerumot, who obligates one who inherits his paternal uncle to pay the father's debt, another scenario remains if the divorced father remarries, has additional children, dies, and then the children of the first marriage die. The children of the second wife could claim to inherit their siblings directly. There is also a dispute whether inheritance between brothers operates directly or by representation through their father. Rabbi Akiva Eiger concludes that the law is highly contestable and that the simpler position is that one who inherits his brother need not pay their father's debts, noting that no such practice had ever been heard. This last statement itself requires thought because we generally rule that a creditor does not collect expected property, or at least that the matter is doubtful. It may refer to the mitzvah to pay debts or to a half-male inheritance note which expressly includes expected as well as presently held property. Responsa Shav Yaakov, siman 18, indeed rules that if brothers inherit through representation they must pay their father's creditors according to the view that creditors collect expected property, or must satisfy a daughter holding a half-male inheritance note that expressly includes both expected and possessed assets. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 132, rejects a proof for representative inheritance drawn from Tosafot in Bava Batra 108b. See further below, siman 281, seif katan 2 and seif 7, seif katan 10.
