A creditor does not take property that is only expected. The Gemara at the end of Perek Mi SheMet also says the heir can say, "I come by force of my grandfather." The Shach suggests that a creditor should perhaps take expected property because the entire sugya of firstborn inheritance treats appreciation as expected, while a creditor can collect improvement. The distinction is clear. Appreciation that arose during the seller's lifetime is not expected property at all. It already entered the seller's estate. Appreciation arising only after the seller's death is indeed expected, and Nimukei Yosef rules that an ordinary creditor does not collect it. Rama follows this in siman 115. The rule that a grandson can say, "I come from my grandfather," requires a second layer of analysis. Ordinarily the son is the prior heir of the grandfather, and one could say the son's creditor steps into the son's inheritance before the grandson. The answer is that the creditor has no lien on property that never came to the son's estate. A lien created during life is no stronger than Rabbi Meir's power to transfer future property, and even Rabbi Meir does not allow transfer of property acquired only after death. One might still say that heirs have a mitzvah to pay their father's debts even from property not formally subject to a lien. That obligation applies where they inherit only through their debtor father. Here the grandson can trace his right directly to the grandfather under the verse, "Instead of your fathers shall be your sons." Since he can inherit without passing through his debtor father, he has no personal mitzvah to satisfy the father's creditors from that property. This explains why both reasons are necessary. First, the creditor has no lien because the property was expected and never entered the father's ownership. Second, the grandson's general duty to pay inherited debts does not apply because he can say, "I come directly from my grandfather." Accordingly, in the Mishnah where a house falls upon a father and son and it is uncertain who died first, a creditor cannot seize the grandfather's property merely by saying he follows an opinion that creditors collect expected property and another opinion that rejects direct grandson inheritance. The Mishnah itself rules against such collection. One cannot combine two minority positions to contradict an explicit Mishnah. Where a law is expressly established by the Mishnah, seizure based on two separate "I follow" arguments does not help.
