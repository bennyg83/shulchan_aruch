For a creditor does not collect property that was merely prospective. This ruling of the Shulchan Aruch is the Mishnah at the end of the chapter Mi SheMet in Bava Batra. The early authorities disagree about the reason. Some explain, as the Rama and Tur write, that a creditor does not collect from property that was merely prospective, while others explain that the sons can say, "we come in the place of our father's father." I will clarify the root of this dispute in detail. First, however, we must consider the practical difference between the two explanations. Hagahot HaRosh writes that a difference arises where property falls to the debtor after death from his maternal grandfather, so that his paternal brothers can inherit it only through him. A creditor can collect from that property only if a creditor collects prospective property as though it had already been possessed. Maharshal and Rama quote this in their responsa, and the Shach also cites it. The point requires study. If the brother died before his maternal grandfather, his paternal brothers do not inherit that estate through him at all, because a son does not inherit his maternal grandfather while in the grave in order to transmit that inheritance to his paternal brothers. If he was still alive when his maternal grandfather died, then the instant he acquired the property the creditor's lien attached, and the property was no longer merely prospective in any sense. Either way, the creditor should collect. I found no author addressing this difficulty until, after writing it, I found that Rabbi Eliyahu ben Chaim raised the same point in a responsum and likewise left it unresolved. It appears to me that the matter can be explained as follows. Mahari Weil, responsum 2, discusses Maharam's ruling in the chapter Na'arah that if a husband's father died during the husband's lifetime and the father's property was then subject to the ketubah lien of his wife, and only afterward the husband died and that lien was removed, the husband's widow does not collect her ketubah from the father in law's property because it was merely prospective property to her husband, since during his lifetime it was subject to the other woman's ketubah. Yet in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in Maharam ruled that if Reuven died leaving a daughter, and throughout the daughter's lifetime the property remained subject to the ketubah of Reuven's wife, then when the daughter died and that lien was removed, the daughter's husband inherits the property, although a husband does not inherit prospective property from his wife. This indicates that for inheritance the property is treated as already possessed. The two rulings appear contradictory. Panim Meirot, responsum 77, cites numerous responsa discussing this contradiction, and Maharashdam, Even HaEzer 101, likewise challenges Maharam from a responsum of the Rosh ruling that a firstborn takes a double portion even though, at the time of death, the property was subject to a woman's ketubah. Mahari Weil himself, however, answers in responsum 74 that there is a distinction between inheritance and collection of a debt. With respect to an heir, the property is treated as possessed rather than prospective, because many authorities hold that such property is not called prospective for inheritance. Collection of a debt, however, requires removing property from another's possession, and for that purpose we treat it as prospective. Inheritance comes automatically and is therefore treated as possession, whereas collection of a debt does not. See there, for this resolves the entire difficulty. Accordingly, one might think there is a practical difference in the following case. Yaakov dies while his property is subject to his wife's ketubah. His son Reuven later dies. After Reuven's death the ketubah lien on Yaakov's property is removed, and Reuven's creditor seeks to collect from Yaakov's estate. If the rule is that a creditor does not collect prospective property, he cannot collect, because vis a vis the creditor the property was prospective, as explained. But if the entire rule rests only on the heirs' claim, "we come in the place of our father's father," that argument would seemingly not apply, because for purposes of inheritance the property was considered possessed, so Reuven had already acquired it during his lifetime and his creditor should collect. Nevertheless, it appears that Hagahot HaRosh did not present this example because the rule that property is not considered prospective for an heir is a legal advantage given to the heir and based on his established status. That advantage should not be used to weaken the heirs. Reuven's sons can therefore say: during our father's life this property was in truth only prospective and our father never acquired a present right in it. Had he possessed even a partial present right, it would not be called prospective, for even fields that return in Yovel are not considered prospective and a firstborn takes a double portion in them. We therefore invoke the property's prospective character, while declining to use the special rule that treats it as possessed for purposes of our inheritance against ourselves. We say instead that we come in the place of our father's father, and the creditor has no claim to the property. The rule that prospective property may be treated as possessed for inheritance exists for the heirs' benefit, and they are not compelled to use it to their detriment. For that reason Hagahot HaRosh gives a different practical distinction. The maternal brother was alive when his maternal grandfather died, but at that moment the grandfather's property was subject to a woman's ketubah. When the maternal brother later died, that lien was removed. His paternal brothers then inherit the property through him. Although heirs generally do not inherit truly prospective property, with respect to inheritance this property is not deemed prospective, as explained. If the maternal brother's creditor then seeks to collect from it, the two rationales yield different results. If a creditor does not collect prospective property, the creditor has no right at all, because toward a creditor the property remained prospective even though toward inheritance it is treated as possessed. But if the reason is merely that descendants can say, "we come in the place of our father's father," that claim does not apply to these paternal brothers. They have no independent relationship to the maternal grandfather's estate and can inherit it only through the deceased brother. The distinction is therefore clear, and many practical differences follow from the principles stated above. Although the language of Hagahot HaRosh is abbreviated, this is unsurprising, since that work commonly condenses material from longer books and often shortens precisely where fuller explanation would have been useful. The foregoing, however, is correct as practical law.
However, as to the underlying question whether a creditor collects prospective property, the Rama in a responsum, Maharshal, and many other authorities regard the matter as an unresolved question of law because the early authorities disputed it. The Shach writes that he inclines to rule that a creditor does collect prospective property, and that the present case is governed instead by the heirs' claim, "we come in the place of our father's father," as other authorities explain. In my opinion there is no unresolved doubt. We rule that a creditor does not collect prospective property. The principal sugya is at the end of Mi SheMet in Bava Batra. A son sold property that he expected to inherit from his father while the father was still alive. The son died, and only afterward the father died. The son's son removes the property from the purchaser. The Gemara asks how this can be, for the purchaser should say, "your father sold it and you now remove it." The Gemara initially answers that the grandson can say, "I come in the place of my father's father," based on the verse, "in place of your fathers shall be your sons." The Gemara rejects this because that verse was said as a blessing. It then challenges from the Mishnah of a collapsed house, the very ruling recorded here in the Shulchan Aruch. If we do not recognize the argument, "I come in the place of my father's father," why should the creditor not take the property as the inheritance of his debtor? The Gemara answers that the Mishnah concerns other heirs of the father, and so forth. There is a major difficulty from the Mishnah at the end of Yesh Bekhor. It teaches that a firstborn and a woman collecting her ketubah do not take prospective property, and concludes, "all of them do not take prospective property as they take property already possessed." The Gemara asks what additional case the final phrase includes and answers that it includes property of the father's father. Rashi explains that one might have thought such property is not prospective because the descendants will certainly inherit it, so the Mishnah teaches otherwise. But according to the explanation just given, what does this have to do with prospective property? Even if the property were treated as possessed, the claimants still should not take it because the heirs can say, "we come in the place of our father's father." This is especially clear regarding a woman's ketubah, for she is no better than any other creditor. Yet the Mishnah says "all of them," indicating that the exclusion even of a ketubah is a novelty specifically because the property is prospective, as Ramban writes in his novellae. How can the Mishnah attribute the result to prospective property when, on this approach, the actual reason is the entirely different claim that the heirs come directly from the grandfather? The same difficulty exists for a firstborn. One would not need the law of prospective property at all. The younger heirs could simply say, "we did not inherit this from our father, but from our grandfather. Why do you claim firstborn rights in our grandfather's property?" Rama explicitly writes in a responsum that the argument "I come in the place of my father's father" applies even to deny a firstborn's double portion. The difficulty therefore arises from every side. It is strongest according to the simple reading of the Mishnah, which applies its conclusion to all the listed cases, including a woman's ketubah, as shown by the word "all." Rama noticed part of this difficulty in his responsum but understood the Mishnah as referring there only to the firstborn. That is not correct, for the phrase plainly includes the ketubah as well. There is a similar difficulty from the Yerushalmi cited by Ramban and the Rosh at the end of Yesh Bekhor. It expounds the verse concerning payment from "the best of his vineyard" as excluding prospective property, and explains the case as one where a person caused damage and died, and only afterward his father died. Again, even without the concept of prospective property, the heirs should be able to say that they come in the place of their father's father. Why is a verse needed to exclude prospective property? Indeed it is difficult to identify another ordinary case of prospective property in the law of damages. Debts may become subject through Rabbi Natan's rule, while the examples Ramban gives elsewhere, such as priestly gifts or ransom payments, do not readily fit. In an ordinary written debt one might speak of a specific lien, but a tort obligation arises directly from Torah law, and movables of heirs are not generally subject to collection. The Yerushalmi therefore itself identifies the relevant prospective property as the estate of the tortfeasor's father who died only after the tortfeasor. Yet why is that not disposed of simply by the heirs' direct claim from the grandfather? Nor can one say that the Yerushalmi's phrase "his vineyard, excluding prospective property" is merely borrowed language meaning the direct inheritance claim, because Ramban could not then prove from the verse that prospective property is not liened, and such a reading would contradict the Yerushalmi's own wording. These difficulties are resolved if we follow Rashbam and the Tur, who hold that a creditor does not collect prospective property. For an ordinary debt, the fact that the property was only prospective is sufficient by itself to prevent the lien from attaching, for even property acquired later is not automatically subject absent the relevant undertaking, all the more so property not yet acquired at all. A sale, however, gives the purchaser a stronger legal hand than an ordinary lien, as Rama writes that in several respects the force of a sale exceeds that of a lien. To invalidate a sale we therefore also need the claim, "I come in the place of my father's father." If prospective property could itself become subject to the sale, that claim alone would not suffice, because the purchaser could answer that the father had already sold his interest before the grandchildren came through the grandfather. But since prospective property does not become subject, and Rashbam writes that just as it cannot be liened it cannot be sold, the son's sale never took effect during the grandfather's life. It could take effect only upon the later inheritance. At that stage the grandson can say that he comes directly in the place of the grandfather. This additional reasoning is not needed for an ordinary debt. A debtor's property is liable because it stands as surety for him. When the debtor dies, that personal surety framework has ended, and the descendants can then say that they come directly through the grandfather. Thus, for sale and similar transactions two elements work together: the property's prospective character and the direct inheritance claim. Rama's responsum likewise indicates that according to Rashbam these are essentially two sides of one structure, and Maharshal, responsum 49, writes that the claim of coming through the grandfather is weak by itself and requires the additional principle that prospective property cannot be sold. This is clearly Rashbam's view when his words are studied carefully. For this reason, in siman 104, where the Tur deals with a debt, he does not rely on the claim of coming through the grandfather, because the creditor's lien predates the inheritance. He instead gives the reason that prospective property was never subjected to the lien. In siman 211, however, concerning a son who sold expected inheritance, the prospective character alone would not sufficiently defeat the stronger force of the sale, so the Tur adds the reason that the descendants come through their grandfather. The two together are effective. See also what I wrote in Even HaEzer siman 113 concerning a daughter's tenth share of the estate. There remains, however, a serious difficulty. How can the Gemara bring support for the rule that the grandson removes the property and voids the sale from the Mishnah of the collapsed house? In the Mishnah the issue is a creditor, and if the property is prospective it is obvious on this view that the creditor cannot take it. But to void the stronger force of a sale we also need the direct inheritance claim. The Gemara had just rejected that claim by saying that the verse was stated as a blessing. This is a substantial difficulty, and I am surprised that Rama and Maharshal did not address it, since it remains throughout their distinction between debt and sale. A similar question arises in Tosafot, who ask on their own approach that a creditor does not collect prospective property. Even according to Rashbam, if the sale is stronger than a debt, what proof can be drawn from the collapsed house, whose subject is merely a creditor? One might answer, albeit with difficulty, that according to Rashbam the superiority of sale over debt applies only according to the view that liens are not d'oraisa. According to the view that liens are d'oraisa, a debt has similar force to a sale, and Rashbam himself writes that loan and sale are alike regarding prospective property. The Gemara's challenge from the collapsed house would then rest on Beit Shammai, who say the parties divide because a document standing for collection is treated as though already collected. Such a position presupposes a strong lien. Indeed, Rabbah's view that a firstborn takes a double share once land has been collected, because it is then treated as possessed, is linked by the Gemara to the view that liens are d'oraisa. All the more so if the document is treated as already collected. It would then be difficult to distinguish debt from sale. But this answer is strained. It is preferable to say that the Gemara reasons in the opposite direction. If a son's sale of his father's property during the father's life is effective even when the son dies first, then the son already has a legally meaningful interest in the property during the father's life, for he can sell it and the sale stands. If so, the property cannot be described as entirely prospective even for a creditor, and the creditor should collect it as property in which the debtor already had a present legal hand. The Gemara's proof is therefore valid. It could also have challenged from the Mishnah in Bekhorot that a ketubah does not collect prospective property, but that passage admits various distinctions, so the Gemara chose the clearer case. It then answers that the sale may indeed be valid and the creditor may collect, while the collapsed house Mishnah concerns different heirs rather than a son. According to that proposed answer, the Mishnah in Bekhorot that "all of them do not take prospective property" could not be referring to an inheritance from the father's father in a case where the son could sell it, because if sale is possible the property is no longer prospective even for a lien. The Mishnah would instead concern other kinds of prospective property, such as the priestly gifts and similar items listed by Ramban. Rama too notes in his responsum that other forms of prospective property exist. But once we rule that this attempted answer is rejected, and we follow the message sent from Eretz Yisrael that the son cannot sell the expected inheritance effectively, the property is truly prospective, and a creditor does not collect it. The sugya in Bekhorot can then properly say that the phrase "all of them" includes an inheritance from the father's father. The claim of coming through the grandfather is not needed for a mere creditor, because that claim is not what defeats the lien. The controlling reason is that the property is prospective. As explained, sale requires both elements, whereas debt does not. This also clarifies the Tur's formulation that a son sold property of his father for purposes such as his father's burial. The Bach asks why the Tur did not establish the case like Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh, where the son sold a specifically identified field, a transaction that can sometimes take effect, as the Tur himself writes at the beginning of siman 211. The Perishah likewise asks how we know that where the sale was for the father's burial, which is a rabbinic allowance, the grandson can still remove the property. Perhaps the enactment itself was intended to protect the purchaser. It appears that Rabbenu Tam did not read the Gemara's general phrase "a son who sold his father's property" as limited to burial expenses, so he explained it as a sale of a specified field. The Tur, however, inferred that even where the son sells what he expects to inherit immediately, the grandson later removes it. Otherwise, the Gemara's challenge from the collapsed house would fail for the reason stated above. One cannot answer that because the son has the ability to sell a specified field, such property is never prospective. The Mishnah might concern a father with several sons, where no individual son can identify the specific field he will receive and thus has no present power to sell it. The Gemara's challenge would then disappear. We must therefore say that the Gemara assumes that if the grandson can undo even a sale of a specified field, the same is true of a sale of "what I shall inherit from my father today." There are circumstances in which a son can sell expected inheritance while the father is still alive, particularly near the father's death, and even where the estate includes many properties, as Rama writes in siman 211. That degree of present disposition means the property is not fully prospective, and the Gemara's challenge is sound. Rabbenu Tam's interpretation and the Tur's ruling therefore support one another. The Tur chose the burial case to teach the stronger point that even there the grandson can remove the purchaser, unlike Rashi's narrower understanding. But according to Tosafot and those who follow them, who hold that a creditor does collect prospective property while the grandson nevertheless removes property from the purchaser without even returning the purchase money because he says, "I come in the place of my father's father," and who use the same reason for the collapsed house case, the earlier difficulty returns with greater force. How can Bekhorot explain "all of them do not take prospective property" as including the grandfather's property? Even a creditor, who according to Tosafot ordinarily collects prospective property, cannot collect this property because of the separate direct inheritance claim. The exclusion is therefore unrelated to the law of prospective property. Moreover, according to Tosafot the Mishnah initially lists specifically a ketubah rather than every creditor, yet its conclusion says "all of them" in a way that would supposedly include a case in which even an ordinary creditor does not collect. This seems incoherent. It appears that the Rosh at the end of Yesh Bekhor had this very difficulty in mind when he challenged the view that a creditor collects prospective property from the explicit Mishnah of the collapsed house. Ma'adanei Yom Tov explains that the Rosh did not accept the Gemara's answer placing that Mishnah in a case of other heirs because we rule like the message sent from Eretz Yisrael concerning the grandson. One might ask what that proves, since Tosafot themselves give the distinct reason that the descendants come through their grandfather. The Rosh's question, however, is precisely ours. Since Bekhorot says that "all of them" excludes property of the father's father as prospective property, it follows that the direct inheritance claim is not the operative reason in the creditor context. The collapsed house Mishnah would therefore remain unexplained if a creditor generally collected prospective property. The Rosh's objection is thus strong. The same is true of the Yerushalmi's derivation from "his vineyard" discussed above. Accordingly, it is clear that the authorities who hold that a creditor does collect prospective property must follow Rabbenu Ephraim and Rabbi Shimshon of Lunel that the message sent from Eretz Yisrael concerning the grandson was not accepted as practical law. Rather, the law follows the view that the case remains difficult in monetary law and that the verse "in place of your fathers shall be your sons" is merely a blessing, not a source for the grandson's claim. On that basis all is coherent. The statement from Eretz Yisrael, which did accept the claim that the grandson comes directly through the grandfather and accordingly established the collapsed house Mishnah in a case of a son, would indeed not read the Mishnah in Bekhorot as referring to the grandfather's property. It would apply the phrase "all of them" to some other kind of prospective property. The anonymous Gemara, however, proceeds according to the practical ruling that we do not accept the direct inheritance claim and that the collapsed house Mishnah concerns other heirs, not a son. It can therefore properly explain "all of them" as including property of the grandfather, because under the practical law there is no independent defense of "we come in the place of our father's father." The only relevant issue is prospective property. This is clear.
Likewise, this appears to be Tosafot's position, and it can be learned from another place. Ri explains the case of the son who sold his father's property as one where the father had transferred the property to him "from today and after death." This is difficult, because we rule like Reish Lakish that the purchaser acquires, since ownership of the fruits is not equivalent to ownership of the underlying property. We must therefore say that Tosafot did not regard the message sent from Eretz Yisrael as the practical halakhah. Rather, the law remains that the case is difficult in monetary law, and the ruling follows Reish Lakish. This fits everything stated above. Since we do not rule like that message, Rabbi Shimshon of Lunel was correct in Baal HaTerumot, gate 44, part 4, when he wrote that a creditor collects prospective property and that this differs from the Rif's ruling. The Shach wonders why the Rif could not also hold that a creditor collects prospective property, while the case of the son who sold is governed by the independent reason that the descendants come in the place of the grandfather. According to what I have written, however, anyone who rules like the message sent from Eretz Yisrael must necessarily understand the direct inheritance argument as rooted in the property's prospective character. Otherwise the Mishnah in Bekhorot cannot be explained. The entire basis of the authorities who allow a creditor to collect prospective property is that they do not accept that message as practical law. This is clear. Accordingly, since the Rif, Rosh, Rambam, Tur, Rashba, Rashbam, and many other authorities cite the message sent from Eretz Yisrael as practical law, and the Mechaber likewise rules that way below in siman 211, it necessarily follows, without room for doubt, that a creditor does not collect prospective property. The Tur himself, although he understands the case of the son who sold as an actual sale and answers Tosafot's question about selling something not yet acquired by explaining that the sale was for the son's sustenance, where the Sages allowed the sale, nevertheless rules that the heirs need not return even the purchase money. The reason is that the purchaser's claim for the money is now merely an ordinary debt, as Tosafot state explicitly, and a creditor does not collect prospective property at all. The questions of Maharshal and Rama against the Tur are therefore answered. This is also indicated by Tosafot's own question. They ask that even if the purchaser is owed the money, this is at most an oral loan, so how can he collect from prospective property? See Maharsha there. One must ask why the claim is necessarily only oral. Perhaps the property was sold with responsibility and the document included after acquired property. The answer must be that this was a document that could not validly have been written as a binding conveyance, because the sale itself had no legal substance at the time and the writing was like a shard. The money claim therefore remains only an oral debt. Mishneh LaMelekh, Hilkhot Mekhirah 22, cites authorities who similarly hold that where one sells something not yet in existence through a document, the money becomes only an oral loan. Still, one might ask whether the sugya could follow Rabbi Meir, as Tosafot initially suggest. Even if Rabbi Meir agrees that where the son dies before the father the sale itself cannot take effect, the document was nevertheless capable of being written, because had the father died first the sale would have been effective according to Rabbi Meir. The purchaser's money claim could then be treated as a written debt, and a written creditor may collect prospective property according to Tosafot. The answer is that according to the initial approach, where the Gemara's issue was whether the sale itself remained valid, as Rashbam explains, the ruling that the grandson removes the purchaser was practical law, and it did not conflict with Bekhorot for the reasons given above. The Gemara could therefore be speaking even according to Rabbi Meir. Although we do not rule like Rabbi Meir, the teaching could still tell us that even according to him the grandson removes the purchaser. But according to Tosafot's interpretation, where the Gemara's question concerns repayment of the purchase money, the anonymous Gemara in Bekhorot, which reflects the practical halakhah, shows that the grandson does not prevail through the independent direct inheritance claim. Tosafot therefore properly ask how the purchaser could collect when his claim is only an oral debt that could not validly be documented according to the actual halakhah. It is no answer that Rabbi Meir would permit a document, because we do not rule like him. Otherwise the two sugyot would conflict as practical law. This is precise. There is therefore no real doubt in the matter. These authorities left room for me to clarify a point that follows necessarily from the accepted ruling concerning the grandson. Once that ruling is not itself in doubt, the associated rule that a creditor does not collect prospective property is likewise not doubtful. I can add another proof that Tosafot in Bava Metzia and the Rosh also hold that a creditor does not collect prospective property. They struggle extensively with the statement in Bekhorot that daughters receiving maintenance, which is a condition of the ketubah, are governed by the leniencies of a ketubah. Tosafot explain this as referring to appreciation in the heirs' property, and from there enter a lengthy difficulty because an ordinary creditor also does not collect such appreciation. But why assume that the leniency refers to appreciation? It could refer to prospective property. An ordinary creditor, on the opposing view, collects prospective property, while daughters supported under the ketubah condition do not. The statement in Ketubot that a condition of the ketubah has the same law as the ketubah regarding appreciation need not settle the point, because that statement may concern the additional ketubah amount and collection from purchasers, not support owed by heirs. Daughters' maintenance from heirs could still be excluded from prospective property. Indeed, Ramban in Milchamot at the beginning of Bava Metzia appears to say that one cannot explain the ketubah leniency as referring to prospective property because even an ordinary creditor does not collect prospective property. This indicates that Tosafot likewise assumed that a creditor does not collect it. Admittedly, according to Tosafot's distinction that a written creditor with an after acquired property clause may collect where an oral creditor does not, the daughters have no such clause, and the question may still be reformulated. Yet this only strengthens the need to identify the exact type of obligation under discussion. The Shach argues from Ramban's proof from the Yerushalmi's phrase "his vineyard, excluding prospective property" that an ordinary creditor must collect prospective property. Otherwise, he asks, why would Scripture need a special exclusion for a tort creditor? Why should he be better than an ordinary creditor? I do not understand this argument. The same Yerushalmi derives from "the best" an exclusion of movables. Would we therefore say that ordinary creditors collect from heirs' movables? Certainly not. Rather, the tort rule can serve as a model for ordinary creditors. The same is true of prospective property. From the tort law we can learn that a creditor does not collect prospective property. The Shach's proposed distinction creates a further difficulty. We hold that a creditor can collect a debt owed to the debtor through Rabbi Natan's rule, derived from the verse "that which he gave to him." If an ordinary creditor collected prospective property but a tort creditor did not, then Rabbi Natan's rule should perhaps apply only to an ordinary lender, because a debt in the hands of a third party is itself prospective property, as Tosafot in Bava Batra and Bekhorot write. A tort claimant, who according to the Shach cannot collect prospective property, should then be unable to invoke Rabbi Natan's lien. Yet the Gemara in Bava Kamma assumes that Rabbi Natan's rule also applies between tortfeasor and injured party. That indicates there is no such broad distinction. The Shach's reasoning is therefore not correct on this point. Tosafot themselves, after concluding that a creditor may collect prospective property in the relevant setting, ask why the purchaser should have a lien against the father's estate at all. Why should he be better than an oral lender who cannot collect from heirs? Maharsha explains that although we generally rule that an oral loan may be collected from heirs, a loan of this sort is different because no lien ever attached during the debtor's lifetime, since the son died before the father. Such a claim does not bind the heirs. Tosafot's earlier conclusion that a creditor collects prospective property must therefore concern a case where the debtor expressly included after acquired property. Then the lien can extend to the heirs. But the Gemara states the case simply as "a son who sold," without any valid written after acquired property undertaking. Tosafot can therefore ask why an oral claim of this unusual type should bind the heirs at all. The Rosh in Yesh Bekhor follows the same distinction. He challenges the opinion that a creditor collects prospective property from the Mishnah of the collapsed house and notes that the Mishnah can involve a document containing an after acquired property clause. At first glance that clause seems unnecessary against heirs, since heirs generally take subject to existing liens. But the Rosh understood like Tosafot that with prospective property there is a real distinction. Without an explicit undertaking reaching property later acquired, no lien ever attached. Thus the Rosh had to specify that the Mishnah can involve such a clause. This is correct. One might still ask what basis Tosafot had to invent a distinction between one oral loan and another. Perhaps every oral loan can be collected from heirs. The answer is that according to the view that a creditor generally collects prospective property, Ramban's proof from "his vineyard, excluding prospective property" must still be explained. The necessary distinction is that a creditor collects prospective property only where he wrote an after acquired property clause. A tort obligation, though treated as a Torah written obligation, contains no such contractual undertaking. Property acquired later is therefore like property outside an oral lien and cannot be collected. Tosafot's distinction is thus compelled, and their question is well founded. This also resolves a difficulty in the chapter Yesh Nochalin. Regarding a firstborn whose father left a debt, Rav Nachman says that if the heirs collected land on the debt the firstborn does not take a double share. The Gemara challenges him from Rav Nachman's own ruling that heirs who collected land on their father's debt are treated in a particular way. According to Tosafot, why is this difficult? Perhaps the collected land was prospective property, so a firstborn does not take double, while a creditor, who may collect prospective property, can nevertheless take the land the heirs collected. According to what I have written, the answer is that Rav Nachman's statement is unqualified and therefore includes a creditor whose document contains no after acquired property clause. In such a case even the creditor does not collect prospective property. Why then should land later collected by the heirs be treated as though the father had possessed it? At the initial stage, before the Gemara invokes Rabbi Natan's rule, the only explanation would be that the creditor's collection operates retroactively, so the land is considered to have been collected by the father himself. If so, the firstborn too should receive a double share because the father is treated as having possessed it. The Gemara's challenge is therefore sound. This also answers Ramban's difficulty and further shows why the Shach's distinction cannot stand. It may be possible to understand the Shach's proof more narrowly. Perhaps he meant that from the special verse excluding prospective property in tort law we infer that an ordinary creditor can collect prospective property only when he has an after acquired property clause, in accordance with Tosafot. If every creditor, even with such a clause, were excluded from prospective property, the verse in tort law would seem unnecessary, because the tort claimant certainly has no such clause. On this reading, the verse distinguishes only between a creditor who explicitly extended the lien and one who did not, not between ordinary debt and tort as such. This approach can also answer the earlier problem according to Rashbam. If sale is stronger than an ordinary lien, why does the Gemara challenge the sale from the collapsed house? According to Rashbam, the Gemara may reason as follows. From the tort verse one could infer that a creditor can collect prospective property when there is an effective after acquired property undertaking. One could not say that the tort verse merely teaches the direct inheritance claim, because at the initial stage the Gemara has not accepted that claim. The verse therefore leaves room for a creditor to collect prospective property in some cases. If a purchaser, who is already in possession, has the stronger argument, he can rely on that side of the law. The Gemara's challenge from the collapsed house is then that the creditor there too should have some corresponding strength unless a different rule intervenes. At the initial stage, however, one cannot yet explain the tort verse as teaching the direct inheritance claim because the Gemara had not accepted that principle. Once the Gemara ultimately accepts the direct inheritance structure for practical law, the tort verse can be understood in that light, and no proof remains that a creditor generally collects prospective property. This may be one way to align Rashbam with the Shach's observation. I discussed this at greater length in my novellae. One further point should be noted regarding the argument that because the son can sell for his father's honor, the property is already treated as possessed. The fact that the Sages allowed such a sale may not prove a full present property right. Even Rabbi Meir, who ordinarily allows transfer of something not yet in existence, does not acquire property that falls to a dying person in certain circumstances, as Tosafot write. One can answer that where the Sages themselves enacted validity for a particular sale, they may have made no distinction and treated the enactment uniformly. But that only applies within the rabbinic enactment. According to Rabbi Meir there was no need for such an enactment merely because of a rare deathbed circumstance. The Gemara could therefore still classify the property as prospective in his framework. Another possible explanation of the Gemara's challenge from the collapsed house is that the rule whether a creditor collects prospective property was itself uncertain. The verse concerning tort could be read either as a special exclusion limited to tort, as the Shach suggests, or as the source for all creditors. In a debt case, where the claimant seeks to remove property, the burden of proof would therefore prevent collection. A purchaser already in possession, however, could invoke the opposite side of the uncertainty and retain the property. This would explain why the Gemara asks, in effect, what legal basis there is for removing the purchaser. The Gemara could have reformulated the collapsed house ruling in several ways, but its point would remain that the heirs are the possessors and the creditor cannot prove a right to prospective property. Here too, with additional doubts such as which of father and son died first and whether prospective property is collectible, possession becomes decisive. This is a possible supplementary explanation.
And Rambam wrote in his Commentary to the Mishnah in Bekhorot that nowadays, after the Geonim enacted collection from heirs' movable property, a creditor also collects prospective property. Kesef Mishneh cites this in Hilkhot Ishut 16, yet the Shulchan Aruch omits it. It appears that the Beit Yosef understood the Geonic enactment according to its reason. The Geonim acted because most contemporary commerce and wealth is in movables. How would that rationale apply to an inheritance from the debtor's father that comes only after the debtor's death? Is such a case common, or do creditors and wives rely on it when extending credit or entering marriage? The subjects are not comparable. It therefore appears that Rambam's statement concerned a woman's ketubah and a different kind of prospective property. Rambam may hold, like many authorities, that by strict law a woman does not collect her ketubah from a debt owed to her husband by others because such a debt is prospective property, and that Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to a ketubah, as Maharshal writes in the responsum cited above. With respect to that kind of prospective property, namely outstanding debts, Rambam could say that nowadays most livelihood and commerce is itself conducted through credit. Just as the Geonim enacted collection from movables because parties now rely on them, so too reliance on ordinary debts in commerce justifies treating those debts as collectible. This explains the widespread practice, attested by the Rosh and Maharam, of collecting a ketubah from outstanding loans. Rambam did not necessarily mean that a woman can collect from an inheritance falling to her deceased husband's estate from his father after the husband's death. That case has no special connection to the Geonic enactment concerning heirs' movables. Accordingly, the Mechaber could rule that a woman may collect from an ordinary debt but still omit any general rule that a creditor collects every form of prospective property. In a case like the collapsed house, even today, the creditor does not collect. This comparison between an outstanding debt and heirs' movables rests on the same underlying idea, that in both cases the creditor did not originally rely on the item as a present source of payment. Ramban in Yesh Nochalin, citing Ri Migash, speaks in a way that supports the comparison and writes that when the debtor dies, an outstanding loan in his hands takes on the character of movable property. This also helps explain Tosafot in Ketubot 81b. Tosafot write that if, according to Rabbi Meir, heirs' movables were subject to collection, then even without Rabbi Natan's rule the woman should collect because the debt would be treated like land. Maharsha asks that even land itself would require Rabbi Natan's rule, as the Rosh writes. According to the foregoing, however, the need for Rabbi Natan's rule in land is because otherwise the outstanding debt is prospective property. But if Rabbi Meir holds that even heirs' movables are subject to collection despite the lack of original reliance, that itself shows that collection can operate without such reliance. Prospective property should then be no worse than heirs' movables, and Rabbi Natan's rule would indeed be unnecessary. Tosafot's question is therefore exact. The Sema writes that a creditor does not collect wages owed for labor because those wages are prospective property. Rama cites this in Even HaEzer regarding a ketubah. The Shach objects that Mordechai and Rama said this only of a ketubah, following the view that an ordinary creditor collects prospective property while a ketubah does not. According to what I have written, however, an ordinary creditor likewise does not collect prospective property, so the Shach's objection on that ground falls away. Yet another difficulty remains. Wages owed for labor are subject to Rabbi Natan's rule, so why call them prospective property at all? Mordechai may follow authorities who hold that Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to wages, as discussed above in siman 86. But according to the accepted law, even though wages are payable only at the end, the obligation accrues continuously from beginning to end, and each perutah becomes subject through Rabbi Natan's rule. The question therefore remains. It appears that Rama follows his own view in a responsum, also stated by Maharshal, that a woman's ketubah is not subject to Rabbi Natan's rule in the same manner as an ordinary debt. The reason a woman may collect from an outstanding loan is not that Rabbi Natan transfers the third party's debt directly to her, but that the husband once possessed the value and his ketubah lien attached to it. His later lending of that money did not extinguish her prior lien. Maharshal considered this explanation strained, but both he and Rama appear to have overlooked Mahari Weil, responsum 40, who states this reason explicitly. Mahari Weil indeed holds that where the husband made the loan before the ketubah arose, the debt is not subject to the ketubah, unlike Rama, who apparently applied a general no distinction rule and included all such debts. Wages for labor are different because the money never came into the husband's possession at all. There is therefore no earlier ketubah lien that can be said to persist. The wages remain prospective property, and since Rabbi Natan's rule does not operate for the ketubah on this approach, the woman cannot collect them. This is why Rama states the rule in the ketubah context. An ordinary lender, by contrast, can collect wages owed to his debtor because Rabbi Natan's rule does apply. Mahari Weil, responsum 40, seems to go further and hold that even during the husband's lifetime a woman cannot collect from his wages because they are prospective. He proves this from the sugya in Ketubot concerning one who was a creditor of his brother, where absent Rabbi Natan's rule the woman could not collect even though the husband was treated as though still alive. Mahari Weil notes, however, that Rambam writes that if a man divorces his wife and presently lacks funds to pay the ketubah, he remains obligated to pay whenever he later acquires means. That future acquisition is prospective, implying that Rambam disagrees and holds that the concept of prospective property does not bar collection during the debtor's lifetime. Mahari Weil therefore presents the matter as a dispute among authorities. According to this, Rama, who cites Rambam's rule as law in Even HaEzer siman 70, cannot maintain broadly that prospective property is excluded even during the debtor's lifetime. I am in fact astonished by the premise. If the property later actually comes into the husband's possession, why would the wife not collect from him personally, even from the garment on his shoulders? There is no property more clearly prospective than an inheritance from his father before the father dies, yet the Mishnah of the collapsed house shows that if the father died first and the son remained alive even briefly, the wife collects her ketubah from the property the son inherited. What difference can there be whether the husband obtained the money by inheritance or by some other later means? Once it reached him, she plainly collects. Mordechai's rule must therefore concern property that never actually reached the husband, as in Mahari Weil's case where the husband had apostatized, disappeared, or otherwise was unavailable. There is an additional powerful difficulty if prospective property were excluded even during the debtor's lifetime. A debt owed by others would then be prospective absent Rabbi Natan's rule. In Bava Kamma, however, when a married woman injures another person and her liability is difficult to collect, the Gemara asks why she should not sell her ketubah rights to someone else for their present discounted value. If her ketubah itself were prospective property and a tort claimant did not collect prospective property, her ketubah right would not be subject to the injured party at all. Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to a ketubah according to Ran and the authorities cited above. If the husband's obligation to her were itself not subject, then certainly her own ketubah right could not be subjected onward to the injured party. This is a major difficulty. It therefore appears that even though prospective property is not itself subject to direct collection while in another's hands, during the debtor's lifetime the debtor personally remains obligated to do everything possible to pay. Beit din can compel him to pursue a debt owed to him and use the proceeds for payment. If the debt is not yet due and cannot presently be collected, he can be compelled to sell the debt at its current value and use the proceeds. Thus in the Bava Kamma case the woman can be compelled to sell her ketubah expectancy in order to satisfy the damage claim. But if the woman herself is absent or has apostatized, the creditor cannot simply collect from the husband, because he can say, "you are not my litigant," like the Gemara's argument in the case of one who was a creditor of his brother. Accordingly, the statement that one does not collect wages for labor applies where the debtor himself is unavailable. If he is present, beit din compels him to pursue the wages and pay his creditor. This differs from Mahari Weil's broader formulation, and his words require substantial study. Quite apart from this, Mahari Weil's suggestion that Rambam's rule requiring a divorced husband to pay the ketubah when he later acquires means might itself be disputed is very difficult. That ruling is an explicit Gemara in Nedarim 66, where the ketubah is not torn up merely because the husband presently lacks assets. Ran and the Rosh explain explicitly that she collects when he later obtains funds, and an arrangement leaving him with protected basic assets does not extinguish the obligation. The matter therefore should not be treated as a doubtful dispute.
