The rule of priority applies to land, but if he acquired it between the two loans. Sema holds that land acquired between two loans is subject to the later creditor but not the earlier one unless the earlier document contains an after acquired property clause. The Shach disagrees and says that as against the debtor himself, both creditors can collect even without such a clause, because after acquired property language matters mainly against purchasers. In my view Sema is correct. A creditor with an effective after acquired property clause possesses an actual lien in the property. A creditor without such a clause may still collect from the debtor personally, but only because repaying debts is a mitzvah, like an oral loan without a property lien. The Gemara in Perek Mi SheMet says that if after acquired property is not subject to a lien, the creditor reaches it only through the heirs' mitzvah of paying their father's debt, and asks that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. This shows that absent an after acquired lien, the property itself is not encumbered. Therefore, where one creditor has an actual after acquired lien and another has only the debtor's general mitzvah to pay, beit din cannot use the first creditor's encumbered property to satisfy the second creditor's personal obligation. This is especially clear according to Rif's view that a later written loan can defeat an earlier oral loan where only the later creditor has an actual property lien. Even according to the Shulchan Aruch, who gives an earlier oral loan priority over a later written loan because Torah liens can exist on property owned at the time of the loan, after acquired property is different. Torah law does not automatically subject property acquired later. An after acquired lien exists only through the recognized contractual mechanism. Without it, the earlier creditor has no property lien at all in the later acquired asset. Tumim cites Tosafot concerning redemption of the firstborn, where a lien can attach to property acquired later because the obligation continually renews. That is not analogous. The obligation of redeeming the firstborn rests on the person at every moment. Each later acquisition can therefore meet a presently existing obligation, as though a new debt arises continuously. An ordinary loan obligation arose at the time the money was borrowed. Property acquired later is classic after acquired property and requires a separate lien clause. Sema and Bedek HaBayit are therefore correct.
Whoever first collected acquired, even if he was the last creditor. Rabbi Yonah limits this to judicial collection. If the creditor took land on his own, even after appraisal, the seizure does not help. Rosh agrees regarding land but says movables are different and private seizure can be effective. Beit Yosef cites Rabbeinu Yerucham in the name of Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia that whoever collected first acquired in all cases, and thinks this may even include collection outside beit din. The Shach limits the broad language to cases where beit din or the debtor himself transferred the land, arguing that simple seizure of land is never effective. I agree that mere private seizure of land normally does not amount to final collection. The phrase "what he collected" naturally refers to property that beit din or the debtor gave him. Rabbi Yonah explicitly requires beit din. The proof from a purchaser who holds improvement does not establish otherwise. The purchaser's possession of appreciation arose through the seller's sale, but the creditor's right to part of that appreciation does not mean the seller specifically conveyed the creditor's share to the purchaser. Indeed, the creditor takes the whole land and pays the purchaser the value of the protected improvement. The purchaser therefore is not a simple possessor of the disputed land share. This also explains authorities who allow equal creditors to keep land once properly collected. The rule is not based on physical seizure as such. Once beit din completed collection for one equal lienholder, his lien has been converted into ownership. Before that, the land itself remains equally encumbered to both and one cannot simply seize the other's lien. The same analysis resolves Nimukei Yosef's rule that where two creditors have equal after acquired liens they divide, and if one has seized more than half it can be removed from him. This division is not merely the result of factual uncertainty. Both liens truly attach equally to the land. Because the property itself is subject to each lien in equal measure, private possession cannot override the other's equal right. Only completed judicial collection can convert one lien into ownership. Thus the rule that first collection wins refers to a valid legal collection, not ordinary physical seizure.
