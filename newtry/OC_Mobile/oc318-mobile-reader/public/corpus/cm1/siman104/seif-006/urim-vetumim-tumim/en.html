And whoever first collects, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that this applies even if the creditor collected on his own without a judicial collection. The Shach cites the Shiltei Giborim that no authority holds such a view, because Rabbenu Yonah's objection regarding the rule that a creditor collects half the enhancement from a purchaser is that there can be no greater seizure than the purchaser's possession itself. In truth, one who studies the Baal HaTerumot, shaar 43, part 4, din 13, will see that the Rambam and Ramban hold that mere seizure is effective, and Rabbenu Yerucham, netiv 6, part 1, says the same in the name of the Ramah. Moreover, Rabbenu Yonah's objection is independently difficult according to the authorities cited by Nimukei Yosef who explain that in the case of two successive loans the creditors divide because the law itself is in doubt. If so, the purchaser is in possession, and as to a doubt in the law mere possession certainly suffices, as I explained in my rules of seizure. This purchaser is especially strong because he took possession lawfully and, according to what I wrote there, even before the doubt arose. It is possible that this is the objection intended by Mordechai there. The answer must be that these authorities understand the Gemara's statement in Bava Metzia that a creditor collects the enhancement because the purchaser's deed contains the undertaking, "I will stand in your place and satisfy any claim," as follows. One might otherwise ask why such a clause is needed. The creditor's document includes after acquired property, and the enhancement is itself after acquired property, so it should obviously be liened. Why, then, in the case of a gift where no such undertaking is written, should the creditor not collect the enhancement as well? See below in siman 115 and the authorities there, and see the Or Zarua to Bava Metzia. These authorities therefore explain that the Gemara's initial difficulty is that despite the creditor's lien on after acquired property, the purchaser is already in possession and should retain all of the enhancement. The Gemara answers that the purchaser accepted the seller's warranty clause when he bought, and therefore entered the transaction on the understanding that the creditor could collect the enhancement. Possession cannot help one who acquired expressly subject to that liability. This is why the creditor collects half the enhancement. With a gift, where no such warranty is written, the creditor indeed cannot remove the enhancement from the recipient's possession. On this explanation, one must adopt the Ramban's view that with a gift the creditor collects enhancement exceeding the recipient's expenses, but must reimburse the expenses. The expenses themselves are a debt owed to the recipient and are therefore analogous to the case of two creditors. One must also say that, as against a purchaser, after acquired property need not have been expressly written, for in the case of a gift no such clause was written. Even if the Gemara is explained as Rashi does, the underlying answer remains correct according to Nimukei Yosef's view that the creditors divide because the law is doubtful: since the deed contains the seller's warranty undertaking, the purchaser knowingly accepted this liability and thereby waived the protection of his possession. Accordingly, where the authorities themselves are divided, the rule is that the burden of proof rests on the one seeking to extract, and property is not removed from the person who is already in possession.
