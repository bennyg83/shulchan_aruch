An oral loan takes precedence. See Baer Heitev, who cites Radbaz as saying that this applies specifically where the holders of the written documents admit the oral debt. In Rabbi Akiva Eiger's glosses to the Shulchan Aruch he writes: "If a creditor with a written document and a creditor with an oral debt come to collect from orphans, and the written creditor admits the oral debt, then if the estate is insufficient for both, they divide, because the written creditor admits the oral creditor's debt. If the estate is sufficient for both, however, the written creditor takes his entire debt, for he can say to the oral creditor, 'the assets are before you.' The oral creditor cannot collect from the orphans merely on the written creditor's admission. So writes Beit Meir, siman 104." See Responsa Panim Meirot of my grandfather, part 2, siman 92, concerning a dying person who declared before his death that he owed a certain person one hundred by oral debt. After his death several written debts bearing witnesses and kinyan, as well as several promissory notes, emerged against him, and the estate was insufficient even to pay the written creditors. The question was whether the oral creditor must also receive a proportional share, since the deceased made a complete admission at the time of death and a person presumably does not sin for no benefit to himself. He answered that at first glance this seems supported by Shevuot 42b, where the Gemara distinguishes a dying person because a person does not sin for no benefit to himself, and Rashi explains that one does not sin merely to benefit his heirs. This reasoning would appear to apply even toward an ordinary person. The statement that there is a presumption that a person does not conspire against consecrated property would likewise be based on this reason, since a healthy person may conspire even against consecrated property. However, Nimukei Yosef at the end of the chapter Get Pashut explains that this presumption is said specifically regarding consecrated property and not regarding an ordinary person. If so, in our case the dying person's admission is not believed to the detriment of others. This is also explicit in the Shach below, siman 255, end of seif katan 8, who writes that although a dying person who says, "I owe one hundred to so and so," is believed, he is not believed against purchasers or other creditors. At the end of the responsum Panim Meirot writes that this is nevertheless surprising, because even regarding an ordinary person we say that a person does not sin for no benefit to himself, as in Bava Metzia 5a regarding an ordinary shepherd. Otherwise, how could we entrust our animals to a shepherd in violation of placing a stumbling block before the blind? We are therefore forced to distinguish that a person may sin for the benefit of his children, whereas with consecrated property he will not sin even for his children. This requires study. See Shaar Mishpat, siman 255, who writes that there was never a difficulty. We rely on the presumption that a person does not sin for no benefit to himself only to avoid disqualifying him from testimony where the shepherd's disqualification itself is merely doubtful. Since an ordinary shepherd is only suspected of grazing in others' fields, this presumption allows us to retain his prior presumption of fitness. But we do not use this presumption affirmatively to believe him and extract money from others. That stronger use exists only regarding consecrated property. See there.
