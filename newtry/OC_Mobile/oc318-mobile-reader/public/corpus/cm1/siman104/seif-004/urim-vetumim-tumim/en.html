Unless beit din appraised it, etc. With land, where collection requires judicial action, it is obvious that a formal appraisal is likewise necessary. How could beit din transfer land without an appraisal? We may presume that beit din will follow the law and transfer only after appraisal and announcement, except in a place where the accepted practice is to transfer property without such procedures, as explained above in siman 103. Even there the matter requires study where an earlier creditor exists. With movables, however, we rule like the Rosh that judicial collection is unnecessary and a creditor may acquire by his own seizure. The Bach nevertheless holds that an appraisal is still required, for otherwise the objection "who appraised it for you?" remains. He argues that no proof can be drawn from the case of detached produce, where whoever first seizes acquires, because there the value is known by the market rate and no appraisal is necessary. The Shach questions this, and I do not understand the Bach's position. The phrase "who appraised it for you?" does not mean merely that no valuation occurred. In the case of the widow, the property had already been announced and appraised by a court of ordinary judges. Rather, as Rashi and Tosafot explain, the phrase means, "who placed you in possession of these assets?" Beit din must place the creditor in possession, and three ordinary appraisers are insufficient for that purpose. Accordingly, if a creditor simply seizes property on his own, then even where its value is known the question remains: who placed you in possession? We must therefore say that movables are different, because seizure itself acquires them and no judicial act placing the creditor in possession is required. If so, a separate appraisal is likewise unnecessary, because an appraisal alone would not answer the objection of "who placed you in possession?" This is clear. Rabbenu Yonah writes in the Rosh, in the chapter HaKotev, that even if a creditor collected on his own through an appraisal, where the objection "who appraised it for you?" is absent, his collection is ineffective. This does not undermine what I have written. Rabbenu Yonah's principal discussion concerns the enhancement of land in the purchaser's possession. There the question "who placed you in possession?" plainly does not apply, because the purchaser is already in possession and the improvement belongs to him. Nevertheless, Rabbenu Yonah holds that mere self collection does not establish priority. Outside that context, whenever beit din has not actually placed the creditor in possession the objection of "who placed you in possession?" remains. Alternatively, Rabbenu Yonah may interpret the phrase as the Ritva reports in the name of the Geonim, namely, that because a person appraised property for himself perhaps he did not value it carefully. According to that interpretation, where beit din appraised property for transfer to an earlier creditor and a later creditor then seized it, the later creditor cannot be challenged with a defective appraisal, although he still lacks formal judicial collection. But according to the interpretation adopted by most commentators, namely that of Rashi and Tosafot, so long as beit din has not actually transferred the property the objection of "who placed you in possession?" remains.
