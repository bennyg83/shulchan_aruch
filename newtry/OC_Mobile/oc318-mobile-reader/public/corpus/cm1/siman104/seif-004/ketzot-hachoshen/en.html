It is not considered collection unless beit din appraised the movables. Bach reads this strictly: merely handing movables to a creditor without prior appraisal is not judicial collection, and appraisal without delivery likewise is insufficient. He later says detached fruits may be different because their prices are usually fixed. In my view movables do not require formal appraisal for priority purposes. True judicial collection ordinarily involves appraisal and final transfer, but movables differ because a creditor acquires a pledge. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch rule that whether a creditor takes a pledge through beit din, by force, or with the debtor's consent after the loan, the pledge acquires a strong legal status. The creditor need only return necessary clothing at the required times; the pledge does not become ordinary movables in the debtor's heirs' hands and Shemittah does not cancel the debt. Therefore, once one creditor takes movables as a pledge, his lien becomes specially strong. Even if there has not yet been final appraisal as collection, his acquired pledge right can give him priority over another creditor who has only the ordinary personal repayment right. This explains Rosh's distinction: in land, private seizure does not help because a creditor does not acquire a land pledge and final collection requires beit din; in movables, private seizure can create an acquired pledge and therefore what was seized remains with the creditor. If, however, both creditors already have equal specific liens on the movables through a valid clause subjecting movables together with land, the first creditor's private seizure may not improve his right. Both already possess a land like lien. Perhaps actual judicial collection through appraisal and transfer is then necessary to create priority. On the other hand, a pledge still has special advantages, such as protection from Shemittah, and may remain stronger. The point requires further study.
