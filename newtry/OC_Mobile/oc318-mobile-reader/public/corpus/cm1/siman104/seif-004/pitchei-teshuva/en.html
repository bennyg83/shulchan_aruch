Priority. See Baer Heitev, who writes that the Bach rules like Responsa Maimoniyot. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 118, likewise rules so in practice and cites Piskei Rabbi Menachem Recanati, siman 524, who explicitly writes that there is no distinction between a case where the other creditors are present and demanding their portions and one where they are not present. Their shares must always be set aside. See there. See Responsa Panim Meirot of my grandfather, part 2, siman 20, concerning Reuven, who sued Shimon, claiming that Shimon took from Levi four barrels of honey that Reuven had already purchased from Levi because Levi owed him one thousand gold coins. Levi and Reuven had made a binding commercial agreement concerning that honey. Shimon answered that Levi also owed him five hundred gold coins. He came to Levi's town while Levi was away and asked Levi's wife whether her husband had left money for him. She answered that he had left no money, but had instructed her to give Shimon four barrels of honey by weight at the known price. Shimon accepted the honey and claimed that he had no knowledge, either then or afterward, that the honey belonged to Reuven, and that perhaps Levi had sold him different honey. They litigated, and the ruling was that the honey is not taken from Shimon, who seized it first. Even if Reuven proves by witnesses that he acquired the honey through a commercial mode of acquisition customarily used among merchants, namely striking hands together, such an acquisition may prevent the seller from retracting but does not necessarily place the goods so fully in the buyer's possession that seizure by another creditor is ineffective. This is evident from Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 124, cited by the Shach at the end of siman 126, concerning gold rings that Shimon deposited with Reuven and later instructed him, in the presence of all three, to give them to Levi. Even according to the Shach there, who disagrees and holds that once one has acquired by any valid acquisition the object is fully his and another creditor cannot seize it, that applies where the parties admit that the specific gold rings were transferred in the three party arrangement to Levi. Here, Reuven cannot prove that the four barrels of honey he purchased are the very barrels now in dispute. Even if he had a written document that he purchased four barrels of honey from Levi, perhaps Levi sold Shimon four different barrels. Since Reuven cannot make a definite claim against Shimon but only a doubtful one, strict law would not even permit imposing a cherem upon Shimon. To satisfy Reuven's concern, however, beit din ruled that Shimon should accept a cherem that he did not know that these four barrels had been purchased by Reuven from Levi, but that Levi's wife gave them to him believing that she was carrying out her husband's instruction. Once he accepts this, Reuven has no further claim against Shimon. These are his words. See there.
