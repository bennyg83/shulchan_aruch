Bach rules that where a seller sold merchandise on credit and the actual merchandise remains identifiable, that seller is prior to all other creditors in taking back his own merchandise. I cited this above in siman 96. At first glance this is difficult from the rule that a creditor collects half of a purchaser's improvement. Nimukei Yosef explains that the purchaser's improvement is conceptually treated as though the purchaser sold that improvement back to the seller, and the seller then resold it with responsibility. Thus the improvement is still, in substance, the purchaser's own merchandise that entered the seller's estate only through a legal construct. Yet the creditor and purchaser divide, suggesting that even identifiable merchandise does not always give the seller absolute priority. One can reconcile Bach according to his own approach in siman 115. He understands the purchaser's right to half the improvement not as reimbursement for the improvement itself, since such reimbursement does not collect from encumbered property, but as part of the seller's responsibility for the purchase principal. Once the claim is framed as responsibility for the original purchase price, the improvement is no longer the very merchandise sold by the creditor. It is different property standing as security for the purchase price. The creditors can therefore divide.
