Documents, etc. The Bach, in his responsa, discusses at length that if one creditor holds two promissory notes, he does not collect according to the number of documents, but according to the number of creditors. He therefore receives only one share, as though he held only a single note. His proof is from Bava Batra, where the Gemara states that if a person has one note for one hundred, we do not divide it into two notes of fifty each, because it is preferable for the debtor that his document be impaired. This implies that if the debtor agrees, we may do so. Yet by creating two notes the creditor could thereby disadvantage other creditors in a later division. The Bach therefore infers that the number of documents does not increase his share. The Shach above in siman 53 rejects this proof. First, he suggests that when the original note of one hundred is divided into two notes of fifty, each new document records that it derives from the original note of one hundred. Thus at collection it remains apparent that there was only one original claim and the creditor still receives only one share. Second, he writes that we do not suspect the existence of other debts merely because of this possibility, since the creditor does not know of them and the document was not impaired by falling. The author of Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 116, followed the Shach. These answers are strained. In many places we take into account that a debtor may have other creditors, such as later promissory notes and similar cases. The Shach's statement that the creditor does not know of them is difficult to understand. On the contrary, the concern is precisely that he knows the debtor has many obligations and therefore contrives to obtain two documents so that he will receive a larger portion. Are we bound by his own assertion that he knows of no other debts? Such conduct itself can constitute an artifice. The first answer, that the derivative nature of the two documents would be written into them, is also strained. Had that been the law, the authorities should have stated it rather than speaking without qualification. Moreover, Rabbi Abbahu states there, and the Sema cites, that if the holder asks to turn a note of one hundred into a note of fifty we do not do so because of concern for a receipt. If the replacement document expressly stated that it derived from the earlier note, as the Shach assumes is the Gemara's case, what concern would there be? The Shach's explanation therefore cannot stand. The Tzemach Tzedek likewise rules against the Bach. Apparently he did not see the Bach's responsum, because he does not mention it, and it is difficult to say that he would dispute his teacher without citing him. He rejects the proof by saying that the new documents are written with the later date and therefore create no loss to other creditors because they are later. This answer does not fully address movables or property acquired afterward. In truth, where the two new notes are dated from the later date and are written with both parties' consent, there is no difficulty at all. What prevents the debtor today from writing a thousand documents? There is no artifice to fear if he freely agrees. The real question is why the Gemara says that we do not write two documents without both parties' consent because of impairment of the debtor's document. That concern may not apply where the document gives the creditor explicit credibility even after impairment. Why did the Gemara not instead give the Bach's reason that dividing the note could cause the creditor to collect more than his lawful portion? Moreover, the Tzemach Tzedek himself ruled above in siman 53 that the discussion concerns writing from the original date, as in the case of a document that was erased and beit din replaces it. If so, the Bach's concern is even stronger. Perhaps the creditor seeks the division precisely as an artifice. Nevertheless, as a matter of law the Tzemach Tzedek's conclusion is persuasive. The essential lien created by a promissory note is embodied in the document itself. The creditor can sell the document by writing, "Acquire it and all the liens contained in it." We rule that the lien on the debtor's assets is transferable. This proves that the document itself includes a distinct lien upon the assets. Each separate promissory note therefore carries its own lien. What difference is there between one note and another? There is no need for the Tzemach Tzedek's additional proof from the rule that we do not calculate one note against another, because there one could say that, since the parties separated the transactions into two documents, they intended the two matters not to be intertwined. Here, by contrast, the lien arose at the time of a single loan, and one might ask what difference later division into two documents makes. But the answer lies in the very nature of the documents. Each note independently carries a lien. Even if the obligation originally arose together, once separate notes are validly written, the creditor acquires a distinct documentary lien through each one, and each note has its own independent right and lien. The Bach's proof can be answered in two ways. First, the Gemara may concern a case where the original note had an expressly designated apotiki. In that case there is no concern that division into two notes will affect the proportional division among creditors. We need not fear the exceedingly unusual possibility that the same field was made an express apotiki to two different debts on the same day. Second, as I have written several times, the Sages generally protect against these concerns when an improper collection would later be reversed. There is then genuine injustice to prevent. But where, after the fact, a creditor who collected first keeps what he collected, the Sages were less concerned about structuring the documents so as to prevent every possibility that he might receive more than his proportional share. If the law itself is that a creditor who first collected the whole amount retains it, then allowing a structure that may enable him to collect more than his ideal proportional share is not a comparable injustice. This is the same principle explained above in the name of the Ran concerning the instruction to give to the claimant whose proof is weakest. The matter is clear and correct. This applies, however, only to full promissory notes that create asset liens. With a handwritten acknowledgment or similar writing that creates no lien and has merely the status of an oral loan, the result is different. There, the writing does not create any new lien. The loan itself is what gives rise to the debtor's personal and basic property obligation under Torah law. Accordingly, if a single loan of two hundred gold coins was made at one time, later dividing it into two handwritten acknowledgments or two commercial notes cannot possibly create two independent liens. When did a separate lien arise for each amount? At the time of the loan, the entire two hundred arose as one obligation. At the later time of writing, the handwritten instruments create no lien at all. See the Sema above in siman 48, who holds that with a handwritten obligation a new loan after cancellation of the prior lien is not treated like a promissory note with a renewed lien, and see what I wrote there. It is therefore clear that in such a case the creditor receives only the share appropriate to his total debt as one creditor. If he first lent one amount and later made another separate loan, that is different. At each loan the debtor became separately obligated. It makes no difference whether the two loans were made to one person or to two different people. But where the loan itself occurred as one transaction, later multiplication of non lien instruments cannot increase his share. See Responsa Pnei Yehoshua, where a responsum of another scholar is printed in siman 10. It likewise implies that if the loan was made as one transaction and was merely divided into several documents, the creditor does not collect according to the number of documents. Regarding full promissory notes, however, the law is as the Tzemach Tzedek and Shevut Yaakov hold. Regarding commercial acknowledgments and handwritten obligations, there is no doubt that where the loan itself was one transaction, the creditor cannot collect more merely because the debt was divided among several instruments. The Tzemach Tzedek spoke only of promissory notes, not handwritten obligations. Some scholars have suggested that with a bearer note, which may be transferred to whoever presents it, and similarly under the custom in Metz concerning a document known in German as an "Aporter," everyone would agree that collection is according to the number of documents, because the holder could simply transfer one note to one person and another to another person. I do not understand this. Are we dealing with wicked liars? In beit din they will be asked who actually lent the money, and they must admit that all the funds came from the same lender. The matter then returns to a single creditor. On a bearer note or similar instrument there is no asset lien merely by virtue of the paper. The underlying loan is what creates the obligation. There is therefore no basis to increase the share. The author of Shevut Yaakov introduced another consideration regarding commercial writings. Because such a writing states that it is governed according to the custom of commercial paper, he argues that the obligor accepted upon himself the law governing such notes, including the sovereign's law that payment follows the instruments. His words require study. If so, one should also divide according to the amount of each claim, following Rav Chanina's view, whenever that is the commercial rule. This would displace Torah law. We have never heard such a principle, and his position requires study. In truth, I have a further difficulty regarding movables. Above in seif 1 I concluded that no asset lien attaches to movables at all. If creditors collect from movables, why should they not divide proportionally according to Rav Chanina's approach? The Rif's reason for equal division is that the lien of the creditor owed one hundred attaches just as fully as the lien of the creditor owed two hundred. That reasoning applies to land, where a lien truly attaches. With movables, however, no asset lien takes effect and there is no law of priority. Collection is only because repayment of a debt is a mitzvah and beit din compels the debtor to pay even from the garment on his shoulders. The mitzvah is upon the debtor to repay. If he owes one creditor one hundred and another two hundred, and he possesses two hundred gold coins, is the mitzvah really that he should pay one creditor his entire two hundred and the other only one hundred? That does not accord with ordinary reason. One would expect the mitzvah of repayment to require division according to the relative amounts of the loans, since no proprietary lien has attached to the movables. One might with difficulty say that the debtor's person itself is encumbered, and with respect to the personal obligation the lien for one hundred attaches just as the lien for two hundred does. After death, once the Geonim enacted that movables should answer for debts, perhaps they treated the movables as though the Torah lien itself attached to them. This requires study. I have not found the authorities discussing the matter in terms of a lien on the debtor's person. They consistently describe the lien as attaching to the property. The matter therefore still requires study.
