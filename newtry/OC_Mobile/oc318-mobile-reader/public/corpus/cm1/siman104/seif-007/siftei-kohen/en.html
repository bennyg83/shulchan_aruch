He borrowed and wrote for him, etc. The Rambam's wording appears to address a first loan containing an after-acquired-property clause, followed by a second loan without such a clause, and property acquired only afterward. Bedek HaBayit and the Bach infer from the Rosh and Baal HaTerumot that the first creditor alone takes the property. Tzeror HaKesef, however, rules that while the property remains in the debtor's hands both creditors share equally. The first creditor's clause matters only if the debtor later sells the property, for he may then collect from purchasers whereas the second creditor may not. In my view, Tzeror HaKesef is correct. As long as the land remains with the debtor, the personal obligation makes it available to both creditors once acquired. Rabbenu Yerucham states explicitly that where one borrowed from two creditors and afterward acquired property, both share equally, even if the first received an after-acquired-property clause and the second did not, provided the property remains with the debtor. If it was sold, only the creditor whose document contains the clause may collect from purchasers. His separate ruling that a creditor may sometimes take all the improvement where his clause preceded the purchaser's clause is not contradictory. Improvement in a purchaser's hands is treated as though the seller acquired and resold it, so the competition is over property already transferred away from the debtor. The Rosh himself explains it in precisely those terms. Baal HaTerumot's language that one without such a clause cannot “collect” is likewise best understood as collection from purchasers. Accordingly, the controlling rule is this: whenever both creditors had some right in the property at the moment the debtor acquired it, they are equal when collecting from the debtor himself. If the debtor then transfers it, only the creditor whose document reaches after-acquired property may collect from the transferee. Thus, even if the second creditor alone has such a clause, both share while the property remains with the debtor, but only the second may pursue purchasers. Conversely, if the first creditor had no such clause, the debtor then acquired land, and only afterward borrowed from a second creditor, the first and second share in property still held by the debtor, because the first had a personal right of collection from the moment of acquisition. The second alone may collect from purchasers if his document contains the appropriate clause.
