And afterward he acquired the field and then borrowed again, etc. This is the Rambam's wording. The Bedek HaBayit and Kesef Mishneh write that at first glance it implies that the reason the later creditor has priority is specifically because the debtor acquired the field before the second loan. If he acquired it only after both loans, even if he wrote an after acquired property clause to one creditor but not the other, they would divide. They cite the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. Shmuel says that a creditor collects the increased value of property. The Rosh asks why Shmuel does not say that the creditor collects only half the increased value, and answers that no single formulation would cover every case. If the debtor wrote an after acquired property clause to the creditor but not to the purchaser, the creditor collects all of the increase. This shows that the Rosh holds that even where the property was acquired later, the party who has the after acquired property clause takes all. The Baal HaTerumot rules the same way, and the Beit Yosef accepts his view as law. The Bach likewise agrees. The Shach attacks this conclusion at length and says there is no proof from the Rosh. Otherwise Rabbenu Yerucham would contradict himself. Rabbenu Yerucham expressly rules that where one creditor has an after acquired property clause and the other does not, they divide, yet regarding increased value he writes that if the creditor has an after acquired clause and the purchaser does not, the creditor takes all the increase. The Shach therefore seeks to distinguish the law of increased value. The authorities treat increase as though it had been in the debtor's possession and then sold. In such a case there is no unencumbered property, only property in the hands of a purchaser, and the creditor whose document contains an after acquired clause alone may seize it. His distinction is neither clear nor convincing. The authorities' comparison of increased value to property that came into the debtor's hands and was sold is stated for the purchaser's benefit, to require an after acquired clause before the creditor may collect the increase. It cannot reasonably be used to worsen the purchaser's position. The purchaser himself labored and expended effort to create the increase, and the increase is presently in his possession. If the same increase were still in the debtor's possession, the purchaser would, according to this view, receive half. It is difficult to say that merely because the property is now in the purchaser's own hands his position becomes worse and he receives nothing. Torah reasoning does not support that result, and the Shach's lengthy discussion is unnecessary. In truth, the Rosh's statement proves nothing for a different reason. Since we rule that a lien is of Torah origin, if Reuven owes Shimon and afterward acquires a field, then throughout the period Reuven remains indebted, any property he acquires immediately becomes subject to the debt. Why should the Torah lien take effect only at the precise instant the loan is made? The borrower remains a servant to the lender until payment. Whenever land comes into his possession, the creditor's Torah lien attaches. Otherwise, if property acquired later were not subject to the Torah lien merely because it was absent at the time of the loan, it would be like movables, and just as movables of heirs were not subject to creditors under the original law, later acquired land should likewise not be collectible from heirs. Yet we rule that land acquired after the loan is collected from heirs. This proves that the Torah lien continually attaches while the debt remains outstanding. Tosafot in Bekhorot, Perek Yesh Bekhor, explicitly write in the discussion of redeeming a firstborn son that if property was acquired only after the redemption obligation arose, the issue is not one of an after acquired clause. As soon as the property was acquired, the redemption obligation attached to it, for that obligation has the status of a written debt. They speak of a written debt because the discussion there concerns collection from encumbered property. For unencumbered property, an ordinary creditor likewise needs no written note for the Torah lien to attach when property is acquired. Accordingly, suppose the debtor borrowed from Reuven in Nisan and wrote him an after acquired property clause, then borrowed from Shimon in Iyar without such a clause, and acquired the field in Sivan. Reuven's lien could not attach before the field existed, according to many authorities, as will be discussed below. When the field is acquired in Sivan, Shimon's lien likewise attaches immediately by Torah law. The difference is only that Reuven has the status of a written creditor as to this field, while Shimon has only the status of an oral creditor, as is clear from the Bava Batra discussion of after acquired property. Since below in seif 13 we rule that, with respect to unencumbered property, an oral creditor and a written creditor stand equally, both liens attach to this field together and they divide. Thus the Rambam's apparent implication is correct. This reasoning applies if an oral and written creditor are equal regarding unencumbered property. According to a view that even there a written creditor has priority, then certainly the creditor with the after acquired clause has priority, because as to him the claim is written while the other is only oral. The authorities state this throughout, and as explained below in seif 13, according to the view that liens are not of Torah origin a written creditor has priority. There would be no need to state the point separately. The Rosh's statement concerning Shmuel therefore poses no difficulty. The Rosh sought only to explain why Shmuel could say broadly that a creditor collects increased value rather than half. Shmuel himself holds that liens are not of Torah origin and that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. On his view, a written creditor has priority, while no asset lien attaches at all without an after acquired clause, even against heirs. Therefore a creditor with an after acquired clause can indeed take all the increase. The Rosh's formulation does not determine the law according to our ruling that liens are of Torah origin. However, this does not fully resolve Rabbenu Yerucham. He rules as law that, in the increased value case, a creditor with an after acquired clause takes all the increase, yet in the cited Netiv he also rules that an earlier oral creditor precedes a later written creditor. That shows he holds liens are of Torah origin, as stated above. It appears that Rabbenu Yerucham understands the seller's warranty obligation as follows. If a person sold land in Nisan with warranty and the purchaser was dispossessed in Tishrei, the seller does not retroactively become indebted to the purchaser for the purchase money from Nisan. If that were so, once the sale was nullified by dispossession the purchase money would retroactively become a loan, and the purchaser's consumption of produce and increase before dispossession would resemble ribbit. Rather, the seller's monetary obligation arises only from the time of dispossession onward. What he does in Nisan is to encumber his property from that time to secure the liability that may arise later in Tishrei. A person can encumber property today for an obligation that will arise later, as Rashi writes in Ketubot 19b, s.v. edim. See what I wrote above in siman 39, seif 13 in the gloss. This is conceptually correct. Accordingly, when property later comes into the debtor's hands before dispossession, the purchaser has no ordinary Torah lien on it because the seller is not yet indebted to him. Only an expressly created warranty lien can reach backward to that property. If the purchaser's document does not contain an after acquired clause, no lien can attach until the day of dispossession, by which time the creditor's lien has already attached to the increase. This explains Rabbenu Yerucham. With two ordinary creditors, one with an after acquired clause and one without, property acquired later becomes subject to both at the moment of acquisition, as stated, and they divide. With a purchaser's increase, however, the seller owes the purchaser nothing until dispossession. The creditor's lien attaches to the increase earlier, while the purchaser has no lien unless his warranty document expressly extends to after acquired property. If it does not, his claim arises only later, after the creditor's lien has already attached. The creditor therefore takes the whole increase. This is clear and correct without distortion, and there is no contradiction in Rabbenu Yerucham. There remains a difficulty in Rabbenu Yerucham's own view. In the cited Netiv, part 8, he holds that an after acquired property clause is a scribal presumption. If so, a document without such a clause exists only where the parties expressly excluded it, as the Sema writes below in siman 115. But then how can the creditor collect even from unencumbered property later acquired by the debtor? The debtor expressly waived the lien on after acquired property, and monetary conditions are valid. It is strained to say that the waiver applies only against purchasers and not to unencumbered property. This requires study. In any event, there is no contradiction to the Rambam's apparent rule, and Rabbenu Yerucham explicitly supports equal division except in the increased value setting. The Baal HaTerumot, however, in sha'ar 43, part 4, din 11, writes: if one borrowed from Reuven and afterward from Levi, wrote both an after acquired property clause, and it is established that only after both loans he acquired land or movables, then if he wrote to the first creditor both an agav clause and an after acquired clause, while writing nothing to the second, the first collects, because the property became encumbered to him either from the original time or at least from the time of acquisition, while to the second it was never encumbered. One who lacks agav and an after acquired clause cannot collect at all against one whose lien attached first. This is the source from which the Beit Yosef derived the rule that the creditor with the after acquired clause alone collects. The Shach tries to reject this proof by saying the Baal HaTerumot speaks only after the later acquired property was sold. In that case, clearly only the creditor with an after acquired clause can collect from purchasers. The Shach infers this from the verb "collects by seizure," which often describes collection from encumbered property. This is a very weak inference. The Baal HaTerumot says only by way of an a fortiori argument that if a creditor cannot collect from a purchaser who acquired later, he certainly cannot displace a creditor whose lien is earlier. According to the Shach's reading, the central factual element, that the debtor sold the property, is entirely missing from the text. Moreover, this entire sha'ar is devoted to clarifying the law of after acquired property. It would be extraordinary for the Baal HaTerumot to omit the fundamental case where the later acquired field remains in the debtor's hands and one creditor has an after acquired clause while the other does not. The Shach's rejection therefore cannot stand. Still, the Baal HaTerumot's wording admits several possible explanations. He repeatedly speaks of one creditor having "agav and after acquired property." Why is agav needed at all? If the purpose is simply to include movables, he could state the rule for land, from which we would automatically know that movables encumbered together with land follow the same law. Gidulei Terumah also notes that the passage begins by saying both creditors had after acquired clauses but ends by discussing a case where only one did. One possibility is that the phrase "agav and after acquired property" relates to money received by the debtor from a purchaser. If the debtor sells the later acquired field, the creditor can say: "The money you received from the purchaser is encumbered to me through the agav clause. Give me that money." Money can be encumbered through agav like other movables, as stated in seif 5. This is especially strong where the document also contains an after acquired clause, so the debtor cannot say the sale proceeds entered his hands only later. If the debtor gave away the money, the creditor could certainly collect from the recipient. What difference should it make that the debtor used the money to purchase land? If the creditor cannot collect the entire field directly, he can demand the encumbered purchase money. The seller who received the money would then have to return it, and in turn would reclaim the field from the debtor because his sale becomes a mistake: he never intended to sell his own land without receiving usable consideration that would remain his rather than be immediately taken by the debtor's creditor. It would be pointless to reverse the transactions one after another. The creditor can therefore proceed directly against the field purchased with money already encumbered to him through agav and after acquired property. This reasoning is plausible, and I found a similar principle in Responsa Maharit, part 2, Choshen Mishpat siman 8. Still, it is difficult to create a new law on this basis, and more difficult to say that this is the Baal HaTerumot's intended meaning without his explicitly distinguishing a field purchased with money from a field received by inheritance or gift. In the latter cases the reasoning would not apply, and according to the Rambam the creditors would divide. However, none of this is necessary. In sha'ar 61 the Baal HaTerumot cites Rav Hai Gaon, who holds that an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan regarding unencumbered property. The Baal HaTerumot says the scribes corrupted Rav Hai's text and that his ruling is mistaken, and he accepts instead the Rif's rule that a written loan, even if later, precedes an earlier oral loan. See there. Accordingly, in the Baal HaTerumot's case, even if both liens first attach to the field at the same moment of acquisition, the creditor whose document includes the after acquired clause has the status of a written creditor, while the other has only the status of an oral creditor with respect to that field. Since the Baal HaTerumot holds that a later written creditor has priority over an earlier oral creditor, certainly the creditor with the after acquired clause has priority in every such case. This is straightforward and entirely resolves the Baal HaTerumot. The Mechaber, however, rules below in seif 13 like Rav Hai Gaon that the earlier oral loan has priority. According to that view, in the present case the creditors should divide because both liens attach upon acquisition and the oral creditor is not disadvantaged relative to a written creditor regarding unencumbered property. As stated above, this itself is disputed among the authorities. Rabbenu Yerucham and Tzeror HaKesef therefore appear to support equal division. The Shach, however, treats the matter as though no dispute exists and everyone agrees to equal division. I maintain that there are additional authorities supporting the opposite result. The Rashba holds that one who grants an after acquired property lien may retract it before the property comes into existence. His proof is from the rule that where a person borrowed, borrowed again, and then acquired property, the creditors divide. The Rashba understands an after acquired clause as creating its lien immediately from the time it is written. If so, why should the creditors divide after the property is later acquired? It must be that when the debtor later writes an after acquired clause to the second creditor, he partially retracts the exclusivity of his earlier clause and intends that both creditors share the property. The Mordekhai likewise explains the equal division as resulting from partial retraction, and see what I wrote above in siman 112. The Ri Migash in his Bava Batra novellae also appears to explain the law through retraction, and the Rashbam on Bava Batra 157b, s.v. ve'im timtzei lomar, appears to do the same. The Ramban in his Bava Batra novellae concerning one who bought a cow and garment likewise implies that the after acquired lien can attach from the original time. According to all these authorities, the reason for equal division when both creditors have after acquired clauses is specifically that the debtor later retracted the first creditor's exclusive position by granting the same clause to the second. If he wrote an after acquired clause only to one creditor and not the other, there was no retraction. On the contrary, by omitting it from the second loan he confirmed the first creditor's exclusive lien. The creditor with the clause therefore takes the entire later acquired property. Even if these authorities also hold that an earlier oral loan ordinarily precedes a later written loan, they nevertheless agree with the Beit Yosef and Baal HaTerumot in this particular after acquired property case. There is therefore a major dispute among the authorities, not a universally accepted rule of equal division. Returning to the Rambam, according to what was explained above, if the debtor borrowed without an after acquired property clause, acquired property, and only afterward borrowed again, then even if he gives the second creditor an after acquired clause, the first creditor should have priority while the field remains in the debtor's hands. The first creditor's Torah lien attached immediately when the field was acquired, and he is then like an earlier oral creditor. The second creditor's later document is a written obligation. Since we rule that an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan against unencumbered property, the first creditor should prevail. The Sema in seif katan 15 writes that the second creditor prevails because a written loan is treated like a sale, while a creditor lacking an after acquired clause cannot remove property from encumbered hands. This reasoning is not correct according to the opinion that an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan. If a written loan were literally equivalent to a sale, an earlier oral creditor should never be able to collect against it. The very rule that the earlier oral loan can prevail shows that we do not treat the written loan as a full sale in this context. The Shach tries to answer that if the debtor had no property when he borrowed, property acquired later never becomes subject to the original creditor's lien at all. The creditor collects it while it remains in the debtor's hands only because repayment is a mitzvah, not by reason of a property lien. His words are not persuasive, for the reason stated above. If no lien ever attached, how could that later acquired property be collected from heirs? The Bava Batra Gemara connects collection from heirs with whether an oral loan is collectible from heirs. According to the Shach there would be no connection: an ordinary oral loan against existing property could have a Torah lien, while later acquired property would have none. Gidulei Terumah is therefore correct that the law depends upon the dispute whether an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan. Were it not for the Maggid Mishneh, I would have said that the Rambam himself holds that a later written loan precedes an earlier oral loan, despite also holding that liens are of Torah origin. He could follow the Baal HaTerumot's reasoning that a written obligation resembles a purchaser. One would then ask: if the debtor borrowed from two creditors, wrote an after acquired clause to one but not the other, and afterward acquired the field, why does the Rambam apparently hold they divide? The creditor with the clause is a written creditor and should have priority. The answer could be based on concern for closing the door before lenders. When property was acquired only after both loans, no lender could reasonably have relied upon that future property when making the loan. There is therefore no policy reason to prefer the written creditor. Both liens first encounter the property at acquisition, and they divide. But where the debtor borrowed, then acquired a known field, and only afterward took a second written loan secured by that field, the later lender extended credit in reliance on an existing asset. Concern for closing the door before lenders therefore supports giving the second written creditor priority over the earlier oral creditor. This would reconcile the Rambam. With this principle in mind, I initially thought to explain the Baal HaTerumot's precise wording. He says that where the first creditor has an after acquired clause and the second does not, the first takes the property. Why does he choose the first creditor? If the second had the clause and the first did not, both documents were written before the property was acquired. What difference should it make whether the clause appears in the first or second note? Perhaps the Baal HaTerumot holds like the Sema that a written lien is analogous to a sale. In an ordinary sale, although we rule that liens are of Torah origin and an earlier oral creditor should theoretically collect even from purchasers who bought later, the Sages protected purchasers because an oral loan lacks publicity. That protection does not apply to after acquired property on which the lender did not rely at the time of the loan. Accordingly, if the first creditor has an after acquired clause, it is as though the future field was already sold to him. When the debtor later borrows from the second creditor, even though the second creditor's Torah lien attaches when the field is acquired, the field has already been committed to the first creditor and there is nothing left for the second. If, however, the first creditor lacks the clause and the second has it, the first creditor's oral lien may already have attached at the moment of acquisition, and the later written loan may not displace it where there is no closing the door concern regarding property not yet acquired. This is conceptually attractive. But it is difficult to say that the Baal HaTerumot intended such a distinction without stating it, especially in a sha'ar devoted to all the detailed laws of after acquired property. More likely, his phrase "the first" simply means "one of them," and a scribal error substituted "the first" for "one." In that case the passage does not prove a difference based on which creditor received the clause. The Maggid Mishneh, however, explicitly writes that the Rambam holds an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan. The original difficulty therefore remains. There is another difficulty. Suppose the Rambam truly holds that if the debtor borrowed without an after acquired clause, acquired property, and then borrowed again, the later creditor gets the field, as the Sema understands; but if he borrowed from two creditors first, wrote an after acquired clause to only one, and then acquired property, they divide. The Gemara's rule that later dated documents are valid when an after acquired clause is written becomes problematic. Perhaps the debtor borrowed from both creditors in Nisan, wrote an after acquired clause only to one, and acquired the property in Iyar. Under the assumed law, both creditors share the field. If the document containing the after acquired clause is then intentionally dated in Sivan, the record makes it appear that the sequence was: first an oral type loan without after acquired property, then acquisition of the field, then a later written loan with the clause. The later creditor would then appear entitled to the whole field, though in truth he owned only half. How could the Sages permit antedating or postdating arrangements that create such a distortion? One might answer that whenever the law is equal division, if one creditor collected the whole amount first his collection stands. There is therefore no substantive injustice if the altered document enables him to obtain the whole field. He could have achieved that result lawfully by collecting first. The Ran makes the same point in Perek HaKotev concerning "give it to the one whose proof is weakest." He asks why the Sages allow a rule that disadvantages the earlier creditor and answers that because if the later creditor collected first the collection stands, the Sages were not concerned to prevent every route to that result. See there. This answer requires some study, because the Mordekhai at the end of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui appears not to accept such reasoning. Otherwise his question and proof would lose their force. See what I wrote above in siman 112. The Beit Yosef himself writes that the Rambam's wording does not conclusively prove equal division in the case where only one creditor has an after acquired clause. It can be read consistently with the Baal HaTerumot, that the creditor with the clause takes all. This is, in my view, the more accurate conclusion. To explain the remaining sources, note the Baal HaTerumot in sha'ar 61, citing a Gaon, who writes that a document lacking an after acquired clause nevertheless allows collection from heirs after the debtor later acquires and leaves the property to them. The Gaon compares this to the later Geonic enactment making heirs' movables subject to debts. Shmuel's requirement of an after acquired clause for inherited property is only because Shmuel holds an oral loan is not collectible from heirs. The passage initially speaks in terms of a Geonic enactment and then explains Shmuel's Talmudic position, which requires clarification. The Gaon's intent is that under Talmudic law itself, according to the opinion that an oral loan is collected from heirs, later acquired land is likewise collected from heirs even without an express after acquired clause. Yet the Gaon holds that no ordinary lien actually attaches to the later acquired asset, because the loan date alone creates the lien, not subsequent acquisition. Otherwise, once the field entered the debtor's possession while the debt remained outstanding, a Torah lien would immediately attach, and a creditor holding a written loan should even be able to collect it from purchasers: the loan itself already had publicity, so the usual concern that a hidden oral loan lacks notice would not apply. The fact that an after acquired clause is still required against purchasers shows that no ordinary property lien arises automatically. How, then, can heirs be charged? The answer is that the Sages enacted liability of later acquired land against heirs, just as the Geonim later enacted liability of movables against heirs. The Gaon's comparison to the Geonic enactment is therefore not merely historical but conceptual: just as the Geonim placed heirs' movables within the collection system despite the absence of a Torah lien, the Sages had already placed later acquired land within the collection system against heirs, to preserve the ordinary rules of creditor collection without distinction. This is the meaning of the passage. The responsum of the Raavad cited by the Tur later in this siman, letter 15, should be understood similarly. Reuven borrowed orally from Yaakov and died. Reuven's son Shimon then borrowed on a written document. When both claims come for collection, Shimon's written creditor precedes Reuven's earlier oral creditor, because a later written creditor precedes an earlier oral one. But if Shimon took his written loan during Reuven's lifetime and the document contained an after acquired clause, then after Reuven dies, Reuven's earlier oral creditor precedes Shimon's written creditor. At first glance this is astonishing. What difference is there whether Shimon borrowed before or after Reuven's death? If Shimon's document contains an after acquired clause, then at the instant of Reuven's death the inherited field should become subject to Shimon's written lien. Why should Reuven's oral creditor then have priority? Therefore, it appears that the Raavad follows the Rashbam's view in the sugya of after acquired property. According to the Sages, one cannot transfer something not yet in existence. The reason a person can nevertheless encumber after acquired property is that the Sages strengthened a creditor's lien so that borrowers would not be denied access to credit. Thus, under the strict law an after acquired clause should be ineffective; its validity is rabbinic and rests on the policy against closing the door before borrowers. The Raavad can then also follow the authorities who explain that a later written loan precedes an earlier oral loan because the lien of an oral loan is rabbinic when one holds liens are not of Torah origin, whereas an expressly written lien has Torah force. A Torah obligation displaces a merely rabbinic one. If Shimon borrows only after his father has died, the inherited field is already his and the written lien attaches directly. His creditor therefore precedes the earlier oral creditor of the father. But if Shimon borrowed during his father's lifetime and can reach the inheritance only through an after acquired clause, then his right to the future field is itself only rabbinic, because the property did not yet exist in his ownership. Reuven's oral creditor likewise has only a rabbinic claim. One rabbinic basis is no stronger than the other. The claims are then like two oral loans, and the earlier one precedes the later. The Raavad's distinction is thereby resolved. The Rambam may hold both propositions: an after acquired lien is fundamentally rabbinic, and later acquired property collectible from heirs without such a clause is likewise subject only by enactment, not because a full lien automatically attaches. The Raah and Ritva at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui explain the Gemara's initial possibility that a later creditor who collected first keeps what he collected. Tosafot ask that an earlier creditor can even collect from a purchaser. They answer that with a purchaser there is a special concern for closing the door before lenders, but between two creditors there is no reason to prefer one lender's access to credit over the other's. "Why is this creditor different from that creditor?" Thus policy can strengthen one type of transaction without making it universally equivalent to Torah law. Accordingly, if a debtor borrowed without an after acquired clause, later acquired a field, and afterward borrowed from a second creditor, the second creditor can have priority, as the Sema says. The first creditor has no actual lien in the later acquired property, only the rabbinic right to collect from heirs or from the debtor under the general collection rules. The second creditor takes a genuine lien upon an existing field. He therefore prevails. If, however, the debtor borrowed from two creditors before acquiring the field, gave an after acquired clause to one but not the other, and later acquired the field, the creditor with the clause has at least the special rabbinic lien established to prevent closing the door, while the other creditor has no such lien at all. The former therefore takes the field. The Rambam's case of borrowing, acquiring, and then borrowing teaches a greater novelty. One might have thought that because an after acquired clause is only a rabbinic strengthening designed to protect access to credit, it should operate only against later purchasers, but not against a later creditor whose lien on an existing asset is of Torah force. Why should policy favor the first lender over the second? The Rambam teaches that the Sages made no such distinction. Once they strengthened the after acquired lien, they treated it like a full lien in all these respects, and the first creditor with the clause retains priority even against a later creditor. This is coherent and precise. Having clarified all this, most of the authorities support the Beit Yosef and Bach rather than Rabbenu Yerucham and Tzeror HaKesef. The creditor with the after acquired clause should therefore collect the entire later acquired property. There is an additional practical reason to rule this way. According to the opposing view, the creditors divide. Thus the creditor with the after acquired clause is certainly entitled to half, and the dispute concerns only the other half. This resembles the case of a definite heir and a doubtful yavam discussed in Perek HaCholetz, where because the yavam is certainly entitled to one half and has a claim to the other half, we say that a doubtful claimant cannot remove property from one whose entitlement is definite, and he takes all. Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Metzia ask why, in the case of two people holding a garment, one claimant should not receive three quarters by the same reasoning. See their answers and the additional explanations in Shitah Mekubetzet. According to most of those explanations, especially the strongest one that in Bava Metzia each claimant asserts a definite claim and we cannot use the rule of definite versus doubtful to extract from one who himself asserts certainty, or that the one who says "half is mine" could have claimed the entire garment and should not lose merely because he admitted the other half, the reasoning does not apply here. The Ritva additionally answers that physical possession of the garment itself creates a presumption that each has a share. None of those answers applies here. The creditor with the after acquired clause has a definite right to half under all opinions, while his right to the remaining half depends on the dispute among the authorities. The other creditor has no corresponding definite claim against that second half. This is therefore genuinely analogous to the definite and doubtful heir case. It is clear that the creditor with the after acquired clause takes the whole field, in accordance with the Beit Yosef and Bach.
