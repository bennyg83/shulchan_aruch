And he wrote to him, 'Whatever I will acquire in the future,' etc. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length. If one borrowed without writing an after acquired property clause, then acquired property, and afterward borrowed by note from a second lender, the property is encumbered only to the later lender, as implied by Shulchan Aruch and Rambam and as Sema writes, unlike Shach. If he borrowed twice, writing an after acquired property clause to one creditor but not the other, and only afterward acquired property that remains free in his possession, Beit Yosef and Bach hold that it is encumbered solely to the creditor whose note contains the clause. Shach disagrees and holds that they divide. He has support among the authorities, including Rabbeinu Yeruham and Tzeror HaKesef. Nevertheless, I concluded in Tumim that Beit Yosef's position is primary, and the property is encumbered solely to the creditor with the after acquired property clause. Certainly this applies to improvements in the hands of a purchaser. If the creditor has an after acquired property clause and the purchaser does not, the creditor takes the entire improvement. All the more so if the creditor's note contains both agav and after acquired property language and the field was purchased with money, for the creditor may argue that the purchase money itself was encumbered to him. See there.
The field is encumbered, etc. If he took two oral loans and only afterward acquired the field, the two creditors likewise divide. Even if the second loan was documented and the note expressly stated that after acquired property would not be encumbered, we do not say that by writing this he waived even his rights against free property. Rather, the exclusion removes his lien only with respect to property in third party hands. Against property still in the debtor's possession, his basic creditor's right remains. I proved this in Tumim from Rabbeinu Yeruham. See there.
