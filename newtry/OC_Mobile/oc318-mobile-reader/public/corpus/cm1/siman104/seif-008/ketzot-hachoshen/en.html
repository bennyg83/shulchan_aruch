The one who first collects acquires. Maharshal Damascene ruled that where a father owed money to his son and also to other creditors, and then died, the son as heir is treated like a creditor who first collected. Because the inherited property is already presumed in his hands as heir, he may retain it for his own debt even without a new act of seizure. Maharit rejects this. I agree with Maharit. Rabbi Yonah proves that mere possession of land does not count as collection from the rule that a creditor takes half the purchaser's improvement even though the purchaser is physically holding the improvement. A purchaser's possession of land is at least as strong as an heir's. Yet it is not treated as prior collection. Therefore mere inheritance does not allow the heir creditor to defeat other creditors. Land requires actual legal collection, not merely the fact that the heir presently holds title or possession.

The Shach asks how the Shulchan Aruch can say that two debt instruments dated the same day have equal priority and divide, since Ran's rationale for equality depends upon his view that oral loans have no priority. But the rule is an explicit Mishnah: where all documents emerge at one time and only one hundred is available, they divide equally. Ran explains Rif's distinction between ordinary debt instruments and gift documents. With gifts, Shmuel can apply shuda deDayanei because perhaps one document was truly delivered first. With debts, Ran says the lien begins only from the time shown by the document, so the creditors are legally equal. Tosafot in the name of Rabbeinu Tam gives a different reason. Where it is possible that one recipient truly came first and the other is false, shuda can be used. But with two creditors or two ketubah holders dated the same day, the Sages made them divide so that creditors should not suffer complete loss and women should not lose ketubah rights. This approach allows actual earlier lending to exist even within the same day. Therefore the Shulchan Aruch's rule does not depend on denying priority to oral loans. It can follow Rabbeinu Tam's policy rationale. The same resolves the dispute over an instruction, "I owe money to Tuviah," when several people named Tuviah exist. Ran says there is no useful estimate of whom the debtor favored, because people often accept loans or deposits even from persons they do not especially love. Under Rashi's theory of shuda based on probable intent, that removes shuda. Rabbeinu Tam, who allows judicial choice independent of such estimates, would ordinarily use shuda, but where the matter is a debt the policy against causing a creditor total loss leads to division. Thus Rama's rule that debt claimants named Tuviah divide can be accepted under both approaches, though for different reasons. Deposits may differ under Rabbeinu Tam because the anti creditor loss policy does not necessarily apply in the same way.
