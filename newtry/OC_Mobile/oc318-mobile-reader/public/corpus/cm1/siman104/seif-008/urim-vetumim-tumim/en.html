If he had many creditors, etc. The Ran in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui explains why we do not apply judicial discretion in this case as we do where two deeds of sale or gift bear the same date. Here judicial discretion is inapplicable because even if the debtor borrowed from one creditor in the morning and from the other in the evening, their liens take effect together, since a lien expressed only by the date of the document does not take effect until the end of the day. Even with creditors, since the precise time is not evident from the document, the earlier loan is treated like an oral loan for purposes of priority, and as I explained above, when an earlier and a later oral loan come to collect, the creditors divide. Therefore, with ordinary loan documents and acknowledgments they certainly divide, unless the documents themselves effect a kinyan, for then the debtor bound himself from the time of the kinyan. The Mishnah does not discuss such a case, and even Rav would agree that judicial discretion applies there. The Shach asks that according to our ruling below in seif 13 that an earlier oral loan has priority over a later written loan, judicial discretion should likewise apply to ordinary loan documents, and he leaves the matter requiring study. I also find difficulty in the Mechaber's unqualified ruling. Why did he not explain that if there was a kinyan in the documents we do not divide, but use judicial discretion, as the Ran states? There is a further difficulty in the Ran's explanation. How does he understand the Mishnah in Ketubot, which under Rabbi Meir says that wives whose ketubot bear the same date divide equally? A marriage and chuppah are themselves comparable to a kinyan, because the ketubah obligation arises by enactment of beit din and the written ketubah is merely evidence of that obligation. Yet the Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer all rule that they divide equally. Perhaps according to the Rif, who holds that where it is customary to write a ketubah the husband can plead payment unless she possesses the document, one may say that the lien does not arise until the ketubah is written. Still, this is strained. Maharsha explains Tosafot like the Ran, whereas Tosafot disagree with the Rif and hold that even where ketubot are customarily written the document itself is unnecessary. This requires study, unless Tosafot's case concerns a husband who wrote the ketubah while she was still betrothed. The Shach's question is likewise difficult against the Rif. The Rif challenges the authorities who hold that Shmuel's rule of judicial discretion applies even to loan documents, arguing that the Gemara should then have challenged Shmuel from the Mishnah that where all the documents bear the same date the creditors divide. But what is the difficulty? Those authorities may follow Rav Hai Gaon, who holds that an earlier oral creditor precedes a later oral creditor. If so, even with loan documents one creditor's lien could have arisen before the other's, and judicial discretion would be appropriate. This answer, however, works only if liens are d'oraisa, so that even an oral loan creates a lien and the earlier one has priority. According to the view that liens are not d'oraisa, an oral loan has no priority at all, as I wrote above. Shmuel himself holds that liens are not d'oraisa, so even those authorities would agree that ordinary loan documents whose exact time is unknown should be divided, and the Mishnah presents no difficulty. It therefore appears that the Rif's reasoning depends on the Gemara's discussion that if the witnesses who sign the deed effect the transaction we divide, while Shmuel holds that the witnesses to delivery effect it. See the Ran in the chapter HaMegaresh, who asks how this can fit the Rif, since according to the Rif witnesses to delivery are still required, and see his answer there. The essential point is that Shmuel's judicial discretion is tied to the need for witnesses to delivery. I wrote at length about this above in siman 51. The Rosh writes in the first chapter of Gittin that loan documents are merely evidentiary instruments, and even Rabbi Elazar agrees that they do not require witnesses to delivery. Accordingly, with such documents division follows automatically. But the Rosh's statement that loan documents are merely evidentiary and therefore need no witnesses to delivery is true only according to the view that liens are d'oraisa, for then the document merely proves and publicizes an already existing lien. If liens are not d'oraisa and the lien arises only through the document, then the witnesses themselves create a property right. For Rabbi Elazar, signing witnesses cannot create such a kinyan or lien without delivery witnesses. Thus, where liens are not d'oraisa, loan documents themselves require witnesses to delivery. The Rif's challenge can therefore be stated as a dilemma. If those authorities hold liens are d'oraisa, then all agree that signing witnesses suffice for a mere evidentiary instrument and the rule should be division. If they hold liens are not d'oraisa, then witnesses to delivery are required and their reliance on judicial discretion is understandable, but the Mishnah becomes a direct difficulty because it nevertheless requires division. This also explains Tosafot's discussion in Ketubot. They ask how Shmuel can hold both that witnesses to signing acquire retroactively and that witnesses to delivery effect the deed. They then note that the same apparent contradiction exists in Abaye. One might ask why they needed to establish Shmuel's position first, since Abaye against Abaye is already a strong question. According to what I have written, however, Abaye could hold that liens are d'oraisa. In evidentiary loan documents he would then not require witnesses to delivery, so no contradiction would arise. The main difficulty is Shmuel, who holds that liens are not d'oraisa and would therefore require delivery witnesses even for loan documents. According to our final ruling that liens are d'oraisa, all agree that signing witnesses suffice for loan documents, and the rule that the creditors divide is therefore clear without relying on the Ran's rationale. There is, however, an even more straightforward explanation. The rule of judicial discretion, as I wrote above in siman 240, depends on estimating which recipient the transferor most likely intended to favor, since the same field cannot fully belong to both. With loans, by contrast, it is entirely ordinary for a person who owns only one field to borrow repeatedly from different people, nearby or distant. There is therefore no meaningful basis to estimate whom he intended to favor. Where no judicial estimate can be made, all agree that division is used. This accords with the Yerushalmi, which challenges Shmuel from the Mishnah that says they divide equally and answers, in substance, that Shmuel spoke only where judicial discretion is possible. See also the Ran, who writes that the Mishnah concerns a case where no decisive preference can be established. In loan cases, according to this explanation, no such preference can ever be established. It is possible that the Rif himself meant this. Indeed, the converse may explain his challenge: if division here results from irreducible doubt, the division must be equal, regardless of the respective amounts of the debts. If one creditor is owed one hundred and another two hundred, and a sum of money is genuinely in doubt between them, we do not divide the doubtful property in proportion to the amounts of their claims. This would explain the Rif's difficulty with Rav Natan's view that division follows the amounts. In any event, the foregoing explanation of ordinary loans is sound. More plausibly still, the Mechaber may principally follow Rabbenu Tam, who explains that same date creditors divide because of the concern that otherwise people will refrain from lending, and a woman's ketubah because of marital favor. On the strict law, judicial discretion would apply, consistent with the explanation above. This too removes the question. One might add that according to Rabbenu Tam, the concern that a person will refrain from lending because perhaps another document of the same day exists arises only where such competing claims have no public notice. If there were an earlier document from a previous day it would have a public voice, whereas a same day document does not. This suggests that an oral loan, which likewise has no public voice, should also have no priority. Thus, Rabbenu Tam may indeed hold that an earlier oral loan has no priority at collection. That is why he did not adopt Tosafot's other answer. According to our ruling that an earlier oral loan does have priority, we would not follow Rabbenu Tam's enactment based on reluctance to lend, and the Shach's difficulty returns. All of this assumes that an earlier oral creditor who is later displaced by a creditor who actually collected may remove the later creditor. But according to what I discuss below in seif 13, if the later creditor who collected retains what he collected, there is no difficulty. Where improper collection would have to be reversed, division would create an actual distortion of law, while judicial discretion might still identify the proper creditor. If, however, the later creditor who first collected is entitled to retain it, then division itself does not create an injustice, and there is no reason to prefer judicial discretion. This is correct, although the underlying ruling itself still requires study, as explained below.
