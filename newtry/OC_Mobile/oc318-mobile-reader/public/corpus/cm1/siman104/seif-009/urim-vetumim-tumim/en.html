But if the debtor sold it, etc. Suppose a creditor is owed two hundred, the debtor sold land worth one hundred, and the purchase money was paid to that creditor toward the debt. The creditor then seeks to collect the very land that was sold, on account of the remaining hundred. The Tzemach Tzedek and his son in law, the renowned Rav Gershon Ashkenazi, rule that the creditor may do so. This is also indicated by the Gemara in the chapter Almanah, which questions whether a widow who sold property for her maintenance may later recover that property for collection of her ketubah. If a creditor could never collect land sold specifically to generate funds for payment of his own debt, all the more so where the creditor himself made the sale, there would be no question. This shows that the limitation applies only when the creditor himself sold the land in a manner that gives rise to personal warranty. The Ran likewise writes that if a creditor has two distinct debts against the debtor and he sold land for payment of one debt, he cannot later recover that same land for the second debt, because he accepted responsibility for his own act. From this it follows that where the debtor made the sale, the creditor may plainly recover the land, since the rationale of accepting responsibility for one's own act does not apply. The matter is simple. The responsa Shevut Yaakov, siman 153, however, infers from the Shulchan Aruch's failure to state this precise case that where the purchaser's money was itself paid to this creditor, the creditor may not later recover the land for the balance of his debt. There is no substance to this inference against a rule supported by reason. What difference does the source of the money make? Property purchased from the debtor is regarded, for lien purposes, as though it remained in the debtor's hands, as the Sema writes in seif katan 25. Just as a creditor may repeatedly collect from the debtor where the debtor reacquires property, as is explicit in the Gemara in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui concerning the small parcel of land, so too may he collect from a purchaser. The purchaser has no better position than the debtor merely because the debtor sold him this land. There is no need for lengthy discussion. The inference from the omitted case is exceedingly weak. One can say simply that the responsum described the facts that actually occurred. Moreover, it teaches a greater novelty where a different creditor had already been paid, for one might have thought that once that creditor's debt was satisfied he implicitly transferred whatever strength he had to the purchaser, since he had nothing left to lose. The ruling teaches that we do not say so. All the more so, where the same creditor remains unpaid in part, why should we assume that he surrendered his own lien to the purchaser and weakened his own position? He plainly may collect, and there is no genuine doubt.
