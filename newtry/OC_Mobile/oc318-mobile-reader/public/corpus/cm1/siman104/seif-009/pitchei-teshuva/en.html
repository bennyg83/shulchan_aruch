But if the debtor, and a later creditor. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 153. Reuven owed Shimon two hundred zuz by note and lacked funds to pay. He sold land to Levi for one hundred and paid those one hundred to Shimon. Shimon then sought to seize that same land from Levi for the remaining one hundred that Reuven still owed him. Levi argued that because the sale proceeds were paid to Shimon on account of the debt, it is as though Shimon first foreclosed upon the land and then sold it to Levi for that one hundred. Shevut Yaakov ruled for Levi. A single creditor cannot foreclose upon the same parcel from a purchaser twice, collecting and then collecting again, because his lien on that parcel was discharged when he accepted its proceeds. He infers this from the wording of the Shulchan Aruch here, which says that a later creditor may foreclose from the purchaser, but does not say that the first creditor may again collect if the debtor still owes him a balance. The same applies when two people exchange houses: the creditor of one cannot collect both houses merely because the debt equals the combined value. This is contrary to Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 4. The Tumim, seif katan 9, disagrees with Shevut Yaakov and rules that the same creditor may seize the land for the remaining one hundred. Property acquired by the debtor is treated as though it remained in the debtor's hands, as the Sema explains. Just as a creditor may collect repeatedly from the debtor himself, as shown by the Gemara in the case of the small parcel in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui, so too he may collect from a purchaser, whose rights are no stronger than those of the debtor from whom he bought. The Tumim also rejects Shevut Yaakov's inference from the wording of the Shulchan Aruch as very weak, since the responsum from which that ruling was copied may simply have involved a later creditor, and the wording may also teach an additional point.
