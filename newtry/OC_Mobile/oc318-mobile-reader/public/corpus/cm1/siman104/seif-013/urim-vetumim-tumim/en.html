A loan evidenced by a promissory note, etc. The Shach cites many authorities who hold that a later loan evidenced by a promissory note has priority over an earlier oral loan. His statement is correct. This also appears to be the view of the Rosh, who writes in Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui that when two loan documents bear the same date the creditors divide because the lien of a document does not take effect until evening. This creates a difficulty: an oral loan's lien takes effect immediately, as the Ran writes. One must therefore say that once the documentary lien takes effect at the end of the day, it displaces the oral lien that took effect earlier that day. The Tur writes this as well. It is true that Rabbenu Tam holds that the rule of equal division between two loan documents of the same date is a special enactment. This might imply that an earlier oral loan has priority. But this can be rejected. Indeed, even if under the strict law an earlier oral loan might precede a later written loan, once we see that the Sages were concerned for closing the door before borrowers even in the exceedingly uncommon case of two written loans on the same day, as Tosafot note, and even though no one knows whether a judicial discretion rule would actually cause loss, how could they fail to be concerned that lenders would refuse to lend on written documents because an oral loan from long before could defeat them? If they feared closing the door for a difference within a single day, all the more so over a longer period. It is strained to distinguish them. Therefore, what I wrote above in Tumim, seif katan 8, that the Mechaber follows Rabbenu Tam cannot stand, because the Mechaber rules that an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan. In truth there is a substantial majority of authorities on the other side: the Rif, Ramban, Raavad, Rosh, Tur, Rabbenu Tam, the Raah in Ketubot in Perek Almanah and Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui, who explicitly follows the Rif, and the Ritva in Kiddushin, although perhaps the printed attribution there was exchanged with Tosafot Rid, because in Ketubot the Ritva writes the opposite. All of these hold that the later written loan has priority. Those holding that the earlier oral loan precedes are Rav Hai Gaon, and according to the Maggid Mishneh perhaps the Rambam, together with the Rashba, the Ritva in Ketubot, and Rabbenu Yerucham. They are a smaller group. In any event, the matter is clearly a serious dispute. I have said that the issue may depend upon the dispute between Rashi and Tosafot. The Ran and Ritva's principal proof is the rule that a widow may sell property without specifying the purpose, thereby strengthening her position. The Yerushalmi explains: if an oral creditor comes, she says, "I sold for maintenance"; if a creditor holding a promissory note comes, she says, "I sold for my ketubah." They infer that the written creditor must necessarily be later than the ketubah. Otherwise, what advantage would there be in saying that she sold for the ketubah? She could say she sold for maintenance, which is an earlier oral obligation. The Yerushalmi therefore appears to show that a later written loan precedes an earlier oral loan. The Rashba answers that the Sages treated a widow's maintenance leniently, regarding the obligation as though each day's maintenance arose only on that day. This answer itself requires study. In Gittin the Gemara says that one does not collect a wife's or daughters' maintenance from purchasers because those obligations are not fixed in amount, or according to another opinion because they are not written. According to the Rashba, there would be an additional reason: the obligation is regarded as arising only each day. How then could such maintenance ever be collected from purchasers? Yet Gittin indicates that the stated reasons are necessary even for a wife's and daughters' maintenance. One must therefore understand the Rashba's point as identical to the Gemara's reason that the amount is not fixed. Precisely because maintenance is not fixed until each day arrives, the Sages treated the obligation as arising day by day. The leniency derives from its lack of fixed amount. The Yerushalmi makes its statement concerning Rabbi Yose, who says that the widow sells without specifying the purpose and thereby strengthens her position. It says that if an oral creditor comes she says, "I sold for maintenance," while if a written creditor comes she says, "I sold for my ketubah." Rabbi Yose follows his own view in Gittin 50, where he understands the tikkun olam rule as based on obligations not being fixed. Rashi explains there that under the strict law such obligations would not be collectible from encumbered property without the enactment. On that approach one must say that no property lien attaches at all to an obligation whose amount is not fixed. If a lien did attach, it would be collectible from purchasers apart from tikkun olam. Rather, no lien takes effect, although the debtor personally must pay even from the garment on his shoulders. See what I wrote above in siman 60 regarding the Rambam's view. If no lien attaches at all, then the principle that an earlier oral loan precedes a later written loan has no application to maintenance. That priority derives from a Torah lien, while the maintenance obligation has no such lien. The Yerushalmi's rule is therefore readily explained without proving anything about an ordinary oral loan. The authorities labored unnecessarily. Even if the Rashba did not mean precisely this, the explanation is true and leaves room to clarify the matter. According to Tosafot in Gittin, however, s.v. vekhi mah, who explain the exclusion from purchasers as a tikkun olam enactment rather than absence of lien, maintenance against unencumbered property has the status of an ordinary oral debt. The authorities' inference from the Yerushalmi would then be sound. The issue thus depends upon the Rashi and Tosafot approaches. Now, there is support for the Rashba from Ketubot 96b. Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Yose dispute whether a widow who sells must specify whether she sold for maintenance or for her ketubah, or may sell without specification because that strengthens her position. The Gemara labors to identify the basis of their dispute. According to Tosafot's question, one could seemingly explain it very simply. Tosafot ask how the widow may employ an artifice against purchasers by claiming that she sold for maintenance when in truth this harms them. They answer that under the strict law maintenance too should be collectible from purchasers, and the only reason it is not is that the amount is not fixed. Therefore her conduct is not improper. If so, perhaps Rabbi Yose follows his own view in Gittin that the reason is lack of fixed amount. He may therefore leave the sale unspecified, because doing so does not unjustly harm purchasers. Rabbi Yehudah could follow the first Tanna there, who says the reason is that the obligation is unwritten and that whether it is fixed is irrelevant. On that view she must specify the purpose, because otherwise she could manipulate the characterization and cause an unlawful loss to purchasers. Why did the Gemara not say that this is their dispute? According to the Rashba's explanation, however, the matter is resolved. If Rabbi Yehudah held that the only defect of maintenance is that it is unwritten, then why would he require a widow who sells for her ketubah to write specifically, "I sold for my ketubah"? Why would she do something so foolish instead of selling for maintenance and preserving her ketubah lien against purchasers? If the case already involved collection from encumbered property, everyone would know it was for the ketubah. One must therefore use the Yerushalmi's explanation that she is concerned that a written creditor may come and, if she sold for maintenance, take the property from the purchaser. But then why does the earlier oral maintenance claim not precede the later written creditor? The Rashba's answer that maintenance is treated leniently as a day by day obligation works only according to the view that the problem is lack of fixed amount. According to the view that the sole problem is lack of writing, maintenance is like every other oral debt against unencumbered property, because writing is irrelevant there. Rabbi Yehudah must therefore also accept the lack of fixed amount rationale. Once that is so, the simple suggested distinction between Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Yose disappears. The Gemara correctly seeks another basis for their dispute. This supports the Rashba. In truth, I would offer several approaches to reconcile Rav Hai Gaon and his school, who hold that an earlier oral loan has priority. Even according to the opposing authorities, one must first understand why the widow, when a creditor arrives, should ever say, "I sold for my ketubah." Why not always write that she sold for maintenance? If sufficient property remains to collect her ketubah, what does she gain by telling the creditor that the earlier sale was for her ketubah? The creditor can simply collect from the remaining property. If he instead collects from what she sold, she can use her ketubah against the remaining property. The result is the same. If no property remains except the parcel she sold, then if she sold for maintenance and the creditor comes to remove it, she can assert her earlier ketubah and collect from the creditor. Either way she appears to lose nothing. It is strained to say that the parcel she sold had been made an express apotiki to the creditor, so that if she sold for the ketubah the later creditor could not take it because her ketubah was earlier, while the creditor could not proceed against the remaining property because he had an apotiki; whereas if she lets the creditor take the apotiki she could not then collect her ketubah because he would say, "I left you a place from which to collect," causing her to lose maintenance. It is unlikely that the widow's legal strategy was framed around the unusual possibility of an express apotiki, which has publicity, as Bava Kamma and many other passages state. Rather, she may fear that the creditor's promissory note does not contain an after acquired property clause, and the remaining land was acquired by the husband only after both the creditor's loan and the marriage and ketubah. If she tells the creditor that the parcel she sold was sold for her ketubah, then when he proceeds against the subsequently acquired remaining field, she can come against that field for maintenance. Although he has a written loan, since the field was acquired only after both obligations and his document lacks an after acquired clause, his claim against that field has only the status of an oral loan. When two obligations arise and the property is acquired later, they divide. But if she says she sold for maintenance, and the parcel sold had already belonged to the husband at the time of the creditor's loan and the ketubah, the written creditor collects that parcel, the remaining property must be used for the ketubah, and she loses her maintenance because nothing remains. The Yerushalmi's strategy is therefore intelligible. On this basis we can also reconcile Rav Hai Gaon. The oral and written creditors mentioned can both have claims arising before the marriage and ketubah. The particular parcel sold by the widow was acquired by the husband only after the marriage. Both the creditor and the ketubah can have after acquired property clauses, because we rule that such a clause is a scribal presumption. When a written creditor comes and she says, "I sold for my ketubah," he cannot say, "My debt is earlier," because with two loans followed by acquisition of the property, if the later creditor collected first, that collection stands. But if she says, "I sold for maintenance," there is no after acquired lien for maintenance, while the written creditor does have one. The written creditor therefore has priority, as explained at length above according to the Baal HaTerumot and his school. This also resolves the previous question if no other land remains. When she sold the parcel and says, "I sold for my ketubah," she has effectively collected, and what she collected remains hers. Although the Ran writes that the money may still be in the purchaser's hands, this is not compelling. The creditor may be coming against the woman to reclaim money she allegedly received improperly. In any event the sale took place with authority of beit din and the sale proceeds stand in her presumptive possession. It is as though she has already collected. If, however, she says, "I sold for maintenance," there has been no collection on the ketubah. As against maintenance, the written creditor has priority. If she then asserts the ketubah, she has not yet collected it. According to many authorities mere seizure without final collection is ineffective. Since the property was acquired after both obligations, the claims divide like two loans followed by acquisition. Everything is thus coherent. The Ran also proves from a widow who sells for maintenance and later collects for her ketubah that, although the ketubah and maintenance obligations both begin at marriage, the written ketubah has priority. This too can be answered. The field she sold may have been acquired later, while the ketubah contained an after acquired clause. The ketubah then precedes the oral maintenance claim with respect to that unencumbered property, as explained above, and she can return and collect because her ketubah has an after acquired lien while maintenance does not. Alternatively, Rav Hai Gaon may follow the Rosh and other authorities that if the husband wrote the basic one hundred or two hundred of the ketubah from the time of betrothal and did not later include it in a new consolidated ketubah at marriage, she collects that amount from encumbered property from the time of betrothal. The Gemara's question can concern such a case. The ketubah lien then arose at betrothal, whereas the maintenance obligation arose only at marriage, and the ketubah truly has priority. Thus, with Hashem's help, Rav Hai Gaon's position can be reconciled in several ways. The Shach writes that Rav Hai Gaon would agree that if the written creditor's document expressly states that the property is encumbered, the written loan precedes an earlier oral loan. This distinction is not found in the authorities. If it were accepted, why did the authorities find the Yerushalmi difficult? They could simply establish the written creditor's document as containing an express lien. Nor is there conceptual reason to distinguish. We rule that responsibility and lien language is a scribal presumption. If so, what difference is there whether the document explicitly writes the lien or the law treats it as written? The Shiltei Giborim itself testifies that the Rashba in a responsum does not accept this distinction. On what basis should we disagree with him without a clear proof? However one decides that dispute, regarding two oral loans, one earlier and one later, it appears clear that the earlier oral loan has priority. None of the reasons offered for preferring a later written loan apply. The authorities speak of a written document as analogous to a sale, or they invoke concern for closing the door before lenders. Those considerations concern a later written loan, not two oral loans. When both claims are oral, why should the earlier one not take priority in creditor collection, since we rule that liens are of Torah origin? It is a general rule that we do not disagree with a Gaon without clear proof from the Talmud. Rav Hai Gaon explicitly says that an earlier oral loan has priority, and no proof has been found against him in the case of one oral loan against another. The proof from the widow who leaves the purpose of sale unspecified and a written creditor comes demonstrates at most that a later written loan can defeat an earlier oral obligation. The Ramban's proof from "they inherit" likewise concerns a written loan. There is no proof at all that a later oral loan equals or precedes an earlier oral loan. One might try to infer otherwise from the Yerushalmi's statement that if an oral creditor comes, the widow says, "I sold for maintenance," apparently to protect the ketubah's ability to collect from purchasers, as Rashi explains the Bavli. This seems to imply that some unencumbered property remains for the ketubah, for otherwise what has she gained. Why not say, "I sold for my ketubah"? If the oral creditor then comes against the remaining unencumbered property, she can assert her earlier maintenance claim. If an earlier oral loan has priority over a later one, her maintenance should precede him, and it would make no difference whether she says the sale was for maintenance or ketubah. But according to what was explained above, the remaining unencumbered property is property the husband acquired later. If she says, "I sold for maintenance," her written ketubah remains and has an after acquired property clause, whereas the oral creditor has no such clause. The ketubah therefore collects the entire subsequently acquired property. If she says, "I sold for my ketubah" and then asserts maintenance against the remaining property while the oral creditor also claims it, they divide. The rule that an earlier oral loan precedes applies only to property the debtor possessed when the earlier loan arose. With property acquired only after both obligations, it is the case of two loans followed by acquisition, and they divide. She would therefore lose half. This resolves the inference. It is thus clear as a matter of law that an earlier oral loan precedes a later oral loan without doubt. This is the accepted ruling, and the Tur explicitly distinguishes in this manner. See there.
Since witnesses, etc. The Sema asks that here the Rama rules without mentioning a dissenting view, which indicates that he accepts this ruling as halakhah. Yet at the beginning of seif 1 he rules that with an oral loan, if the later creditor collected first, his collection stands. In seif katan 31 the Sema answers by distinguishing between a loan in which the debtor expressly subjected his property in the presence of witnesses, where a later creditor's collection is reversed, and a case without such witnesses. He adds that this resolves what appears to be a contradiction in the Tur's presentation of the Raavad. If the Sema means that the debtor expressly subjected particular property before witnesses, I have found no authority who explains Rav Hai Gaon and those who follow him in this way. If that were the case, why would the Ramban be troubled by the case of a written creditor who comes against a widow and she cannot say that she sold property for maintenance? A widow's maintenance has no specific lien on property at all, as the Beit Yosef writes in siman 90. See also the Ramban's responsum, siman 49. A creditor with an express lien would obviously take priority. Likewise, the ketubat benin dikhrin does not involve property expressly subjected to the beneficiaries, so the Ramban's proofs would present no difficulty. If the Sema instead means simply that an oral loan was made before witnesses, as opposed to a private handwritten acknowledgment or another obligation in which the debtor cannot plead payment, there is some basis for the distinction in Tosafot to Bava Batra 178. Tosafot ask that according to the view that liens are d'oraisa, how can partial admission ever produce a Torah oath, since land should always be liened, and answer that a d'oraisa lien exists specifically where the loan was made before witnesses. On that approach the Sema's distinction is correct, because the entire force of priority for an oral loan derives from the principle that liens are d'oraisa, and Tosafot would limit that principle to loans before witnesses. But Tosafot in Bava Metzia, the Rosh in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in, and the Tur above in siman 88 write that a Torah case of partial admission is possible only where the debtor owns no land or the creditor waived his lien. They evidently disagree with that limitation and hold that every enforceable oral obligation creates a d'oraisa lien. According to them, the Sema's distinction has no basis. There is also some difficulty from Shevuot 41b, which distinguishes between one who merely admits owing money before witnesses, who need not repay before witnesses because the creditor trusted him, and one who actually borrowed before witnesses, who must repay before witnesses because the creditor did not trust him. According to the Sema, even where a loan was made before witnesses one could still argue that the creditor trusted the debtor personally, and that the witnesses were taken only to secure priority over later creditors. This point can be answered, since the very use of witnesses still shows that the creditor did not fully trust him. Nevertheless, the Sema's distinction has no root in the authorities, and had the Rama intended it he should have stated it. Nor do I understand the claimed contradiction in the Tur. The Tur first cites Rav Hai Gaon, who gives an earlier oral creditor priority over a later written creditor. He then cites the Rif, who disagrees, followed by a responsum of the Raavad agreeing with the Rif that the later written creditor takes first. There is no contradiction. The Baal HaTerumot too mentions witnesses merely as a means to establish which oral loan was earlier, not as a distinct substantive requirement. Indeed, the Sema himself writes above in siman 48, seif katan 1, that an earlier bearer instrument takes priority over a later one, although both are handwritten instruments. The Sema's first answer in seif 1, distinguishing initial distribution from a case where the later creditor has already collected, is much more compelling. Tosafot at the beginning of the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui write that a later creditor who collected is no worse than a purchaser. Accordingly, just as an oral creditor cannot collect from purchasers, he cannot necessarily remove property already collected by a later creditor. There is some support for this from the Rashba's answer to Rav Hai's view concerning a written creditor and a widow's maintenance. The Rashba says that the Sages weakened maintenance by treating its lien as arising only now, so the written creditor is earlier. If that were true in all respects, then where an oral creditor comes against a widow who sold for maintenance, he too should be earlier. It is strained to say that the Sages weakened maintenance only against a written creditor but not against an oral creditor, and no such distinction appears in the Rashba. The better explanation is that once she has actually sold and collected for maintenance, the earlier oral creditor cannot undo that collection, consistent with what I have written. It is true that the Maggid Mishneh understands the Rambam's broad phrase, "whoever's debt is earlier collects earlier," as including oral creditors, and therefore infers that the Rambam follows Rav Hai Gaon. Yet the Rambam also states explicitly that if the later creditor collected first, his collection is reversed. This would imply that in the Rambam's view even an earlier oral creditor removes property collected by the later creditor. More likely, the Rama ruled pragmatically in light of the underlying dispute whether an oral creditor has any priority at all. Once the later creditor has collected, he can say that he follows the authorities who deny priority to an oral creditor. The burden of proof therefore rests on the party seeking to remove property from the possessor. This reconciles the Rama's rulings even according to the Maggid Mishneh.
