And it is like beit din that erred, whose sale stands. The Gemara's conclusion that an agent is like a widow applies to an agent of a beit din of three, who has no greater authority than those who appointed him. A court of fewer than three has no independent judicial authority, and therefore its solitary agent is treated like a widow: if he errs even slightly, the sale is void. But a judge appointed by royal authority to adjudicate alone and sell land, whose acts are legally effective even if he errs, stands differently. If that judge appoints an agent to sell and the agent errs, the agent has the same status as the judge, even though the agent is one person, because the appointing judge is himself one person. It is true that if the judge wishes to cancel his own agent's sale he may say, "I sent you to improve, not to impair." But insofar as royal authorization gives the agent the judge's status, his acts stand. All the more so if the judge himself sold without an agent, his act stands by virtue of royal authority. This is the Rama's intent, taken from Rivash, siman 179. I do not know why the law needed to be stated. It is obvious that dina demalchuta dina applies to the king's legal enactments, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 68 and 369. If the novelty is that although the royal authorization was granted only to the judge personally, we nevertheless say by logic that his agent is like him, this is surprising. By the same reasoning, an individual expert judge who may adjudicate alone should have an agent like himself, while only the agent of an ordinary beit din of three would be like a widow. That is implausible. One could just as well argue: just as the widow is not an expert, so too her agent is not, whereas an expert judge differs. Moreover, according to this reasoning, if a beit din sent three agents they should have the court's full status, which is certainly not so. Even an widow can act through ordinary lay judges, yet Tosafot in Ketubot 100a explain that she is still counted as one person.
