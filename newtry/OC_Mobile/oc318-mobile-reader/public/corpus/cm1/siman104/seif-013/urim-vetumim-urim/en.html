And an oral loan, etc. Certainly an earlier oral loan has priority over a later oral loan. See Tumim. Many authorities disagree and hold that even a later documented loan takes priority over an earlier oral loan, and Shach writes likewise. In truth this is a matter of unresolved law. But between an earlier oral loan and a later oral loan, practical law gives priority to the earlier one, and this is how we rule. The reason for Shulchan Aruch is that since property liens are of Torah origin, the lien of the first lender takes effect first. The Sages removed the lien of an oral loan only with respect to purchasers, because it lacks publicity, not with respect to property still in the debtor's possession. The dissenters hold that once the Sages removed the lien, they removed it completely. This is not persuasive regarding two oral loans. With a later documented loan, however, one can understand that the Sages preferred it, either because the written loan resembles a sale or because otherwise lenders would be reluctant to lend by note out of fear of undisclosed earlier oral debts. This is correct. Know that all of this concerns property the debtor already owned at the time of the loans. Property acquired only after all the loans, where no after acquired property clause was written, and ordinary movable property without a lien together with land, have no priority at all and are divided.
Has priority in collection, etc. Rama above in seif 1 writes that with an oral loan, if the later creditor already collected, his collection stands. Sema first distinguishes between an oral loan witnessed by others and one without witnesses. This distinction does not appear correct, as I wrote in Tumim, and Sema himself does not adopt it above in siman 48. His second answer is that although the earlier creditor has priority initially, once the later creditor collected, his collection stands. I brought proof for this in Tumim from Rashba. Maggid Mishneh, however, does not accept this distinction. I therefore wrote in Tumim that because the very existence of priority for an oral loan is disputed by major authorities, a later creditor who already collected may say that he relies upon those authorities who deny priority, and therefore the property is not removed from him. This explains the ruling well.
Since witnesses testify that it preceded. This applies where the earlier loan is still within its term so that the debtor cannot claim repayment, or where he stipulated, 'Do not repay me except in the presence of witnesses.' Bach.
