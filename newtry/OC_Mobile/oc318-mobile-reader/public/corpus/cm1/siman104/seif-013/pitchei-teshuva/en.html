An oral loan takes precedence. See Baer Heitev, citing the Radbaz, that this applies specifically when the holders of the written notes admit the oral loan. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, in his glosses to the Shulchan Aruch, writes as follows: where a creditor by note and an oral creditor seek payment from heirs, and the creditor by note admits the oral debt, if there are insufficient assets to satisfy both, they divide, because the written creditor's admission establishes the oral debt as against his own share. If the estate is sufficient for both, however, the written creditor takes his entire debt and may say to the oral creditor, "The assets are before you," while the oral creditor cannot collect from the heirs merely on the basis of the written creditor's admission. Beit Meir, siman 104, rules this way. See also Responsa Panim Meirot, volume 2, siman 92, concerning a deathly ill person who stated before his death that he owed a certain person one maneh orally. After his death several witnessed and formally executed debt instruments and several commercial notes appeared, and the estate was insufficient even for the written creditors. The question was whether the oral creditor must nevertheless receive a proportional share because the deceased made a complete admission at the time of death, and one ordinarily does not sin for no personal benefit. Panim Meirot initially notes that Shevuot 42b appears to say that this principle applies to a deathly ill person, because a person does not sin without benefit even for his heirs, whereas in a healthy person collusion is feared. Yet Nimukei Yosef at the end of Get Pashut indicates that this presumption is invoked specifically with respect to consecrated property, not ordinary private creditors. Accordingly, the deceased's admission cannot prejudice others. This is also apparent from the Shach below, siman 255, end of seif katan 8, who writes that although a deathly ill person who says, "I owe one maneh to so-and-so," is believed in some contexts, he is not believed against purchasers or other creditors. At the end of the responsum Panim Meirot raises a difficulty: even in ordinary matters the Gemara in Bava Metzia 5a relies upon the presumption that a person does not sin without personal benefit, for otherwise one could not entrust animals to an ordinary shepherd because of the prohibition of placing a stumbling block. He suggests that a person may sin for the sake of his children, but not even for his children when consecrated property is involved, and leaves the matter requiring study. Shaar Mishpat, siman 255, answers that the presumption is sufficient to avoid disqualifying a person from testimony when his disqualification is itself only doubtful, but it is not strong enough to believe him affirmatively so as to extract money from others. Only with consecrated property was such evidentiary force granted.
