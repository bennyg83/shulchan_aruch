A debtor declared himself insolvent and unable to pay his earlier and later creditors, so they came before beit din for collection according to law. The earlier creditors held heter iska notes which provided that each month the debtor could swear that no profit had been earned. Many months had passed without his taking such an oath, so under the terms of the notes he owed the stipulated profit. The question was whether that profit enjoys the same priority as the principal. At first glance Shevut Yaakov thought that it should, as siman 104, seif 15 indicates regarding interest, and that one cannot distinguish between interest owed to a non-Jew and permitted profit owed to a Jew, since the source is a responsum of the Rashba concerning a Jew who obligated himself to support his wife. Upon further analysis, however, he concluded that the two cases differ. A person may obligate himself by instrument from the present date, with a complete lien, to provide future support or similar obligations, and the lien can attach from the date of the instrument even though the obligation will mature later. By contrast, interest between Jews cannot be created as an ordinary debt; it is permitted only through an iska arrangement. If no profit was earned and the recipient swears in accordance with the condition, nothing is owed. Thus the lien for profit does not attach from the date of the document. Shevut Yaakov therefore ruled in practice that the iska profit has no priority. For purposes of collection from encumbered property it is treated like an oral loan, although the debtor cannot plead payment because the instrument contains a clause of credibility. He further ruled that from the day the debtor openly declared that he had no assets, provided there was no fraud, he need not pay any further iska profit.
