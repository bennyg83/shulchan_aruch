Rules of priority. See Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 30, concerning two creditors who held a promissory note against a debtor arising from a purchase of honey. The debtor retained their money and never delivered the honey. He later brought hides, and the two creditors, with the aid of the municipal authorities, seized the hides. A third person then claimed that he had become guarantor for those very hides, which the debtor had bought on credit from a non-Jew. This claimant had no written instrument, although the debtor admitted his claim. The rabbi who posed the question ruled that the hides remain with the creditors who held the note because they had already seized them. Noda BiYehudah answered that this was correctly decided. Even had the guarantor also possessed a written note, there is no rule of priority among creditors with respect to movable property. Even according to the Bach at the end of siman 96, who rules that when merchandise bought on credit remains intact, or where its identifiable proceeds remain, other creditors have no share in it, that ruling would not aid this guarantor. The Bach's view itself is disputed, as noted in Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 117. Moreover, the Bach's rationale must be that had the original seller known the purchaser was not creditworthy, he would not have supplied the merchandise, so the transaction is treated as a mistaken sale and the merchandise remains in the ownership of its original owner, as do its traceable proceeds. This explanation is also given by Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 90, Divrei Mishpat, siman 91 and the end of siman 96, and Pri Tevuah, end of siman 31. Here, however, the claimant was only a guarantor. The hides were never his property, so it cannot be said that they remained in his ownership. One might question the seizure because it was carried out through the secular authorities. Baer Heitev cites Maharshal or Rashdam to the effect that such a seizure is ineffective, and Maharik, shoresh 154, also writes similarly. Yet Maharik's ruling concerns a case in which later clarification by witnesses could still affect the right to retain the property. He therefore feared that one who had first used secular force might again resort to it and refuse to accept the testimony. By contrast, where a creditor may seize movable property and the seizure conclusively determines possession, what difference is there whether he seized it personally or by the force of the governing authority? One might further object that the Shulchan Aruch in seif 4 requires appraisal by beit din, whereas here the appraisal was made by non-Jews. The Shach, however, holds that if the creditor has already seized the property, the seizure is effective even without any appraisal. Since these creditors are presently in possession, the property cannot be taken from them. All this would apply even if the guarantor had a note. Here his claim is merely oral, and he is plainly not believed at all, even though the debtor admits the claim, because the debtor has no migo. This is explained by the Shach, siman 99, end of seif katan 6. The ruling of the rabbi who posed the question was therefore correct.
Priority. See Baer Heitev, who records that the Bach ruled in accordance with the responsum of Maimonides. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 118, likewise ruled so in practice, and cites Piskei Rav Recanati, siman 524, which states explicitly that no distinction is made between other creditors who are present and demand their shares and those who are absent. Their shares must always be set aside. See also Responsa Panim Meirot, volume 2, siman 20. Reuven claimed that Shimon had taken four barrels of honey from Levi, although Reuven had already purchased those barrels from Levi because Levi owed him one thousand gold coins and had contracted with him for the honey. Shimon answered that Levi also owed him five hundred gold coins. When he came to Levi's residence and found him absent, he asked Levi's wife whether her husband had left money for him. She answered that he had left no money, but had instructed her to give Shimon four barrels of honey at the known weight and price. Shimon therefore received the honey, and said that he had never known that these barrels belonged to Reuven and that perhaps Levi had sold him four different barrels. Panim Meirot ruled that the honey is not removed from Shimon, who seized it first. Even if Reuven proves by witnesses that he acquired honey by a commercial mode of acquisition customarily performed by striking hands, called a handshlag, although such an acquisition prevents the seller from retracting, it does not necessarily place the goods fully in the purchaser's domain so that another creditor's seizure is ineffective. This follows from Maharam Alshich, responsum 124, cited by the Shach at the end of siman 126, concerning gold rings deposited with Reuven and later transferred to Levi in the presence of all three parties. Even according to the Shach there, who disagrees and maintains that once property has been acquired by any valid act of acquisition it is fully the purchaser's and another creditor cannot seize it, his case involved parties who admitted that those particular gold rings had been transferred to Levi. Here Reuven cannot prove that these four barrels are the barrels he purchased. Even a written contract for barrels of honey would not prove that these were the specific barrels sold to him, for Levi may have sold Shimon four different barrels. Reuven therefore has only an uncertain claim against Shimon. Strictly speaking, he could not even impose a ban upon him. To satisfy Reuven's concern, however, the court ruled that Shimon should accept a ban before beit din affirming that he did not know that Reuven had purchased these four barrels from Levi, and that he received them from Levi's wife believing she was carrying out her husband's instructions. Once he does so, Reuven has no further claim against Shimon.
It is not deemed a seizure. See the Shach, seif katan 9, who writes that the Sema's statement, that the creditor could forgive the debt if he wished, is imprecise. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 3, siman 150, answers that there is no difficulty. By virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien, the debtor of the debtor is equally encumbered to all the creditors. Therefore one creditor's seizure cannot diminish the shares of the others, since, were it not for their lien, his own share could have been defeated by forgiveness. They therefore divide. In any event, the Sema's explanation is unnecessary, because we rule in accordance with Beit Hillel that a debt instrument standing for collection is not regarded as though already collected. Thus merely seizing the note is ineffective because the underlying lien remains with the original creditor. Shevut Yaakov was also asked about four brothers, each of whom held a note against their mother for one hundred reichstaler, all dated the same day. Their mother held a note against a third party. During her illness she gave that note to one son so that he could collect a certain amount for her support, which he did, and the note remained with him. After her death he hastened to collect the entire remaining balance and seized it toward part of his own debt. The brothers argued that a seizure after death is ineffective and demanded an equal division. He ruled that had the son merely seized the note before collecting it, the seizure would unquestionably be ineffective, as stated in siman 104, seif 3, because seizing debt instruments is not the same as seizing money. But once he actually collected money, even a seizure made after the mother's death is effective. The brothers also have no claim against the debtor for paying only one brother before all appeared, because brothers who are partners in a debt do not require writing and delivery from one another, as explained by the Shach in siman 76, seif katan 18. All the more so here, where each has a share in the debt by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien. If the son who seized the money still remains owed additional sums and there are other assets of their mother from which to collect, he participates with the other brothers in those assets and collects his remaining debt proportionately with them. Shevut Yaakov rejects the contrary ruling of Perach Mateh Aharon, volume 2, siman 26, and records that this was the practical ruling of his court.
