If he seized debt instruments, it is not treated as seizure of money, and they divide. Mere possession of debt instruments does not transfer the underlying liens because ordinary assignment requires writing and delivery. The original creditor may also retain the power to waive. Therefore a creditor who simply grabs the documents does not thereby acquire their debts. If the debtor himself transfers the instruments to a creditor with proper writing and delivery, the transfer is effective. The recipient then stands like a purchaser, and even an earlier creditor cannot ordinarily recover them because debt instruments are movables and ordinary creditors do not follow sold movables. If the original debtor had subjected movables together with land, the earlier creditor can sometimes follow even sold instruments because the market overt enactment does not protect this type of debt instrument. For our common bearer notes, formal writing may not be necessary and waiver may not be effective, but delivery is still required because the instrument's body is not itself ordinary money. Transfer by delivery requires another person's intentional act of conveyance. Therefore if the creditor merely seizes such a note on his own, the seizure does not acquire it. If the debtor himself delivers the bearer note to him, the transfer works even without separate assignment language. This is the correct distinction.
