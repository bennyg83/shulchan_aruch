Otherwise, why should the earlier creditor lose his priority merely because the later creditor seized them first? The Shach in seif katan 7 writes that promissory notes are different because they are property that the debtor acquired only later, and therefore the creditors divide them. I do not understand this. The debt represented by the notes is subject to the creditor's lien by virtue of Rabbi Natan's rule, and the concept of after acquired property does not apply to that lien. Tosafot in Bava Batra say so explicitly, and I wrote at length about this above in siman 86. Only where heirs collected movables is it treated as though those movables were in the hands of their father. If so, even if we regard the notes as though they were movables in the debtor's hands, the question remains that on the Shach's premise such movables should initially be awarded to the earlier creditor. The Sema's inference is therefore compelling. The source in Rabbenu Yerucham, however, does not discuss property in the hands of a living debtor, but a creditor who seized from heirs. Movables in the hands of heirs are not subject to the debt at all by strict law, and are collectible only because of the enactment of the Geonim. In that situation all creditors certainly divide equally, because the earlier creditor has no underlying lien that would create priority. Thus, no proof can be brought from there to movables in the hands of a living debtor. Still, there is no need for this distinction, because Rabbenu Yerucham writes explicitly in netiv 6, part 1, that there is no priority at all in movables and the creditors divide equally. It further appears that if beit din itself transferred promissory notes to a creditor, then since we hold above in siman 66 that a debt instrument can itself be collected by judicial transfer, and the creditor requires neither a written assignment nor delivery and the original creditor can no longer forgive the debt, such a transfer is certainly a completed collection. The case here must therefore concern mere seizure without a judicial transfer. This is also evidence that the Mechaber holds that collection of movables does not require action by beit din. Otherwise, why speak particularly of promissory notes? Mere seizure would be ineffective with all movables. It also seems that the present ruling concerns the money ultimately collected from the debtor. If the debtor refuses to pay unless the note is returned to him, and one creditor holds the note and refuses to surrender it, that creditor cannot be forced to give up the paper itself. Seizure is effective with respect to the physical document just as it is effective against heirs, as I explained at length above in siman 64. There is no difference in this respect between heirs and another creditor.
