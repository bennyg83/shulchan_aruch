See Ketzot HaChoshen, who writes concerning a gift that according to the view that one may acquire on behalf of another even when it is not in that person's presence, if the donor sent an agent carrying the deed of gift to deliver it to the recipient and the donor died before the deed reached the recipient, the recipient acquires the gift. In my humble opinion this is certainly incorrect. Death cannot be less than a retraction, and retraction terminates the donor's power, as explained in many places. Ketzot's proof from a case where the donor sold the field in the interim is not comparable. There, even though he sold it, and according to the Rosh such a sale constitutes retraction, most authorities hold that it is not a retraction from the gift deed at all. We can say that he intended the later sale as a sale of property that would not be his if the first gift ultimately took effect, so that if the first recipient acquired under the gift deed the second sale would be nullified retroactively. Upon the donor's death, however, his power and authority over the object have ended entirely. I was uncertain about the following. If a person hires people who are not legally capable of agency, and they are his workers, to perform an act of chazakah, such as building or digging in ownerless land or in a house being acquired, or if he causes meshichah or lifting through workers who are not capable of agency, is the acquisition effective? This question arose because we see that regarding one who lifts a lost object for his fellow, even according to the view that the fellow does not acquire it through an ordinary agent, if the lifter claims that he is a hired worker the employer does acquire it, because a worker's hand is like the employer's hand. This demonstrates that the law of a worker is not based upon ordinary agency. If so, why should people who lack the capacity for agency be excluded? Were it not for the verse "you also," which excludes those who are not members of the covenant and those who lack legal capacity, we would not have excluded them from agency. The principle that the hand of a worker is like the employer's hand is plainly not derived from the law of agency, since it applies to a found object where ordinary agency does not. The Shach likewise writes in siman 105 that where one seizes for a creditor to the detriment of others, ordinary agency does not help, while acting through a worker does. Yet even the Gemara in Bava Metzia 10, where a worker's lifting is effective for a found object, and the law that a Canaanite slave's find belongs to his master, are difficult. What is the source for this result? Even a Canaanite slave, whose hand is like his master's, is treated in Gittin 21 and 78 as no more than a courtyard. If a get is placed in the hand of a slave it is effective only when the slave is restrained, because otherwise he is a moving courtyard, and a moving courtyard does not acquire. It follows that the slave is not treated as a separate agent but only as a courtyard. Why, then, should a worker's lifting acquire a lost object for the employer, if ordinary agency does not apply and the only basis is that his hand is like the employer's, while a moving courtyard cannot acquire? Afterward I obtained the work Machaneh Ephraim, whose author, an early great authority, also discusses this doubt in Hilchot Mekhirah, siman 15, whether lifting and meshichah through gentile workers are effective. He concludes that they are not, for the reason just stated: even though a worker's hand is like the employer's hand, the worker is like a moving courtyard. He also writes in Hilchot Sheluchin, siman 11, in the name of the Rashba and Ritva, that a worker is no better than a Canaanite slave. However, he did not address the difficulty I raised from Bava Metzia 10: if so, why does a worker's lifting acquire a found object? A moving courtyard should not acquire. He further suggests that perhaps a gentile worker was excluded from agency only regarding matters that require agency, meaning matters where an act done without the owner's authorization is ineffective. Where agency is not required, such as when one tells a gentile to build a parapet and he does so, the act itself is effective. I do not understand how this helps here. Acquisition by meshichah or lifting certainly requires authorization. If done without the owner's consent it accomplishes nothing. Why, then, should a gentile worker be effective? It therefore appears to me that there are two distinct forms of acquisition by lifting. First, when the object is in a person's hand he acquires it even without lifting it at all, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 29, s.v. Ela, because his hand is no worse than his courtyard. I questioned the Sema on this in siman 198. Second, one can acquire because the object was lifted through his force even without taking it into his hand, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 70, s.v. Ho'il, and as I discussed in siman 198. Accordingly, Gittin 78 is understandable. With a get, lifting that does not place the get in her hand is insufficient, because the Torah specifically requires her hand. Therefore placing it in the hand of her slave does not work when the slave is moving. Her own hand is not considered a moving courtyard because, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 9, it rests with her body and is carried along together with it. A slave who is independently walking, however, is literally a moving courtyard. In ordinary monetary acquisition, by contrast, it is enough that the object be lifted through the buyer's force. It need not actually rest in his hand. Indeed, Tosafot in Bava Kamma 98 explain that even if a person strikes a nest and causes the birds to raise themselves, he can acquire them. Thus even causing an object to become lifted can constitute acquisition. If so, when a person commands his workers to lift an object, there is no greater causal act than this. A worker's hand and force are regarded as the employer's hand and force. The same is true where a worker effects meshichah. Since even calling an animal so that it comes into one's domain is effective, any drawing of the object into the buyer's domain through his causal act is effective. When he directs a worker to draw it into his domain, that too is a direct causal act. Indeed, it appears that even if he sets a dog upon an animal so that the dog drives it into his domain, the acquisition should be effective, comparable to calling the animal and causing it to come. However, if a gentile worker draws the object into the worker's own domain, it is not effective even if the worker was appointed as an agent, because there is no agency for a gentile. We find only that a worker's hand is like the employer's hand, not that the worker's independent domain is the employer's domain. There is therefore no valid meshichah into the buyer's domain. Likewise, meshichah in an alley does not work through a gentile worker, because the reason meshichah works in an alley is that the place becomes the domain of the person standing there, as Tosafot explain in Kiddushin 25. Drawing into the gentile worker's temporary domain is not drawing into the employer's domain. If the person tells a gentile who is not his worker to draw the object into the buyer's own domain, it also appears ineffective. Since the gentile is not a legal agent, his hand is not the buyer's hand, and the buyer is not legally regarded as the cause of this meshichah. This is unlike setting his dog upon the animal, because a gentile acts on his own initiative and is not legally identified with the employer's force. The same distinction appears regarding chazakah. If a gentile worker locks, fences, or otherwise improves the property, the acquisition is effective, because even placing or removing a stone from afar, where the buyer merely causes an improvement to the field, is effective. The same should therefore hold where the improvement is performed through his gentile worker. Maharshal's responsum in Choshen Mishpat, siman 376, likewise states that digging through workers is effective. This also follows from the Rosh in Bava Batra 52, who proves that the chazakah of locking does not mean repairing a lock from the statement in Gittin that one should go and lock, whereas repairing a lock is not permitted on Shabbat even through instructing a gentile. This proof assumes that repairing the lock through a gentile would otherwise be a valid chazakah, and only the Shabbat prohibition prevents its use there. Although a kinyan requires intent to acquire, the worker's act can operate with the employer's intent. A worker is fully treated like the employer, except that the worker's hand itself cannot acquire merely as a courtyard because it is a moving courtyard. In my view, chazakah through workers is effective even if the workers themselves have no intention to acquire, provided the employer intends to acquire through their work. Since the field is improved through his causal action, it is as though he performed the chazakah himself. There is no difference whether he digs through his ox or through his worker. What the worker digs in obedience to the employer's voice and command is comparable to the employer's ox plowing in response to his command. The Sema in siman 278, seif katan 26, says that if workers do not intend to acquire, the employer does not acquire. That case is different. There the employer himself also did not intend to acquire the wood through the workers' lifting. He merely told them in general terms to build with the property of a convert. When he builds personally, the case must be one in which he expressly says that he does not intend to acquire the wood itself but only the structure, for ordinarily his own hand would acquire what he handles even without explicit intent. Through workers, however, no acquisition occurs automatically unless the employer intends to acquire by causing their lifting. The Machaneh Ephraim's distinction concerning lack of intent where another person conveys title is discussed elsewhere. The resulting rule is as follows. Lifting, meshichah, chazakah, and similar acts of acquisition performed through gentile workers can be effective just as if the employer performed them himself. The same acts performed through gentile agents who are not workers are ineffective. Through workers, the acquisition is effective where the employer intends to acquire through the act performed by the workers even if the workers themselves do not have acquisition intent. But where the acquisition depends specifically upon the worker's hand as a hand, the employer does not acquire. Meshichah through workers is effective only when they draw the object into the employer's domain. If they draw it into the worker's own domain, or into an alley where the worker's standing gives him the relevant domain, it is ineffective if the worker lacks legal agency. A legally competent agent, by contrast, can acquire in such a case. One might object that the Yerushalmi at the beginning of HaOmer indicates that an agent appointed by the recipient cannot receive a gift unless the owner of the money appoints him, and the Nimukei Yosef cites the Ran in Bava Metzia to the same effect. This seems to imply that meshichah performed by an agent appointed only by the recipient would not help. Why, then, should an agent of a buyer be able to perform meshichah? The answer appears to be that a purchaser appoints the agent concerning the purchaser's own money and acquisition. He thereby makes the agent stand in his place as the relevant party, so that when the seller intends to convey to the principal and the buyer authorizes the agent to act as purchaser, the agent's meshichah is treated as the buyer's own meshichah. In a gift, by contrast, the recipient seeks to appoint an agent over property that still belongs to another. Therefore the donor must authorize that person when handing over the property, so that he acts as the proper agent of the owner for delivery. According to this, the Shach's statement in siman 105, seif katan 1, that a seizure for a creditor to the detriment of others can be accomplished through a hired agent by virtue of his status as a worker, cannot stand as written. I noted there a contradiction in his words, and the analysis here likewise undermines it. Meshichah by a worker is effective only when the object is drawn into the creditor's domain. If it is drawn into the worker's domain or merely into an alley, it is ineffective where ordinary agency is unavailable. If the worker draws it into the creditor's domain, however, it would be effective even without the special law of a worker, because property that comes into the creditor's own domain is already seized for him. As for acquisition by lifting merely because the object was raised through his force, I do not think this works for one who seizes on behalf of a creditor. We have found only seizure where the property actually comes into the creditor's possession or domain. There is no source that a remote kinyan of lifting, based only on causation, should constitute such a seizure. I later saw that Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchot Sheluchin, siman 11, brings a proof that workers are effective from the Yerushalmi in Peah: where gentiles harvest for themselves the field is exempt because the verse requires "your harvest," not the harvest of gentiles, while if gentile workers harvest for the owner the field is obligated. In my humble opinion this proof is not persuasive. The exclusion there concerns gentiles harvesting for themselves. If they harvest for the Jewish owner, even without pay, the field remains obligated. See the Bartenura and Tosafot Yom Tov there.
And if he expressly said. In my humble opinion the Rama was too brief where explanation was required. He should have stated that if the recipient took the measuring vessel in order to measure merchandise for others, he is liable for the vessel even if the sender expressly said to send it with the minor. In Bava Batra 88 the Gemara explains the dispute between Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages as a case where one took the vessel to measure for others, and they dispute whether a borrower without the owner's knowledge has the status of a borrower or of a robber. Rabbi Yehudah, who exempts him, holds that he is a borrower. This shows that according to the view that such a person is a robber he would be liable even though Rabbi Yehudah holds that the owner sent the vessel with the minor. Ramban states this explicitly in his novellae there. Accordingly, since we rule that a borrower without the owner's knowledge is a robber, one who takes the vessel to measure for others is liable even if the owner explicitly said, "Send it to me with the minor."
