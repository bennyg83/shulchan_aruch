Because they are not persons of legal capacity. There is room to ask what happens if the principal was legally competent when he appointed the agent but became mentally incompetent before the agent completed the mission. Do we look to the time of appointment, when the agent validly stood in the principal's place, or to the time of completion, when the principal himself lacked capacity? The early authorities disagree. Rambam holds that if a husband was competent when he appointed an agent to deliver a get and afterward became mentally incompetent, the agent's delivery remains effective by Torah law; the Sages disqualified it only lest people say that a mentally incompetent person can divorce. The Tur holds that such a get is invalid by Torah law. Pri Chadash rules like Rambam and extends the rule even to a person who became moribund after appointment. According to Rambam, the rabbinic concern is unique to divorce and should not invalidate an ordinary monetary agency. According to the Tur, agency itself has ended and the same would apply in monetary law. Rambam's view raises a further question. If the agent remains the principal's representative even after the principal becomes incapable, why does the Mishnah say that if one said, "Give this get to my wife" or "this bill of emancipation to my slave," and then died, it may not be delivered after death? Tosafot says the novelty is precisely that an agent appointed in life does not remain equivalent to the principal after death. The answer is that death differs from incapacity. If a master sold his slave after appointing an agent to emancipate him, the agent could no longer free the slave because the master himself has no remaining authority over that slave. Likewise, after a husband's death the woman is already released from him and is no longer subject to his power of divorce. Agency cannot supply authority that the principal himself no longer possesses. Mental incompetence or a moribund condition is different. The wife remains his wife and the slave remains his slave. The defect is only the principal's lack of present legal capacity. Since the agent was validly appointed while the principal was competent, the competent agent continues to stand in his place. Rashi's wording supports this. He explains the rule against posthumous emancipation by saying that once the master died, his authority over the slave ended and the heirs' authority began. This implies that the essential problem is loss of substantive authority, not simply the absence of a living principal. There is even reason to say that an agency chain does not automatically disappear when the first agent dies or becomes unavailable, because a second agent appointed validly by the first can continue. The decisive question is whether the principal's underlying legal power over the subject still exists. One remaining difficulty concerns the doctrine that witnesses' signatures can acquire rights retroactively from the time of signing. If an emancipation document was signed before the master's death and later delivered, perhaps the acquisition should relate back even though the master's authority had ended. This depends on the dispute whether that doctrine applies to bills of emancipation and to gift documents. The sugyot cited by Rashi can be read according to the view that it does not apply in this setting. The central principle remains that death ends the principal's substantive authority, whereas later incapacity does not necessarily revoke an already valid agency.
Neither a male minor nor a female minor appoints an agent. Rashi in Bava Metzia explains that a minor lacks agency because the Torah's agency model is stated regarding an adult and one capable of separating terumah. This is difficult because a child close to majority whose vows are legally effective can, according to the accepted view, separate terumah by Torah law. Such a minor would then fit the description of one capable of terumah. Elsewhere Rashi gives a different source. The primary law of agency is written concerning divorce, "he shall send" and "she shall send," and the male principal described there is necessarily an adult because a male minor cannot create kiddushin or divorce. That explains why a male minor cannot appoint an agent. But a female minor can sometimes receive her own get when she has no father, so the verse concerning the woman's agency does not by itself exclude her. Rambam supplies the answer. He says a female minor cannot appoint an agent to receive her get even though her courtyard can acquire it, because appointment of a receiving agent requires witnesses and testimony is not received concerning a minor. Once the Torah's divorce model excludes both male and female minors from appointing agents in practice, the same limitation carries into other areas because divorce is the source of the general law of agency. Raavad instead derives the rule from the verse concerning terumah, "you also," which teaches that both principal and agent must be persons of legal capacity. This corresponds to Rashi's first explanation. The dispute may reflect their disagreement concerning a child close to majority. Rambam holds such a minor's vows and terumah can have Torah force, so the verse about terumah would not exclude every minor. Raavad holds that this law is not biblical and can therefore derive the exclusion of minors directly from the terumah verse. Thus Rambam's and Raavad's explanations are consistent with their broader positions.
