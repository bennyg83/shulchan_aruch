A Jew cannot become an agent for a non-Jew. One Jew sold grain as the purported agent of a non-Jewish nobleman and later retracted and sold it to another Jew. It appears that no full acquisition arises through the initial agency arrangement until the second buyer performs the act that actually acquires the grain. A Jew cannot act as the legal agent of a non-Jew, so the first purported sale through agency does not create ordinary acquisition. Even if the transaction is a particularly favorable sale for the non-Jew, so that one might otherwise speak of acquiring a benefit for him, acquisition for another here would itself depend on agency, which does not operate for a non-Jew. The same difficulty applies in the opposite type of transaction where the Jew obtains a bargain for the non-Jew. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 71b discuss this principle. Nevertheless, the moral sanction of 'Mi Shepara' may apply. The arrangement is no weaker than a case in which one contracts to buy something readily available in the market, which can create enough commitment for Mi Shepara under Choshen Mishpat 209. Perhaps it also resembles property lacking only one or two acts of completion, which some authorities treat as effectively available. Here the intermediary is certainly appointed to arrange the matter, knows that the nobleman will not depart from what he does, and may hold written authorization. Still, my inclination is that property not owned by him cannot be treated like his own property that merely lacks a finishing act. It also requires study whether, if the intermediary brought the grain in reliance upon the prospective buyer before reselling it, the principle that one labored and acquired in reliance on another's good faith applies. Perhaps where the intermediary's primary motive in obtaining the grain is to serve the nobleman and retain his favor, his effort cannot be attributed sufficiently to the prospective buyer. At minimum, waiver or release by the buyer should be effective while the grain has not yet been brought. If the buyer authorizes resale to another, that certainly suffices, and all the more so if he receives back the purchase money he had advanced. Daily practice also indicates that meshichah carried out by a non-Jew on behalf of a Jew does not acquire for the Jew, because agency for a non-Jew does not exist. The authorities similarly state that a non-Jew's courtyard does not acquire through the agency theory. The same should apply to acquisition by chazakah, such as locking, fencing, or otherwise improving land, when the act is done by a hired non-Jew. Even if the Jew says 'perform chazakah and acquire for me' and stands present, the non-Jew's act is no more the Jew's legal act of acquisition than if a trained animal performed it. Although a Jew can fulfill the mitzvah of a parapet through a non-Jew because the required physical result now exists, acquisition by chazakah is not merely the physical improvement of land. If the same improvement were performed by an animal it would not create acquisition, so performance by a non-Jew should likewise fail despite being done for the Jew's benefit. Netivot HaMishpat writes that a hired worker may in some respects be treated as the employer's hand, even where the worker is a minor or non-Jew, based on the Gemara concerning one hired to gather. Perhaps that principle helps only when the worker acquires something for his employer, because the employment relationship makes his hand function like the employer's hand, similar in some respect to a courtyard. It may not enable the worker to convey his employer's property to a third party. Nor can the rule concerning a minor, who may eventually become legally capable of agency and for whom others can acquire, automatically be extended to a non-Jew. According to all views, where the person is one's hired worker it may also be unnecessary to use the exact formula 'acquire for me'; an instruction to give may sometimes be treated as an instruction to acquire.
