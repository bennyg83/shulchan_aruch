[Seif 10 in the gloss] This requires study, since this verse concerns damage by tooth and foot. It may be reconciled as follows. The verse indeed addresses tooth and foot, as stated at the beginning of Bava Kamma, but it is also needed to establish the exemption of horn. In Bava Kamma 26a the Gemara asks whether horn should perhaps be liable in the public domain by an a fortiori argument: tooth and foot, which pay full damages in the injured party's domain, are exempt in the public domain, so horn, which pays only half damages in the injured party's domain, should certainly be exempt there. Rabbi Yochanan answers from the verse “they shall divide,” teaching that half-damages is not divided between public and private domains. One might then ask why horn should not also be liable in the owner's domain by the same verse. The answer must be that in the owner's domain the a fortiori argument remains operative. The verse “they shall divide” is applied to the more reasonable case, namely the public domain, but not to the owner's domain, where there is an independent reason for exemption: had you not entered my domain, you would not have been injured. The primary exemption of tooth and foot in the owner's domain derives from “and it consumed in another's field,” meaning only when it went and damaged in another's field. The Rambam therefore cites this concept regarding the exemption of horn in the owner's domain, because that exemption is derived through the a fortiori comparison to tooth and foot, whose exemption rests on the verse “and he shall send forth his animal,” and so forth.
