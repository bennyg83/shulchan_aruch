To the one for whom it is designated for produce, liability for tooth and foot, etc. In the Perishah and Derishah I proved that whenever an animal damages produce, the ruling calls it tooth and foot, based upon the verse "and he shall send his animal and consume," which concerns an animal damaging through tooth and foot. Whenever an animal damages another animal, the ruling calls it horn, based upon the verse, "If a man's ox gores his fellow's ox." With this the Mechaber's language becomes clear. When he says, "and it damaged through tooth and foot," he means it damaged produce. When he says, "through horn he is exempt," he means that the ox belonging to the person authorized to keep oxen there damaged the animal of the person authorized to keep produce there, who brought the animal in without permission. The later clause, "if the animal of the produce owner damaged the ox owner's animal through horn," likewise means that it damaged the other animal. The final phrase, "through tooth and foot he is exempt," means both parties altered the ordinary use. The produce owner brought in an animal and the ox owner brought in produce, and the animal damaged the produce. It is exempt. This final clause refers to the animal just mentioned, saying that if that animal damaged the ox owner's property through tooth and foot, it is exempt. One should not explain the first "horn" as meaning that the ox belonging to the person authorized for oxen kicked or lay upon the other's produce. If so, it would be difficult to distinguish this from the rule below where a courtyard is designated to both for oxen but to one for other objects, and one brings in his garment. The Tur and Mechaber rule that if an ox tramples the garment the ox owner is liable. Nor can one infer from seif 13 that horn damage to produce is exempt. There the ruling says that he is liable "even for tooth and foot," meaning certainly for horn, which is liable everywhere, even in the public domain. According to the interpretive framework just stated, the same phrase can mean that liability applies even where a small animal damages another animal by an act called horn, all the more so where ordinary tooth and foot damages produce. See the Perishah for further proofs.
For regarding these it is considered the injured party's courtyard. Meaning with respect to tooth and foot. The reason is that he has permission to bring in his produce. The later phrase, "regarding these it is considered the tortfeasor's courtyard," is likewise not literal. It means only that it is not the injured party's courtyard, because the space was not designated for his produce but for the tortfeasor. Similarly, the Tur and Mechaber's earlier statement that horn liability exists because the place is considered the injured party's courtyard is not literally necessary. Horn is liable even in the public domain, a jointly owned courtyard, or a place belonging to neither, except in the tortfeasor's own courtyard. Because this particular courtyard is designated specifically either to the tortfeasor or injured party for the relevant use, the ruling states the factual characterization.
