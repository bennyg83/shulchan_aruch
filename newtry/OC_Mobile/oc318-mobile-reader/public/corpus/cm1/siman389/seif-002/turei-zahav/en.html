For example, workers who enter, and so forth. In the chapter HaManiach, the first tanna and “Others” dispute this matter. The first tanna holds that if workers entered the employer's house to demand their wages and his dog bit them, the homeowner is exempt. Others hold that workers are permitted to enter to demand their wages, and therefore the homeowner is liable. The Gemara explains that if the employer is commonly found in the marketplace, everyone agrees that they have no permission to enter his house without authorization. If he is never found there, everyone agrees that he is liable, because the workers must go to his house to collect their wages. They dispute a case where he is found in the market only sometimes. The workers came, called to the homeowner at the doorway, and he answered, “Yes.” The first tanna understands this as, “Yes, remain where you are,” whereas Others understand it as, “Yes, enter.” The Rosh writes that because the Rif omitted this baraita, he evidently held that the rule has changed in present practice. Since employers do not ordinarily carry money in the marketplace and workers ordinarily enter the employer's house, all agree that the homeowner is liable for their injuries. From the time I first considered this matter, I have been astonished at the Rosh's construction. He overturns an explicit baraita and changes the law on the fragile basis that the Rif omitted it. The opposite should be inferred. The homeowner is exempt according to all opinions even when he answered “Yes,” unless he expressly invited them inside. The dispute concerned workers only because the first tanna did not remove workers from the rule governing other people, under which the homeowner is evidently exempt, whereas Others held that workers differ from ordinary visitors. Since the Rif omitted the special rule concerning workers, they remain included with everyone else, and the homeowner is exempt whenever he did not expressly authorize entry. I later saw that Maharshal, in HaManiach, also strongly questioned the Rosh for ruling contrary to the Gemara, especially where the result is extraction of money. Maharshal explains the Rif's omission differently. It appears to me that practical law follows Maharshal's conclusion that the homeowner is exempt even regarding workers, although not for his explanation of the Rif. The explanation I have given is, in my humble opinion, clear and straightforward.
