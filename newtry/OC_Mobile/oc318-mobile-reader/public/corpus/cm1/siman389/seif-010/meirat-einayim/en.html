To exempt them in a domain belonging to both. The verse says "another person's field," implying a field belonging to another, namely one designated to the injured party, not a field belonging jointly to both. Certainly the owner of the damaging animal is exempt from tooth and foot in the public domain, where all animals are entitled to walk. Since it is normal for an animal to eat with its tooth what is fit to eat and to tread with its foot upon objects lying in its path, the injured party should protect his property from being left where everyone passes. This differs from horn and similar abnormal damage. Since such conduct is not normal, the injured party is not expected to guard against it, and the owner of the damaging animal must pay half damages even in the public domain, as the Mechaber writes in the next seif. It appears that Rama's question can be answered as follows. The Rambam and Mechaber's phrase, "and it is explicit in the Torah, etc.," does not explain only the immediately preceding rule. Rather, it explains the opening statement that liability applies only when the animal enters the injured party's domain, excluding also a jointly used domain. This follows from the verse "another person's field." One might ask whether that verse perhaps excludes only the tortfeasor's own domain. The Rambam therefore first explains that no verse is needed for that case. Logic alone exempts him, because he can say, "Had you not entered my domain, you would not have been injured." The verse is therefore available to exclude a domain shared by both.
The rule that he is exempt in the tortfeasor's domain, etc. This is an independent subject. The Tur likewise writes it after explaining that if the injured person entered the tortfeasor's domain and was injured, the tortfeasor is exempt. The Tur then discusses workers who enter the owner's domain and explains that sometimes the owner is liable and sometimes exempt. The Rosh concludes that nowadays a householder ordinarily does not send his workers to a shopkeeper for payment but pays them in cash, and it is not customary to pay them in the marketplace but at home, where his money is kept. Therefore workers entering the house for payment are always considered to have entered with permission, and the householder is liable for injuries caused to them. See the Tur.
