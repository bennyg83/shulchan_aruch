(In the Tur, seif 11) The Mishnah in Kol HaNishba’in states: If one said to a shopkeeper, “Give me produce for one dinar,” and the shopkeeper gave it to him, and then said, “Give me that dinar,” while the customer answered, “I gave it to you,” the customer swears that he gave the dinar. If the customer said, “Give me the produce,” and the shopkeeper answered, “I gave it to you,” the shopkeeper swears. The Gemara cites a baraita: Rabbi Yehudah said, “When does this apply? When the produce is piled and lies between them and they dispute over it. But if the customer has slung it behind him in his basket, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.” Since the Tur generally follows Tosafot and his father the Rosh, their approach should be stated. They asked how the Mishnah could require an oath, since the hesset oath had not yet been enacted. They answered that in the first clause the shopkeeper measured the produce into the customer’s vessels, but the vessels and produce still remained in the shopkeeper’s domain. In the second clause the customer gave a dinar to the shopkeeper, but the shopkeeper had not yet taken possession of it. The novelty is therefore that each claimant may swear and collect. The Mishnah concerns those who swear and collect. Rabbi Yehudah disagrees, holding that a shopkeeper does not normally measure produce unless he has first received the dinar, and therefore the customer takes without an oath. Accordingly, the baraita means: “When did the Sages disagree with me? When the produce is piled between them. But where the customer has slung it behind him, they agree with me that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.” After citing the Mishnah and baraita, the Rosh writes: “But if he slung it behind him, the Sages agree that the customer does not swear; the law follows the Sages where the produce is piled and lies between them.” It is clear that he follows Tosafot, namely that Rabbi Yehudah comes to disagree with the Sages and that their dispute is whether the customer swears and collects. This is unlike Beit Yosef, who says that the Rosh follows Rif and that Rabbi Yehudah merely explains the Mishnah; Beit Yosef’s reading of “they agree with him” as a loose expression is very strained. This also explains the Tur. When he says that the shopkeeper “measured,” he means into the customer’s vessels, as Tosafot wrote. Otherwise, why mention measuring at all? His statement that the produce remained in the shop follows Tosafot. They explain that one swears and collects because the goods have partly left the other party’s possession. The Tur likewise says that a shopkeeper does not ordinarily remove his produce, meaning that he does not remove it at all from his shop, whereas here it has partly left his possession. In the second clause the customer has partly removed the dinar from his own possession, even though the shopkeeper has not yet taken it. Although the reason that a shopkeeper does not ordinarily measure before payment is Rabbi Yehudah’s reason, the first tanna also accepts the factual premise. Rabbi Yehudah therefore exempts the customer without an oath, while the first tanna allows him to swear and collect rather than requiring the shopkeeper to swear. We should therefore rule according to Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur, whose path we ordinarily follow. Sema also writes that another approach appears correct to him, and perhaps he intended what I have written. [The author’s gloss:] I saw Shach forcefully attack the earlier authorities and claim that the great scholars failed to understand the passage of “Give me produce for one dinar.” He would imprison fixed halachot and freely declare the earlier authorities mistaken, asserting that he alone established the correct path. I have therefore undertaken to defend the earlier authorities, from whose words we live and who are our lights, and to answer Shach’s arguments one by one. First, Shach calls Raavad’s interpretation very forced, because it places the produce in the customer’s hands in the first clause and leaves it piled in the second. There is no difficulty. The Mishnah itself says in the first clause, “and he gave it to him,” meaning to the customer, while it omits those words in the second clause. Rabbi Yehudah’s baraita makes the distinction still more explicit by saying, “When the produce is piled,” etc. Shach further objects that Rabbi Yehudah’s phrase “when does this apply” cannot partly explain the second clause and partly disagree with the first. This is no objection. The first tanna taught two laws: that the customer swears when he has slung the produce behind him, and that the shopkeeper swears where the produce is piled. Rabbi Yehudah says, “I agree with you in one, the piled-produce case, and disagree in the other, the slung-produce case.” The same structure appears in the law of a hired worker, where the Sages say that the worker swears whether the employer partially admits or denies everything; Rabbi Yehudah agrees in partial admission and disagrees in total denial. The expression “when does this apply” fits such a formulation. Other Talmudic uses differ only because there agreement and dispute occur within one clause. Shach promised a decisive objection later, but there too his argument has no substance. Raavad’s approach is therefore clear and correct. Second, Rashi’s approach can likewise be reconciled with Raavad. Rashi’s brief language may mean that when Rabbi Yehudah says, “when the produce is piled, the customer swears,” he disagrees with the first tanna, who would have the shopkeeper swear. Rabbi Yehudah reasons that since a shopkeeper who does not sell on credit does not ordinarily measure before receiving payment, the customer’s claim is stronger. The first tanna does not accept that inference and therefore requires the shopkeeper to swear. This reading is especially plausible because the reason “a shopkeeper does not ordinarily,” etc., is stated only in Rabbi Yehudah’s words. The first tanna therefore need not accept it. The Mishnah’s second clause, “the shopkeeper swears,” can then concern produce piled in the public domain. If it were still entirely in the shopkeeper’s store, why would the first tanna require him to swear? The reason that a shopkeeper does not ordinarily part with produce before payment belongs to Rabbi Yehudah. Where the produce is piled in the public domain, however, the ruling is understandable. Thus Rashi too follows Raavad on this point. Our duty is to reconcile authorities where possible, not multiply disputes. Rashi’s statement on the Mishnah that Rabbi Yehudah disagrees with the first clause but not the second is no contradiction. It means only that Rabbi Yehudah’s Mishnaic phrase, “whoever holds the produce has the stronger hand,” can linguistically apply only to the first clause. In the baraita, however, he may indeed disagree with the second clause as well. A similar phenomenon occurs at the beginning of Eruvin regarding a mavoi higher than twenty cubits or wider than ten cubits. All the more so here, where a fuller formulation would have required length. Even under Shach’s own standards his objections fail. If Rabbi Yehudah exempts the customer in the stronger case where the Sages require him to swear after slinging the produce behind him, certainly in the case where the Sages require the shopkeeper to swear, Rabbi Yehudah can require the shopkeeper to swear, because the customer has already given the dinar and the shopkeeper now says that he gave the produce long before payment and that the customer carried it home, conduct contrary to ordinary commercial practice. This is an unanswerable a fortiori argument. Shach’s linguistic objections were already raised and satisfactorily answered by Raavad and Tosafot. Talmudic teachings often explain a later clause before an earlier one. Rashi’s approach therefore remains clear and clean. Third, as to Rabbenu Tam’s interpretation cited by Tosafot, there is no need to answer objections Tosafot themselves already raised, only Shach’s new arguments. Where one case illuminates another, the Gemara need not repeat every detail. Shach asks why the Sages treat the shopkeeper differently from a moneychanger. The parties do not dispute ordinary reality. The Sages may hold that although a shopkeeper does not usually give produce before receiving the coin, occasionally he does so. Shach asks why the Gemara did not formulate the necessity of both cases differently. The Gemara’s formulation is preferable because it states the central practical distinction: a shopkeeper often extends credit because produce may spoil. Similar answers apply in the reverse direction. Talmudic style normally states the first available necessity, and in any event even the Sages agree that a moneychanger does not ordinarily extend credit. Shach’s promised later objection is likewise answered below. Fourth, regarding Rif, Rambam, and those who follow them, Shach objects that “he gave it to him” and Rabbi Yehudah’s phrase “in his hand” imply actual possession. Ran already answered that because produce is not ordinarily left in the public domain before a completed sale, produce set apart there is treated as in the customer’s hand. Shach says Rif’s reading might cause confusion in the second clause, but there is no confusion because Rabbi Yehudah necessarily comes to disagree. Nor is Rabbi Yehudah’s general expression, “whoever has the produce in his hand has the stronger hand,” problematic. The Mishnah at the beginning of HaZahav similarly says, “whoever has the money in his hand has the stronger hand,” although the practical dispute concerns only the seller’s right to retract. It could have said more briefly, “the purchaser cannot retract,” yet used the general formula. This also answers the preceding objection. The phrase “he slung them in his basket” is needed because otherwise one might think the produce must actually be carried into his house. The request “Give me the produce” is parallel to “Give me the dinar,” because so long as the produce is not legally in the customer’s domain he may demand it. Rif’s interpretation is therefore defensible. Semag’s explanation, however, is exceedingly difficult insofar as it places both parties’ hands upon the dinar in the second clause, for why should the shopkeeper’s power be stronger than the customer’s? Perhaps Semag follows a different premise: both shopkeepers and moneychangers sometimes extend credit. Once the dinar has partly left the customer’s possession, circumstances indicate that he already received the produce or small coins, so the shopkeeper or moneychanger has the stronger claim. This inference does not help them after the produce has left their possession and lies in the public domain, for the customer has already acquired it. Rabbi Yehudah disagrees regarding produce only in the first clause; regarding a moneychanger he disagrees in both clauses, exempting the customer in the first and denying the moneychanger collection in the second when both hold the dinar. This explains Semag’s changed language. We now examine Shach’s own approach. His proofs do not compel it; indeed, the interpretation is impossible. His argument that the tanna unnecessarily lengthened the customer’s statement, “I placed it in your pouch,” is weak. Mishnayot often include realistic detail to strengthen a speaker’s assertion, as in Ketubot, “What is the status of this man? He is a kohen and my father’s brother’s son,” although the precise kinship is irrelevant. The tanna speaks in the ordinary manner. Shach’s further question, why the tanna does not expressly say that the shopkeeper denies the customer as in the case of the shopkeeper’s ledger, is astonishing. The Mishnah already states that the shopkeeper or moneychanger demands the dinar, which necessarily means that he denies receiving it. In the ledger case, by contrast, the Mishnah must expressly state that the shopkeeper and workers contradict one another. More fundamentally, Shach reads the Mishnah as an uncertain claim by the shopkeeper, because the dispute arose while the parties were still in the shop and the customer had not yet carried the produce home, even though it was slung behind him. If the shopkeeper has only an uncertain claim, why should the customer swear? Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah hold that when one says, “You owe me one hundred,” and the other says, “I do not know,” the certain claim prevails, and the claimant collects without an oath. Ketubot and Bava Kamma show this plainly. Likewise in the borrower case, the Mishnah’s liability is understood without an oath, as shown by the contrast with the later clause, “the renter swears.” It is implausible to say that Abaye’s argument proceeds through an unstated intermediate stage based upon presumptions. If even one who seeks to extract money from a fully established possessor can collect without an oath when his claim is certain and the possessor’s uncertain, then where the produce has entirely left the shopkeeper’s possession, the customer should certainly retain it on his definite claim without swearing against the shopkeeper’s uncertainty. The special uncertain-claim oaths enumerated by the Sages rest upon the concern that the holder rationalizes his conduct; no such reason exists here. This is a decisive objection to Shach’s interpretation. It would also have been necessary to list this among those who swear without a definite claim. Shach’s proof from the Jerusalem Talmud is not compelling. The Jerusalem Talmud often speaks elliptically. Its statement means: Rabbi Hiyya says that their dispute concerns a basket lying between them, where the Sages require an oath and Rabbi Yehudah exempts. But if it has left the hand of one of them, can there still be a dispute? Certainly not. This reading fits the Jerusalem Talmud’s use of “still,” which is unnecessary and even contrary to Shach’s reading, for according to him there is no dispute while the basket lies between them and the dispute arises only when it leaves one party’s hand. The continuation also treats produce and money under one rationale and explains the lodging-house case with reference to the basket between them. Thus the Jerusalem Talmud supports, rather than defeats, the earlier interpretations. It is astonishing to suggest that Rif, who constantly cites the Jerusalem Talmud and cites it in this very chapter, overlooked it, or that Tosafot and the other Geonim did so. They omitted it because it neither refutes nor proves any particular reading. The Tosefta likewise does not refute Rif merely because it does not repeat Rabbi Yehudah’s phrase, “whoever has the produce in his hand.” Mishnah and baraita often supplement one another, one lengthy and the other brief. This particular Tosefta is plainly not exact in every formulation, for it gives a reading regarding whether workers swear only in the shopkeeper’s presence that differs from the Bavli; were it a separate ruling, the Bavli should have cited it. Shach’s linguistic objection to Rashi and Raavad, that “if it left the hand of one of them” and “the burden of proof rests upon the claimant” must apply symmetrically to either party, also fails. At the beginning of HaZahav the Mishnah says generally, “whoever holds the money,” though it effectively refers to the seller. In Ketubot the baraita says, “the burden of proof rests upon the claimant,” even where under ordinary circumstances one particular party is the claimant; the Gemara itself explains that the phrase follows the usual case. Here too Rashi applies Rabbi Yehudah to the first clause because the referenced basket exists there; in the second clause the shopkeeper claims that the produce was previously carried home and there is no basket between them. The general language therefore follows the usual facts. The same answers Shach’s objection to Raavad that Rabbi Yehudah should have said only, “if it left the customer’s hand, he is exempt.” General Mishnaic formulations often encompass the practical rule without specifying the usual party. Since all the earlier authorities can be maintained and Shach’s own interpretation cannot, the established law remains crowned in its place. Shach may retain the novelty he personally introduced, but all the sages of Israel disagree with his interpretation. We therefore return to the general rule that no oath is imposed on an uncertain claim except in the cases expressly enumerated by the Sages. There is no need here to elaborate upon every derivative law.
