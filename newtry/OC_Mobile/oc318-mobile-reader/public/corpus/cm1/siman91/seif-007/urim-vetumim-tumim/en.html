If he did not set, etc., a fixed amount. The Tur follows Baal HaTerumot, who gives as the reason the chazakah that an agent carries out his agency. The difficulty is that Baal HaTerumot follows his own view that where the shopkeeper alone sues and the workers do not contradict him, the shopkeeper is entitled by strict law to collect without an oath by virtue of that chazakah. The same chazakah can therefore operate here. But the Tur earlier sided with the Ramah that the employer never pays the shopkeeper without an oath, because by strict law the employer owes the shopkeeper nothing. How then can the Tur rule here that he collects without an oath and invoke the very presumption he rejected? The Bach, Sema, and Shach, citing Maharshal, answer that here it is known that the shopkeeper paid at least part of the amount, and only the total is uncertain. In such a case, they say, even the Tur agrees that he may collect without an oath. I do not understand the distinction. Regarding the remainder the employer can still say, “I do not know whether I ever became indebted to you.” If the answer is that partial certainty creates the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, Baal HaTerumot himself writes explicitly, gate 59, part 2, ruling 8, that here the employer had no reason to know, and I explained this above. The Derishah already calls this answer strained, and the Taz likewise objects that the Tur should not have stated the reason as “an agent carries out his agency.” It seems instead that the resolution is what I wrote above in explaining the Ran. The Tur also accepts that there is a meaningful presumption that an agent performs his mission. But he agrees with the Ramban that ordinarily the shopkeeper spoiled his own agency by failing to pay before witnesses. How can he invoke the chazakah of performing the agency when he performed it improperly by paying without witnesses? The Gemara’s statement in Bava Metzia that the employer did not tell him to pay before witnesses is itself part of the special enactment, an additional burden the Sages placed on the employer. The Tur holds that this applies where the employer gave a definite instruction, such as, “Give the worker one sela.” In that case the employer did not indicate special trust in the worker. The shopkeeper likewise should not have trusted him, because workers under pressure for wages may sometimes demand payment twice, and he should have paid before witnesses. But if the employer tells the shopkeeper, “Give the workers whatever amount they demand from you,” he has necessarily demonstrated that he trusts those workers. They might otherwise demand more than their true wages and say, “You fixed a higher wage,” or “You owed us from before,” or “We already returned money to you.” A person does not expose his funds to such claims unless he regards the workers as trustworthy. Once the shopkeeper sees that the employer himself trusts them, he is not at fault for paying without witnesses. The ordinary presumption that an agent performs his agency therefore remains intact, and he is believed without an oath. This resolves the Tur well.
Without an oath means that he does not require even hesset, because there is no definite claim against him, contrary to the Sema. The authorities ask why this differs from one who incurred authorized expenditures, where, when one party knows and the other does not, the one who knows must swear while holding a sacred object. The Shach writes that Baal HaTerumot and the Tur explain that because the employer left the amount dependent on what the workers would request, it is treated as though the amount had been fixed. I still do not understand the distinction between saying, “Go improve the field with whatever work it requires,” and saying, “Give the workers whatever they ask.” What conceptual difference is there? The Taz raises the same difficulty. It seems necessary to say that here the employer knows the workers’ actual wage obligation; he simply did not specify to the shopkeeper how much to give because he did not know whether the workers would collect the entire amount from him. Once the shopkeeper says that he paid the amount of their wages, there is a presumption that they took what was due. Why should the workers gratuitously waive any portion, and why did they not return to the employer if unpaid? The employer also suffers no unexpected loss, because this is an amount he truly owed. In the field case, by contrast, perhaps the improvement did not require all the claimed expenditures. There is no comparable presumption, so the Sages required an oath. According to this distinction, if the shopkeeper claims that he paid the workers more than the amount of their wages, there is a real doubt whether the workers took more than they were entitled to, and he certainly must swear. That is like one who incurred expenditures. Likewise, if the employer told the shopkeeper, “Give the workers whatever food and drink they require,” and the shopkeeper says that he spent a dinar, he must swear like one who incurred expenses, because perhaps they did not consume that much and the employer would lose from his own pocket. This is exactly analogous to one who incurred expenses improving his wife’s property, who cannot collect without an oath. This may also explain the Rama’s wording in the gloss, “Some say that even if the workers,” etc. The word “even” appears odd, because it implies that an oath may be required even when the workers are absent, which ordinarily is not true. According to what I have written, however, the Rama speaks where the shopkeeper claims that he gave more than the amount properly due. Then even without any contradiction he must swear, because he is like one who incurred expenses. The Rama therefore adds that even when the workers contradict him, where one might have thought the shopkeeper cannot collect at all, he may at least collect by oath. This is conceptually sound. Yet Baal HaTerumot, at the end of that ruling, appears to say that the shopkeeper collects without an oath even when he gave more than the workers were entitled to. Perhaps one can distinguish that a person who enters an arrangement of authorized expenditures knows from the outset that if he later seeks reimbursement he will need an oath; he enters on that basis. Here, however, the shopkeeper did not enter on the basis of an oath. He understood from the employer’s unrestricted instruction that the workers were trusted and would not take more than was proper, so he never contemplated having to swear. This still requires study. Were I not hesitant, I would say that Baal HaTerumot understands the Mishnah’s rule that one who incurred expenses must swear to refer to a husband who was not expressly instructed by his wife, but who acted on his own under the authority granted to him by rabbinic law to improve her property. The Sages granted him that authority subject to an oath. Here, however, the employer expressly instructed the shopkeeper to pay. What basis is there to impose an oath afterward when the principal himself authorized the expenditure? This too requires study.
