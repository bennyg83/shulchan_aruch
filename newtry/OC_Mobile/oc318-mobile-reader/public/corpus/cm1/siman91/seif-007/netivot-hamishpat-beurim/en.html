He takes a shevuat heset that he did not instruct him. See Shach seif katan 26, who disagrees with the Mechaber and rules that there is neither a d'oraisa oath nor a shevuat heset because that would amount to an oath upon an oath. In my view the Mechaber is primary, and it appears that even the Shach can agree once the issue is properly framed, so there is no true dispute. Ran in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, in the sugya of a loan secured by a pledge, discusses a claimant who demands fifty for a loan and fifty for bodily injury while the injured party has not yet sworn concerning the injury and the defendant denies everything. Although the claimant can establish the fifty for injury by swearing, the defendant is presently exempt from an oath because that fifty is still only a cause that may lead to money: who says the claimant will in fact swear? But after the injured party swears concerning the injury and continues to claim the additional fifty for the loan, while the defendant continues to deny it, Ran writes that the defendant is liable under Rabbi Chiyya's first rule. Once the injured person swears, it is as though witnesses establish that fifty is owed for the injury. Although no witnesses existed at the moment of the initial claim, the later evidence creates the partial-admission structure. Ran distinguishes this from a defendant's own admission that initially emerged in exemption, because there the subsequent liability would be based upon his earlier admission; here liability is based upon later evidentiary proof. Ran thus states explicitly that even if the claimant had not yet sworn at the time of suit, once he later swears the case becomes one of modeh bemiktzat. Shach in siman 87 seif katan 13 writes that this applies only if he had already sworn, not if he merely proposes to swear now. With respect, it appears that he saw the middle of Ran's discussion but not its conclusion. Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 87 likewise recognizes the point, and it is straightforward. The same applies here. If the shopkeeper sues the employer and says, "You instructed me to pay," the employer denies it, and the shopkeeper has not yet sworn that he paid the workers, then the shopkeeper cannot yet impose either the d'oraisa oath of modeh bemiktzat or an oath based on a monetary denial. At that stage the employer's denial concerns only something that may lead to money, because perhaps the shopkeeper will never swear, exactly as Ran writes regarding the injured claimant. But if the shopkeeper is prepared to swear now, or has already sworn that he paid the workers, while the employer continues to deny that he instructed him and the shopkeeper continues his monetary demand, then the employer must swear that he gave no instruction. The denial is now a genuine denial of money. Were he to admit the instruction, he would have to pay because the shopkeeper has satisfied the required oath. Just as an admission in such circumstances becomes a monetary admission once the claimant has sworn, so too the continuing denial becomes a monetary denial. All poskim, including the Shach, can agree. The Shach did not distinguish the cases because he follows his statement in siman 87 in Ran's name that if the claimant had not yet sworn at the time of the claim, even his willingness to swear later does not create a monetary denial. As explained, Ran's concluding words were overlooked. Once the claimant's oath converts the matter into money, the defendant's continued denial is a monetary denial. The principle that "there is no oath upon an oath" applies where even if the defendant admitted the allegation, his admission would obligate him only to take another oath. For example, if one claims, "You were my partner," and the defendant denies the partnership, even an admission would create only the partners' oath. Requiring an oath merely over whether another oath is due is an oath upon an oath. But where the claimant himself has an oath to take and has already taken it, the defendant's denial is now about actual money, not another oath. A single witness is different. There the Shach's result is correct. The rule is that if two witnesses would not obligate the defendant to pay solely through their testimony but only when combined with an oath by the claimant, then one witness does not obligate the defendant to swear. Here, even if two witnesses testified that the employer instructed the shopkeeper to pay, the employer would not have to pay until the shopkeeper swore that he actually paid the workers. Therefore a single witness to the instruction does not create the one-witness d'oraisa oath. Even after the shopkeeper has sworn, we do not combine the claimant's oath with the single witness's statement to manufacture a new witness-based oath where two witnesses alone would not have imposed payment at the outset. Modeh bemiktzat is different. Whenever the admission and denial have become admissions and denials of money, the Torah oath applies. Even if originally they concerned only a contingent obligation, once the claimant swears and they become monetary, the oath can arise, as Ran teaches. Tumim explains the Mechaber by saying that payment to the shopkeeper is due by strict law and only the shopkeeper's oath is a rabbinic enactment, so the employer's denial is already monetary. Ketzot HaChoshen follows him. But I wrote above that even according to the view that the shopkeeper's payment is ordinarily by strict law, that is only where the employer will make one payment, under the Shach's reasoning that there is no ordinary chazakah of money because the employer certainly must pay someone. Where he must make two payments, all agree that the shopkeeper's second recovery is by enactment. The employer stands against him like one who says, "I do not know whether I ever became obligated," which is why the shopkeeper's heirs do not collect. Once the shopkeeper has sworn, however, the payment becomes a strict monetary liability, comparable to one who descended into another's property with permission, where the other party trusted him subject to the required oath. In that posture the employer's denial of instruction is a genuine monetary denial and the appropriate oath applies.
