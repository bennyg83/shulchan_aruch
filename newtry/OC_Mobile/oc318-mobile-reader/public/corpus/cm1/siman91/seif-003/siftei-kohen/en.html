In the case of a shopkeeper who extends credit, etc. In that case, even if at the time payment became due the employer directed the workers to the shopkeeper, and they agreed and released the employer, they may later retract, as stated below in siman 126. However, if from the outset, at the time they were hired, the employer directed them to the shopkeeper, and they agreed to receive payment from the shopkeeper, they have no claim against the employer. The Bach rules this in seif 4. Although the Bach offered this as an interpretation of Rabbeinu Yeshayah, that interpretation is incorrect. Rather, Rabbeinu Yeshayah appears to follow the Jerusalem Talmud cited by the Rif in HaMekabel and the view that establishes the Mishnah there where the shopkeeper was not present, as the Rif records. The Tur’s objection that Rabbeinu Yeshayah’s view is unconvincing is because the Tur follows the Rif there, as does the Rosh, and this is our ruling below in siman 126. The Beit Yosef writes that according to Rabbeinu Yeshayah the employer is released even if the arrangement was made outside the shopkeeper’s presence. I do not understand the basis for such a ruling unless Rabbeinu Yeshayah followed Rav Sheshet in HaMekabel, which would be surprising. My explanation is therefore preferable. Nevertheless, the Bach’s substantive ruling may be correct. Examine carefully.
“And I will give the workers a rock.” For the last explanation, I will give the workers a rock that I have in your hand and with you.”
And the workers agreed. The term “agreed” is not literal, according to the Sema. Whenever the workers remained silent and did not expressly say, “We have no dealings with the shopkeeper,” they are considered to have agreed. Otherwise, why does the seif conclude, “But if this was not in the presence of all three”? It should instead say, “But if they did not agree.” My teacher and father, of blessed memory, wrote in the margin of his Sema that this proof is invalid, because the seif teaches that even where they expressly agreed outside the shopkeeper’s presence, they may retract. He was correct. The Sema’s own explanation leaves the same difficulty, for the seif should have said, “But if they did not agree,” meaning that they did not remain silent. Necessarily, the seif distinguishes between different forms of agreement. The Mechaber himself writes below in siman 126, seif 9, that according to some authorities, namely the Rosh and those who follow him, the creditor may retract so long as he did not expressly release the original debtor. This means that he said, “I release you and will rely upon the shopkeeper,” as explained there, and the Rama rules accordingly. Necessarily, “the workers agreed” here means that they expressly released the employer. It is possible that even the first view there agrees that an express agreement is required, and that mere silence is insufficient. At the beginning of the seif, concerning a shopkeeper who extends credit, even if the workers expressly released the employer, they may retract, as Rabbeinu Yerucham and the Rama write below in siman 126, seif 2, unless they released the employer completely. In that event, they have no further claim against him, as explained there.
The workers have no claim against the employer, etc. The Sema writes that this is true even according to the Rosh and those who follow him below in siman 126, seif 2, who rule that one directed to a third party in the presence of all three may retract. There, the designated payer admits that he has not yet paid the recipient and merely postpones him from day to day. Here, however, the shopkeeper says that he already paid the worker what the employer instructed him to pay. In my humble opinion, this distinction is incorrect. If the worker may retract, the employer’s obligation remains. What difference does it make that the designated payer claims to have paid him? This is clear from the fact that the Rif, Rosh, and Tosafot in HaMekabel had to explain that the Jerusalem Talmud places the Mishnah of the shopkeeper upon his ledger where the employer did not direct the workers to the shopkeeper. Had he directed them to the shopkeeper, the employer would owe them nothing, because the Jerusalem Talmud follows Rav Sheshet rather than Rabbah, although we rule like Rabbah that a person directed to a shopkeeper may retract. According to the Sema’s distinction, they could have said that the Jerusalem Talmud accords with the accepted law, and that the case is different because the shopkeeper says he already paid them. Although the Jerusalem Talmud concerns a shopkeeper who extends credit, it still compares the matter to the dispute between Rabbah and Rav Sheshet. It should have distinguished between retraction before payment and a claim of payment, as the Ba’al HaItur expressly writes in the section on assignment. He rules that the recipient may retract unless he expressly released the original debtor, and that the Jerusalem Talmud applies even according to the view permitting retraction because there the shopkeeper says, “I paid you.” If it is known that he did not pay, the dispute between Rabbah and Rav Sheshet applies. The Rif, Rosh, and Tosafot evidently rejected this distinction. Examine carefully. The Bach in this siman, beginning of seif 4, and in siman 126, seif 12, likewise clearly implies that no such distinction exists. He writes only that the Tur here follows the Rif rather than the Rosh. In my humble opinion, that conclusion is not necessary. The phrase “the workers agreed” here may mean that they expressly released the employer, saying, “We release you and will rely upon the shopkeeper,” as I wrote in the preceding seif katan. Because these rules are not the principal subject here, the Tur did not elaborate and relied upon his fuller discussion below. The Mechaber may be understood the same way.

And some say that this case too, etc. This is difficult. These “some say” necessarily disagree with the Rif, who writes in HaIsh Mekadesh that the creditor’s oath is taken while holding a sacred object. The Ba’al HaTerumot in gate 29, part 2, likewise states that Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash and Rabbi Ephraim disagree with the Rif. In gate 50, part 3, he cites the Ra’avad in language implying that the two cases have the same law. The Ran in HaIsh Mekadesh also implies this, and the matter is straightforward. It is therefore difficult that below in siman 121, seif 9, the Mechaber rules without qualification according to the Rif and cites no dissenting view, whereas here he cites the opposing view. As a matter of law, it appears that here too the claimant must swear while holding a sacred object, as ruled below in siman 121. Examine carefully.
When the defendant does not know how much was spent, the claimant swears and collects. The Sema writes that Rama states this without qualification, saying that the claimant swears and collects. He understands this to depend upon the dispute above in seif 2 concerning workers who died and a shopkeeper who alone seeks payment. According to the Mechaber there, the shopkeeper need not take a stringent oath, whereas according to the dissenting view cited by Rama he must do so here as well. The Sema adds, however, that according to Maharshal, cited below in seif 7, one must distinguish between the cases. There, perhaps the shopkeeper paid the workers nothing at all, whereas here it is certain that expenses were incurred and only their amount is unknown. Therefore even according to that view a hesset oath suffices. This is incorrect in two respects. First, when the Mechaber rules in seif 2 that he collects without an oath, this means even without a hesset oath, as the annotator and Beit Yosef state explicitly from Baal HaTerumot, cited below. Second, Rama's ruling here that he swears and collects refers to a stringent oath while holding a sacred object, and all agree to that here. This is explicit in Maharik, who writes that justice requires him to swear how much he spent and collect, as the Mordechai states in the chapter HaIshah Shenaflu. That language places the case among those who swear and collect in tractate Shevuot. The same is explicit in the responsa appended to Maimonides. The distinction is straightforward. In seif 2, because there is a presumption that an agent performs his agency, the shopkeeper collects without an oath. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes that since the shopkeeper is taught together with the worker, the two have the same law. Just as a worker collects without any oath, even hesset, when no shopkeeper contradicts him, so too a shopkeeper collects without an oath when no worker contradicts him. The cases of one who spent money on his wife's property, one who recognizes his utensils and books in another's possession, and similar cases, where the claimant swears and collects despite there being no one who contradicts him, are different. In the shopkeeper's case there are two supporting factors, the presumption that an agent performs his agency and a definite claim against an uncertain response. In the other cases there is only a definite claim against an uncertain response, without the presumption. Therefore a stringent oath is required lest the claimant inflate the amount spent. His words also clearly imply that whenever one party knows and the other does not, and the knowledgeable party swears and collects, the oath is a stringent oath while holding a sacred object. Whenever the presumption that an agent performs his agency is absent, Baal HaTerumot compares the case to expenses on a wife's property, where the oath is a Mishnaic oath. This is also explained in Even HaEzer 88:7, and below in Choshen Mishpat 158:8 and 375:8. Cited cross-references in the source include: 80, 86, 150, 370, 707.
The same applies to every similar matter. The Sema refers to siman 14, where Rama, based on a responsum of the Rosh, rules concerning a recalcitrant litigant against whom expenses were incurred that the claimant must prove how much he spent and is not believed merely by taking an oath. There, however, the expenses were imposed against the recalcitrant party's will, whereas here the claimant spent money for the other party's benefit. For the same reason the Tur writes in siman 93:15, “Regarding everything that Reuven proves by witnesses,” because there too the expenditure was not for the other party's benefit, even though it was necessarily incurred. My father and teacher, the Gaon, of blessed memory, wrote that here the expenditure was initially made with the other party's authorization, and by entrusting the matter to him the other party accepted his credibility under oath. He means that this case is unlike simanim 14 and 93, where the expenditure was not made with the other party's authorization. This is correct. See Responsa Maharshal, simanim 34 and 172.
