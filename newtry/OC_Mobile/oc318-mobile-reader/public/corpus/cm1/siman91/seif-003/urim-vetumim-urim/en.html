With a shopkeeper who extends credit. Shach and Bach write that if, when the workers were hired, both the shopkeeper and the workers agreed to this arrangement, everyone agrees that the workers can no longer return against the employer. See Tumim, where I wrote that this is not convincing unless they expressly stipulated, by a legally effective monetary condition, that the workers would have no claim against the employer. Such a stipulation is included in the rule that every monetary condition is valid. See there.
And he said to him, 'Give the workers a sela.' Below, in siman 126, there is a dispute whether he must say, 'Give a sela from what you have in your possession,' or whether it is enough to say simply, 'Give a sela.' The same dispute applies here, as Shach writes.
And the workers agreed. Sema holds that even if they said nothing and merely remained silent, this constitutes a ma'amad sheloshtan acquisition. See below, siman 126, and what I wrote in Tumim.
The workers have no claim against the employer. Sema writes that even according to Rosh, who holds below in siman 126 that the creditor may nevertheless return against the original debtor, that applies only where the shopkeeper does not say that he already paid them. If the shopkeeper claims that he already paid, all agree that the workers have no claim against the original debtor. Shach challenges this and writes that according to that view they may nevertheless return against the employer unless they explicitly released him, as stated below in siman 126. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length, answered all of Shach's objections, and demonstrated from Tosafos and Rosh that Sema's position appears correct. That is the primary ruling.
He takes a hesset oath. He swears hesset to the workers, for their legal claim is against him. They have no claim at all against the employer, and the employer likewise has no claim against the shopkeeper, as Sema explains.
The shopkeeper swears to the employer. Shach writes that the workers must swear first, for until they swear the employer has no claim against the shopkeeper. His words are not compelling, because Baal HaTerumos explicitly writes that the shopkeeper swears first. See Tumim, seif katan 6.
For this too is hesset. Since the employer now makes only one payment, and is uncertain whether the shopkeeper paid the workers, only hesset is required. Shach leaves this difficult because below, siman 121 seif 9, where a debtor sends an agent to pay a creditor, the agent says that he paid and the creditor says that he did not receive payment, the Mechaber rules without qualification that the agent must swear while holding a sacred object. See Tumim for what I wrote explaining why there is greater reason here to require only hesset.
The claimant swears. Sema thought to compare this rule to that above in seif 2. According to the view that when the workers are not claiming their wages and the employer need not make two payments, the shopkeeper is exempt from an oath, here too only hesset would be required. According to the view that the shopkeeper must nevertheless swear, here too an oath while holding a sacred object would be required. Shach challenges this, for above the exempting view exempts even from hesset. Rather, Baal HaTerumos explains that the cases are not comparable. There, with the shopkeeper, there are two factors, a definite claim against an uncertain claim, and the presumption that an agent performs his agency. Here that presumption is absent, and therefore all agree that the claimant must swear while holding a sacred object. This is correct. At first glance one might infer that if a person authorizes another to undertake some matter and spend up to one hundred zuz, and the agent does so and demands the full one hundred zuz, he should collect without an oath and the principal should be unable to impose an oath, because here too there is the presumption that an agent performed his agency. In truth, however, the cases may be distinguished. There the worker certainly would not waive any portion of his wage, and the employer instructed that the entire wage be paid, so there is a presumption that the agent did what he was sent to do. Here, however, if the matter could have been accomplished for less, the principal never authorized the agent to spend more than necessary. That presumption therefore does not apply. See what I wrote in Tumim.
In every matter where this one knows, etc. Sema explains that the rule applies specifically where the expenditure was for the other person's benefit. But where a recalcitrant litigant caused his opponent to incur expenses, the opponent is not believed to collect merely by swearing, because the expenses were not incurred for the recalcitrant party's benefit. Shach answers that here the expenditure was made with the other person's authorization, whereas there it was not made with his authorization, even though it was authorized by beit din. See what I wrote below in siman 93 seif 13.
