And pays. In that case, even if at the time of payment the workers were directed to the shopkeeper in the presence of all three, accepted the arrangement, and released the employer, they may still retract, as stated in siman 126. However, if at the outset, when the workers were hired, they were directed to the shopkeeper and agreed to receive payment from him, they have no claim against the employer. The Bach rules accordingly.
Agreed. The Sema writes that this is not literal. Any workers who remained silent and did not expressly say, "We want no dealings with the shopkeeper," are considered to have agreed. Otherwise, why does the Mechaber continue, "but if it was not in the presence of all three"? He should simply have said, "if they did not agree." The Shach cites his father's response that this is not the reason. The ruling teaches that even if they agreed outside the shopkeeper's presence, they can retract. This is correct, because even according to the Sema the latter clause should have said, "if they did not agree," meaning that they did not remain silent. Rather, the distinction is within cases where they did agree. The Mechaber himself states in siman 126, seif 9, that according to one opinion the assignee can retract so long as he did not expressly release the original debtor, and the Rama rules accordingly. Therefore, "agreed" here must mean that they explicitly released the employer. So writes the Shach. The Taz agrees.
Anything. The Sema writes that even according to the Rosh, cited by the Tur in siman 126, who holds that an assignee may retract even after an assignment in the presence of all three, this case differs. There the assignor admits that he has not yet given the assignee anything. Here, however, the shopkeeper says that he already gave the worker what the employer instructed him to give. The Shach objects: What difference does the assignor's claim of payment make if the worker's obligation still remains upon the employer because the worker may retract? See there for his proof from the Rif and Rosh.
Swears. He swears to the workers and is thereby exempt from them. Toward the employer he need not swear, since he does not seek to collect from the employer. Sema.
Shevuat heset. The Shach questions this. These authorities necessarily disagree with the Rif, who writes that the lender's oath is taken while holding a sacred object. Baal HaTerumot states the same explicitly and implies that the two cases have the same law, as does the Ran. The Shach therefore rules that here too the oath must be taken while holding a sacred object, as stated in siman 121.
And collects. The Rama states simply that he swears and collects. The Sema suggests that this depends upon the dispute in seif 2 where the workers died. According to the Mechaber there, the shopkeeper need not take a severe oath; according to the Rama's cited opinion, here too he must take a severe oath. Yet in light of Maharshal cited below in seif 7, one can distinguish. There it is uncertain whether the shopkeeper gave the workers anything at all, whereas here it is certain that he incurred expenses and only the amount is unknown. Therefore a shevuat heset suffices even according to the stricter opinion. The Shach rejects both points. In seif 2 the meaning is that he collects even without a shevuat heset. Here, by contrast, the Rama's "swears and collects" means a severe oath while holding a sacred object, and all agree. The distinction is that in seif 2 there is a presumption that an agent performs his agency, so he collects without an oath. Baal HaTerumot states this explicitly. Where there is no such presumption, the case is compared to a wife claiming from her husband's estate, whose oath is the Mishnah oath. See Even HaEzer 88, seif 7, and Choshen Mishpat 158, seif 8, and 375, seif 8.
That he knows. The Sema writes: See siman 14, seif 5 in the gloss regarding a recalcitrant litigant who caused another to incur expenses, where the claimant must establish how much he spent and is not believed merely by oath. There, however, the expenses were incurred against the recalcitrant party's will. Here they were incurred for his benefit. For the same reason the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state in siman 93, seif 15, "for everything Reuven establishes by witnesses," because there too the expenditure was not made for the other party's benefit, even though Reuven was compelled to incur it. The Shach, citing his father, offers a different distinction. See also Responsa Maharashdam 52 and 172 and Responsa Avodat HaGershuni 119.
