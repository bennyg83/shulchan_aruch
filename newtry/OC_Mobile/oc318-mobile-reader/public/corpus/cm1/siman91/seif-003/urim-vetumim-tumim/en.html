Regarding a shopkeeper who extends credit, etc. The Bach writes that if at the outset, when the workers were hired, the employer referred them to the shopkeeper and they agreed that payment would be made by the shopkeeper, they have no claim against the employer. The Shach, seif katan 15, agrees in practice. I do not understand their intent. If at the time of hiring the employer made an explicit and legally effective condition with the workers that they were being hired on the condition that their wages would be due only from the shopkeeper and that they would have no claim against the employer, no proof or special ruling is needed. Any valid monetary condition is effective. Why would this be different from every other monetary condition? But if there was no binding condition and the employer merely said at the time of hiring, “You will receive payment from the shopkeeper,” and the workers agreed, they can still say, “In any event you owe us our wages. If the shopkeeper pays us, well and good, but if he does not, you remain liable. We never agreed to lose our wages.” This is clear. Otherwise, why did the Rif and Rosh labor to explain the Yerushalmi’s statement that if the employer placed the workers with the shopkeeper they have no claim against the employer, interpreting it according to Rav Sheshet, who holds that the employer cannot retract, even though that is not the final halachah? They should simply have explained that the referral occurred at the time of hiring, in which case everyone would agree that the employer is discharged, according to the Bach and Shach. It is therefore clear that there is no distinction between a referral made at the time of hiring and one made when payment becomes due, unless there was an actual binding condition. Their words are thus not understandable to me.
The workers have no claim against the employer. The Beit Yosef challenges the Tur for copying this ruling, while below in siman 126, concerning ma'amad sheloshtan, he cites the Rosh that the transferee may always return against the transferor. The Sema answers that this applies only where the depositary does not claim that he already paid the transferee, but once he says that he paid, the transferee can no longer return according to any opinion, and even the Rosh agrees. The Shach, seif katan 18, objects: what difference does that make if the transferee retains a right to return? His proof is that otherwise why did the Rif and Rosh strain to explain the Yerushalmi on the Mishnah of the workers who swear and collect. The Yerushalmi says that this applies only when the employer did not place the workers with the shopkeeper, but if he did, they have no claim against the employer. The Rif and Rosh explain that the Yerushalmi follows Rav Sheshet, who holds that the worker cannot return to the employer, whereas according to Rabbah, who holds that he can return, this ruling does not stand. Yet one could have distinguished that Rabbah and Rav Sheshet disagree only where the shopkeeper has not claimed payment; where he claims that he paid, even Rabbah agrees that the worker cannot return, even in a setting where ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply, such as a shopkeeper extending his own credit. Baal HaIttur indeed writes that the Yerushalmi can accord with all opinions, because a claim of payment is different. This objection can be answered. The Rif and Rosh may hold that since the Yerushalmi speaks of a shopkeeper who extends credit from his own property, where no acquisition of ma'amad sheloshtan exists at all, why should the workers lose their claim against the employer merely because the shopkeeper says he paid? What legal bond is there between the workers and the shopkeeper? By contrast, where the shopkeeper holds the employer’s money, ma'amad sheloshtan applies and the shopkeeper becomes obligated to the worker. Even if the Rosh allows the transferee to return in some circumstances, once the shopkeeper says he paid, the transferee cannot simply reverse the transaction. There is no reason to create an unnecessarily broad dispute among the authorities. Baal HaIttur holds that even where the shopkeeper has no funds of the employer and is merely extending credit, so that ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply at all, once the shopkeeper says he paid the worker cannot return. All the more may the Rif and Rosh accept this reasoning where a genuine ma'amad sheloshtan transaction took effect because the shopkeeper held the employer’s money. Moreover, even if there were room to say that whenever the intermediary claims payment the worker cannot return, the Rif and Rosh understand the special requirement that all three be present together as a halachah without an explanatory rationale. Where that enactment does not apply and one seeks to discharge the employer for some independent reason, what legal significance would there be to whether all three happened to stand together? Thus, in the Mishnah of a shopkeeper on his ledger, where the shopkeeper extends his own credit and ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply, if the Yerushalmi’s basis were merely that the shopkeeper says he paid, what difference would it make whether the employer formally placed the workers with the shopkeeper? No ma'amad sheloshtan enactment exists in this case, so the common presence of the three should add nothing. One cannot ask that even according to Rav Sheshet, why does formal placement with the shopkeeper matter if ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply. Rav Sheshet holds that the worker’s inability to return is based on waiver. Tosafot ask whether fools are involved, since the shopkeeper could retract while the workers waive the employer. Tosafot and the Rosh answer that according to Rav Sheshet the shopkeeper also cannot retract, because by his undertaking the workers release the employer and he is therefore like a guarantor at the time the money was advanced. This is the Yerushalmi’s meaning in saying that the employer placed them with the shopkeeper: the shopkeeper must accept upon himself the obligation to pay the workers, and in reliance on that undertaking they release the employer. Without such placement the shopkeeper never accepted liability, so he may certainly withdraw, and the workers’ supposed waiver would have been mistaken. Thus the Rif and Yerushalmi need this formal placement according to Rav Sheshet. But according to the Shach, who would make the Yerushalmi accord with Rabbah even though there is no ma'amad sheloshtan and the worker can otherwise return, solely because the shopkeeper later claims payment, what purpose would the placement serve? Even after formal placement, Rabbah allows return. The Shach’s explanation therefore has no foundation, and there is no difficulty against the Rif and Rosh on this basis. Baal HaIttur, and one resolution of Tosafot in Bava Metzia 112, must instead say somewhat awkwardly that the parties relied more heavily on the arrangement when the employer formally placed the workers with the shopkeeper, but that is difficult to read into the Rif and Rosh.
Now, it appears that the Tur has support for the Sema’s distinction from the Rosh himself. At the end of chapter HaMekabel, the Rosh explains the Yerushalmi according to Rav Sheshet: since the worker cannot return to the employer, the shopkeeper likewise cannot reject the worker, because he became liable as a guarantor. See what I wrote at length below in siman 126. The Rif, Rosh, and all the authorities accept that the enactment of ma'amad sheloshtan, a halachah without an explanatory rationale, applies specifically where the intermediary holds money of the transferor, not where a shopkeeper merely extends his own credit. This creates a difficulty according to Rav Sheshet. If even a shopkeeper who merely extends credit, outside the enactment of ma'amad sheloshtan, creates a transaction from which the worker cannot return, and by the same logic the shopkeeper cannot return, why did the Sages need to enact ma'amad sheloshtan at all in a case where the intermediary actually holds the transferor’s money? Even without the enactment, ordinary law should prevent both the transferee and the shopkeeper from retracting. The transferor certainly could not retract if the shopkeeper is bound, for the shopkeeper could say to the employer, “You cannot withdraw and leave me as guarantor while you reclaim the original obligation.” It is strained to say that the enactment was needed only for a forced transfer against the shopkeeper’s will, where guaranty would not arise and outside ma'amad sheloshtan everyone could retract. It is implausible that the whole enactment was instituted merely for that circumstance. The Rosh in the first chapter of Gittin discusses whether ma'amad sheloshtan works against the intermediary’s will and does not prove that it must, even according to Rav Sheshet, on the ground that otherwise there would be no purpose to the enactment. Nor can one say that the enactment was created primarily for gifts, where guaranty does not apply. The Tur below, siman 126, cites a responsum of the Rosh that the principal purpose of the enactment was commerce, and that gifts were included incidentally. The Ritva in Kiddushin likewise indicates this, as written below in siman 126. One might suggest that Rabbah and Rav Sheshet disagree precisely whether ma'amad sheloshtan requires the intermediary to hold the transferor’s funds or can apply even when he merely extends credit. But Rav’s formulation, “I have a maneh in your hands, give it to so-and-so,” clearly implies that he must actually hold the transferor’s money. How could Rav Sheshet dispute Rav’s defining statement? Further, instead of disputing specifically about a shopkeeper, they should have disputed generally whether ma'amad sheloshtan requires “a maneh in your hands” or also works on credit. Tosafot themselves raise a similar objection to explaining their dispute as whether waiver requires a kinyan. Nor does the distinction, cited by Nimukei Yosef from a gaon, between a regular shopkeeper who customarily extends credit and an ordinary person resolve the Rosh, for the Rosh draws no such distinction. There is another difficulty. The Rosh holds that even after a full ma'amad sheloshtan transfer the transferee may in some circumstances return to the transferor. How can we attribute to Rav Sheshet the far stronger view that even where no ma'amad sheloshtan acquisition exists because the shopkeeper has none of the employer’s money, the worker can never return, while according to Rav Nachman even a full ma'amad sheloshtan transfer can be reversed? What basis is there for creating such an extreme dispute? One would also expect the Gemara to frame the dispute directly in the general law of ma'amad sheloshtan. It therefore appears that the dispute of Rav Sheshet and Rabbah, whether the worker can return, is not speaking where the shopkeeper admits he has not paid. In that circumstance, according to the Rosh, the arrangement would certainly be no stronger than an ordinary ma'amad sheloshtan transfer from which the worker may return. Rather, their dispute concerns the Yerushalmi’s setting as understood by Rav Sheshet, where the shopkeeper says, “I already paid him.” In a full ma'amad sheloshtan transaction, everyone agrees that once the intermediary claims payment the transferee cannot return, as the Sema explains even according to the Rosh. Here, however, the transaction is not a technical ma'amad sheloshtan, because the shopkeeper merely extends credit. Rav Sheshet holds that since the employer formally placed the workers with the shopkeeper, and both employer and worker relied on the shopkeeper to pay, the worker thereby accepted the shopkeeper’s credibility on the question whether he later says he paid. Although an ordinary guarantor who says he paid does not prevent the creditor from returning to the principal debtor, here the Rosh says that the arrangement is akin to waiver: the worker waives the employer’s liability if the shopkeeper says that he paid. That is why the Rosh calls it a waiver and why this arrangement is stronger than ordinary guaranty. Rabbah, by contrast, holds that because the worker has no real legal connection to a shopkeeper who merely extended credit, no acquisition having occurred, the shopkeeper’s claim of payment does not cut off the worker’s claim against the employer. According to this explanation, the Shach’s original question disappears, because the very point of the dispute is whether a shopkeeper’s claim that he paid prevents the worker from returning. Thus the Yerushalmi must follow Rav Sheshet, not Rabbah. Rabbi Yeshayah, cited by the Tur, follows Tosafot’s interpretation that Rabbah and Rav Sheshet dispute the prohibition of delaying wages. He can therefore understand the Yerushalmi as according with all opinions: once the shopkeeper says he paid, everyone agrees that the worker cannot return to the employer, just as Baal HaIttur writes and just as the Shach suggested. This explains why the Tur cites Rabbi Yeshayah as holding that even where the shopkeeper is merely extending credit, if both parties agreed that the shopkeeper would pay and the worker would receive from him, the worker has no further claim against the employer. The Tur’s difficulty is that we rule like Rabbah that one may return. The Shach attempted to explain Rabbi Yeshayah as following the authorities who extend ma'amad sheloshtan even to a shopkeeper extending credit, with Rabbah and Rav Sheshet discussing only a case lacking a formal ma'amad sheloshtan. That is strained, as the Rif notes, and if Rabbi Yeshayah held such a general view the Tur should have cited him in siman 126 concerning ma'amad sheloshtan generally. The Bach and Derishah likewise point this out. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. Rabbi Yeshayah follows Baal HaIttur that once payment is claimed everyone agrees the worker cannot return, because the parties relied on one another. Their Gemara dispute concerns only the prohibition of delaying wages and a case where no payment was claimed. Thus the Tur properly cites his ruling. This interpretation is simple and sound. Although this reasoning is correct in practice, since we should not multiply disputes, as Baal HaIttur holds this even where the shopkeeper extends credit, Rabbi Yeshayah can be read likewise, and many authorities in any event hold that after ma'amad sheloshtan the transferee can never return, it remains difficult to force this reading into the Rosh’s actual words, which are rather remote from it. A more natural reading of the Rosh may therefore be that when he says, in explaining the Yerushalmi according to Rav Sheshet, that the worker cannot return and consequently the shopkeeper is bound by guaranty, he speaks where the worker explicitly released the employer. One might otherwise have thought that such a release is mistaken because the shopkeeper may retract. The Rosh therefore says that the shopkeeper becomes a guarantor through the worker’s express release of the employer, eliminating the mistake. All this requires an explicit statement by the worker, “I release you.” If the transferee merely remains silent and accepts the referral, he can certainly return, and the shopkeeper does not become a guarantor. This is where the shopkeeper is extending his own credit. By contrast, where the shopkeeper actually holds the employer’s money and ma'amad sheloshtan applies, the shopkeeper becomes liable even if the transferee merely remains silent and does not expressly release the original debtor. This would resolve the earlier difficulties. Nor can one ask why the Sages needed to enact ma'amad sheloshtan if the creditor could simply release the debtor explicitly and thereby make the depositary a guarantor. The creditor does not necessarily wish to extinguish the original debtor’s obligation altogether. He may want the right to return if needed, as discussed below in siman 126.
It is true that this reading is also strained, because the Rosh does not say that the worker expressly released the employer. Moreover, if the dispute of Rav Sheshet and Rabbah concerns only a case where no express release was made and therefore the shopkeeper never became a guarantor, perhaps everyone would agree that where the worker expressly releases the employer and the shopkeeper becomes a guarantor, he cannot return, especially once the shopkeeper claims payment. How then does the Yerushalmi prove that its ruling follows Rav Sheshet? Perhaps the Mishnah speaks without express release and the Yerushalmi’s special case speaks with express release, which would fit all opinions. See what I wrote below in siman 126 in resolving these matters. Nevertheless, the Rosh’s own proof that the Yerushalmi follows Rav Sheshet because this is a shopkeeper extending credit, rather than saying additionally that even if this were full ma'amad sheloshtan the transferee can in some circumstances return, suggests the Sema’s distinction that once payment is claimed one cannot return from a full ma'amad sheloshtan transfer. This is even more strongly supported by Tosafot there, who offer three interpretations of the dispute between Rabbah and Rav Sheshet. In their second interpretation, the dispute is whether, when the shopkeeper wants to pay the worker, the worker may nevertheless return to the employer and demand payment directly. Their third interpretation concerns the prohibition of delaying wages. Tosafot write that under the third interpretation the Rif’s reading of the Yerushalmi as Rav Sheshet does not work, because the Amoraim would not be disputing the relevant point. But the same objection would seem to apply to their second interpretation, for there, if the shopkeeper refuses to pay, everyone agrees the worker may return to the employer, while in the Mishnah the shopkeeper says that he already paid and refuses further payment. Why then can the worker not return? Tosafot must hold that the two questions are linked. According to Rav Sheshet, once the worker cannot return to the employer while the shopkeeper remains ready to pay, the shopkeeper’s later statement, “I already paid,” likewise prevents the worker from returning. Only if the shopkeeper says, “I did not pay and will not pay,” may the worker return. According to Rabbah, however, the worker always remains connected to the employer, so the shopkeeper’s assertion that he paid does not eliminate the worker’s claim. From Rav Sheshet’s logic we can infer the law under Rabbah in a full ma'amad sheloshtan transaction. Although the Rosh allows return in some cases, he explicitly agrees that so long as the intermediary is ready to pay the transferee cannot demand directly from the transferor. Therefore, once the intermediary says that he already paid, the transferee can no longer return to the transferor. This is compelled by Tosafot’s structure, and we should not create a new dispute. It also illuminates Tosafot’s first interpretation. There they explain that if the worker merely said, “I rely on you,” Rav Sheshet holds that the employer is fully discharged and, correspondingly, the shopkeeper becomes obligated as a guarantor; Rabbah says that the employer is released only if the shopkeeper actually pays. Tosafot infer from this that waiver does not require a kinyan, because if there were a kinyan then according to Rabbah it should certainly have effected complete release. In their second interpretation, Tosafot say that the worker expressly reserved a right to return if the shopkeeper does not pay, and they again infer that waiver does not require a kinyan because otherwise Rabbah’s position would be difficult. One might ask why, in the first interpretation, Tosafot could not say that there was indeed a kinyan, but Rabbah still holds that the employer was not released if the shopkeeper later refuses to pay. The kinyan would at least have the effect that while the shopkeeper is ready to pay, the worker cannot return, which Tosafot themselves classify as a form of waiver in the second interpretation. The answer follows the distinction just stated. If the worker cannot return while the shopkeeper is ready to pay because of a true waiver, then when the shopkeeper later says, “I already paid,” the employer faces only a doubt whether the waiver took effect or was mistaken. That is comparable to a creditor who certainly waived but later there is doubt whether the waiver was valid, which is treated as “I do not know whether I ever became liable,” as the Rashba’s case discussed above in siman 75. Therefore, under that structure the employer would be exempt even according to Rabbah once the shopkeeper claims payment. Since the Rif reads the Yerushalmi as Rav Sheshet alone, Tosafot understood that Rabbah must deny that any waiver took effect at all. Consequently, if a kinyan had been made for the worker’s undertaking, there would be no explanation for Rabbah’s position. Tosafot therefore prove that waiver does not require a kinyan. Under their second interpretation they likewise classify the temporary inability to return as a waiver and derive the same point. Thus the Sema’s ruling is strongly supported, and the Shach himself below, siman 126, seif katan 58, notes that Maharit was uncertain whether, according to the opinion allowing return, the transferee may still return after the intermediary claims payment. In my view the primary ruling is as stated. We do not create a distant additional dispute where the authorities can be read consistently.
For this too is only a hesset oath. The Shach, seif katan 21, asks that these authorities disagree with the Rif regarding one who sends an agent to deliver money to a creditor in payment of a debt. The agent says, “I gave it,” and the creditor says, “I did not receive it.” The Rif holds that the creditor swears while holding a sacred object and collects, whereas these authorities require only hesset. If so, the Mechaber, who below in siman 121:9 rules without qualification like the Rif that the creditor swears and collects, should not have cited the opposing view here, since the two cases are the same. This requires study. It seems, however, that there is a distinction. Why should the creditor be obligated there to take an oath while holding a sacred object when there is no definite claim against him? If the definite claim derives from the agent’s statement that he paid, the agent is interested in the matter, and as explained above in siman 75, an interested person’s report cannot even create the definite claim needed to impose an oath. Yet Tosafot and the Ran themselves ask that once the agent swears that he delivered the money to the creditor, he is no longer interested, because he had a migo that he could have returned the money to the debtor and sworn accordingly. They answer that there is a scriptural decree preventing him from serving as a witness until he swears. That concerns full testimony. But once he has sworn and is no longer interested, his statement can certainly create a definite claim sufficient to impose an oath on the creditor. This is why Baal HaTerumot explicitly writes that the agent swears first and only afterward the creditor swears, because the agent’s oath converts the matter into a definite claim. The Shach objects to this both here and below in siman 121, but his objections are not conclusive, since an oath while holding a sacred object requires a definite claim. This works below because the agent can claim a migo of having returned the money. Even if the debtor told him, “Give it,” he has a migo that the funds were lost by unavoidable accident. Although that alternative might have required a Torah oath, once he is in any event swearing hesset, the difference in the grade of oath does not render him interested; one who is willing to swear falsely in hesset is likewise suspect regarding a Torah oath. Here, however, where the employer told the shopkeeper, “Give to the workers,” the workers acquire a right through that instruction, and the shopkeeper cannot say that he returned the money to the employer, because the employer would still have to pay the workers. See Shach below, siman 121:42. Nor can the shopkeeper say that the employer’s coin was lost by accident, because, as the Sema writes in seif katan 9, the employer gave him a coin to spend and to pay the workers with other currency. The shopkeeper therefore has no migo at all. Even after he swears, he remains interested, so his statement cannot create the definite claim necessary for the creditor’s heavier oath. The Mechaber therefore properly regards the Rif’s reasoning as compelling in the later case, but here, where no definite claim exists, there is room to follow the authorities who require no oath while holding a sacred object and only hesset. Thus there is no contradiction.
One who incurred expenses, etc. See Urim, seif katan 21. There is a point requiring precision in the Ran. He agrees with the Ramah that even when the workers do not contradict the shopkeeper at all, strict law would not allow the shopkeeper to collect from the employer, because the employer can say, “I do not know whether I ever became indebted to you.” Why then, in the case of one who incurred expenses with authorization, does the person who knows swear and collect? One cannot say that this too is merely a rabbinic enactment and that by strict law he would not collect, because the Ran answers Tosafot’s question why the Mishnah in Shevuot does not list one who incurred expenses among those who swear and collect by saying that the Mishnah lists only those who by strict law would have no right to collect at all. This indicates that one who incurred authorized expenses does collect by strict law. Nor can one answer that there it is certain he spent at least a perutah, making the case one of partial knowledge and partial uncertainty, so that the rule of one who cannot swear must pay would apply. The Ran follows the Rambam that this can generate payment, but then where an employer tells a shopkeeper to give a sela to each of two workers, one worker admits receiving and the other contradicts him, the Ran should likewise allow the shopkeeper to collect without an oath. The employer certainly owes one sela, and this is exactly the sort of case the Ran should have explained. One can say that in the shopkeeper case the employer had no reason to know whether payment actually occurred. His hands and eyes are not constantly with the shopkeeper. Even the Rambam would agree there that inability to swear does not force payment. By contrast, in a case of authorized expenditures, the owner can often estimate beforehand what expenses will be required and is therefore more responsible for knowing. More likely, however, the essential reason in the shopkeeper case is the Ramban’s point that the shopkeeper should have paid before witnesses. The Gemara’s statement that the employer did not tell him to pay before witnesses is part of the special enactment by which the Sages imposed liability on the employer despite this defect. With authorized expenditures, by contrast, it is impossible to station witnesses at every small item of expense. Thus strict law itself allows the person who incurred the expenses to swear and collect. This still requires study.
