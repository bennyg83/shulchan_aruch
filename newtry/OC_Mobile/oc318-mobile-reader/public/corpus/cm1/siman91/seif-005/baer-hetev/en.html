To the matter. The Shach gives the following example. Reuven gave Shimon one thousand zuz for an investment partnership without a document, within four months before Shimon's death. Shimon died without a will, and after his death a handwritten ledger was found containing a record of pledges taken from Reuven's thousand zuz on a half-profit basis. Reuven's name appeared on every note relating to those pledges. The Rosh returned Reuven's money to him even though Shimon left a minor son. Responsa Maharanach, beginning of siman 15, rules likewise. See also Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 137; Mabit, part 2, siman 74; Maharshdam, siman 200; Maharit, siman 72; and the end of siman 250. Responsa Mahari ibn Lev, part 2, siman 50, states that if fifty is written in the ledger and fifty is not, this does not create the status of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear. He adds that the Rosh speaks only of a person who records his business immediately in a ledger lying before him. If he delays, he may have forgotten and failed to record an item. Even if the person's signature is not on the ledger, if the handwriting is authenticated from another source the ledger may be relied upon even to collect from heirs. Radbaz, part 1, siman 108; Maharanach, part 1, siman 15. A single sheet is not considered a ledger. Mahara Sasson, siman 130. This does not mean only a literal single sheet; even a notebook containing one account is not considered a ledger if the person does not record all his accounts there, as appears from Peletat Beit Yosef, siman 18. Nevertheless, if that notebook corresponds to another principal ledger of a person whose accounts are accepted, one may say that death prevented him from copying the entry into the principal ledger. Kenesset HaGedolah.
