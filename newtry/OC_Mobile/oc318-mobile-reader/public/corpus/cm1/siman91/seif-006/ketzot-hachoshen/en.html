The Tur, following Baal HaTerumot, says the employer must swear. Ran, citing Raah, says that even hesset is unnecessary, because even if the employer admits he instructed the shopkeeper to pay, the shopkeeper still cannot collect without first swearing. The employer's denial is therefore not yet a denial of money, for who says the shopkeeper will swear? Baal HaTerumot writes differently. If the employer admits fifty and says he does not know about the remainder, he is one obligated to swear but unable to swear, and must pay. His reasoning depends on treating something that causes money as money itself. He follows the view of Rabbi Shimon that a thing causing monetary liability is treated as money, and he links this with the law of actionable indirect causation. Just as one who burns another's debt instrument and admits part can incur a partial admission oath because the burned instrument caused money, so too the shopkeeper's claim, although dependent on his later oath, is sufficiently money causing to count as a monetary claim. This is clearer from Rashi in Shevuot, who explains the case of one witness where the opposing party is suspected: even before the oath is taken, the witness is at least a cause of money because most people do not swear falsely and would therefore pay. According to Rabbi Shimon, such a cause of money is treated as money. Baal HaIttur similarly connects oath liability to the doctrine of a thing causing money. This approach, however, is difficult for the Tur, because elsewhere the Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule that documents are excluded from oaths and overreaching. If a thing causing money were fully treated as money, documents should not be excluded. Perhaps the Tur accepts Rabbi Shimon only regarding things whose essence is monetary, while a document is not itself monetary in essence, as the Gemara distinguishes. Baal HaTerumot may hold more broadly that once actionable indirect causation is accepted, even documents acquire sufficient monetary status. A further difficulty is the accepted rule that one does not impose an oath about another oath. If a claimant says, "We never divided," and the defendant says, "We already divided," no new oath is imposed merely over the question whether a prior partnership oath is still due, because this is not a direct denial of money. If a thing causing money were treated as money, why should an oath not be imposed over a disputed oath obligation? The Gemara concerning one witness suggests that according to Rabbi Shimon even a witness who would generate an oath can be treated as causing money. The answer may be that the rule against an oath upon an oath concerns rabbinic oaths. The Geonim explain that one rabbinic enactment is not made upon another. But where the disputed first oath is Torah law, hesset can be imposed on the denial because it at least causes money. This may be the reason Rav Hai requires hesset when a defendant denies an already existing Torah oath obligation. According to Baal HaTerumot, if a one witness Torah oath had already been created and the defendant denied the matter giving rise to that oath, one could even argue for a Torah oath against the witness. Yet perhaps a thing counts as causing money only where the first witness knows that a false oath would be required, because most people do not swear falsely. Where the defendant says he is telling the truth, the court cannot assume the first oath would necessarily lead to payment. Thus the causative money theory may not extend to every disputed oath obligation.
The rule here is that where the employer says he does not know, he is one obligated to swear but unable to swear. This seems difficult because in siman 90 the Shulchan Aruch says no oath is imposed against the defendant when the claimant himself still must swear. The difference is that here, according to the Shulchan Aruch's accepted approach, the shopkeeper's underlying right is valid by strict law and his oath is only a rabbinic condition. If he cannot swear, for example because he is suspected, his substantive right does not disappear merely through the rule of inability to swear, since that rule does not apply to rabbinic oaths in the same manner. Therefore the employer's denial is a denial of real money. If the employer says he does not know, he must pay under the rule of one obligated to swear but unable to swear, and the shopkeeper then takes the oath he can still take. Although Rambam rules that a suspected creditor does not collect, that is a special penalty imposed on a suspected person, not because every inability to take a rabbinic oath destroys the underlying right. Thus if the employer's own plea makes it impossible for the shopkeeper to swear, the shopkeeper does not thereby lose his right. By contrast, in siman 90 the claimant has no right to collect at all except through the special rabbinic oath. Until he swears, no money is legally due to him. Therefore the defendant's denial is not a present denial of money. This distinction also explains Rambam's rule that where a borrower says a document was paid and demands that the lender swear before collecting, beit din tells the borrower first to bring the money, and then the lender swears and collects. If the borrower says he has no money, he must swear that he has none. Although the lender has not yet taken the required oath, the borrower's denial concerns a substantive debt that already exists. The lender's oath is only a rabbinic collection condition. Ran, citing Raah, appears to follow Ramban's different view that the shopkeeper's entire right exists only through the oath. Therefore until he swears there is no present monetary right at all. This also explains the Gemara's phrase, "Who says he will swear?" in a case where the opposing party's right itself arises only through a rabbinic shifted oath. The Tur is harder to reconcile. His language elsewhere suggests he views the shopkeeper's right as rabbinic, because he still requires the shopkeeper to swear even when the workers are absent. Yet here he follows Baal HaTerumot and requires the employer to swear. Perhaps the Tur really follows Baal HaMaor that the shopkeeper has a strict legal claim, but the Sages nevertheless imposed an oath on shopkeepers as they did in other reimbursement cases, such as one who expends money on his wife's property. The oath is then only a rabbinic safeguard and not the source of the right itself. This distinction also vindicates Beit Yosef at the end of siman 22. If a person who was subject to a Torah oath tells the claimant, "You swear and collect," and the claimant immediately swore without holding a sacred object, the first party cannot retract. The claimant need only repeat the oath in the proper form. If the first party now denies having authorized the reversal, Beit Yosef requires him to swear that denial. Although the claimant still has to repeat the oath with a sacred object, his substantive right has already been fixed. The remaining oath form is only a rabbinic strengthening. Therefore the denial is a denial of money and an oath is appropriate.
