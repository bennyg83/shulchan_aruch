Swears. The Sema writes that this is unlike the Ran, who holds that the shopkeeper need not take even a shevuat heset. Since even if the employer admitted that he instructed the shopkeeper to pay, the shopkeeper could collect only after an oath, the employer's denial is not considered a denial of money, as explained at the end of siman 87. The other authorities distinguish. There, even when the defendant admits, he may swear and be exempt, so there is no definite monetary liability. Here, by contrast, upon the employer's admission the shopkeeper would swear and collect, and therefore when the employer denies the instruction he must take a shevuat heset. The Shach writes that there is no dispute. The Ran speaks where the workers contradict the shopkeeper and say they received nothing. In that situation the shopkeeper could collect only upon an oath, so the employer need not take a shevuat heset. Here, however, the workers admit they received the money, while the employer claims he never instructed the shopkeeper to give it. In that case the shopkeeper would collect without an oath, so the employer must take a shevuat heset.
Pays. Baal HaTerumot explains that we rule like Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for indirect damage. Just as one who burns another's promissory note is liable, and if he admits part while saying regarding the remainder, "I do not know," he is liable because one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay, so too regarding the shopkeeper. The Shach writes that in his view no law of indirect damage is needed here. Since the shopkeeper gave the money on the employer's behalf and by his instruction, the employer himself is considered the borrower. What difference is there whether the lender gave the money directly to him or to his agent for him? Therefore, although in siman 386, seif 2 the Shach disagrees with Baal HaTerumot regarding one who burns a promissory note, the ruling here is correct according to all authorities.
