The employer swears, etc. How can the employer be obligated to swear if even an admission would not create an immediate monetary obligation, since the shopkeeper cannot collect except by taking his own oath? The Sema answers that the underlying matter is nevertheless monetary. His words are difficult unless they follow the Rambam’s view above that by strict law the shopkeeper should collect without an oath and the Sages imposed an oath only to reassure the employer. According to that view the Torah law remains a full monetary claim, sufficient to make the employer’s denial a denial of money. But according to the Tur, who agrees with the Ramah that by strict law the shopkeeper could not collect even with an oath, the difficulty remains: the employer is not denying a claim that would obligate him in money merely upon admission. We must therefore explain, as the Derishah does, that there are witnesses that the shopkeeper gave the workers the money, or, as the Shach writes, that the workers admit that they received payment from him, while the employer says only, “I owed the workers nothing and never instructed you to pay them.” If he admitted that he gave the instruction, the shopkeeper could collect the money directly without an oath. This answer is sound, but requires further clarification. The Tur concludes that the employer swears hesset and is exempt, and that the shopkeeper must litigate with the workers. What claim can the shopkeeper have against them? The entire dispute is whether the employer owed the workers anything. Yet the shopkeeper says that the employer instructed him to pay them, so according to his own account the employer was plainly liable, while the employer’s denial is false. What remains to litigate between shopkeeper and workers? The case must therefore be as follows. The shopkeeper says, “You told me that if a certain worker came and said he had performed a particular job for you for a wage of one sela, I should pay him. He came, made that statement, and I paid him.” The employer responds, “I never instructed you, and the worker did not perform any work for me.” If the worker actually performed the work, the employer is liable in any event, especially according to the Tur, who holds that one who pays another’s debt without authorization may recover. The worker says, “I did the work and received my wage.” Thus if the shopkeeper’s account is true, the employer must reimburse him without any oath. But because the employer denies the instruction, he swears and is exempt. The shopkeeper still has a claim against the worker: perhaps you did not in fact perform the work, in which case you were not entitled to receive anything, and why should I lose my money? The fact that the employer authorized me to rely on your statement does not make you personally trustworthy against me. Thus the shopkeeper litigates with the worker, and the Tur’s language is resolved.
Now, the Derishah also suggests that the worker contradicts the shopkeeper and says that he never received the money, but the employer believes the shopkeeper that he paid it. This too is difficult. Why would the employer then be liable by the rule that one who cannot swear must pay? The employer would not be obligated to swear at all. Had he simply refused to believe the shopkeeper, no monetary denial sufficient to create an oath would exist, as explained. If he voluntarily believes the shopkeeper and says, “I did instruct you,” that should not create an oath against himself, since he has a migo that he could have denied believing the shopkeeper, and the Tur accepts a migo to exempt from an oath where the alternative is not an audacious claim. One would have to say that the shopkeeper’s original claim was stronger: “You instructed me to pay the workers and expressly trusted me to be believed without an oath.” If the employer admits that, he would be directly liable in money. But that reading is strained.
