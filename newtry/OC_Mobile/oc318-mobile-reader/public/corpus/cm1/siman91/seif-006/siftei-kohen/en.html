The employer takes a hesset oath. Darkei Moshe and the Sema write that this disagrees with the Ran in the name of the Raah, who says that the employer need not take even a hesset oath. Their reason is that even if the employer admitted instructing the shopkeeper to pay, the shopkeeper could not collect from him without first taking an oath. The employer's denial is therefore not a denial of money. In my opinion there is no dispute. The Raah and Ran discuss a case in which the workers deny receiving payment. Since the shopkeeper could collect only by taking an oath, the employer does not take a hesset oath. Here, however, the workers admit that they received payment, while the employer claims that he never instructed the shopkeeper and therefore owes them nothing. In that case the shopkeeper would collect without an oath. This is proven by the Tur above, siman 75:17, who cites the Ramah without qualification that wherever two witnesses do not obligate the defendant to pay except through the claimant's oath, we do not apply the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. Yet here, when the employer says, “I instructed you to give fifty, and as to the other fifty I do not know,” the Tur applies that very rule. Necessarily the cases differ as explained. Cited cross-references in the source include: 70.
If he said, “Fifty...” Baal HaTerumot, gate 29, part 2, writes that if the employer admits fifty and says regarding the rest, “I do not know,” he must pay because one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay. We rule like Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for indirectly caused damage and treats something that causes monetary loss as though it were money itself. Thus, if one burns another's promissory note and the owner claims it represented one hundred while the burner says it represented only fifty, the burner takes the oath of one who admits part of a claim. If he denies burning it, he takes a hesset oath. If he admits part and says of the remainder, “I do not know,” he must pay because he cannot swear. So too concerning the shopkeeper, since there is a presumption that an agent performs his agency and something causing monetary loss is treated as money, it is as though the employer were being sued for the money itself. In my opinion, liability here exists even without the law of indirectly caused damage. Since the shopkeeper paid on the employer's behalf and at his instruction, the employer himself is considered the borrower. What difference is there whether the shopkeeper lent directly to him or lent to his agent on his behalf? Therefore, although below in siman 386:2, seif katan 10, I disagree with Baal HaTerumot concerning one who burns a promissory note and hold that no Torah oath applies there, the ruling here is correct according to all authorities.
