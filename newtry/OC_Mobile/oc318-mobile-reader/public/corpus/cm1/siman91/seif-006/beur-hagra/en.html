The shopkeeper says, etc. The mishnah does not speak of a case where he merely says, 'It is written in my ledger,' etc. Sefer HaTerumot, at the beginning of gate 29, explains that in such a case the employer takes a hesset oath like any other defendant. This is the meaning of the clauses, 'If he admits fifty, etc.,' and, 'If he says, etc.' See Be'er HaGolah. Ran, however, citing Raah, writes that in such a case the employer does not even take a hesset oath, because even if he admitted the claim, the shopkeeper could collect only with an oath. Such a demand is not a direct monetary denial. Shevuot 35a teaches that liability for false testimony applies only to a monetary claim, and Shevuot 32a states concerning one who adjures a single witness that all agree a single witness comes only to create an oath obligation. Abaye there adds that the claimant's recovery remains uncertain because perhaps he will be suspected of swearing falsely. The same principle applies to judicial oaths, as the Yerushalmi derives from the requirement of a monetary claim. Rambam and Sefer HaTerumot, however, follow their own view that where the claimants do not contradict one another, collection may occur without an oath. That is the case addressed here. Shach explains the passage this way. See citation 26.
And if he admits, etc.; and if, etc. We rule like Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for directly caused damage. Thus, just as one who burns another's promissory note and admits that it contained only fifty must take a Torah oath, and if he says regarding the other fifty, 'I do not know,' he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot and therefore must pay, so too here. There is a presumption that an agent performs his agency, and the employer should know what he instructed him to do. See Sefer HaTerumot there, part 2.
