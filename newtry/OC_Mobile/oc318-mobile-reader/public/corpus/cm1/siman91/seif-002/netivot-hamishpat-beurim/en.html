Or the workers died. See Shach seif katan 10, who asks that if so, when they died their heirs should simply swear, "Our father did not instruct us otherwise," and collect. Tumim writes that this applies only where the worker and shopkeeper contradicted one another before beit din or before witnesses during their lifetimes. Otherwise, even if the heirs say that their father instructed them that he was unpaid, this is not a full bari claim, because the father never asserted that certainty directly against the shopkeeper. "My father told me" is not considered the requisite bari claim, as explained in siman 75. The Shach argues that the workers are entitled by strict law even without an oath because the employer's position is like one who says, "I do not know whether I paid you." Why then should the heirs lose that right? Rambam's reason may be that the employer says with certainty that he owes only one maneh to one of the two claimants, not two maneh to both. For this purpose that is treated as a bari claim, as Shach writes in siman 76 seif katan 34 and Tosafot explain in Bava Metzia regarding two depositors, so as not to contradict Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah, who hold bari preferable to shema. Liability in "I do not know whether I paid" is itself also grounded in bari against shema, as Tumim explains in siman 75. The Gemara in Bava Metzia 3 explicitly compares the shopkeeper-on-his-ledger case to the two-depositor case. Alternatively, because the uncertainty arose through the employer's own negligence, he is liable. The Gemara says that the workers can ask, "What do we have to do with the shopkeeper?" meaning, as Shitah Mekubetzet explains, that the employer's negligence created the uncertainty. As to the shopkeeper's heirs, if the employer is required to make only one payment, it appears that he pays those heirs according to the Shach in seif katan 12, who holds that the shopkeeper's right in that situation follows strict law rather than a special enactment. But where the employer must pay twice, the shopkeeper does not collect by strict law, because the Shach's reasoning, that there is no ordinary monetary chazakah where the employer necessarily owes one payment, no longer applies. Likewise, the presumption that an agent performs his mission helps only where the employer need not also pay the workers. If he must pay the workers because of the continuing chazakah of debt, as in "I do not know whether I paid," that chazakah stands against the presumption that the agent performed his mission. Where one person incurred expenses for another, if there is measurable benefit, then even if the spender dies his heirs collect. Ran at the beginning of the chapter Kol HaNishba'in writes that one who spends with authorization is entitled by strict law because the other party received benefit. This applies where payment is limited to the amount of the expenditure up to the value of the benefit. But where payment of expenses is due even without any benefit, Ran's rationale does not apply and the basis must be the special rule Shach gives in seif katan 23: the other party originally trusted him only upon an oath. It resembles contractual ne'emanut in siman 71, where one can say, "I trusted him, not his heirs." Moreover, the original trust was only subject to his oath. Even in ordinary ne'emanut Sema and Shach at the end of siman 71 discuss the argument that he was trusted only with an oath, and their answers there do not apply here. Similarly, in siman 76, where the negligent party must pay both claimants after they swear, if one claimant dies before swearing, it appears that payment is made to his heirs. Since the liability there is imposed by strict law because the defendant was negligent and should have distinguished the deposits, the heirs inherit the monetary claim and collect upon the appropriate heirs' oath. If both the shopkeeper and the workers died, then according to the Shach, who holds that the workers' heirs collect while the shopkeeper's heirs do not, the result is straightforward. According to the Mechaber, who holds that the workers' heirs do not collect, it appears that because both sides are dead and Rambam's Gemara-based rationale cannot operate in its ordinary manner, the money must be removed from the employer and held in suspense when the original parties contradicted each other with definite claims, since one of them was certainly false. But where they never contradicted one another and there is no certain liar, and neither side sued the employer before death, the doubt is evenly balanced and they divide. The statement that when the workers died the employer need not pay their heirs applies specifically where the workers themselves made no claim while the shopkeeper did. Their silence supports the inference that the shopkeeper paid them. Where neither side claimed before death, that inference is absent and the uncertainty is equal. When Shach speaks of a case "where they have no heirs," he means that the employer himself is their heir. When he says that the heirs do not know that the deceased was a worker, he means that for that reason they do not sue, equivalent to their being overseas.
