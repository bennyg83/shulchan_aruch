For there is no, etc. Shach asks why Rambam did not simply state the reason that no one is making a definite claim against the shopkeeper, and how could he be made to swear on an uncertain claim? Shach explains that the case is where the workers initially contradicted the shopkeeper, but before the oath they died or departed. Alternatively, Rambam may follow the authorities who hold that a shopkeeper always collects only with an oath, even where there is no definite opposing claim. See Tumim. This second explanation appears correct, for if the workers contradicted him and then left, although the shopkeeper does not take the special Mishnah oath since the employer now pays only once, he must nevertheless take hesset. See there.
One payment. Shach agrees with Ramban that if the workers died, their heirs take the heirs' oath that their father did not instruct them otherwise and collect. Here the case is one in which the workers have no heirs, meaning that the employer himself is their heir, for obviously no person exists without legal heirs. See Tumim. I wrote that even Ramban said this only where the workers contradicted the shopkeeper during their lifetime but did not have time to swear before they died. If they never contradicted him during their lifetime, their heirs certainly have no power to raise the claim and collect. See there.
And he does not know whether he paid, etc. This is a case of 'I do not know whether I paid you.' By strict law the claimant would collect without an oath. The oath was instituted only to ease the employer's mind because he is paying twice, whereas here he is not paying twice. See Tumim for what I wrote concerning Rashi's textual reading.
Perhaps he gave nothing. This is a case of 'I do not know whether I am liable to you.' Without the enactment, therefore, he would not collect even by swearing, and on what basis should he collect without an oath? Shach discusses this at length and concludes like those who maintain that even here strict law allows him to collect without an oath. See Tumim, where I wrote that his proof is not decisive. This applies only where the shopkeeper had been contradicted by the workers. If the workers never contradicted him and then died, it appears correct as a matter of law, as Shach writes, that he collects by strict law without an oath. See there.
