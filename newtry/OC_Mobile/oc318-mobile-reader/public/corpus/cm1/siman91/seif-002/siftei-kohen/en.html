Or the workers died, etc. The Sema writes that the employer then makes only one payment, and he admits that one payment is certainly due. The Rambam and the Tur likewise rule this way. This is contrary to the Maggid Mishneh’s citation of the Ramban, according to whom, if the workers died, their heirs collect from the employer after swearing that their father did not instruct them otherwise. According to that view, the employer would again make two payments. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe likewise discuss this. However, the Darkei Moshe did not reject the Ramban. On the contrary, he concludes that the Ran rules like the Ramban. The Ran in HaIsh Mekadesh indeed rules this way, contrary to the Rambam, and writes that other commentators disagreed with the Rambam. The Beit Yosef also did not reject the Ramban. The Rama’s failure to comment here on the Mechaber’s ruling is because the Mechaber cited the Ramah’s view, which is even more novel: even where the employer will not pay the workers’ heirs, the shopkeeper must still swear. This siman addresses only whether the shopkeeper must swear; it does not address whether the workers’ heirs collect. Alternatively, the Rama may understand the Rambam as I explained above. The Maggid Mishneh was forced to offer a strained explanation according to those who say that the workers’ heirs do not collect by oath, and he left their view requiring study. He writes that perhaps their reason is that this is not a written loan but an oral obligation, and one witness, namely the shopkeeper, testifies that he paid them, though he is an interested witness, while the heirs claim only on uncertain knowledge. The Ba’al HaTerumot at the end of gate 29, part 3, also follows the Ramban, and this is the primary ruling. The rule that a person does not transmit an oath to his children does not apply here. On the contrary, we do not extend that exceptional rule beyond its stated limits, as explained below in siman 108. In my humble opinion, the Ramban may agree as well and intended only to say that where there is only one payment, the shopkeeper collects without an oath. He may be speaking where the workers left no heirs, where the heirs do not know that their father had worked for this employer, where the heirs are absent, or in a similar circumstance. I later saw that the Bach also writes that the primary ruling follows the Rambam and Ramah, not the Ramban. He argues that this is also indicated by a responsum of the Rashba, cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 89, which quotes the Jerusalem Talmud concerning a hired worker who died: the heirs do not collect from the employer, because the enactment was made for the worker and not for his heirs. The Tur likewise writes below in siman 108 that in cases of a hired worker or a robbed person the heirs do not swear and collect. I have already explained, however, that the Rambam and Ramah provide no proof. The Bach’s proof from the Rashba and the Jerusalem Talmud is also imprecise. There the employer claimed to the worker, “I paid you.” The Ba’al HaTerumot himself writes at the end of gate 14, part 2, that in such a case the worker’s heirs do not swear and collect, deriving this from that Jerusalem Talmud. That is also the case addressed below in siman 108. The reason is that, by strict law, the worker would not have collected even with an oath, and the Sages enacted a special right for the worker alone. Here, by contrast, the workers would by strict law have collected even without an oath, because the employer certainly owed them and it is uncertain whether they were paid. It is therefore clear that when they die, their heirs swear that their father did not instruct them otherwise and collect. The present ruling must therefore concern one of the circumstances I described. Examine carefully. See also below, siman 121, seif katan 56.

And some say, etc. The Tur cites this in the name of the Ramah and writes that this view is most reasonable. The Levush rules likewise. The Maggid Mishneh cites this view in the name of several commentators, the Ramban, and the Ramah. Their words imply that even where the employer pays the shopkeeper only once, and the workers did not contradict the shopkeeper during their lifetime, the shopkeeper nevertheless may not collect without an oath. The Ran in Kol HaNishba’in likewise writes that the Rambam’s ruling is incorrect and that the shopkeeper must swear in every case. Although the Ran in HaIsh Mekadesh appears to disagree with the Rambam because the workers’ heirs collect, so that the employer suffers a loss by paying twice, careful analysis shows that the Ran disagrees for two reasons. First, because of the employer’s double payment. Second, because the shopkeeper seeks to collect while it is uncertain whether the employer became obligated to him, and therefore the shopkeeper must swear. According to this second reason, even where the employer will not pay the workers, such as where they left no heirs, the shopkeeper must swear. As a matter of law, however, it appears to me that the Rambam’s view is primary, as I will prove. Those who disagree argue that by strict law the shopkeeper has no claim against the employer, because this resembles a person who says, “You owe me one hundred,” while the other says, “I do not know,” in which case we rule that the defendant is exempt. In my humble opinion, the cases are not comparable. In the ordinary case of “You owe me one hundred” and “I do not know,” Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah hold in Bava Kamma 118a that the defendant is liable, because a definite claim prevails over an uncertain response. Rav Nachman and Rabbi Yochanan, who exempt him, explain that the money must remain in its owner’s possession. That reason applies only there, where the money presently rests with the defendant and we say that it should remain where it is. Here, however, this money will in any event leave the employer’s possession and does not remain under his ownership. Moreover, in Ketubot 12b, concerning one who married a woman and did not find signs of virginity, the Gemara states that even Rav Nachman and Rabbi Yochanan concede where another presumption opposes the presumption of possession, namely the woman’s presumption that she had been a virgin. A similar principle appears in Ketubot 76a concerning physical defects, where the husband is liable because we preserve her prior presumption that she had no defects. Here too, since in many places we rule that an agent presumptively performs his agency, we presume that the shopkeeper fulfilled his mission. I found that the Ba’al HaTerumot in gate 29, part 2, cites a lengthy responsum of Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat to the Ba’al HaMaor. He writes that the shopkeeper and the worker require oaths only where both are present and contradict one another, in accordance with the Mishnah’s language: “He says, ‘I paid,’ and they say, ‘We did not receive.’” But where only one claimant comes before the employer and the other is absent and does not contradict him, he collects without an oath. This applies even where the shopkeeper alone comes and claims against the employer. It is unlike “You owe me one hundred” and “I do not know,” because there the claimant has a definite claim while the defendant has the presumption of possession. Here the shopkeeper has two supporting factors: his definite claim and the presumption that an agent performs his agency. Those two factors overcome the employer’s presumption of possession. Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat proves that two such factors overcome possession from the Mishnah concerning one who marries a woman and does not find signs of virginity, where Rabban Gamliel believes her because her bodily presumption joins her definite claim against his uncertain claim. Likewise, concerning defects, the woman collects her ketubah without an oath where her bodily presumption joins her definite claim against his uncertain claim. This displaces the husband’s presumption of possession. Furthermore, because the Mishnah groups the shopkeeper together with the worker, they must have the same law. Just as the worker collects without an oath when no shopkeeper is present to contradict him, the shopkeeper also collects without an oath when no worker is present to contradict him. This is also why the case differs from one who claims expenditures on his wife’s property, or one who recognizes his utensils or books in another’s possession, and similar cases, where the claimant must swear and collect even without a direct contradiction. Here the shopkeeper additionally benefits from the presumption that an agent performs his agency. The plain language of the Mishnah supports this. It first states, “And the shopkeeper upon his ledger,” and then explains: “How is the case of the shopkeeper upon his ledger? The employer said to the shopkeeper, ‘Give my workers a sela.’ The shopkeeper says, ‘I gave it,’ and the workers say, ‘We did not receive it.’ The shopkeeper swears and collects, and they swear and collect.” The Mishnah sets out to explain the shopkeeper but also describes the workers. Had the shopkeeper always needed an oath simply because he sought payment, it should have said only: “How is the case of the shopkeeper upon his ledger? The employer said, ‘Give my workers a sela,’ and the shopkeeper says, ‘I gave it’; he swears and collects.” Necessarily, the shopkeeper requires an oath only because the workers contradict him and also collect. Otherwise, we presume he fulfilled his agency and, since the employer makes only one payment, he collects without an oath. There is a further proof from the beginning of Bava Metzia. Regarding the shopkeeper upon his ledger, where one party swears and collects and the other also swears and collects, the Gemara asks why the employer’s money should not be removed and held until Elijah comes, since one of the claimants is certainly lying. The Gemara answers that the shopkeeper says to the employer: “I performed your agency. What claim do I have against the worker? Even if he swears to me, I do not trust him. You trusted him by failing to instruct me to pay him before witnesses.” The worker similarly says: “I performed work for you. What claim do I have against the shopkeeper? Even if he swears to me, I do not trust him.” Therefore, both swear and collect from the employer. I am aware that the Ramban writes in Milchamot at the end of Shevuot that, by Torah law, the shopkeeper upon his ledger should depart empty-handed, because his case is “You owe me one hundred” and “I do not know,” where the defendant is exempt. According to the Ramban, the reasoning of the Geonim that the employer failed to instruct payment before witnesses explains only the rabbinic enactment, not the strict law. The employer should still have arranged matters properly and not exposed himself to loss. In my humble opinion, however, this is not persuasive. The Gemara’s reasoning appears to explain the strict law. Had it merely explained an enactment, the Mishnah in Shevuot itself should have stated so. Rather, the shopkeeper is entitled by law to collect. The Gemara asks only that since one claimant is certainly dishonest, the money should perhaps be held. It answers that the employer caused his own loss by failing to instruct payment before witnesses, and therefore the money is not held. Rashi’s explanation there likewise implies this. The same follows from the statement at the end of Shevuot that a case of two creditors, one debtor, and two notes is comparable to our Mishnah. If the shopkeeper’s right were merely an enactment, the comparison would be strained. The plain meaning is that both claimants collect by law, just as in the case of two notes. I also do not understand the Ramban’s assertion that the employer should have arranged matters properly and not caused distortion, as I explain below in siman 92, seif 9. A careful reading of the Ba’al HaTerumot, gate 29, part 2, indicates that the Ba’al HaMaor accepted Rabbi Yosef ibn Plat’s response that the shopkeeper has the law of the worker and collects by strict law without an oath when uncontradicted. This is explicit in the Ba’al HaMaor at the end of Shevuot, which I cite below in siman 92, seif 9. The Ba’al HaTerumot himself also rules this way and writes that this was the view of Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash and the Rambam. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 16 of Hilchot Malveh likewise writes that this was the view of the early authorities. Rashi and the Bartenura also appear to support it. They explain that both parties swear and collect because the shopkeeper says, “I do not trust the workers on oath; you trusted them by not instructing me to pay before witnesses,” and the workers similarly say that they do not trust the shopkeeper. Their language implies that the shopkeeper asserts a legal claim just like the workers. This is the primary ruling.
Specifically if the shopkeeper died, etc. The Tur cites the Ramah as ruling that even if the shopkeeper did not die, where the workers came first without the shopkeeper and claimed against the employer, and the shopkeeper was not present to be questioned, the workers collect without an oath. It appears that all authorities agree to this. The Ba’al HaTerumot in gate 29, part 2, writes plainly that the rule is not limited to where one party died. Even if he merely traveled overseas, although he may return today or tomorrow and say that he paid, since he is presently absent the claimant is not required to swear. The employer may, however, impose a general ban upon anyone who received this money and now claims it a second time. The Ba’al HaTerumot attributes this view to Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash and the Rambam. Other authorities rule likewise, as do the Rambam and Mechaber below in siman 121, seif 10. The Ramah’s phrase that the shopkeeper is not present therefore means that he traveled overseas. If he is nearby and it is known that he will soon return, the employer may say, “You will suffer no loss. Wait briefly until he arrives, or swear and collect immediately.” Where the shopkeeper traveled overseas and the workers collected without an oath, it appears that even if he later returns and contradicts them, forcing the employer to pay him as well, the workers need not then swear. The Sages instituted the oath only because the claimants were coming to collect. Once the workers have already collected, they now seek only to retain what they hold. There is no oath imposed upon one who answers an uncertain claim. Examine carefully.
