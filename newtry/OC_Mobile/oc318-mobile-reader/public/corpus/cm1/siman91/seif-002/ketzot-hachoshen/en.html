If the workers died, the Shach explains the case as one where they have no heirs, or where their heirs do not know that their father had worked for this employer. The first explanation is difficult, because no Jew lacks heirs. The second is also difficult, because since the employer knows he still may owe the workers, he does not discharge his obligation until he returns the money to their heirs. It may be answered from the rule in siman 75, as explained by the Taz and Shach, that where a person comes on his own and says, "I owe you a maneh but do not know whether I repaid you," he is exempt if the other party never claimed a loan and cannot state a certain claim concerning the loan itself. Thus here too the employer can be exempt from paying the workers' heirs. Although as to him the case is "I do not know whether I paid," and although we ordinarily advance the father's claims for heirs, if the heirs themselves do not even know that their father was this employer's worker, they cannot present a certain underlying loan or wage claim. The situation then resembles one who was never sued and is exempt despite saying he does not know whether he paid.

The possibility that the shopkeeper gave nothing is Ramban's view, under which the shopkeeper can collect only by rabbinic enactment because the employer says, in effect, "I do not know whether I became liable to you." Ramban writes that those who explained the enactment by saying the employer failed to tell the shopkeeper to pay before witnesses gave only a rationale for the enactment, not strict law. Even if the employer was negligent by failing to require witnesses, that negligence alone would not impose liability. The Shach in siman 92 objects from the case of Avimi, where liability arises only if the sender specifically told the messenger to take the document. The answer is that in Avimi's case the messenger did not intend to return and sue the sender, so failing to pay before witnesses was not negligence unless the sender specifically reminded him about the document. A shopkeeper, by contrast, gives the money intending to come back and collect from the employer. He therefore should have paid only before witnesses. The Shach himself makes this distinction in siman 130 regarding a guarantor who pays without taking the debt instrument: because the guarantor intends to return and collect from the borrower, failing to take the document is negligence. According to Baal HaMaor, however, the shopkeeper collects by strict law and his payment without witnesses is not treated as negligence even though he intends to recover from the employer. The Shulchan Aruch appears to follow that approach. This creates a tension with the law of a guarantor, where failure to take the document is negligence. Perhaps the distinction is that when a document exists, one who intends to recover should not pay without obtaining it; where there is no document, people do not ordinarily arrange witnesses merely because they intend later to recover. Nevertheless, the matter remains difficult, and Ramban's approach appears more persuasive.
