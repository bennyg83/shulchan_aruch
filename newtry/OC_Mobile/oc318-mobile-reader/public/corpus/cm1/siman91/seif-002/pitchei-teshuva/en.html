And the workers swear to the employer. Teshuvot Panim Meirot, part II, siman 17, discusses a community that owed salary to its cantor and gave him an instruction addressed to the communal trustee, who held communal funds, directing payment of a specified sum. The trustee later died. His ledger stated that he had paid the cantor, and the communal instruction bore a receipt, yet the document itself remained in the cantor's possession. The cantor claimed that the trustee initially intended to pay and therefore had him write a receipt, but then postponed payment repeatedly and ultimately returned the document. The trustee's wife confirmed this account. The responsum rules that this resembles the law of the shopkeeper and workers: because there was no transfer in the presence of all three parties, the worker, namely the cantor, swears that he was not paid and collects from the community. Indeed, even had the trustee been alive and claimed payment, the cantor's possession of the original communal document with the receipt would strongly support him, for a receipt found in the creditor's possession ordinarily does not impair the debt. Here the trustee is not even making a definite claim, since his ledger entry may have been written when the receipt was first prepared, before the money was actually delivered. The wife's testimony, though not formal evidence, reinforces this factual presumption. At most the community may require the cantor to take a heset oath that he did not receive payment, after which they must pay him. The responsum also discusses whether the community may recover from the trustee's heirs.
