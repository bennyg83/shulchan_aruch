Or the workers died, etc. The Shach writes that it is difficult to say that the Rambam disagrees with the Ramban, who holds that when the workers die their heirs swear and collect. The workers themselves were entitled by law to collect even without an oath, and the oath was imposed only to reassure the employer. It is inconceivable that their death would cause their heirs to lose the claim. The Shach is correct in this. He therefore writes that the Rambam speaks of workers who have no heirs. This is strained, since there is no Jew who has no heir. Independently, the Shach asks why the Rambam gives the reason that only one payment is due and does not instead say that there is no definite claim, as the Rambam himself writes in Hilchot Sheluchin. The Shach suggests that the Rambam speaks where the workers and shopkeeper contradicted one another and the workers then left and died, so there had already been a definite claim, yet no Mishnah oath remains because only one payment is due. This explains why there is no Mishnah oath, but why should the shopkeeper be exempt even from hesset? He can say to the employer, “What does it matter to me whether you will also pay the workers? You owe me nothing at all.” Why may he collect from the employer property that the employer says is not owed to him? Certainly a hesset oath should be required on a definite claim. A definite report through the worker also counts as a definite claim, as the Rambam rules in Hilchot Sheluchin that if the creditor says, “I did not receive payment,” while the agent is absent, the agent must swear hesset. Thus a report by the creditor can create a definite claim for hesset. Why should the shopkeeper here be exempt from hesset? With respect to hesset there is no apparent distinction between whether the employer will be required to pay twice or only once. The shopkeeper is still being sued on the claim that the employer need not pay him because the workers received nothing. It therefore appears more correct, as the Shach writes in his second answer, that the Rambam’s reason, “because he is liable for only one payment,” is needed for the shopkeeper. Ordinarily, even if the shopkeeper is not directly sued, he must swear because he is like one who incurred expenses on another’s behalf, who must swear how much he spent. The Razah writes this as cited in Baal HaTerumot, gate 29, part 2, and the Ran agrees. Here, however, since the employer is liable for only one payment, once the workers’ claim has disappeared the employer has no further concern with the shopkeeper and no oath is required. This also resolves the apparent contradiction with the Ramban. When the Ramban says that the workers’ heirs collect, he means where the workers had already appeared and contradicted the shopkeeper in beit din but died before taking their oath. In that case their heirs may collect. If a worker died without ever making any claim or contradicting the shopkeeper, and only afterward his heirs say, “Our father instructed us that the employer still owed him and that he received nothing from the shopkeeper,” the Ramban would agree that they do not collect. Their report is not sufficient to create a new contradiction of the shopkeeper and to collect through an oath that their father did not instruct them otherwise. Consider one who sends an agent to pay oral debts, and many days later the creditors die. If their heirs then say, “Our father instructed us that you still owe him,” surely their word alone cannot create liability. The Ramban spoke only where the worker himself contradicted the shopkeeper before beit din and died before taking the oath. Thus there is no contradiction at all between the Rambam and Ramban, and the law is correct in every respect. According to what I shall write in the following seif katan, it is also possible that even the workers’ heirs do not collect. See there.
Perhaps he gave nothing. The Shach, seif katan 12, argues at length in support of the Rambam and Mechaber that even the shopkeeper collects without an oath when the employer will be required to make only one payment. Since, as stated in the preceding seif katan, this can arise in two ways, the issue must be analyzed carefully. One possibility is that the workers already contradicted the shopkeeper, but did not take their oath before dying, and there is effectively no heir asserting their claim, either because the employer is their heir or because their heirs waived all claims. The second is that the worker died or left without ever contradicting the shopkeeper. The Shach rejects the argument that by strict law the shopkeeper cannot collect even with an oath because the employer can say, “I do not know whether I ever became indebted to you.” He says that ordinarily the reason for such a defense is that money remains in the possession of its owner, whereas here the employer certainly must pay this money to someone, so the money will leave his possession in any event. This exact reasoning appears in Tosafot and the Rosh in Bava Metzia 37 concerning Rabbi Akiva’s view that one who robbed one of five people and does not know which one must pay each of them. They explain that this does not contradict Rav Nachman, who holds that a definite claim does not prevail over an uncertain response, because there the defendant knows with certainty that he owes one of them, though he does not know which. Likewise here, the employer certainly owes one wage payment, but does not know whether it is owed to the shopkeeper, who claims to have already paid the workers, or to the workers, if the shopkeeper did not pay them. Yet this argument supports only the opinion that Rabbi Akiva’s ruling in the case of one of five is strict law and applies even to an ordinary purchase. We do not rule that way. We hold that Rabbi Akiva imposed multiple payment only as a penalty in a case of robbery, whereas in an ordinary purchase from one of five, where the buyer does not know from whom he purchased, he pays only once. The Rif explains explicitly that the reason is that we rule like Rav Nachman that a definite claim does not prevail over an uncertain response. Yet there too the buyer certainly owes someone. Since he does not know whom, he is exempt from paying each claimant individually. The same should apply here. He does not know whether to pay the shopkeeper or the workers. Thus the Shach’s general rationale has no firm legal basis according to our ruling.
However, the Shach also adopts the reasoning of the gaon Mahari ibn Palat, cited by Baal HaTerumot in gate 29, who compares this to a woman who says, “I was violated after betrothal.” She is believed despite the husband’s uncertainty whether he ever became liable for the full ketubah, because the Gemara answers that where a chazakah supports the definite claim even Rav Nachman agrees. Here too, says Mahari ibn Palat, there is a chazakah that an agent carries out his agency. The Shach develops this at length, essentially following Baal HaTerumot. If so, however, there would be no proof against the Ramban from his discussion in Milchamot at the end of Shevuot. Baal HaTerumot, gate 26, part 1, ruling 4, indicates that the Ramban holds even in the case of “I was injured after betrothal” that she must swear while holding a sacred object. Perhaps he views even that as a rabbinic enactment like other claimants who swear and collect, including the shopkeeper. If so, he is consistent, but that view is not our practical ruling, for Even HaEzer 68 holds that she need not swear at all. Still, the chazakah argument itself requires examination. First, the Gemara gives two explanations for why she is believed, one based on migo and one based on bodily chazakah, with a practical difference where she admits being previously injured but says it occurred after betrothal. The Tur, Rambam, and Shiltei HaGibborim adopt the first explanation to the detriment of the claimant and do not rely on the chazakah in that latter case. How, then, can money be extracted here by relying on such a chazakah? One can answer, as I have written elsewhere, that the chazakah is fundamentally valid, but under the first explanation we do not rely on it there because another chazakah, that of personal fitness, stands against it. This still requires study. See Beit Shmuel 68:22. Second, how can one build the case on the presumption that an agent performs his agency when Gittin 64b states that this presumption is used for stringency, not for leniency? It is therefore only a doubt. In monetary law, a doubt favors the possessor. Perhaps Mahari ibn Palat follows the Rash, who rules in Eruvin 32 that even in a Torah prohibition we rely leniently on the presumption, explaining that because failure to perform the agency would cause the principal to violate a prohibition, we assume the agent certainly acted. That might apply here because the employer would otherwise violate the prohibitions of robbery and withholding wages. But Tosafot write that Behag rules like Rav Nachman that we do not rely on this presumption leniently in Torah law, and the Rambam likewise rules in Hilchot Terumot that for a Torah prohibition we do not rely on it leniently. How, then, can we extract money based on such a doubt? This is difficult for the Shach. Third, the presumption that an agent carries out his agency applies where there is no suspicion that he is retaining another’s money. Where there is such suspicion, the presumption falls away. Proof is from kiddushin effected through two agents with money. Most authorities hold that the kiddushin are not valid on a Torah level because the agents are interested witnesses. If the presumption were so strong that they certainly carried out their agency, why would the kiddushin fail? We would know that they performed the agency. The same agents, if they claimed to have paid from their own money, could extract reimbursement from the husband who appointed them, once they established delivery. Yet without a migo their statement is not accepted. Clearly, where personal interest exists, the presumption does not resolve the matter. Furthermore, the presumption itself rests on the idea that upright Jews do not lie. But there is an opposite presumption regarding the workers, that they would not falsely deny receipt and brazenly claim that they were unpaid. Why should one presumption be stronger than the other? I am especially astonished that the Shach treats the presumption that an agent performs his mission as sufficiently strong to extract money, yet also rules that the workers certainly collect by strict law because the employer says, “I do not know whether I paid you.” Here the employer’s position is effectively supported by a definite presumption that the shopkeeper, his agent, paid. If that presumption is equivalent to certainty, this is a definite claim against a definite claim. If it is only a doubt, how can money be extracted for the shopkeeper? The Shach’s approach thus combines incompatible rationales. Tosafot in Ketubot 87, and likewise the Ran, state explicitly that the cases listed in Shevuot are those in which one might have thought that even with an oath the claimant could not collect. This implies that by strict law the shopkeeper had no right to collect even by oath, contrary to the Shach’s approach. One cannot argue that this is true only according to the view that a definite claim does not prevail over an uncertain response, whereas according to the opposing view the shopkeeper and certainly the workers would collect by strict law, which would make it difficult why the Mishnah lists this case. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 37 explain that Rabbi Tarfon’s ruling concerning one who robbed one of five and does not know which is not inconsistent with the view that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain response, because there the defendant says with certainty, “I owe only one of you, though I do not know which.” The same applies here. The employer can say with certainty, “I owe only one payment, either to the shopkeeper or to the workers.” Thus even according to that view, each individual claimant cannot collect by strict law. This remains true even according to Rabbi Akiva, for in an ordinary purchase Rabbi Akiva agrees with Rabbi Tarfon. Therefore, the correct practical distinction is as follows. If the workers never contradicted the shopkeeper at all, the shopkeeper should collect by strict law. There is no basis to presume that he failed to perform his agency where no one contradicts him. In that limited situation the Shach’s reasoning is sound, because the employer must in any event make the wage payment and there is no competing claimant. This differs from the case of one purchaser and five sellers, where multiple claimants contradict one another. If only one person appears and says, “I sold to you,” and no one else claims to have sold, the buyer plainly pays him and cannot defer payment on the theory that perhaps someone else will later appear. The same is true here. Common sense also requires this result. If a person tells another, “Go pay on my behalf,” without instructing him to pay before witnesses, it would be intolerable for the principal later to refuse reimbursement by saying, “Who says you actually paid?” Such a rule would destroy ordinary dealings and would be worse than ordinary indirect damage. Thus when Baal HaTerumot cites Mahari ibn Palat, he should be understood as speaking where the workers never contradicted the shopkeeper. With no contradiction, we rely on the presumption that the agent performed his mission. In this situation even the Ramban, who requires an oath from the woman claiming she was injured after betrothal, could agree that the shopkeeper need not swear, because there the husband confronts her with a present basis of doubt, whereas here there is no opposing claim at all. This disagrees with the Ramah cited by the Tur, whose language implies that even where the workers do not contradict the shopkeeper, strict law would not allow him to collect. But where the workers did contradict the shopkeeper and then died, and the employer is their heir or their heirs waived the claim, the strict law is that the shopkeeper cannot collect. This is a case of “I do not know whether I borrowed from you,” comparable to purchasing from one of five, because there were competing claims. The fact that only one payment ultimately remains due does not eliminate the contradiction. Once the workers contradicted the shopkeeper, the presumption that the agent performed his mission is neutralized by the equally relevant presumption that workers do not claim unpaid wages falsely. The employer stands in the workers’ place and says with certainty that the shopkeeper did not pay. This is precisely the situation discussed by the Ramban in Milchamot, which is attached to the Mishnah. He is therefore correct that the shopkeeper has no strict-law claim and can collect only by virtue of the enactment. This also explains the Mishnah’s wording, “A shopkeeper on his ledger, how so? The workers say, ‘We did not receive.’” It does not state simply that whenever the shopkeeper says he paid, he swears and collects. Where there is no contradiction at all, he collects without an oath. It likewise explains why the Mishnah lists “shopkeeper on his ledger” rather than the workers, although both swear. The special enactment allowing collection contrary to strict law is needed specifically for the shopkeeper, not for the workers. It also explains Rabbi’s initial statement, before retracting, asking why there should be the burden of two oaths, since on his initial view the workers would collect and the shopkeeper would lose. If both were equally entitled by Torah law, why should the shopkeeper be displaced in favor of the workers? One would have to invent a strained policy reason, that to spare the employer double payment the Sages chose to burden the shopkeeper because he could have protected himself by paying before witnesses, whereas workers ordinarily depend on the employer’s referral to a shopkeeper. According to the distinction above, no such strain is necessary. There is, however, an apparent issue of variant readings in the Gemara. Rashi’s text and our printed text state that Ben Nanas says, “How can these and those come to a false oath? Rather, these and those collect without an oath.” If the shopkeeper by strict law could not collect even with an oath, why should Ben Nanas impose on the employer an obligation contrary to law and award the shopkeeper payment without an oath? He could instead say that the shopkeeper collects by oath while the workers, who are entitled by law, collect without an oath. Then only one oath remains and there is no inevitable false oath. This seems to imply that both parties are entitled by strict law to collect without an oath, as the authorities and Shach maintain. But the Razah’s version, which he calls precise and which was also the Ran’s version, reads that Ben Nanas says, “The workers collect without an oath, and the shopkeeper with an oath.” According to that version the matter fits exactly as stated: the workers collect by strict law and the shopkeeper only by enactment. The dispute therefore appears to depend on the textual variant, a point the Shach did not note.
Still, even according to our text, which is Rashi’s version, one may explain in the opposite direction, that neither the shopkeeper nor even the workers collects by strict law. Although the employer says, “I do not know whether I paid you,” I wrote at length in siman 75 that this creates liability only where the debtor ought to know. Here the employer had no reason to know whether the shopkeeper actually paid the workers; employers ordinarily refer workers to a shopkeeper and do not supervise the payment. Thus he is not considered negligent in his lack of knowledge. This must also be the position of the Ramah and Ramban, who state categorically that the shopkeeper cannot collect by strict law and do not distinguish a case where the employer told the shopkeeper, “Give a sela to this worker and a sela to that worker,” one worker admits receiving and the other denies. In such a case the shopkeeper claims two sela and the employer effectively admits one, which would ordinarily create partial admission and, if he cannot swear, payment by the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. Their failure to mention this shows that where the employer had no reason to know, that rule does not apply. See Shach siman 72. Alternatively, Rashi may hold that because the presumption that an agent performs his mission is applied at least for stringency, the workers who seek to extract money from the employer despite the shopkeeper’s denial do not have a classic “I do not know whether I paid you” case. The chazakah supports the shopkeeper’s assertion of payment sufficiently to reinforce the employer’s possession, even if it is not strong enough by itself to extract money. The employer therefore stands in the shopkeeper’s place with a claim functionally equivalent to “I paid you.” On this view neither side collects by strict law, and both collect only by enactment. Ben Nanas can then say that both collect without an oath, because neither has a superior strict-law entitlement. There is an apparent proof for this approach from Bava Metzia 6a, where the Gemara asks, according to the view that one suspected regarding money is also suspected regarding an oath, how Rabbi Chiya can teach that both the shopkeeper and workers swear and collect from the employer. Why not say that since one of them is suspected of taking money wrongfully, he is also suspect regarding the oath? Rashi asks why the Gemara does not challenge the Mishnah itself. Tosafot answer that Rabbi initially questioned the burden of the oath, and only after Rabbi Chiya taught that both swear and collect was the matter fixed that way. At first glance, why does it matter whether Rabbi originally thought only the workers should swear and the shopkeeper should lose? Even a single claimant who swears and collects is not merely delaying payment and thus should be subject to the same concern that one suspected regarding money is suspected regarding an oath. One cannot say Tosafot thought Rabbi’s original view was that both collect without any oath, because the Gemara later infers from another teaching of Rabbi that he had not retracted, and that would make no sense if his earlier view involved no oath at all. Rashi’s language should therefore be read as explaining two separate baraitot. Rabbi first stated only, “Why this burden of an oath?” without specifying whether he meant that the shopkeeper should lose while the workers swear or that both should collect without oath like Ben Nanas. A second baraita clarified his intended position. Thus the question remains. A broader point may resolve it. The Gemara’s concern that one suspected of monetary dishonesty should also be suspected of a false oath arises for beit din because a robber is legally disqualified from swearing. Yet ordinary people often suspect another person of withholding money while still assuming he will not swear falsely. Indeed, a claimant who demands an oath reveals that he expects the defendant may prefer to pay rather than swear falsely. Many cases of one who swears and collects involve an oath imposed only to reassure the payer, even though beit din itself accepts the claimant’s underlying right. Baal HaTerumot, gate 29, citing Mahari ibn Palat, likewise says that the workers’ oath is for reassurance. In such a case there is no contradiction in imposing the oath merely because the payer suspects the claimant of withholding money. The Gemara therefore specifically challenges Rabbi Chiya’s formulation concerning both parties, because with respect to the shopkeeper, according to the analysis above, he has no strict-law right to collect and the Sages created his right only by enactment, not merely as reassurance. If legal suspicion of monetary wrongdoing also generated suspicion regarding oaths, how could the Sages create an oath mechanism that may itself invite a false oath? This does not necessarily challenge other oath-and-collect cases where the oath is merely an additional safeguard. One may alternatively explain that according to the Sages the very purpose of requiring both parties to swear is to uncover the truth. Jews are not presumed willing to swear falsely, so whichever claimant is lying should withdraw rather than swear. If, however, anyone suspected of money were also legally suspected of an oath, this mechanism would be incoherent, because one of the two is certainly claiming money improperly and yet the court would be setting up a process in which both might swear. This is the force of the Gemara’s question. In any event, the Shach’s conclusion is not decisive. The practical distinction stated above appears correct.
