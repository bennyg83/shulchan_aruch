At first glance this is difficult, because the rule of whoever is stronger prevails applies where each party claims, “It is mine,” whereas here, whichever way the case is understood, there is difficulty. If the buyer already performed one of the valid modes of acquisition, even the seller admits that the produce belongs to the buyer and merely claims the dinar. If the buyer did not perform any mode of acquisition, and is only somewhat in possession because the seller already measured the produce for him, the rule of whoever is stronger prevails still should not apply, because the buyer too admits that the produce belongs to the seller, since he has not acquired it by any mode of acquisition. Ketzot HaChoshen raises this difficulty as well. It therefore appears that the reason is as follows. In Even HaEzer siman 120, Chelkat Mechokek seif katan 5 and Beit Shmuel seif katan 3 write that if one gave a get on credit, even if payment was not later made, the get is not regarded as stolen in her possession. But if he gave it on condition that he be paid immediately, then if he was not paid, the get is regarded as stolen in her possession. It follows that when a seller sells on condition of immediate payment, we do not say that the money merely becomes a debt owed to him. Rather, if payment is not made, the object itself remains the seller's. This is also explicit in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 77, in the responsum of the Rif cited there: if neither party wishes to retract, the seller has received part of the price and presses for the balance, while the buyer does not want the seller to extend the payment, the sale is not complete, neither in whole nor in part, until payment is made, unless the seller accepts the sale as final and converts the remainder into a debt. Accordingly, here too, when the shopkeeper says that he sold the produce on condition that the dinar be paid immediately and it was not paid, the produce itself belongs to the shopkeeper. According to the householder, who says that he paid the dinar, the produce belongs to the householder. This is therefore precisely a case in which each says, “It is mine,” and the rule of whoever is stronger would apply, except that the Shach rejects this because the shopkeeper has the presumption of original ownership. It appears that this applies even where witnesses saw the buyer perform a valid mode of acquisition. The statement that if the shopkeeper were in possession he would take only a heset oath is also understandable. Although the buyer already made a valid acquisition and the shopkeeper claims the dinar while the produce is in the buyer's possession, this is unlike a loan secured by collateral, where the claimant takes an oath while holding a sacred object, because according to the shopkeeper's own claim, since the dinar was not paid the produce remains entirely his. He is therefore treated as one denying the entire claim and is subject only to a heset oath. Ketzot HaChoshen compares this to siman 108, where, if witnesses establish that the property is from this specific business, one collects from heirs without an oath, because merchandise sold on credit is treated by Baal HaTerumot, with respect to a debtor who fled, like a specifically identifiable business asset. But the cases are not comparable at all. There, in siman 108, even a heset oath is unnecessary, because the business assets are still extant and the father certainly did not repay, which is even stronger than a claim within the due date. But if one sold merchandise on credit, retained the merchandise as collateral, and the debtor claims, “I repaid you,” the creditor must swear while holding a sacred object like any claimant relying on collateral. Here, the reason the shopkeeper need only take a heset oath is as I have explained. As a matter of practical law, the later authorities, including Chacham Tzvi in his glosses to the Taz and the Tumim, disagreed with the Shach, and the primary ruling follows the Mechaber and Rama, who rule like the Rif and Rambam. Thus, whenever the produce is not lying in the public domain but remains in the shopkeeper's domain and the shopkeeper claims the dinar, the shopkeeper takes a heset oath. According to what I have explained, even if witnesses saw the buyer make a valid acquisition in the produce, the seller still takes only a heset oath, because when the money is not paid the sale is void where the sale was for immediate cash payment, as stated by Chelkat Mechokek in Even HaEzer siman 120. His words there show that this applies whenever the sale was for immediate payment. When he concludes, “if he sold it on condition that he pay immediately,” he does not mean a formally expressed condition, for at the beginning of his remarks he makes clear that even a simple cash sale in which payment was not made leaves the get stolen in her possession. Rather, he means that it was delivered on the understanding that payment would be made immediately, even without the laws of formal conditions. By contrast, in a credit sale, even if a payment date was fixed and payment was not made at that time, the sale is not void, because the seller already converted the price into a debt. Likewise, in the first clause, where the produce lies in the public domain and the buyer swears and takes it, it appears that this applies even if the buyer did not perform one of the formal modes of acquisition, since the poskim did not distinguish.
