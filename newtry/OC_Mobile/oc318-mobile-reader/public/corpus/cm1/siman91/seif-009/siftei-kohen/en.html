The purchaser swears in accordance with the enactment of the Sages. Since the produce has already left the seller's possession and lies in the public domain, the purchaser swears and takes it. Had it been in the purchaser's possession, he would take only a hesset oath and retain it. Had it remained in the shopkeeper's possession, the shopkeeper would take a hesset oath and retain it.
“And you carried them into your house.” Maimonides explains that if the shopkeeper says, “I never sold you these fruits lying in the public domain,” the shopkeeper takes a stringent oath that this is so and the fruits are returned to him, since he never admitted selling them. If they were in his shop, he would take only a hesset oath. Bartenura writes likewise, and this is also the view of the Rif, as the Ran reports from the Rif's Arabic commentary, and of Baal HaTerumot. Because the early authorities offer five different explanations of this Mishna and none appears satisfactory, I will set out the interpretation that appears to me correct. The Mishna states: A buyer tells a shopkeeper, “Give me produce for a dinar.” The shopkeeper gives it to him and then asks for the dinar. The buyer says, “I already gave it to you and you placed it in your purse.” The buyer swears. If the buyer gave the dinar and then asked for the produce, and the shopkeeper said, “I already gave it to you and you carried it into your house,” the shopkeeper swears. Rabbi Yehuda says that whoever holds the produce has the stronger position. The parallel rule is stated regarding a money changer. The Gemara adds that Rabbi Yehuda's qualification applies when the produce is piled between them and both dispute it, but once the buyer has slung it behind him, the burden of proof rests on the claimant. The Rif and Maimonides understand the case as produce lying in the public domain, with the distinction that in one clause the shopkeeper admits the sale and in the other he denies it. Several difficulties arise. The wording “he gave it to him” ordinarily means an actual delivery. Rabbi Yehuda's phrase “whoever holds the produce” also implies actual possession. The Mishna gives no hint that the shopkeeper admits the sale in one clause and denies it in the other. Nor is “he slung it behind him” a natural expression for bringing it fully into his domain. The Tur, following Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh, explains that the shopkeeper measured the produce into the buyer's vessels while they were still in the shopkeeper's domain. In the reverse clause the buyer placed the dinar in the shop before the shopkeeper took it. Because each item has partly left the prior possessor's control, the party seeking to retain it swears and collects. Rabbi Yehuda holds that an ordinary shopkeeper does not measure produce before receiving payment. This explanation also remains difficult, because “he gave it to him” sounds like an actual transfer, and Rabbi Yehuda should have stated directly that a shopkeeper does not extend credit. It is also difficult to understand why the Sages would disagree if this particular shopkeeper was known not to sell on credit. Rashi understands the Mishna literally. In the first clause the produce is already behind the buyer, while the dinar remains with him. Rabbi Yehuda says that the buyer need not swear because a shopkeeper who does not sell on credit would not have released the produce without payment. In the second clause Rabbi Yehuda agrees that the shopkeeper swears, because the produce remains in the shopkeeper's domain. This explanation is more direct, but it is still difficult that Rabbi Yehuda's statement appears after both clauses and is expressed generally. The Raavad explains that in the first clause the produce is in the buyer's basket behind him, but the dispute arose before he left the shop. The Sages therefore imposed an oath. In the second clause the produce is piled on the ground. Rabbi Yehuda says that only while the produce is piled between them is there an oath enactment, whereas once it is behind the buyer the burden of proof is on the claimant. This too requires forcing Rabbi Yehuda's qualification partly onto one clause and partly onto another. The Semag, following Rabbenu Chananel, explains that the produce is in the public domain, or that both parties grasp the dinar. Yet it is difficult why the shopkeeper should have a stronger right to swear and take than the buyer. The correct explanation appears to me as follows. This Mishna continues the category of those who swear and collect. Just as the shopkeeper on his ledger and the workers swear and collect although the employer has only an uncertain claim against them, so too here the dispute itself is uncertain on one side. In the first clause it is agreed that the shopkeeper gave the produce, which is already in the buyer's basket behind him. The shopkeeper, not seeing the dinar before him, thinks it was not paid. The buyer says that he paid it before receiving the produce and that the shopkeeper placed it in his purse but forgot because he was occupied measuring the produce. Although the shopkeeper's claim is only uncertain and the produce is in the buyer's basket, the dispute arose while they were still in the shop before the buyer brought it home. The Sages therefore treat the buyer as one who is still collecting from the shop and require a Mishnaic oath. In the second clause it is agreed that the buyer gave the dinar and it remains in the shopkeeper's hand. The buyer, accustomed to carrying produce home in several loads, thinks he has not yet received it. The shopkeeper says that he gave the produce before receiving the dinar and that the buyer carried it home but forgot. Although the buyer's claim is only uncertain, and the dinar and produce are both effectively in the shopkeeper's control, the dispute arose before the shopkeeper placed the dinar in his purse. The Sages therefore treat him as one who is now collecting from the buyer and require him to swear. Rabbi Yehuda disputes both clauses. In his view, whoever presently holds the produce is treated as though he already brought it home, even if the dispute arose in the shop. Only where the produce is piled between them, equally accessible to both, must the party making the definite claim swear before taking it. This explains the baraita's word “when,” which here introduces a limitation and dispute. Rabbi Yehuda does not say that a shopkeeper never extends credit, because he agrees that a shopkeeper may do so. His rule rests on possession. A money changer is different, because a money changer ordinarily does not hand over coins before receiving the dinar. Therefore, even when the money is in the money changer's hand, his claim that he gave change before receiving the dinar is inherently implausible. This reading also explains the Gemara's discussion of why both a shopkeeper and a money changer had to be taught. Produce spoils, so one might have thought the Sages require an oath only there because shopkeepers commonly release produce before receiving payment, whereas coins do not spoil. Conversely, one might have thought Rabbi Yehuda's rule applies only to money changing. The Yerushalmi and Tosefta strongly support this explanation. They state that Rabbi Yehuda spoke in dispute, not merely in explanation, and that when the basket lies between them he agrees with the Sages, whereas when it has left the control of either party the burden of proof lies on the claimant. Their language shows that Rabbi Yehuda's rule applies whichever party presently holds the item. This contradicts the readings of the Rif, Maimonides, Rabbenu Tam, Rashi, the Raavad, and the Semag in their respective details, but it accords with the plain sense of the Mishna, Tosefta, Yerushalmi, and Bavli. Nevertheless, when the produce is entirely in the shopkeeper's domain and both are uncertain whether the dinar was paid, the Sages agree that the buyer cannot swear and take. This is the ordinary case of one who says, “You owe me one hundred,” and the other says, “I do not know,” where the possessor is exempt. Likewise, if the dinar is entirely in the buyer's possession and the shopkeeper claims definitely that produce was delivered while the buyer is uncertain, the shopkeeper does not swear and take. At most, because of Rav Nachman's enactment, the possessor takes a hesset oath that he does not know. There is also reason to distinguish between a dispute that comes before beit din while the produce is still behind the buyer or the dinar still in the shopkeeper's hand, and one in which the item was already carried fully home or placed in the purse before litigation. Rabbi Yehuda certainly exempts once the item has been brought home. It is therefore preferable to say that the Sages disagree only while the parties come before us with the item still in the intermediate state. Moreover, their oath is an enactment, and one should not extend an enactment beyond its stated case. Accordingly, once the buyer has brought the produce home, he is exempt without a Mishnaic oath. If the shopkeeper makes a definite claim, the buyer takes only a hesset oath. The same applies when the shopkeeper has already placed the dinar in his purse. As for produce lying in the public domain where both parties make definite claims, the Rif and Maimonides derived their ruling from their interpretation of the Mishna. Once that interpretation is rejected, the independent law requires analysis. At first glance one might apply the rule that the stronger party prevails, as where neither party possesses a disputed boat. Yet when there is a prior owner, the property remains with that prior owner even if presently in a neutral place. We rule against Sumchus that property standing in a meadow remains with the prior owner, and the same applies in the public domain. Thus the produce remains presumptively the shopkeeper's, and he takes it after a hesset oath. Even if the shopkeeper is uncertain and the buyer is certain, prior ownership ordinarily prevails. In practice, however, this remains difficult because Maimonides rules differently in a related case where both parties are uncertain. Where the shopkeeper measured the produce into the buyer's vessel while it remains in the shopkeeper's domain, the result depends on whether the shopkeeper said, “Acquire through this vessel.” If he did, the buyer's vessel acquired the produce for him, and he is treated as possessor. He therefore takes the produce after a Mishnaic oath, whether the shopkeeper's denial is definite or uncertain. If the shopkeeper did not authorize acquisition through the vessel, the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain does not acquire according to the Rif and Maimonides, and even according to the Rosh the issue is unresolved. The produce therefore remains with the prior owner, the shopkeeper, who is exempt after a hesset oath. If there are no witnesses whether the shopkeeper said, “Acquire through this vessel,” and the buyer claims that he did while the shopkeeper denies it, one might attempt to treat the buyer as possessor through a double doubt. Yet monetary law generally follows possession rather than a double doubt. Since the shopkeeper is the prior owner, it is more reasonable to leave the produce with him. Even if the shopkeeper admits authorizing the acquisition, he may perhaps be believed to say that payment was not made by virtue of a migo that he could have denied authorizing acquisition altogether. This point requires study. If the shopkeeper is uncertain about payment and admits that he authorized acquisition, the buyer is certainly the possessor and swears and takes. If both parties are uncertain, and the produce lies in the buyer's vessel in the shopkeeper's domain without authorization to acquire, it remains with the shopkeeper as prior owner. This differs from ordinary jointly held property where neither side has prior ownership, in which case parties who are both uncertain divide without an oath. The rules attributed to Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh may be reconciled with these conclusions by saying that they discuss a case where the shopkeeper expressly authorized the buyer's vessel to acquire. The first and second clauses would then be parallel, each involving property already in the other party's acquiring domain. Regarding the Semag's case where both grasp the dinar, or where it lies in the public domain, his conclusion that the shopkeeper swears and takes is not convincing. If both grasp it, they divide it under the rules of two people holding a garment, with the required oath. If it lies in the public domain and neither has possession, the stronger party prevails, unless prior ownership can properly be applied. All of this is stated as law for study, not as a practical ruling against the great early authorities. The interpretation of the Mishna and baraita themselves, however, appears correct for both law and practice, since no authority states a rule contradicting it.
