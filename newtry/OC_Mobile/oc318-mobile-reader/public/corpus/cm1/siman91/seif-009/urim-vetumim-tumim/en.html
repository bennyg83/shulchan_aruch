In truth, his objections are not strong enough to justify rejecting the earlier authorities. They depend on excessively fine textual deductions, and if one insisted on such precision everywhere, hardly a tannaitic formulation or clear Talmudic law would survive. The Shach then proposes an explanation of his own, but the gaon Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses to the Taz, raises a powerful objection that refutes it entirely. See there. We therefore need only answer the Shach’s objection to the Semag. The Semag explains that when the produce is lying in the public domain, the shopkeeper demands the money and the purchaser says, “I already paid you,” the purchaser swears while holding a sacred object and takes the produce. When the produce remains in the shopkeeper’s possession and the dinar lies in the public domain, while the shopkeeper says, “I already gave you produce for it,” and the purchaser says, “No, I have not yet received the produce,” the shopkeeper swears. The Shach asks why the shopkeeper’s position is stronger in the latter case than the purchaser’s in the former, such that the shopkeeper may swear and retain. There is no difficulty. In every case we identify the actual subject of the dispute. When the produce lies in the public domain, both parties agree that this produce is there for the purchaser to take. There is no dispute concerning ownership of the produce. This is especially clear according to the Maggid Mishneh, who says that the purchaser already lifted the produce and acquired it through an effective kinyan. The only dispute is whether the purchase money was paid, which is an independent monetary claim. The purchaser therefore swears and is exempt from paying again. Conversely, when the dinar lies in the public domain, the parties do not dispute that this dinar is to go to the shopkeeper. Their dispute concerns the produce: the purchaser demands produce, while the shopkeeper says, “I already gave it to you.” The disputed subject is therefore produce in the shopkeeper’s possession, and the rule is that one who seeks to extract from a possessor bears the burden of proof. Thus the shopkeeper swears and retains. The Shach also relies on the Yerushalmi, which says that when the object emerges from the possession of one of them, “it remains in dispute,” and he infers that even when the object is actually under the purchaser’s control, the first tanna and Rabbi Yehudah still disagree. This conflicts with the Rif, Tosafot, and their followers, who hold that when the object is in the possession of one party no oath is needed at all. But there is no reliable proof from the Yerushalmi. Its text and emendation are notoriously difficult. Moreover, on examining the Yerushalmi, I found the passage reads: Rabbi Chiya says, “It is in dispute.” Rabbi Yehudah says, “When is this? When the basket lies between them. But if it comes out from under the hand of one of them, it is still in dispute.” According to the Shach, “in dispute” would mean the dispute between Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages. This does not fit the language. It would be strange for Rabbi Yehudah to say of his own ruling, “it is still in dispute,” meaning that he himself disagrees. He should have said directly, “If it comes out from under one party, the Sages say such-and-such and I say otherwise.” Nor is Rabbi Chiya’s opening statement, “It is in dispute,” intelligible if he merely means that the Mishnah itself records a dispute between Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages. That teaches nothing. And if he means that they dispute even when the object is under one party’s control, that crucial point is absent from his statement. The better explanation is based on a familiar dispute throughout the Talmud. Where property is in doubt, the Sages hold that the claimant must bring proof, while Sumchus says they divide. Rabbi Chiya is saying that the Mishnah’s rule that either the shopkeeper or the employer swears and takes follows the Sages, who give the property to the stronger possessor, though here they additionally require that possessor to take an oath while holding a sacred object. According to Sumchus, by contrast, the fact that one party has the stronger possession does not give him the entire property; they divide. That is the “dispute” Rabbi Chiya references. Rabbi Yehudah then qualifies the matter: when the basket lies in the public domain, he regards it as certainly remaining in the chazakah of its original owner, so there is no true doubt before beit din. But if it actually comes out from under the hand of the other party, then the familiar dispute remains. The Sages still say that the claimant must bring proof, whereas Sumchus says that since the other party has some possession in it, it is a genuine contested fund and they divide. Thus “it is still in dispute” refers to the well-known Sages and Sumchus dispute found throughout the Talmud, not to Rabbi Yehudah disputing himself. Anyone familiar with the concise idiom of the Yerushalmi will see that this reading approaches the truth. Its words are poor in one place and rich in another.
