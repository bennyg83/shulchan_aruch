The Mishnah in Perek Kol HaNishba'in states: if one says to a shopkeeper, "Give me produce for a dinar," and the shopkeeper gives it, then says, "Give me the dinar," while the buyer says, "I already gave it to you and you put it in your purse," the buyer swears. If the buyer says, "Give me the produce," and the shopkeeper says, "I already gave it to you and you carried it into your house," the shopkeeper swears. Rabbi Yehudah says that whoever has the produce in his hand has the upper hand. The same structure is stated concerning a money changer. Rambam, whose explanation the Shulchan Aruch follows, establishes the first clause where the produce lies in the public domain. The shopkeeper claims the dinar and the buyer says he already paid. Since the shopkeeper admits that he already sold the produce to the buyer and that it was placed in the public domain for him, ownership of the produce has already passed. The shopkeeper now seeks only to extract the price from the buyer. Therefore the buyer swears. If the produce were already in the buyer's hand, he would need only hesset. If it remained in the shopkeeper's hand, the shopkeeper would retain it with hesset. When it lies in the public domain, the buyer takes it through the Mishnah oath. In the second clause the produce also lies in the public domain, but the shopkeeper does not admit that these particular fruits were sold to the buyer. He says the fruits bought for the dinar were already delivered, and these are his own. Since he denies having sold these fruits, he retains the stronger original position and takes the produce with an oath. Ran cites this explanation. Other early authorities offer other constructions, all reviewed by the Shach. Chacham Tzvi analyzes the various approaches and sharply objects to the Shach's interpretation that the Mishnah may involve an uncertain claim. He argues that according to Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah, who hold a certain claim superior to an uncertain one, a certain claimant should recover without an oath. He therefore says the Shach's reading cannot stand. I do not see this as decisive. Even where the law says the certain claimant is believed, that can mean believed with an oath. Ramban, cited by Baal HaTerumot, explicitly says that in the case "from after betrothal I was violated," her credibility can still require a severe oath. Likewise, the phrase "liable" does not necessarily mean without oath. The Geonic view in "I do not know whether I repaid you" can require the claimant to swear even though the defendant is described simply as liable. Indeed, Rav Huna and Rav Yehudah themselves must sometimes require an oath in cases of certain against uncertain. The Mishnah says that one who spends money on his wife's property swears how much he spent and collects, and one who recognizes his stolen utensils in another's hand, after a public report of theft, swears how much he paid and collects. If certain against uncertain always meant collection without oath, these rules would be difficult. Therefore the Shach's interpretation cannot be rejected merely from that argument. Still, I do not seek to add a new reading of the Mishnah itself. My concern is to defend the legal conclusions of Rif, Rambam, Tosafot, Rosh, and Semag. The Shach argues that once the Mishnah is not read like Rif and Rambam, their distinction between produce lying in the public domain and produce actually held by one side lacks source. He suggests that if the produce lies in the public domain and both parties make certain claims, perhaps the rule should be whoever is stronger prevails, or perhaps the original owner's presumption controls. In my view Rif and Rambam are correct. The original owner's presumption applies only when it is uncertain whether a sale occurred, as in the exchange of a cow for a donkey. Here the shopkeeper admits that he sold the produce to the buyer through a valid mode of acquisition. The produce has therefore already left his original ownership. He claims only the money. The buyer is the possessor of the dinar. There is no remaining original ownership presumption in the produce. One might still ask why the shopkeeper is not believed through a migo that he could have denied selling the produce at all. The answer follows Rabbeinu Yonah in siman 150. One who occupies land as a mortgage and also has another oral claim can use a migo of purchase only after he has already consumed produce equal to the second claim. Before that, he cannot continue to hold the land merely through migo, because that would be a migo to extract money. The same applies here. Once the shopkeeper admits that the produce was already sold and does not physically hold it, his claim is only for money. The produce lying in the public domain does not count as his actual possession. His migo would therefore be a migo to extract and cannot help. This also answers the Shach's attempt to rely on original ownership. Once the shopkeeper admits the sale, there is no original ownership presumption. If he himself is uncertain while the buyer is certain, there is also no migo because he cannot assert the alternative denial of sale with certainty. Rambam adds that if the shopkeeper actually holds the produce, he need only take hesset. At first this seems difficult because, after admitting the sale, he is still claiming money against property that belongs to the buyer, and Geonic practice ordinarily requires a severe oath where one holds another's object as security. It appears that Rambam regards continued possession of the actual merchandise sold as stronger. Although the buyer has acquired it, the seller who still holds the very goods for their unpaid price resembles an iska holder and can retain them with hesset. I explained a similar rule in siman 72 concerning one who retains the specific merchandise he sold while claiming its price. This yields a coherent practical structure. If the produce lies in the public domain, the shopkeeper admits the buyer acquired it and does not physically hold it. His migo is extractive, so the buyer swears and takes. If the shopkeeper actually holds the produce, he has a true possessory migo and needs only hesset. The Shach also discusses Tosafot and Rosh where the shopkeeper measured the produce into the buyer's vessels while those vessels remained in the seller's premises. Since the law is uncertain whether a buyer's vessels acquire in the seller's domain unless the seller says, "Acquire with your vessels," the Shach treats the shopkeeper as possessor unless such language is established. If it is uncertain whether he said it, there are two doubts favoring the buyer. The Shach nevertheless says multiple doubts do not overcome a monetary presumption. In Shev Shemateta I explained that the rule that majority or multiple doubts do not overcome a monetary presumption applies to one who is presently actually holding the money or object. Where neither party is presently in actual possession and there is only an original ownership presumption, majority and multiple doubts can prevail, just as majority prevails over ordinary presumptions in other areas of law. Here, once the produce was placed in the buyer's vessels in the seller's premises and it is uncertain whether the seller said, "Acquire," the shopkeeper is not clearly in actual possession. Therefore the multiple doubts can overcome mere original ownership. Semag's explanation concerns a case where both parties hold the dinar or where it lies in the public domain. The Shach objects: why should the shopkeeper have greater power than the buyer and take the dinar with an oath? Chacham Tzvi also finds Semag's reading difficult. But Semag can be defended. Semag explains the first clause like Rif and Rambam: the shopkeeper admits that the buyer already acquired the produce, which now lies in the public domain, and only the price is disputed. The buyer therefore swears and takes the produce. If the produce were still in the shopkeeper's hand, the shopkeeper would retain it with hesset because he holds the specific merchandise for its price. Semag interprets the second clause symmetrically. The buyer admits that he handed over the dinar in a manner by which the shopkeeper acquired it, but claims the produce has not yet been delivered. The dinar now lies in the public domain or both hold it. Since the buyer admits the shopkeeper already acquired the dinar, his present claim is really for the produce or for equivalent repayment. The shopkeeper is therefore stronger and swears. One might object that money does not acquire movables, so even if the shopkeeper acquired the dinar, the buyer could retract before pulling the produce. Tosafot, however, explains that although money does not complete the acquisition of the merchandise, the seller can acquire the specific money such that he need not return those same coins but may repay with others. Once the buyer admits that the shopkeeper acquired the dinar, the specific coin has left the buyer's ownership. The buyer's claim is now for produce or a substitute dinar, and the shopkeeper can be treated as possessor for the disputed coin. The Shach says that where both parties hold the dinar they should divide it like two holding a garment, and where it lies in the public domain whoever is stronger should prevail. He also questions whether original ownership can apply to coins, because money is made to be spent. In Shev Shemateta I explained at length that an original ownership presumption can apply even to a dinar and even where property is in a jointly accessible place. The Shach's objections therefore do not overturn Semag's approach.
