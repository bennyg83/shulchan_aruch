Teshuvot Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 78, discusses a former communal administrator, appointed by the government, who long after leaving office claimed a large amount for communal expenses he had advanced. The local rabbi ruled that because he had not produced witnesses that he asserted this claim when he left office, and because a long period had elapsed without mention of it, he could no longer collect even with an oath. Chatam Sofer approved: otherwise anyone who once handled another's property and spent money on it could indefinitely revive claims and extract funds by oath. If, however, the former administrator names particular people who knew of the claim at the time, those persons may be required to take a heset oath concerning their share. If two valid witnesses confirm his account, their testimony obligates the entire community. A general ban may also be proclaimed seeking testimony. This responsum appears to treat a communal administrator like any other authorized spender, who ordinarily requires an oath to collect, though perhaps that case differs because he was appointed by the government rather than by the community. Teshuvot Avodat HaGershuni, siman 19, citing Mahari Weil, rules that a communal administrator appointed with the community's consent need not render an accounting because they trusted him, and further argues that where such an administrator customarily advances his own money to the communal treasury, he is believed without an oath even to collect from the community. The reason is that requiring an oath would deter competent persons from accepting public office, much as in certain laws of guardians. The same applies to a tax collector appointed by communal consent when it is customary for him to advance his own funds. That responsum also considers a tax collector whose ledger contained suspicious later-looking entries and totals inconsistent with those additions. Although public officials are ordinarily trusted, once a substantial defect appears in the ledger, the community can argue that they never trusted him under such circumstances. The responsum compares this to a creditor granted special credibility who is later shown to have lied, and concludes that the collector cannot recover the questioned amounts, and in that case cannot recover even other alleged balances from the community by oath, because his credibility has been materially undermined.
