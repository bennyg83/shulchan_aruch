Since there are indications, etc. Shach thought to infer that absent such indications one cannot even impose hesset based solely on one's ledger, because the claim would not count as definite, unless the ledger is known to be exceptionally precise. According to him, if the employer says, 'I never instructed you to pay the workers,' the shopkeeper cannot impose hesset merely from an ordinary ledger. See Tumim. I wrote that this is not practical law. There is no need to distinguish between ledgers. If a person knows that he does not enter false items and records his dealings accurately, the entry gives him a definite claim sufficient to impose hesset. If he is himself in possession of money, he may swear that the ledger makes the truth clear to him. The phrase 'since there are indications' explains only why he may actually collect on the basis of the ledger. A ledger entry by itself does not give a shopkeeper a special power to swear and collect as though under secular law, but it can certainly support a definite claim. This is how experienced decisors rule.
On his ledger. The source case involved a lender who said he had lent only ten, but found in his ledger that he had lent fifteen. Rosh ruled that he may swear that the loan was fifteen according to his ledger, even though he no longer remembers the amount independently. According to Shach above, this would require a highly precise ledger. I have already written that such a special requirement is unnecessary.
