(Seif 7) “He fixed how much he would give.” The Tur states this on the authority of Sefer HaTerumot in the name of Ramban, section 39. Ramban holds that the Sages imposed an oath upon the shopkeeper only after the workers contradict him. Although one who spends upon his wife’s property, or one who recognizes his articles and books in siman 357, must swear even where no one contradicts him, those cases differ because two considerations are present: the presumption that an agent performs his agency, and the fact that one party claims with certainty while the other is uncertain. Sefer HaTerumot then writes in Ramban’s name that even if the employer did not fix the amount, the case is treated as though he had fixed it, and the shopkeeper collects by an oath. This makes the Tur’s statement at the beginning of seif 3 difficult. There he says that Ramah’s view is reasonable, that even if the worker died the shopkeeper must swear. All the more so should an oath be required here where no amount was fixed, for Sefer HaTerumot itself must compare an unfixed amount to a fixed one, and the Tur writes here, “even if he did not fix it,” implying that fixing it is stronger. Sema, seif katan 18, writes in the name of Maharshal that here it is known that the shopkeeper gave something, but the amount is unknown. This is exceedingly difficult. Ramah, who holds above that the shopkeeper always swears and derives the law from one who spends upon his wife’s property, as Ran explains and Beit Yosef cites, does not rely upon the presumption that an agent performs his agency. If so, why is this case different from the earlier shopkeeper case? Nor does it help to say that in the cases of one who spends upon his wife’s property and one who recognizes his articles and books, it is likewise known that he spent something and only the amount is unknown. Even there he takes an oath modeled after a Torah oath, for these laws are Mishnayot in HaGozel Batra and Metziat HaIshah. It is likewise clear that Rama’s statement in seif 3, “according to what he spent,” means that he swears and collects by a stringent oath. Sema’s statement that it means a hesset oath is exceedingly difficult, because at the time of the Mishnah the hesset oath had not yet been enacted. It is plainly a Mishnaic oath taken while holding a sacred object. I cannot reconcile the Tur except as follows. The Tur cited this law according to Ramban, who holds above that the shopkeeper swears only when contradicted, and Sefer HaTerumot there follows the same view. Ramban writes that even without fixing the amount the case is treated as fixed. According to Ramah, who requires an oath even where the worker does not contradict the shopkeeper, we can at least derive from Ramban that the oath is effective even where no amount was fixed; we do not say that an oath cannot establish an amount the employer never expressly stated.
