These are the Rosh’s words. The Shach infers that one cannot even impose a hesset oath on the basis of the ledger, because otherwise what is meant by saying that we do not rely on it? Even if we did rely on it, the ledger could be no stronger than the shopkeeper’s own statement. In practice I have not seen the law applied that way. Every day merchants, meat sellers, and wine sellers extend credit and record the debt on a board or ledger. A year or two later they sue according to their books because it is impossible to remember every transaction orally, and beit din nevertheless requires the denying customer to swear. Perhaps this is a commercial necessity and an accepted practice that can affect the rule. Yet I found that the Rambam, Hilchot Malveh 16, writes: if a shopkeeper says to a customer to whom he extends credit, “My ledger says that I have a maneh due from you,” and the customer says, “I do not know,” the customer swears hesset, like any litigant sued by another. This does not derive from the special rabbinic enactment. Thus the Rambam interprets the Mishnah’s phrase, “not that he merely says, ‘Thus it is written in my ledger that you owe me,’” in a manner that still permits hesset. It is difficult to say that the Rosh disagrees with the Rambam on this point. It therefore seems that the Rosh agrees that the ledger can support a hesset claim, but if the shopkeeper seized property on the strength of the ledger he cannot retain it, since that would be possession based on a doubt. The Shach himself writes above in siman 75, concerning an open chest, that it is more reasonable to impose hesset than to permit a claimant to seize another’s property on the basis of a doubtful claim. This may be the Rosh’s meaning in saying that we do not rely on the ledger. If so, however, when the Rosh says that a person may swear on the basis of his ledger, as the Rama cites and as appears in responsa of the Rosh, rule 103, to retain a pledge, one would need to distinguish different qualities of ledgers, as the Shach does. That is strained, for all these cases can speak of a carefully maintained ledger in which the owner knows he does not enter falsehood, even though he cannot independently remember each transaction. In truth, I do not understand the Shach’s difficulty with the Rosh at all. The Rosh is explaining the Mishnah. It speaks of “a shopkeeper on his ledger,” but says this does not mean that merely writing in his ledger lets him collect. One might have thought that because a shopkeeper regularly extends credit and would undermine his own commercial credibility by making false entries, the Sages trusted him to collect from any person by oath. The Mishnah teaches that this is not so. A customer may say, “You have no authority to make yourself trustworthy against me.” The Sages gave the shopkeeper this enhanced status only where the employer said to him, “Give to my workers,” so that there are independent grounds supporting his claim. The Rosh states this clearly in responsum rule 86, and the Tur says likewise: the ledger is not itself proof against everyone. The rejected assumption was that the Sages specifically trusted the shopkeeper to collect by oath because false entries would damage his business reputation. That must be the Mishnah’s issue, since at the time of the Mishnah hesset had not yet been instituted. The initial thought was therefore that a shopkeeper would swear and collect from anyone. The ordinary practice discussed above is entirely consistent with this. The Sema likewise explains that the ledger can create a definite claim, so if the customer denies that he ever instructed the shopkeeper to give anything, the customer swears hesset. According to the Shach’s inference there would be no independent supporting circumstances there, since the employer denies having given any instruction at all.
