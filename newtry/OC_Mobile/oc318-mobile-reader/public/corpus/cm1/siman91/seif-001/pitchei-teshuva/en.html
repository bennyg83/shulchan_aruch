Lend me one maneh. See Baer Heitev. Teshuvot Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat siman 11, discusses a borrower who authorized a lender to give money to the borrower's servant without specifying any amount. The servant brought the borrower one hundred gold coins. The lender later claimed that he had given the servant one hundred fifty, while the servant denied receiving more than one hundred. Beit Ephraim explains at length that this is not governed by siman 121, where a principal who sent an agent to borrow a fixed amount may be obligated according to what he authorized, because there the dispute includes the extent of the authorization and, in the classic case, the agent supports the lender's account. Here no amount was fixed, and the servant denies receiving the additional sum. Nor is this simply the rule of the shopkeeper and workers. The shopkeeper was appointed to carry out the employer's directive, and the employer relied upon him to fulfill that agency; that circumstance underlies the special oath and collection. A lender who is merely permitted to hand funds to the borrower's messenger is not thereby the borrower's agent in the same sense. Moreover, the messenger's definite denial can be treated as furnishing the borrower with a definite claim, since the messenger is not financially interested: he could equally have said he received the money and delivered it to the borrower. Accordingly, the borrower may plead definitely, on the basis of his messenger, that no more than one hundred was received. Even according to the Ramban, who treats the shopkeeper's rule as a rabbinic enactment, one should not extend the enactment to a case outside the defined agency. There is an additional reason: if one invokes a presumption that an agent performs his mission, it would support the messenger's claim as well, for had he received the extra money he presumptively would have delivered it to the borrower. Finally, where the principal left the amount entirely to the lender's discretion, saying in effect, 'Give him whatever you wish,' there is no meaningful presumption that any particular amount was given. Therefore the case returns to the ordinary rule of 'I do not know whether I ever became obligated,' and the borrower is exempt from the additional amount. See the responsum for its full analysis.
