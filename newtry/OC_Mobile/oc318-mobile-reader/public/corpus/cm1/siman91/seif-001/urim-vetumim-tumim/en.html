Both swear. The Shach writes that if one of them is a non-Jew who contradicts the other, who is Jewish, the Jewish claimant who comes to collect swears while holding a sacred object and collects. His proof is from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 185. Reuven gave a ring to a broker to pawn. The broker said that he pawned it with a non-Jew, who sold it without his knowledge or consent, while the non-Jew denied this. The Rashba ruled that the broker must swear, as in the case of a shopkeeper on his ledger. I am astonished. The Rashba is not discussing the Mishnah’s oath of one who swears and collects, for the broker is not collecting at all; he swears and is exempt. The Rashba is speaking only of a hesset oath, as his own words show: “There is not even a hesset oath here because the non-Jew does not contradict him; had the non-Jew contradicted him, he would require an oath like a shopkeeper on his ledger.” His reference is to hesset. He learns only from the shopkeeper case that a definite claim transmitted through another person can impose an oath. From this one may derive that even a hesset oath can be imposed on the basis of such a report. But where is there proof that the Mishnah’s enactment allowing one to swear and collect was instituted where the contradicting party is a non-Jew? Since their statements are not given the same legal credibility, no such enactment was necessarily made, and whenever an enactment can be construed narrowly, we construe it narrowly. Moreover, the Rashba himself requires explanation. If the enactment of a shopkeeper on his ledger does not directly apply there because, as stated, the broker is merely exempting himself, and the Rashba obligates hesset only because of the report made to the claimant, we concluded above in siman 75 that even when the reporter is Jewish, if he is personally interested in the testimony no oath is imposed on the basis of his report. All the more so here, where the non-Jew would be interested, since if he admitted that the ring had been pawned to him and that he sold it, he might be liable to pay. One must say that local civil law allowed him to sell the pledge once the redemption period elapsed and exempted him, so he was not an interested party. This is clear. If so, the Shach’s proof disappears, for in our case a non-Jewish shopkeeper who contradicts the worker is interested in the outcome. Even if he has already received payment from the employer and claims that he gave it to the workers, otherwise the employer would seek recovery from him; certainly if he has not yet been paid, he is interested. How can an oath be imposed on the basis of a statement by one who is interested? The Sages enacted the rule where the parties are Jews, who are presumptively upright and not lying, but where a non-Jew is interested, his statement cannot impose an oath. The Rashba spoke only where the reporter was not interested. It therefore also appears that the Maggid Mishneh does not disagree with the Rashba. His intent is only that where the reporter is interested, no oath arises from the report, just as even the report of an interested Jew does not impose one. Thus the Shach’s ruling has no firm basis, and wherever the enactment can be narrowed, it should be narrowed. Still, one might distinguish between a claimant who would collect by strict law without any oath and where the Sages merely required an oath as an additional safeguard, in which case one could say that they did not impose that requirement merely because of a non-Jew’s statement, and a case where absent the enactment the claimant could not collect at all, while the Sages created a right to collect only by oath. In the latter case, saying that no special oath was enacted for a non-Jew does not by itself allow collection, for by strict law there is no right to collect; the enactment itself grants collection only with an oath. Yet even this requires study, for perhaps contradiction by a non-Jew is not legally considered contradiction at all, in which case the claimants may collect by strict law. The Rashba provides no proof, as explained, because there the non-Jew was not interested. Still, the distinction just stated appears reasonable.
