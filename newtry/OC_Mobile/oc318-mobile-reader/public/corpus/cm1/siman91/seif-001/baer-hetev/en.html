Admits. If the employer denies the entire claim or part of it, he is exempt even from a shevuat heset, because the workers also contradict the shopkeeper. This is unlike the Sema, who writes that he must take a shevuat heset or a Torah oath as in seif 6. The cases are not comparable, as explained there. Shach.
Both. Although one of them certainly swears falsely, the Sages did not wish to obligate the employer until both swear. Once both swear, both collect. The employer certainly owed the worker, and he did not instruct the shopkeeper to make payment before witnesses. The shopkeeper can therefore say, "I assumed they were trustworthy in your eyes." The Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule similarly in siman 58, end of seif 1, regarding repayment through an agent. Sema. Even if the shopkeeper regularly entrusted property to that worker, this does not change the rule. Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 126. The Shach writes that even if the shopkeeper is a non-Jew and contradicts a Jewish worker or employer, the Jew must swear. This follows from the Responsa Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 72 and from the end of siman 185. Although the Maggid Mishneh may imply in siman 149, seif 14, that a shevuat heset is not imposed based solely on the definite claim of a non-Jew, here, where the claimant seeks to collect, all may agree that he collects only upon an oath. If one said to another, "Give one maneh to a certain non-Jew to whom I owe it," and the agent gave it without witnesses, while the non-Jew says he did not receive it and the agent says he did, see Knesset HaGedolah citing Responsa Radbaz II, siman 187, for the ruling. He also cites Responsa Maharashdam, Choshen Mishpat 78, concerning Levi who instructed Reuven to write a certain message to Shimon, Reuven's partner. Reuven says he did so, while the partner says he did not. Reuven and his partner swear, and Levi bears the loss.
The other. This requirement applies initially. If one swore outside the presence of the other, he need not swear again. So writes the Bach. The Shach notes that the wording implies the shopkeeper swears first, and that this appears correct, although he also writes that the Bach's point is not conclusive.
When they are present. If they are not present, each can say to the employer, "Bring my opponent here and I will swear in his presence. I need my money now, so I will swear in his absence." So writes the Ran. Sema.
Lend me. Meaning that one should not think this enactment applies only to a shopkeeper because he commonly gives goods on credit. It applies even where the employer gives such an instruction to another person who is not a shopkeeper. So states Baal HaTerumot, contrary to the Maggid Mishneh in the name of an unnamed authority. Shach.
Together. Hagahot Mordechai explains that this avoids making the oaths appear so plainly as vain oaths. When each swears separately, one can say that at the moment each swears he may be swearing truthfully; if they swear simultaneously, the contradiction is more apparent. Sema.
