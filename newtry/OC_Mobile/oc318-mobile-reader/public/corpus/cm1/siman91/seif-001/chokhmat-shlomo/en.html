Seif 2: When is this said? When both parties are before us, etc. In the gloss: Some say, etc. Note: See Shach, who decides in accordance with Rambam. He reasons that Rav Huna and Rav Yehuda hold that even where one says, “You have a maneh of mine,” and the other says, “I do not know,” the uncertain defendant would be liable, whereas Rav Nachman and Rabbi Yochanan exempt him only because of the presumption of monetary possession. Here, says the Shach, there is no relevant presumption of possession because the defendant must in any event pay this money to someone; the only question is to which claimant. In my humble opinion, one can prove otherwise, and this constitutes a clear proof against the Rambam as well. Even in such a case a presumption of monetary possession can operate. The proof is from Gittin 24b. Regarding a get written to divorce the older of two wives, which may not be used for the younger one, Rava infers that two men with identical names living in one city may each produce a promissory note against others. Abaye challenges him from the first clause: where the husband says, “My name is the same as yours,” the get is invalid. It would follow that the second man cannot use it but the first man can, yet we have a rule that another person cannot produce a debt document against two men with identical names because each can say it belongs to the other. This challenge is difficult if the Shach is correct. We know that a presumption of monetary possession is stronger than the presumption that a married woman remains married, for in many areas we follow majority evidence against other presumptions while we do not follow a majority to extract money. Accordingly, in Rava’s inference, where the two men with identical names seek to collect from another, the debtor admittedly owes the money in any event; the only question is which of them is entitled to receive it. This is precisely analogous to the Shach’s case, and there should be no presumption of possession. Rava could therefore reason a fortiori that if we are not concerned for the marital presumption, all the more so should we not be concerned in monetary law where there is no presumption of possession. But in the first clause, where another person seeks to collect from one of two men with identical names, each defendant can say that he does not owe the money at all. There the presumption of possession is fully operative and stronger than the marital presumption. Abaye should therefore have had no challenge from the get. The fact that Abaye does challenge Rava indicates that even temporary retention of money, where the person concededly must eventually pay one of two claimants, is still a form of monetary possession. Keeping the money for an additional period has monetary value. Thus even where the debt itself is certain, the present possessor still has a presumption of possession. The same should apply to the shopkeeper case. This is a proof against the Shach. One could reject the proof by noting that Abaye follows his own view in Bava Batra 135, where in the case “This one says, ‘You have a maneh of mine,’ and the other says, ‘I do not know,’” he holds the defendant liable. Abaye therefore holds that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one despite possession, and correspondingly he may place greater weight on majority evidence in monetary cases. At the beginning of the second chapter of Ketubot, the Gemara also entertains that a majority functions like a definite claim against an uncertain one. Especially where the claimant’s uncertainty is strong and the defendant’s certainty weak, Abaye may not regard monetary possession as stronger than a marital presumption. His challenge in Gittin can therefore be understood according to his own position, whereas Rava, who exempts the uncertain defendant, need not accept it. On that approach Rava would not need to establish the case specifically with witnesses to delivery under Rabbi Elazar. Yet this creates a serious difficulty for Rambam and the other authorities who rule that two men with identical names cannot divorce except in each other’s presence. According to Rambam and those who follow him, from where is that rule derived if Abaye’s proof depends upon his rejected monetary position? According to the Shach’s reasoning the matter is very difficult. Moreover, with respect, the Shach overlooked Tosafot in Bava Batra 135, who write that where the claimant’s certain assertion is weak and the defendant’s uncertainty strong, even Rabbi Yehuda agrees that the defendant is exempt. Thus the exemption in such a case does not depend solely upon the presumption of possession. In the shopkeeper case the certain claim is weak and the uncertainty strong. What proof, then, can the Shach bring from Rabbi Yehuda’s case, which concerns a strong certain claim and weak uncertainty? The whole matter requires study.
