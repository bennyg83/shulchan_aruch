Both of them swear. The Shach writes that even if the shopkeeper is a non-Jew, where he contradicts either a Jewish worker or the Jewish employer, the Jew must take the required oath. This appears from Rashba's responsum cited by Beit Yosef in siman 72. Even according to what I wrote in siman 149, that the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 14 of Hilchot To'en appears to hold that hesset is not imposed on the basis of a definite claim made by a non-Jew, perhaps all agree that one who comes to collect may not collect without an oath. Yet in my opinion the non-Jew's definite claim itself adds nothing here to the law of the shopkeeper and workers. If the shopkeeper says he does not know and the worker is Jewish, the worker takes payment without an oath. Where only one payment will in fact be made, such as where the shopkeeper died and only the workers claim, the workers collect without an oath even if the shopkeeper contradicted them in his lifetime. Here too there is effectively only one payment if the shopkeeper is disqualified from collecting. Ramban holds, and Rama and Sema follow, that a suspected shopkeeper leaves empty-handed and collects nothing. Even according to Baal HaMaor, who allows a suspected shopkeeper to collect without an oath, that is because Baal HaTerumot cites Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash that this case differs from an ordinary claim of 'you owe me' answered by 'I do not know whether I became obligated.' Here the shopkeeper has a chazakah supporting his definite claim, namely that an agent ordinarily performs his agency. That chazakah exists only for a Jewish agent who is presumed not to deviate. Thus the non-Jew's certainty is not the decisive element. If the worker is uncertain and the shopkeeper is Jewish, the shopkeeper certainly must swear. His right to collect exists only through the special enactment and by oath, and even if the workers died and did not contradict him, he still would collect only through oath under Ramban's approach. The oath is therefore not created by a definite contradiction from the worker but is built into the enactment itself.
The shopkeeper before the workers. The Shach notes that the wording implies that the shopkeeper should swear first. This is reasonable, because if neither swears, the employer must pay the workers and not the shopkeeper. The workers can therefore say, 'Perhaps the shopkeeper will refuse to swear.' This order is correct according to Ramban and those who follow him, who hold that the workers' right is fundamentally stronger and the shopkeeper's right exists only through a special enactment. If the shopkeeper is absent, the workers collect; if the workers are absent, the shopkeeper still collects only by oath. Therefore the shopkeeper should swear first. According to Baal HaMaor and Shulchan Aruch, however, whose view the Shach also accepts, the shopkeeper's claim is a Torah-level monetary claim comparable in strength to the worker's. It then appears that a lottery should determine who swears first.
