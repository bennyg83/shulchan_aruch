And even a shetuki, etc. Responsa Mayim Chaim, Choshen Mishpat 24, concerns a woman who died in a certain town and whose father was no longer known. Many years earlier a stranger had come from afar, married her mother, fathered this daughter, later divorced the mother, and returned to his place. The daughter owned a house which she rented to another person. After her death, her maternal relatives sought to inherit it but were prevented because maternal kinship is not treated as a family line for inheritance. The questioner wondered whether the estate should be treated like the property of a shetuki, which is regarded as ownerless. Mayim Chaim answers that the cases differ. A shetuki's paternal relatives have no established status as relatives and no present inheritance right. Even if Eliyahu were eventually to identify the father, until that revelation they had no legally recognized claim, because inheritance, though not requiring formal testimony, does require an established presumption of kinship, as stated in siman 284. Consequently, one who takes possession of a shetuki's estate takes it permissibly and acquires it as ownerless property. Here, however, the woman's father was known when he married her mother and remained in the town until she was born. Although his identity has since been forgotten, it can in principle be clarified. This therefore resembles siman 264 concerning a lost object that fell from one of two or three people: if the finder did not see from whom it fell and it bears no identifying mark, he must retain it until Eliyahu comes. With property that must remain deposited until Eliyahu comes, siman 300 records two views, whether it remains with the current holder or is deposited with beit din. Here, all would agree that it should be placed with beit din, because unlike ordinary depositors the true heirs did not cause the uncertainty by their own conduct.
