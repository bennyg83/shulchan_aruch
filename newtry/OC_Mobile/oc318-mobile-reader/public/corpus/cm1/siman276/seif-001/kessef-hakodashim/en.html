The Ketzot in siman 276 attempts to prove from Tosafot in Bava Batra 142 that, like the Rambam, they hold that even a fetus that will ultimately be stillborn inherits. This is a serious error. Once it was known that the wife was pregnant, the ordinary presumption is that the fetus would be born viable, since most women give birth to viable children. Therefore, the first person who took possession of the estate did not acquire it. According to Rava, the claim of the possessor is already weakened from the outset, as the Gemara explains, and even according to Abaye the fetus acquires the inheritance according to Rav Sheshet and Rabbi Yose because of the presumption that it will be viable. If one later says that because it was born a nonviable fetus it was revealed retroactively never to have acquired, Tosafot themselves write that we do not reason that way. In the case of slaves, by contrast, we presently regard the person as dead based on the current state. Thus the Ketzot's later extended discussion was unnecessary, since Tosafot's own words in the relevant passage resolve the matter. The Ketzot also writes that one needs a carpenter to dismantle the Rambam's ruling in light of Ketubot 39 concerning whether a deceased bogeret can receive the fine. Aside from the fact that the Rambam's position applies only where fetal development was complete, as the Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef explain, while in that case six full months could not have elapsed, there is a more basic error. The child necessarily died before the mother reached bogeret status, and a son does not inherit his mother in the grave in order to transmit her property to paternal relatives, because in the relevant sequence the child died first. Her later bogeret status therefore arose only after the child's death. Likewise, the Ketzot's criticism of the Ramban concerning emergence of the forehead is unfounded. If a fetus did not acquire inheritance while in utero, the first possessor's original chazakah would already have become effective then and would not be undone merely because the child was subsequently born. The Gemara can therefore properly infer that the fetus acquires. In my view, the Ketzot's discussion here is mistaken, except for his final observation, which is straightforward and did not require elaboration.
