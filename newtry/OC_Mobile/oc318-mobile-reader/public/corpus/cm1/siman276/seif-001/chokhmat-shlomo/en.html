Seif 5, gloss. Specifically if the minor was born, but a fetus does not inherit from its mother, etc. Note: See Sema's challenge to Ir Shushan, and Magen Avraham, Hilkhot Shabbat siman 330, who understands Rambam and the Mechaber as rejecting Tosafot and Sema. In my opinion Rambam actually supports Tosafot and Sema, while another aspect of Rambam supports Ir Shushan. Rambam, Hilkhot Issurei Mizbeach 3:4, excludes an orphaned animal whose mother was slaughtered. Kesef Mishneh notes that the Mishna also says "or whose mother died" and wonders why Rambam omitted it. One can explain that if the mother dies naturally, ordinarily the fetus dies first. If the fetus had already begun emerging, however, it may be treated as born. The Mishna can follow the Sages who do not treat something ready to occur as though it had already occurred. Rambam, however, elsewhere follows Rabbi Shimon that something standing ready for an act is considered as though the act occurred. Thus once the fetus has begun to emerge, Rambam may treat it as born; if it has not begun to emerge, natural maternal death leaves no surviving fetus. He therefore records only the case of slaughter, where the fetus may remain alive even though it had not yet begun emerging. This supports Sema. Yet it also raises a proof for Ir Shushan, because if a fetus that had begun emerging is considered born, why does Rambam require a child of one day old to inherit and transmit property? Even such an emerging fetus should inherit. This suggests that even actual birth followed by immediate death may not suffice in the maternal inheritance context, which supports Ir Shushan's distinction. I later found another resolution to Kesef Mishneh based on Chullin 38. The exclusion of an orphaned offering is needed where the mother's death and the fetus's emergence occur at exactly the same instant. Tosafot notes that exact simultaneity cannot occur in events controlled by Heaven. The Mishna's case of natural death therefore follows the opinion that exact simultaneity is possible even in heavenly events. Rambam rules that exact simultaneity is impossible in heavenly events but possible in human acts. He accordingly records slaughter, where a person can prolong the slaughter until the fetus emerges, but not natural death. This approach also aligns with Rambam's broader rulings on doubtful Torah prohibitions. See Tevuot Shor and Pri Megadim.
