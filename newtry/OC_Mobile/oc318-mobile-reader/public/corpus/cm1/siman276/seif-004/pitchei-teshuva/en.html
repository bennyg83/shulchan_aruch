His mother in the grave. Responsa Shav Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat 17, discusses Yaakov, a widower, who as part of his son Shimon's dowry conveyed by formal kinyan half of his entire house. He also wrote that if Shimon and his wife would pay "the heirs of Rabbi Yaakov" fifteen hundred Reichsthalers one hour before Yaakov's death, then the other half of the house and all its rights would also belong to them. After Yaakov died, Shimon sought to discharge his two surviving brothers, Reuven and Levi, with only one thousand Reichsthalers, claiming that he himself was also one of Yaakov's heirs and therefore entitled to one third of the fifteen hundred. He further claimed that when the document was executed Yaakov had another son, Yehudah, who later died during Yaakov's lifetime, and that Shimon should at least inherit Yehudah's share. Regarding the first argument, Shav Yaakov initially notes that Shimon appears to have some basis: since he had the option whether or not to buy the second half of the house, if he declined to buy it he would inherit a share in that half with his brothers. Buying it should not seemingly diminish his inheritance right in its value. One might answer that Yaakov may already have set the purchase price lower to account for Shimon's expected inheritance share. Shav Yaakov ultimately rejects Shimon's first claim for a textual reason: the document says that Shimon must pay "the heirs of Rabbi Yaakov," which implies heirs other than Shimon himself. Regarding the second argument, that Shimon should inherit through his brother Yehudah who died before their father, there is stronger support. Shulchan Aruch, siman 276:5, states that a son does not inherit his mother in the grave in order to transmit that inheritance to his paternal brothers, a rule derived from the prohibition against transferring inheritance from one tribe to another. This implies that with paternal inheritance, a deceased son can inherit his father in the grave and transmit the property to his paternal brothers where that produces a practical result. Accordingly, when Yaakov died, Yehudah could be regarded as inheriting in the grave his share of the remaining half of the house, and when Shimon exercises the purchase option the monetary value would likewise pass through Yehudah to the brothers, including Shimon. The wording "to the heirs of Rabbi Yaakov one hour before his death" may, however, imply only heirs then living, unless it merely reflects Yaakov's expectation at the time of drafting that Yehudah would survive. Shav Yaakov leaves that textual issue unresolved, but he favors Shimon on another ground. If the original valuation assumed four sons, Yaakov presumably fixed the fifteen hundred Reichsthalers after deducting Shimon's own one-quarter inheritance share from the value of two thousand. Once only three sons remained, Shimon's inheritance interest increased to one third, so he should pay only the value of the other two thirds, approximately thirteen hundred thirty-three and one third Reichsthalers, saving one hundred sixty-six and two thirds. See also Responsa Knesset Yechezkel 101, cited above, siman 42:10:1.
