Rambam rules that even a one day old infant whose months were not complete, if he lived after his mother for one hour and then died, inherits from his mother and transmits the inheritance to his paternal relatives. Maggid Mishneh leaves Rambam's inclusion of an incompletely gestated infant difficult. In the laws of yibbum he interprets Rambam as speaking of an infant whose signs of viability were incomplete. Later authorities rejected Rambam more broadly. Lechem Rav ruled that one cannot say "I am certain like Rambam" where all the sages of Israel disagree with him, and Maharashach discusses the same question.
In my view Rambam is strongly supported by Bava Batra. Rav Nachman says one who transfers property to a fetus does not effect acquisition, but if he says it shall acquire when born, it does. Rav Sheshet says even the fetus can acquire. Rav Sheshet brings a baraita: if a convert dies and Jews seize his property, and afterward they hear that he had a son or that his wife was pregnant, they must return the property. If they returned everything and afterward heard that the son died or the wife miscarried, one who takes possession a second time acquires, but the first possession did not. If a fetus could not acquire, why would a second act of possession be needed? Abaye answers that inheritance arising automatically is different; Rava says the first possessors were uncertain from the outset. The Gemara then cites the Mishnah that a one day old infant inherits and transmits, but a fetus does not. Tosafot asks from the earlier clause that a one day old infant disqualifies and enables eating of terumah, implying a fetus does not, and explains that even Rav Sheshet can maintain fetal acquisition. The reason a fetus does not disqualify can be statistical: combine the minority of miscarriages with the half chance of female offspring, leaving viable males a minority. Tosafot then asks why the first possessors in the convert case did not acquire once they later heard that the woman miscarried, since the fetus that was ultimately nonviable would have had no inheritance rights. They answer that in the terumah case we can presume the fetus had already died in the womb at the time of the slaves' eating. This answer implies that a fetus which is born alive even though it will not survive can be an heir. That is a clear proof for Rambam that a nonviable newborn can inherit and transmit. Sema cited Maggid Mishneh's difficulty as though no clear source existed, but Tosafot supplies one. It is therefore surprising that later decisors say one cannot rely on Rambam because all Israel disagrees. Tosafot stands with Rambam, and one should not extract money against a litigant who says he relies on Rambam and Tosafot. There is nevertheless a difficulty from Ketubot. Rava asks whether a deceased girl reaches majority in the grave, which determines whether a fine belongs to her son or to her father. The Gemara says she could not have conceived and given birth while still a na'arah because the period from na'arah to majority is only six months. If a nonviable infant can inherit, why not say she conceived as a na'arah, gave birth after five months, the infant lived after her for several days, and if she attained majority in the grave the fine went to her son and then to his paternal family? This requires resolution. Ramban, Rashba, Nimukei Yosef, Maggid Mishneh, and Kesef Mishneh hold that a nonviable infant does not inherit at all. Ramban answers Tosafot's proof by explaining "she miscarried" as a case where the forehead emerged and the infant then died. But the phrase "she miscarried" sounds more naturally like a true nonviable birth, and if the forehead emerged while the convert was alive the child was already born and the first possession would plainly have been displaced. This is difficult. Rashbam instead explains the miscarriage as the fetus dying in the womb. According to that approach the first possession still fails because at the time the possessors acted the fetus was presumed capable of being born viable. The beginning of Yevamot supports this. Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish dispute a chalitzah performed while the woman was pregnant and she later miscarried. Reish Lakish says we do not say the later miscarriage clarifies retroactively that there was no viable pregnancy. Nimukei Yosef explains that even though she ultimately miscarried, perhaps at the time of chalitzah the fetus was fit to become a viable child and only later some event caused the miscarriage. So too here. Most women give birth to viable children. At the time of the first possession, the fetus was presumptively one that would be born viable. The later miscarriage is treated as a later event, not proof that the fetus was nonviable from the outset. Therefore the first possession was ineffective because at that time the property was subject to a presumed viable heir. In Yevamot concerning a kohen's pregnant widow and the slaves' terumah, however, the Gemara combines the minority of miscarriages with the half chance of female birth to treat viable male fetuses as a minority. There the question is the fetus's present status for disqualifying the slaves, and the statistical combination is enough. The convert case concerns what the fetus was presumed to become at the time of possession, and the majority of women give birth to viable children. The later miscarriage is therefore attributed to a later cause. This distinction supports the authorities who deny inheritance to a nonviable infant, while explaining why the first possession nevertheless failed.
