But a fetus. Ketzot HaChoshen cites Rambam, who rules that a one-day-old infant inherits and transmits inheritance even if its months were not completed. Ketzot then brings proof from Tosafot in Bava Batra 142 that a nonviable fetus can nevertheless be an heir. These cases are not comparable. Tosafot speak of a fetus that, at the time inheritance fell, was still potentially viable. Since according to Rav Sheshet a fetus can acquire, the fact that it later dies because of some cause does not undo the inheritance, just as an adult who acquires and later dies remains an heir. But if the child has already been born at the time inheritance falls and its months were not completed, so that it is certain to die, it is treated as already dead. In that case even Tosafot agree that it is not an heir. Ketzot also asks from Ketubot 39, where the Gemara asks whether there is bagrut after death and says, "Can she give birth in six months?" According to Rambam, perhaps she gave birth after five months and the child lived for a short time. There is no difficulty. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchot Yibbum, explains that Rambam's reason is that for inheritance we restore the Torah presumption and say even an apparent eight-month child may really be a seven-month child whose birth was delayed. He also requires completed hair and nails. Accordingly, a six-month child is not an heir according to anyone. Moreover, even if such a child could live briefly, if its father is the same man who violated her, the child transmits back to that father and the legal question still falls into the Gemara's conclusion concerning the father. Nor can one construct the case through a second violator, because after three months the timing still does not work properly, and in any event a child of illicit relations whose father is uncertain is a shetuki. His property can be ownerless under siman 248, seif 4, in the gloss. Ketzot also misread Ramban. Ramban does not discuss a case where the forehead emerged and the child then died. His words are in Bava Batra 142, where he speaks of a nine-month fetus that died during the birth process before emergence. His intent is that once the child is full-term, death during the actual birth process can resemble a viable child that died rather than a fetus inherently destined to miscarry. Still, if it died before labor began, it is necessarily treated as a fetus destined to miscarry and does not inherit retroactively. This explains Ramban without producing the difficulty that a nine-month fetus should disqualify slaves from terumah.
She died while pregnant. See Sema, seif katan 7, who distinguishes between natural death and killing, and also between death before or after the fetus began to emerge. If so, it is surprising that Mechaber and Rama omitted these distinctions. In my view there is indeed a difference between natural death and killing, but not for the practical ruling in our case. When the woman dies naturally, the fetus certainly dies first, as the Gemara explains, because its life-force is weaker and the Angel of Death affects it first. But when she is killed, one cannot say with certainty that the woman died first. The blow may kill the fetus first precisely because its life-force is weaker. Even if the woman was still convulsing, the Gemara says even ordinary death can involve convulsing like a lizard's tail. Therefore the Gemara means only that in a killing case, or where the fetus had already begun to emerge, it is possible that the woman died first while the fetus remained alive. It does not mean that this certainly happened. Accordingly, if a woman was killed and the fetus is found dead, the case resembles siman 280, seif 10, concerning a house falling on a mother and child, where the property remains in the chazakah of the mother's heirs. Since we rule that way, there is no practical distinction between natural death and killing. The Gemara's statement that "she certainly died first" was needed only according to the Tanna in Bava Batra 138 who would otherwise divide the property in a simultaneous-death doubt. For us, because the property remains with the mother's heirs in any event, Mechaber had no need to record these distinctions. Tosafot's question in Niddah 44 and Bava Batra, asking whether one can find a case of a fetus inheriting and transmitting, is therefore directed to the Gemara's conclusion concerning reducing the firstborn share. With firstborn rights, even a doubt is insufficient, as explained in siman 277, seif 11, and elsewhere.
