Intangible matters or rights of discretionary benefit. See Shach, seif katan 4, who writes that this limitation applies only when heirs seek to extract from others, but if the right is already in their possession they inherit it. This differs from the view cited by Ketzot HaChoshen from Shelah, who says heirs do not inherit the discretionary benefit in tithe money set aside for charity, and therefore must give to the first poor person who comes. In my view Shach is correct. Proof can be brought from siman 214, seif 8, in the gloss. Shimon did not acquire the airspace above the house. Sema, seif katan 26, citing Rashba, explains that even though future fruits cannot ordinarily be reserved without reserving the tree, the airspace above the house remains reserved automatically because it was never included in the sale. Yet one cannot sell that airspace independently. Would anyone say that when the owner dies his heirs do not inherit the airspace and that it becomes ownerless? Certainly not. An heir stands in the place of his father. Whatever legal right belonged to the father is inherited even if it could not be transferred independently. Bava Batra 148 says that if one says, "So-and-so shall eat the fruits of this palm," he has said nothing, while saying, "My loan shall belong to so-and-so," can work through shechiv mera law because an heir inherits the loan. Tosafot explain that an heir also receives the palm's fruits only because he inherits the palm itself. That is no contradiction. The fruits of a palm cannot even be reserved independently for oneself without retaining the palm, so they cannot be inherited independently either. The same is true of dwelling rights in a house. But airspace, or a right of discretionary benefit, can remain legally with the owner even though it cannot be sold by itself. Such a right can therefore be inherited. This, in my view, is clear.
