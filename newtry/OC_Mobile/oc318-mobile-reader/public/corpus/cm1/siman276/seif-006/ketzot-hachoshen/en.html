Or merely discretionary benefit, which is not monetary property. Shelah discusses charity and tithes and rules that a God fearing person should distribute his tithe funds to the poor during his lifetime. If he dies leaving tithe money, the heirs do not inherit the discretionary benefit because that benefit is not monetary property. He relies on Mordechai, who says that even a courtyard does not acquire discretionary benefit where no other person's transferring intent is present. Rabban Gamliel's transfer to the elders was different because he transferred the courtyard to them as well. Where the owner is not transferring his own property but only priestly or Levitical gifts, the situation resembles ownerless property, which a courtyard does not acquire in the same way. Shelah concludes that one who dies leaving tithe funds leaves no inheritable proprietary right in choosing the recipients. The sons therefore act improperly if they exercise that discretion. Nor can they say their courtyard acquired it for them, since a nonmonetary discretionary benefit is not itself subject to acquisition. The Shach appears to limit the rule. He implies that the inability to transfer or inherit discretionary benefit applies where the property is in another's hands, but if the actual funds are already physically in the heirs' possession, they can acquire and distribute them as they choose.
For practical halachah, I believe Shelah is correct. Tosafot in Nedarim says that even the original owner has discretionary benefit in poor tithe only during the rainy season, not during the summer. Sifrei resolves the verses, "you shall give" and "you shall leave," by saying that in summer the owner must leave the poor tithe in the field for whoever wishes to take, while in winter he may give it to whom he chooses. This shows that the owner's discretionary benefit exists only because the Torah specifically said, "you shall give," granting the owner a power of distribution. Where that verse does not apply, even the owner has no discretionary benefit despite the produce being physically in his domain. He cannot choose recipients but must leave it for whichever poor person takes first. After the owner dies, or where another person acquires the produce, the Torah's grant of "you shall give" no longer applies to that person. Whence should the heirs or finder receive the original owner's discretionary right? If they choose recipients, they exercise a power the Torah never gave them. Therefore, upon the owner's death the discretionary benefit ends completely. The heirs must give to the first poor person they encounter or distribute it openly so that whoever wishes may take. They do not inherit the choice of beneficiary. A question remains from Rama in siman 37. Rama says that even though a charity treasurer may distribute funds to whomever he chooses, according to the view that discretionary benefit is not money, the treasurer's relatives can still testify. How did the treasurer receive power to choose recipients if only the original owner has the Torah's "you shall give" right? One answer is that while the owner is alive and entrusts the distribution to the treasurer, the treasurer acts as the owner's agent. The owner has the right to choose and can exercise it through agency. Alternatively, a charity treasurer is different because he is considered the hand of the poor and the community relies upon him to allocate their funds. His authority derives from the communal office rather than inheritance of the donor's personal discretionary benefit. But an ordinary heir has neither basis. Accordingly, Shelah's practical conclusion is correct.
