Where one purchases from a thief who is not publicly known as a thief, the market enactment does not apply if the movable itself remains in the possession of the person from whom it was stolen. If, however, the item is held by that person's agent or relative rather than by him personally, the market enactment may still apply because the buyer has paid his money in an ordinary-looking transaction. This requires study. The same may apply where a husband sells his wife's clothing, because halachah generally presumes that she consents, and the buyer's awareness that the garment belongs to a married woman does not itself establish wrongdoing. With items contributed in connection with a match, whether the market enactment applies may depend on whether the transfer is functionally a sale or a gift. If the contribution is necessary for the match to proceed, it may have the character of a sale. If a husband sells or mortgages his wife's clothing and she then seizes it, she may be believed to say that she did not consent, though a hesset oath may be appropriate because there is a presumption of consent. Even if the husband says that she agreed, he may not forcibly take the garments from her, because once they are in her possession her hand is stronger with respect to this claim and they may not be snatched away.
