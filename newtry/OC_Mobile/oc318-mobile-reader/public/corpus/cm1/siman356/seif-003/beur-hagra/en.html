The owners despaired, etc.; and some say, etc. See siman 353, seif 3.
(Collected note) Some say that if, etc. This follows Rabbi Yochanan according to Rabbi Zeira, since Rav Pappa does not disagree with him; see Rashi 114a, Tosafos 67b, and what I wrote in siman 361, seif 5.
(Collected note) Siman 354, seif 3, rather he gives, etc. This view holds that even after despair the market enactment is required in such a case. This requires study, for with an heir there is no market enactment, just as with a notorious robber, yet Bava Kamma 111b states Rabbi Rami bar Chama's inference concerning inherited stolen property.
Meaning, etc.; therefore, etc. This follows the passage there, and as stated in seif 10, “and he returns,” etc. See Beer HaGolah; the Mechaber's reading is primary, as shown by Bava Batra 44a, and the Shach raises the same proof.
(Collected note) Therefore the excess, etc. At first glance this is learned from the Gemara and from seif 10, where the owner returns, etc., but the comparison is not exact. The ruling is difficult, since the Tosefta states that even a gift effects acquisition through change of possession. The textual reading on which this ruling rests is not reliable. The Baal HaMaor explains the two versions in Bava Kamma 114a as dealing with the excess value: according to the first version, because it came to him by mistake it is not a complete change of possession as to the excess, while the latter version holds that even the excess passes through change of possession and he acquires all of it.
(Collected note) Meaning, etc. This explanation follows a corrupt reading in the Rambam and the ruling itself is not correct. According to the Baal HaMaor, even the first version disputes only a case where the transfer was mistaken and thus not a complete change of possession.
(Collected note) See. See the Tur in the name of the Rambam and its explanation in Darkei Moshe, seif katan 3.
But some disagree, etc. The Tur writes this in the name of Ri, and it also appears from the discussion at the beginning of chapter 8. According to this view, after despair and change of possession the purchaser acquires, and the remaining dispute is only whether the robbed owner may recover money from the purchaser under the market enactment. This also explains the Rambam's ruling and avoids the great difficulty noted by the Maggid Mishneh concerning his treatment of change of possession followed by despair.
