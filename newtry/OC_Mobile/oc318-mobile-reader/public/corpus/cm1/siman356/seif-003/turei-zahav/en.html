Even so, the thief need not return his money. The Sema says this is the correct reading, but the Rama's explanation that the purchaser certainly gave the money as a gift requires serious study. It contradicts the ruling in Bava Metzia 15b that the purchaser recovers his principal, in accordance with Rav, and the Tur and Mechaber likewise rule in siman 373 that he collects the principal from the seller. The Rama's source is the Mordechai, who writes that market protection was not enacted for articles known to be stolen, and adds that according to the opinion that one who knowingly purchased property not belonging to the seller gave his money as a gift, the purchaser would not even recover from the thief or robber. The Rama assumed that this latter statement follows practical law. It does not. It is cited only to reinforce that the purchaser cannot invoke the market enactment against the true owner. Although Shmuel holds that a knowing purchaser cannot recover even from the thief because the money was a gift, we rule like Rav that he does recover from the thief. Maharshal states this explicitly. The Rama did not examine the matter adequately, and the Sema's claim that the same rule appears in siman 373 is the reverse of what that siman says. I presented this matter to several leading rabbis, and none could justify the gloss. One should not rule in accordance with it.
