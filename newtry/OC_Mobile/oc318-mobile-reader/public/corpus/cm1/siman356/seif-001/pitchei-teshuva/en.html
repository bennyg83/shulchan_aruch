To buy from a thief. Baer HaGolah at the end of Yoreh Deah siman 334 records part of an enactment of Rabbeinu Gershom, cited at the end of Responsa Maharam, imposing a ban against buying stolen property from non-Jews, such as goblets, garments, prayer books, and sacred accessories, because of the danger this creates. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 36, discusses a Jew who bought from a non-Jew a silver vessel that everyone recognized as forbidden merchandise because it belonged to clergy, and then sold it to another Jew. The thief was later caught and admitted selling it to a Jew but could not identify him. Because the authorities were powerful, the community had to appease them with money, and the community sought reimbursement from the second Jewish purchaser. He argued that he bought nothing from the thief, only from the first Jew who had already committed the prohibition, and that he did not cause the community's loss. He also argued that even the first Jew should be exempt because the thief could not identify him, so the community had not rescued him from any concrete danger; any threats by the authorities were their own wrongdoing. Tzemach Tzedek rejects both claims. Even if the thief could not identify the purchaser, the first buyer engaged in merchandise prohibited by an ancient ban specifically because it endangers the community. Such a buyer causes danger and has the status of a pursuer, comparable to one who deals in counterfeiting, because when hostile authorities are powerful and such harm is common and predictable, anyone engaging in the prohibited trade must anticipate the danger. This is especially true where the object itself is plainly of a kind forbidden to purchase. Even broken silver may create such liability if the circumstances should alert a prudent person to danger, because a person is always responsible for his acts. The second buyer's argument that he bought only from a Jew likewise fails. Since he knew the object was of the prohibited kind, he was not permitted to facilitate the first buyer's wrongdoing. The saying that the hole, not merely the mouse, causes the theft applies: the first purchaser is the mouse and the second purchaser who creates the market is the hole. This prohibition is not only ordinary theft law but an independent prohibition against purchasing dangerous stolen goods. Therefore the community may collect from the second purchaser, not only where the first lacks assets but even where the first could pay, because the second participated in the prohibited conduct. Tzemach Tzedek then distinguishes Responsa Mahari ben Lev, siman 5, concerning people who bought stolen goods, fled, and hid, after which a government officer seized innocent Jews and extracted money from them. There the buyers were not treated as pursuers in the same direct manner, and the resulting loss was only indirect causation after the damage had already occurred. In the present case the danger remained active before the settlement, and even ordinary indirect causation can justify excommunication until the wrongdoer undertakes to remove all resulting danger. The community's settlement with the authorities was therefore proper and far smaller than the danger they faced had the thief later falsely identified someone.
