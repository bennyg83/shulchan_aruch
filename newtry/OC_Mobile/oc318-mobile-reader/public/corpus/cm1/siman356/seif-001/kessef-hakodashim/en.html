The market enactment. Non-Jews transporting grain from a nobleman to a Jew sold some of the grain to another buyer. If it is possible that the seller was transporting his own grain as well, the purchase may resemble buying from a thief who is not publicly known as a thief, for which the market enactment can apply. If the claimant seeks a formal acceptance or oath from the buyer, and the buyer denies having bought cheaply, the precise oath obligations require study. If the buyer says only that perhaps the non-Jew stole the grain from the nobleman, while the claimant asserts that it certainly belonged to the nobleman, this may resemble a case of despair not yet known to the owner, unless it is plausible that the nobleman's measuring agent had authority to sell some grain, or had a recognized right to take grain. If the claimant is in possession, the defendant's uncertainty may not be enough to take property from him, particularly where the circumstances did not indicate theft at the time of purchase. Where there was no sign of theft, one cannot fault the buyer for purchasing. In another case, pearls were stolen from a person, and through a non-Jewish judge he searched another individual's possession and found pearls there. He paid the judge their value and received them, but it later became clear that the pearls belonged to someone else. This appears comparable to purchasing from a thief who is not known as such. Once the search located the pearls, he was compelled to pay the judge to obtain them, and because he reasonably believed they were his, his payment resembles a market purchase. Even if he was only uncertain whether they were his, people ordinarily spend substantial sums to rescue property that may be theirs. That practical compulsion may be sufficient for the market enactment even though the transaction was not an ordinary voluntary purchase.
