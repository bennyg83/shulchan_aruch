As a gift. See Baer Heitev in the name of Shach, who calls Rama's ruling an error. Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, siman 96, seeks to defend both Rama and Sema. He concludes that where one buys from a notorious thief, knows at the time of purchase that the item was stolen, and the prevailing law is that all stolen goods must be returned and despair does not transfer title, Rama correctly rules without hesitation that the thief need not reimburse the buyer, because the buyer's payment is treated as a gift. Shevut Yaakov, volume 3, siman 181, likewise defends Rama and distinguishes between land transactions, where a document is ordinarily written and omitted warranty is supplied by legal presumption, so the money cannot be treated as a gift, and movable property, which ordinarily is not documented and therefore has no such implied warranty. The same applies to land where the document expressly excludes warranty, in which case the money may be treated as a gift. He concludes that this is clear law in practice. In my view his words require analysis. Netivot HaMishpat and Divrei Mishpat also defend Rama in other ways. See there.
