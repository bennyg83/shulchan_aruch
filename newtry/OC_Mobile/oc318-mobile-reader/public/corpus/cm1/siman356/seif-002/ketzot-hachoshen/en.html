Because of the marketplace enactment. Rabbi Aharon Sasson rules that the marketplace enactment applies even to slaves, because for rabbinic monetary matters slaves are treated like movables. That proof is not decisive if the reason a creditor does not collect slaves from heirs is that he never relied on them, since they can be hidden or removed. On that theory there is no broad distinction between Torah and rabbinic law. The Shach cites Maharashdam as holding that neither slaves nor animals are included in the marketplace enactment. Yet according to the Beit Yosef and Rama's reading of Rambam, despair plus change of possession gives the purchaser title to the object, while in the case of a notorious thief the purchaser must still restore its value. If animals were excluded from the marketplace enactment, the purchaser of a stolen cow would remain financially interested in testimony concerning it, because he would still owe its value. The Mishnah, Rambam, and the Shulchan Aruch nevertheless allow such testimony after despair and change of possession. This strongly indicates that the marketplace enactment does apply to animals. Rambam also writes that if tax collectors took a person's clothing or donkey and returned another article or donkey, the replacement belongs to him because the transaction resembles a sale and the original owners are presumed to have despaired. His qualification that the property is not certainly stolen is relevant primarily to the obligation to restore value. This likewise implies that the marketplace enactment extends to animals.
And the owners recover from the thief. The Shach cites the rule that if a stolen object, after having been sold, later returns to the original owner's possession, the owner need not reimburse the purchaser even if the purchaser's original payment is known, because the marketplace enactment does not apply once the property is again in the original owner's hands. I discussed this in siman 50.
If the purchaser says, "I intended it for his benefit." Tosafot says that without the marketplace enactment the purchaser can recover what he paid if he bought the stolen object in order to rescue it for the owner, provided the owner otherwise could not have recovered it. Even where the purchaser did not intend to rescue the object but bought it for himself, he can still recover at least the amount by which he benefited the owner when there was no other way to save the property. This follows from the law of one who removes another's loss. The distinction is that one who acted expressly for the owner's benefit can recover his full expenditure, whereas one who acted for himself recovers only the benefit actually conferred. If the owner could have recovered the object without payment, such as by legal claim against people within his house, there is no recovery where the marketplace enactment does not apply. Sacred books are different. Several authorities rule that one who redeems sacred books from a gentile for their fair value is reimbursed even outside the ordinary marketplace enactment, because leaving sacred writings in degrading hands is itself a loss that the community does not tolerate. Therefore a purchaser of sacred books from a gentile can recover what he paid up to their value, even if he acted for himself. Maharam appears to disagree and would require free return even from a notorious thief, but Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hagahot Ashri, and Mordechai support reimbursement. We therefore do not remove the books from the purchaser without payment where those authorities apply.
