Unless the buyer knew that the item he purchased was stolen. One bought from a non-Jew a garment stolen from a Jew. The garment was of a type normally worn by non-Jews, but the buyer himself admitted that he recognized it as stolen because it was long while the seller was short. He also admitted that he had not purchased it in order to rescue the stolen property. We arranged a compromise and informed the parties that the law inclined toward returning the garment to its owner without reimbursement even of the amount paid to the thief. Because other items had also been stolen and the owner suspected the buyer of having purchased them as well, we compromised that the buyer would return this garment without payment and would be exempt from oaths or a ban concerning the other missing items. I nevertheless hesitated to rule absolutely that he must return it without payment merely because he knew it was stolen. Noda BiYehudah discusses the principle that although the descendants of Noah are commanded against theft, the Torah's special command of restitution was given to Israel, so a non-Jewish thief may not have the same corrective mitzvah of return. Likewise, where a Jew steals from a non-Jew, the prohibition applies but the specific Torah law of restitution may not. If so, even where the purchaser knew the item was stolen, he might have thought it was stolen from a non-Jew and that the special restitution regime did not apply. Yet the general wording of the poskim suggests no such distinction for the market enactment. Moreover, even without a special mitzvah of restitution, the garment did not leave the true owner's ownership until despair plus change of possession, and therefore a knowing purchaser remains in a problematic position. Even if the buyer's knowledge was based only on strong circumstantial inference, where he was inwardly convinced that the item was stolen he should at least have refrained from entering into a doubtful prohibition, since passive avoidance is preferable. The fact that a non-Jewish thief is subject to severe punishment does not mean that the property is acquired through payment in the manner of one killed while tunneling, because the punishment there is not based on the pursuer doctrine but on the offense itself. The Gemara's discussion of a non-Jewish thief also indicates that ordinary ownership principles continue to apply. As for a claim that the buyer intended to rescue the item, Tosafot's discussion cited by the Shach appears to rely on a migo and on the purchaser's intent. The legal role of that intent may rest on a form of acquisition for the owner through agency or beneficial acquisition. The matter requires further study.
