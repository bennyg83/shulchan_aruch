Seif 2, in the gloss. "And even the victim of the theft need not return his money to him." Note: See Shach, seif katan 7, who writes, among other things, "Were I not unworthy to disagree with the Mordechai, I would say that even Shmuel agrees here. Only with land, which cannot be stolen, do we say that a person knows that land cannot be stolen and therefore conclusively gave the money as a gift. Here, however, the purchaser may have thought that he would acquire the item through despair of recovery together with a change of possession." With due respect, the explicit language of Bava Kamma 95b escaped him. The Gemara says there, "What, is it not speaking of an ordinary owner who does not know that land cannot be stolen?" This proves that even regarding land there are people who do not know that it cannot be stolen, and nevertheless the Gemara understands the money as a gift. This must be because at least a doubt remains that he knew, and money cannot be extracted on the basis of doubt, or because a minority certainly know and we do not follow a majority in monetary law. The same should therefore apply to movable property. See what I wrote in my novellae to Bava Kamma there. As to the Shach's principal difficulty with the Rama, it appears that the Rama's ruling is sound. People do not ordinarily entrust property to a notorious thief. How, then, could the payment be treated as a deposit, particularly since the Gemara says, "Let him say to him: it was a deposit," and answers that he considered the matter burdensome? Such reasoning does not fit a notorious thief. The Tosafot cited in siman 373, however, discuss one who was not a notorious robber generally, but only a robber with respect to this particular field. The purchaser could therefore have assumed that perhaps he seized it because of an uncertain old loan, as Tosafot ask in the first chapter of Bava Metzia why such a person should become disqualified for testimony and an oath, since perhaps he acted because of an old loan. Tosafot answer there that once witnesses established him as a robber we do not say this as a matter of law. Nevertheless a purchaser might still suppose that toward the particular victim the seller permitted himself to act on account of an old debt, while toward the purchaser himself he remains trustworthy. But a person does not deposit money with a thief notorious to everyone. Especially since the purchaser could simply have said explicitly that the money was a deposit, in such a case all agree that the money is a gift. In my view the Rama's words are correct. Examine carefully.
