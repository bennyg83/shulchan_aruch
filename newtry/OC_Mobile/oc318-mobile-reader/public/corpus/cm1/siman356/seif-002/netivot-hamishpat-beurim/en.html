Because of takkanat hashuk. See Shach, seif katan 4, who writes that if the stolen object returned to the owner's possession after the sale but before it reached the buyer, the market-protection enactment does not apply. In my view his intent is specifically that the owner recovered the object before the buyer ever received it. In that case the owner can say, "I rescued my own item from the thief for myself. What does it matter that you made a kinyan sudar with the thief when your acquisition never took hold in the object itself?" But once the stolen object actually reached the buyer and the buyer paid the thief, the enactment applies. Even if the original owner later seizes the object from the buyer, the owner must return the buyer's money. Seizure cannot defeat the buyer's rabbinically created right to reimbursement under takkanat hashuk.
Even the thief himself need not return. See Shach, seif katan 7, who calls this a mistake. In my view the ruling can be defended because the buyer knowingly entered a doubtful transaction. He did not know whether the sale would ultimately stand. Perhaps the owner had already despaired, in which case ye'ush plus change of possession could acquire. Perhaps the owner would despair later, and according to Rambam change of possession followed by ye'ush could acquire. When a person pays money knowing that the transaction may or may not take effect, the payment can be understood as given on that risk rather than as a deposit. Tosafot in Kiddushin 46 distinguish a case where a person knows a transaction is certainly ineffective, in which case the money can be treated as a deposit, from a case of genuine doubt whether it will work, where the money can be a gift if it fails. Shach's approach would also make it difficult ever to find a true case of change of possession followed by ye'ush where the buyer knowingly bought stolen property, because if his money were automatically a deposit rather than payment, there would be no real sale or change of possession in the relevant sense. Therefore, even in this knowingly doubtful purchase the transfer can count as a sale, and Rambam can consistently recognize later acquisition through change of possession followed by ye'ush.
