And the owners did not despair of recovery. An ordinary theft is presumed to produce despair once it is known that the owners learned of the theft, as stated above in siman 353, seif 3. This applies, however, to a Jewish thief. When the thief is a gentile, even an ordinary case is not presumed to involve despair, as stated above in siman 368.

Because of the market enactment. See what I wrote above in siman 126 in the name of Responsa Maharam Alshikh. It follows here that when the stolen object returned to its original owners after it had been sold, they need not reimburse the purchaser, even if it is known that he originally bought it, because the market enactment does not apply once the object is now in the original owners' possession. Although I disputed that ruling there, I agree here. Responsa Maharashdam rules that the enactment was not made for slaves or animals, though his proof is not compelling; Responsa Maharan Sasson writes that slaves are treated as movable property for this purpose, and the matter requires study. The Beit Yosef cites the Rashba that the enactment does not apply to a gift, whereas elsewhere he understood the Rosh to apply it even to gifts. That reading of the Rosh is incorrect. The market enactment was made in a sale because otherwise people would be afraid to buy movable property lest it prove stolen. That rationale does not apply to a gift, which is even weaker than a thief's payment of a debt or extension of credit, cases in which the enactment was not made. Rashi's responsum, the Hagahot Maimoniyot, Raavyah, Maharam Alshikh, and other authorities state expressly that a gift receives no such protection. The Rosh's statement concerns movable property contractually encumbered together with land: local custom treated the lien as ineffective against both purchasers and donees, so that creditors could not collect from them. It does not concern identifiable property stolen from its owner and then given away, which the owner may recover without payment. Thus the Rosh and Rashba do not disagree, contrary to the Beit Yosef and Darkhei Moshe. The Sema's contrary statement above in siman 252 was addressed there at length.
And if he is a notorious thief, etc. If he is not known as a thief, but only as wicked in other matters, the owner must reimburse the purchaser. This is explicit in the Gemara regarding Hanan the wicked: although his wickedness was public, he was not known for theft. Rav Hai Gaon rules likewise in Mishpetei Shevuot, gate 19.
And some say that even with a thief, etc. Maharshal rules this way, but the Mechaber's ruling appears correct. The Rif plainly records Rava's statement, and the Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Mordechai, Nimukei Yosef, Rav Hai Gaon, Baal HaIttur, Baal HaTerumot, the Geonic responsa, Raavan, Rashba, Rivash, Raavad, Maggid Mishneh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and others all indicate that no market enactment was made for a notorious thief. The Tur likewise ultimately rules that one who purchases from a notorious robber must return the object and pursue the robber for his money, and his wording indicates that the same rule governs a notorious thief. Since this is an enactment that departs from the strict law, one may not extend it beyond what was enacted. Accordingly, the purchaser from a notorious thief is not reimbursed by the owner. As to a purchaser from a gentile, Baal HaMaor denies the enactment, while Ramban cites Rav Sherira Gaon that it applies. Hagahot Maimoniyot, Tosafot, Raavan, Rabbeinu Gershom, Terumat HaDeshen, and the general course of the authorities support application of the enactment, and that is the principal ruling. See also Maharan Sasson concerning purchase from a craftsman.
And even the thief need not return his money, etc. This appears mistaken. The Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot say this only according to the view that when one knowingly purchases property that is not the seller's, the money is deemed a gift, namely Shmuel's view. We rule, however, like Rav, as Rava decided, that the purchaser recovers his money, and all authorities rule so, as stated below in siman 373. The Sema's comparison to the purchase of land from a robber is therefore reversed; Maharshal also rules expressly that the thief must return the money. Even according to Shmuel, one can distinguish land, which cannot be stolen and always remains in its owner's domain, from movable property, where the purchaser may expect to acquire through despair and transfer of possession. Even if the purchaser knows it was stolen, he may assume that the owners despaired, or later would despair, and many authorities hold that transfer followed by despair is effective. The purchaser may also assume that the thief will not be found, unlike land, whose location is fixed. For these reasons, even where the thief is the purchaser's relative, the money should not be treated as a gift. Raavan indeed rules like Rav even concerning one who betroths his sister, and the Rif's omission of the dispute there also suggests that the law follows Rav. The Bach's final explanation of the Mordechai is the correct one.
