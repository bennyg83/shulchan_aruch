According to what I wrote at the end of siman 361, that whenever there are no witnesses who saw the object we advance for the robber's heirs the claim that it was purchased, based on a migo that he could have claimed, 'I returned it,' one must explain every case in this siman as involving witnesses who saw it in the purchaser's possession. The market. See what I wrote at the end of siman 126 in the name of Responsa Maharam Alshich, and apply it here: if after the stolen object was sold it came back into the original owner's possession, the owner need not reimburse the purchaser, even if it is known that he originally bought it, because the market regulation does not apply once the object is presently in the original owner's hands. Although I disagreed with him there, I concede here. See Responsa Rashdam, simanim 212 and 414, which rules that the market regulation was not enacted for slaves or animals. His words are not compelling. Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 218, writes that a slave is treated like movable property for purposes of the market regulation, and this requires study. So writes the Shach. The gloss adds that a manuscript responsum of the author's revered father, Rabbi Aryeh Leib, chief rabbi and rosh yeshivah of the Ashkenazic community of Amsterdam, was asked whether the market regulation applies to a Sefer Torah. He proved clearly that there is no distinction between a Sefer Torah and other items for which the regulation was enacted, contrary to one distinguished scholar who wished to compare a Sefer Torah to documents, for which siman 60 rules that the regulation does not apply because they are uncommon. He also explained the true meaning of the Rosh's responsum that is the source of the rule concerning documents. The Shach further writes that the Beit Yosef's statement that the Rosh holds that the market regulation applies even to a gift, and that this is also the Rashba's view, as he writes at the beginning of siman 60, is incorrect. Certainly the Rosh concedes that the market regulation does not apply to a gift. How could one imagine otherwise? Even in a sale, strict law requires return without payment, and the market regulation was enacted only because otherwise people would refrain from buying for fear that the item was stolen. This concern does not apply to a gift. See there for further proof and for many leading responsa authorities who likewise hold that the market regulation does not apply to a gift. See what I wrote in siman 252, seif katan 5.
Notorious. If he is not a notorious thief, then even if he is notorious as a wicked person in other matters, one must pay him. This is proven by the Gemara's answer regarding Hanan Bisha: although he was notorious for wickedness, that did not make him a notorious thief. Rav Hai Gaon likewise writes this in Mishpetei Shevuot, gate 19. Shach.
And some say. Maharshal, chapter HaGozel Batra, siman 32, rules likewise. The Shach writes that the Mechaber's view appears primary, for this is clearly the Rif's position, and the Semag and other authorities understand the Rif the same way. Since the Rif and Rambam agree, the Gemara also implies this, and many early authorities rule likewise, as do the Tosafot text before us, it follows that since the market regulation is an enactment and strict law requires free return, one may not extend the enactment beyond its terms. We therefore rule that it was not enacted for a notorious thief. See Baal HaMaor, chapter HaGozel Batra, who holds that the market regulation was not enacted for one who purchased from a gentile. This also appears in Responsa of the Geonim, siman 296. Ramban in Milchamot disagrees, writing that Rav Sherira Gaon likewise applied the market regulation to one who purchased from a gentile. So too Hagahot Maimoniyot at the beginning of chapter 5 of Hilchot Geneivah, Tosafot to Bava Kamma 114b, Raavan as cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of this siman, a responsum of Rabbeinu Gershom in Mordechai, chapter Elu Metziot, and Terumat HaDeshen, siman 309, all imply this. Other authorities likewise make no distinction. This is therefore the primary ruling.
A gift. The Shach writes that this is an error. Mordechai explicitly states that this applies only according to the opinion that where one recognized that the seller did not own the property and nevertheless purchased it, the money is deemed a gift. We, however, rule like Rav that he is entitled to recover his money, as all authorities rule. Thus here the thief must return the purchaser's money. I am greatly surprised at the Sema, who writes that a similar case appears in the Gemara, Tur, and Mechaber below in siman 373 concerning one who bought a field from a robber. The very opposite is stated there. See the Shach's lengthy discussion.

The Beit Yosef writes in Bedek HaBayit: These words are not compelling, but I found some support for them in Tosafot, which I will cite in siman 357 regarding one who recognizes his utensils or books in another person's possession. At minimum, a general ban should be proclaimed; everything depends on the character of the person and the assessment of the judges. The Shach cites those Tosafot, Bava Kamma 114b: Without the market regulation, why should he not recover what he paid, since he can say, 'I intended to rescue the property and return a lost object'? Tosafot answer that the case is one where the owner could have recovered from the members of his household even had this purchaser not bought the items. Thus, if the owner could not otherwise have recovered them, the purchaser is believed. I cited Tosafot to teach that where the owner could have recovered from the thief even without this purchaser's acquisition, he need not reimburse the purchaser.
