For this is the present custom. I discussed this in siman 259. Even today despair plus change of possession creates full title. Beit din may nevertheless compel a wealthy purchaser to return the object beyond the strict letter of the law when doing so causes him no loss, but not a poor purchaser. This also resolves the difficulty concerning a woman betrothed with stolen property after despair and change of possession. Her title is legally complete. The later duty to return is only an enforced measure beyond the strict law. Beit din does not compel her to undo a mitzvah of marriage merely in order to satisfy that extra measure, just as other enactments yield to preservation of marriage. Rashbatz similarly writes that although a creditor could technically seize certain property, courts refrained from doing so where such action would undermine marriage, invoking the public policy of marriage. So too here, the woman is not compelled to return the kiddushin object in a way that would nullify the marriage.
But not the interest. Nimukei Yosef discusses orphan funds invested with a wealthy person who deposited a specific object as collateral. If the true owner identifies that collateral, one might ask why the owner can recover it without first reimbursing the orphan fund under the marketplace enactment. Raavad answers that the orphans did not rely upon the collateral alone but upon the investment itself. The special enactment applies where the lender entered the transaction solely upon the strength of the pledge. This resembles the rule that where a thief pays an existing debt with stolen property, the marketplace enactment does not protect the creditor, because he did not originally extend credit on the strength of that property. Hagahot Mordechai, however, appears to imply that the principal itself should not be lost and limits the owner's recovery to avoiding payment of the interest. The practical law therefore requires study.
