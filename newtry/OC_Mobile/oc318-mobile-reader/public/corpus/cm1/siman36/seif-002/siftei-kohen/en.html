And he is believed to say that he did not recognize, etc. The Sema writes that this applies only if the witness volunteers that he did not recognize the other's disqualification, but beit din does not ask him. This implies a presumption that he did recognize it. I do not know the source. The Rif's responsum, cited by the Baal HaItur and Nimukei Yosef, indicates the opposite: when the witnesses are unavailable, the document is validated because perhaps the qualified witness did not know of the other's disqualification. The Rosh likewise argues that, if the matter depends on knowledge, the qualified witnesses should be asked. Thus the Tur and Mechaber mean that, when available, the witness is questioned and believed when he says he did not know.
That he did not recognize, etc. Rabbi Yosef ben Lev ruled that in his case it was evident that the qualified witness did not know the other was a zomem and wicked person, since even the leading scholar who received testimony and the local scribe had not known it. Maharam Alshakar objected that important witnesses had now testified to the man's wickedness and that the witnesses were townsmen familiar with one another. The objection is incorrect. Until those witnesses testified, the man retained a presumption of qualification, and even the officials did not know otherwise. The Rif's rule is that we ordinarily assume the qualified witness did not know of the other's disqualification. Familiarity is relevant to an obvious relationship, such as brothers, where one certainly knows the kinship. Mere residence in the same city does not establish knowledge that another person is wicked or a zomem. Rabbi Yosef ben Lev's ruling is therefore correct.
Since the disqualified witness testified with him before beit din. This follows the second opinion above. According to the first opinion, if they observed the event together and intended to testify, the testimony is void even if the disqualified person never testified. At first glance, the Gemara may support invalidation even where the valid witness did not know of the other's defect. Nevertheless, many authorities accept the Rif's responsum that lack of knowledge prevents joinder, including the Baal HaItur, Ramban, Mordechai, Ran, Nimukei Yosef, and Rashba. Maharam Alshakar's objections are unpersuasive. A witness is ordinarily presumed to intend to testify, but is not ordinarily presumed to know another witness's hidden disqualification. The Rambam and other concise rulings may be understood where the witnesses were familiar enough that the defect was known, as in the example of brothers. The Halachot Gedolot and Rav Hai Gaon do not necessarily disagree. There is a real distinction between ignorance that joining a disqualified witness invalidates the testimony, which does not save it, and ignorance of the person's actual disqualification, in which case the witness intended to join only with someone he believed qualified.
