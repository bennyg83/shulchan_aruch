For the testimony of the unrelated witness remains valid. See Tumim, who writes according to Sema that Tosafot, who hold that the witnesses are joined even if they did not know of one another, disagree with Rif, and so forth. This is surprising, because Sema did not write that at all. Certainly, even according to Tosafot, the principal joining occurs at the time of seeing the event, since the witnesses did not testify within immediate speech of one another. Formal testimony is not required at that stage except insofar as they are among those who establish the matter. Therefore it is unnecessary for the qualified witness to know whether the disqualified person later testified. But at the time of seeing the event, Tosafot may well agree that the qualified witness must know of the other person's disqualification.
And he is believed to say that he did not know of the relationship. See Tumim, who distinguishes between kinship among the witnesses themselves, where we ordinarily presume that they knew of the relationship, and a witness who is related to the borrower, where we ordinarily presume that the other witnesses did not know of that relationship. He further writes that since a woman is not fit to testify, she is not regarded as the second relative or disqualified witness who would nullify the testimony, whereas a person disqualified only by rabbinic law does nullify it. From his words one may infer that the same is true of a non Jew and a minor: their presence does not nullify the testimony of qualified witnesses. I was asked concerning a get signed by witnesses who were second cousins through one husband on the maternal side. The woman then married another man and became pregnant, and afterward her first husband divorced her again with a valid get. This is my response. If the witnesses who observed the delivery were qualified and the only problem was that the document was internally defective because of the signatures, this would be only a rabbinic disqualification. The law is explicit in Even HaEzer, siman 150, that if she already married she need not leave the second husband. The principal question requiring analysis is that the customary practice is to designate the signing witnesses also as the witnesses of delivery. They therefore intended to testify. Choshen Mishpat, siman 36, states that where a relative or disqualified witness is found among those who intended to testify, the testimony of all is nullified. It would then seem that the get lacked both valid signing witnesses and valid delivery witnesses, and would not be a get at all. It appears to me that there is room to permit as follows. In Yevamot 104, regarding an incident of chalitzah performed between the man and woman alone, Rabbi Akiva validated it. The Gemara asks how we know what occurred if it was only between them, and answers that witnesses observed from outside. Nimukei Yosef explains the Gemara's question as arising from the rule that a matter of ervah requires two witnesses, and Tosafot Yom Tov cites his explanation. Nimukei Yosef and Rashba there ask why the Gemara raises this difficulty, since a husband who says, "I divorced my wife," is believed, and similarly a yavam who says, "I performed chalitzah," should be believed. They answer that the Gemara ultimately concludes in Bava Batra, regarding a husband who says he divorced his wife, that his statement is at least a matter of concern. At first glance, Rashba and Nimukei Yosef are very difficult. A husband who says he divorced is effective only when he claims that he divorced her before witnesses. If he says he divorced her without witnesses, there is nothing to the divorce, because neither divorce nor betrothal has any legal force without witnesses. Here, according to Nimukei Yosef, the chalitzah itself took place without witnesses. If chalitzah is a matter of ervah requiring two witnesses, what significance can the yavam's credibility have? Likewise, the Gemara itself should have asked simply that chalitzah without witnesses has no legal force, since a matter of ervah requires two witnesses. And the answer that witnesses observed from outside is also difficult, because in matters of ervah it does not ordinarily suffice that the witnesses see the parties unless the parties also act in the presence of witnesses, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 42, seif 3. It therefore appears that the three areas of ervah, namely divorce, betrothal, and chalitzah, do not all share one identical reason for requiring witnesses. Each has its own legal basis. Kiddushin 65, in discussing one who betroths before a single witness, says that if we derive the word "matter" from monetary law, then just as in monetary law a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses, perhaps the same should apply there. The Gemara answers that in monetary law a person's admission does not create liability for others, whereas betrothal creates consequences for others. Rashba explains that betrothal is learned from monetary law specifically in a case where the act affects third parties: just as monetary liability affecting others requires witnesses, so too betrothal requires witnesses. Pnei Yehoshua asks that the principal Scriptural use of the word "matter" concerns credibility in a case of contradiction, namely testimony that would prohibit a woman to her husband. How do we derive from it that betrothal has no legal existence at all without witnesses? He answers that this follows from logic. The essence of betrothal is to prohibit the woman to all others as something consecrated and to create the prohibition of a married woman. Since without witnesses one cannot impose that prohibition upon others, because with respect to them she retains the presumptive status of an unmarried woman under the derivation of "matter" from monetary law, the betrothal cannot be valid even between the couple themselves. Betrothal cannot exist partially. It follows that if an admission by a litigant were effective even in monetary matters to impose liability upon third parties, then the same would also suffice in betrothal and divorce to establish the legal act itself, because the legal existence of the act depends upon the credibility that establishes it. This creates a difficulty regarding divorce. A husband who says, "I divorced her," is believed because divorce is within his power. It is well known that a person is believed concerning something within his power even in monetary matters where his statement affects third parties. Sanhedrin 30 and Choshen Mishpat, siman 255, regarding one who says, "I saw your father hide money and he said it belonged to so and so," demonstrate this principle, and Urim VeTumim implies that this is so even where the money lies in the heirs' possession. Tosafot in Kiddushin 64 likewise show that the principle of being within one's power can have force even to remove property from possession. A husband who says, "I divorced her," is believed for this reason as well. If such credibility were insufficient in monetary law where third parties are affected, then it would not suffice in divorce either, since the requirement concerning a matter of ervah is derived from monetary law. Thus, where the matter is within his power, his statement can have force even when third parties are affected. Why, then, should the divorce itself not be valid without witnesses if the husband's statement is credible? If a litigant's admission in monetary law could impose liability upon others, the betrothal itself would exist without formal witnesses. Since divorce is within the husband's power and his statement is effective even against others, why should the divorce not itself exist without witnesses? It therefore appears that a get has an additional and different requirement. Tosafot in Gittin 11, in the passage beginning "a shard," as explained by Urim VeTumim, siman 68, seif katan 8, indicate that even in monetary law, a document of conveyance that says, "My field is sold to you," does not effect acquisition if it has neither signing witnesses nor delivery witnesses nor the grantor's own handwriting, even if the grantor admits that he delivered it. A litigant's admission is effective in monetary law, but this paper still does not acquire because it lacks the legal status of a document. A writing is considered a valid legal document only when it carries evidentiary force. The same applies to a get. A woman acquires herself through a document. If the paper has neither signing witnesses, delivery witnesses, nor the husband's handwriting, it is not legally a document and cannot effect divorce. This resolves Rashba's question in Kiddushin 65 regarding why a get written in the husband's own handwriting is valid without signing or delivery witnesses, while a betrothal document written in the man's own hand is invalid without delivery witnesses. In betrothal, which imposes status upon others, his handwriting does not give him credibility against others. Consequently, the betrothal itself is void, because the legal existence of betrothal depends upon the credibility that establishes it. A get is different. The husband is believed regarding his own handwriting against others because divorce is within his power. Consequently, the divorce itself can be established, and the writing also counts as a valid legal document for purposes of acquisition because a document is fundamentally an evidentiary writing. A get written in the husband's own hand is therefore valid. Chalitzah differs from both. It has neither the problem of a legal document nor precisely the same form of witness requirement for the constitutive act. The principal requirement is credibility, which ordinarily requires two. Rashba can therefore properly ask that perhaps it should be valid even without formal witnesses by analogy to a husband who says, "I divorced her," because the matter is within the parties' power. If credibility is established, legal effectiveness can follow. He then answers that even in divorce the rule is only that the husband's statement creates a concern, and it is not fully equivalent to formal proof. This also explains why chalitzah performed privately can be a defective chalitzah that nevertheless disqualifies the woman from yibbum, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 169, while private divorce or betrothal has no effect at all. These three areas differ both in rationale and legal result. We still need to explain Rashba and Nimukei Yosef's comparison of chalitzah to a husband who says, "I divorced her," on the basis that the matter was within his power. At first glance the cases are not identical. A husband can divorce his wife against her will, whereas chalitzah requires the intent of both parties and therefore seems closer to betrothal. Even if both parties later admit that they performed the act before witnesses, in betrothal such an admission does not ordinarily suffice. Why, then, should chalitzah be treated as something within their power? The answer appears to be that when both parties agree, it is indeed a matter within their combined power. Why should they lie when together they can perform the act they describe? The limitation is that credibility based on "within one's power" is effective primarily to permit, but not to create a prohibitory status that could lead to capital liability. Tosafot at the beginning of Gittin distinguish along these lines. Kiddushin 64 treats the argument "why would he lie" as equivalent to a chazakah, and one chazakah does not displace another. Rashi explains that so long as he does not retract, there is a presumption that a person does not sin for no benefit. This corresponds to Pnei Yehoshua's explanation at the beginning of Gittin that where a person has the power to achieve the permitted state directly, we presume he would not abandon the permitted path and consume something prohibited. That reasoning is much weaker when his statement seeks to create a prohibition. Even where a person is believed concerning something within his power to establish prohibition, such as where he says, "Your wine became prohibited," his statement is not necessarily sufficient to impose lashes or capital punishment. Rambam rules in Hilkhot Sanhedrin, chapter 16, that where a witness establishes an ordinary prohibition in a situation not opposed by an existing permissive presumption, the resulting status can support punishment. But where the statement contradicts an existing chazakah, although it may be believed under Torah law for purposes of prohibition, it does not suffice for execution. Even a father, whom the Torah expressly believes in certain matters, does not thereby create a basis for execution, as Kiddushin 63 indicates. Pnei Yehoshua likewise writes this in his Kuntres Acharon. See also what I wrote in Chavat Da'at, simanim 185 and 127. Therefore, in betrothal, since credibility based on "within one's power" would not be sufficient to expose the woman and another man to capital punishment, it is not sufficient to constitute the betrothal itself. The legal existence of betrothal depends upon a form of proof capable of establishing its public status. By contrast, in chalitzah the resulting concern is less severe and can rest upon the kind of credibility Rashba and Nimukei Yosef discuss. This is also why witnesses observing from outside can suffice there. Tosafot in Gittin 17 write that a husband who says, "I divorced her," is not believed where she contradicts him. At first glance this is difficult. Since divorce is within his power, and in monetary law a person is believed concerning something within his power even where others are affected and even in the face of contradiction, while the law of a matter of ervah is derived from monetary law, why should his credibility fail when she contradicts him? There is no difficulty. Tosafot in Makkot 6 ask how we can ever have valid divorce and betrothal performed in the presence of relatives. Even according to Rabbi Yose, who invalidates a group containing a relative or disqualified person only in capital cases, divorce and betrothal resemble capital law. At first glance this is surprising. Kiddushin 65 indicates that the requirement of two witnesses in betrothal comes only from the derivation of "matter" from monetary law. According to Rabbi Yose, a group containing a relative or disqualified person can still function in monetary law where third parties are affected. Why, then, should they be disqualified in divorce and betrothal? The answer is that the Gemara needs the derivation of "matter" from monetary law specifically where both parties admit the facts. In ordinary prohibitory matters, a single witness is believed where the matter is not contradicted, and the resulting status can even lead to lashes in an appropriate case, as Rambam rules in Hilkhot Sanhedrin, chapter 16. Lashes are treated like capital law for this purpose. Therefore, the derivation from "matter" is needed to teach that betrothal should not be treated merely like an ordinary prohibition. Where there is direct contradiction, however, the rule that one witness does not rise against a person comes from a separate verse, and two witnesses qualified even for capital consequences are required because the matter may lead to capital liability. This resolves Beit Shmuel's question in Even HaEzer, siman 42, seif katan 8, regarding the opinion that betrothal before one witness creates a concern. He asks why, then, the presence of a relative or disqualified person would invalidate the other witness. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. Where the parties admit the facts, the requirement is learned from monetary law in a case of admission. There is then no necessary reason to invalidate the act merely because a relative or disqualified person was also present. We can now return to our case. We have established that whatever type of legal proof is valid in monetary law where third parties are affected can likewise establish the legal existence of divorce or betrothal. Rosh Hashanah 26 and Makkot 12 derive from the verse "the congregation shall save" that members of a Sanhedrin who themselves witnessed a killing cannot sit as judges in that capital case. Were it not for that special verse, they could judge on the basis of what they saw. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 90 explain the ordinary rule that a witness cannot become a judge on the ground that testimony before oneself is not subject to the law of plotting witnesses. One must distinguish. Where the judges saw the event at night, they first became witnesses and only later sought to judge. There the ordinary rule that a witness cannot become a judge applies, because their testimony cannot be subjected to the law of plotting witnesses. Where they saw the event by day and can immediately sit as judges, however, they are judges from the outset under the principle that hearing cannot be stronger than seeing. From the moment of judicial observation they do not need the ordinary laws of witness testimony. Judges need not know the exact day and hour in the same formal sense as witnesses, and judges are not themselves subject to the law of plotting witnesses, as Tashbetz writes in siman 1. Even if one were to say that judges can be disqualified when their observation is proven false, they would not receive the special punishment of plotting witnesses, but would be treated according to the ordinary law applicable to dishonest judges or wrongdoers. Accordingly, in Bava Batra 114, regarding three people who entered to visit a sick person and may, if they wish, immediately constitute a beit din, if four people entered and one of them was a relative or otherwise disqualified person, the other three can still form a beit din according to the opinion that the rule "one of them was found related or disqualified" does not apply to judges. Even according to the opinion that a similar principle can apply to judges, it appears clear that if a Sanhedrin saw a person legally subject to execution kill someone, and a relative or disqualified person who was not part of the Sanhedrin stood there intending to testify and even warned the killer, that disqualified witness does not nullify the judges. The judges do not judge because of their status as witnesses. Their observation is not ordinary testimony subject to plotting, since they need not establish through testimony the precise day and hour. They judge on the basis of the principle that hearing is not stronger than seeing. A disqualified witness cannot nullify judges. The two legal roles are distinct. The same therefore applies to three people who entered to visit a sick person. If there were four, three of whom did not intend to testify while the fourth intended to testify but was related or disqualified, the three may still, if they wish, constitute a beit din. The disqualified witness who intended to testify does not invalidate them, because a disqualified witness does not nullify judges. They judge because visual knowledge can function as judicial knowledge, and just as the disqualified person's presence would not affect them if the information had come by hearing, it does not affect them merely because their judicial knowledge arose through seeing. This cannot automatically be extended to all monetary cases. In sanctification of the new month, judges can constitute themselves without anyone's consent. Likewise, with three who entered to visit a sick person, the Torah itself permits them to become judges even against the lender's wishes, as Tosafot explain in Bava Batra 114. In ordinary monetary litigation, by contrast, the litigants must accept the people as judges. Sanhedrin 29 indicates that even where three gathered, they do not write a formal record unless the parties said, "Be judges over us." A relative or disqualified person among those present may therefore affect the legal status of the group, because the others cannot simply declare themselves judges against the parties' will. Noda BiYehudah, Even HaEzer, siman 51, writes similarly. If, however, the litigants afterward voluntarily accept those who saw the event as judges, then because the principle that a witness cannot become a judge does not apply where visual knowledge itself may serve as judicial knowledge, as explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 12, they judge not by virtue of formal testimony but as judges. The ordinary laws governing witness testimony, such as the requirement that the testimony be capable of plotting or the disqualification created by joining with a relative or disqualified witness, should then not apply to them. This appears clear. We can now apply this to divorce and betrothal. Noda BiYehudah writes that where three persons see a betrothal or divorce, they can in an appropriate case form a beit din themselves, just as in sanctification of the new month or the three who visited the sick person, because matters of divorce, betrothal, and other prohibitions do not require the consent of litigants in order for the observers to act judicially. If they wish, they can judge on the basis of what they themselves saw. Accordingly, if a Sanhedrin saw one man betroth a woman and afterward saw another man, himself legally subject to execution, violate her, the Sanhedrin can impose the appropriate punishment even if a relative or disqualified person stood among those present and intended to testify. The Sanhedrin judges as judges, not as a body of witnesses, and the relative or disqualified person does not nullify their judicial knowledge. Nor can one exempt the offender under the verse "the congregation shall save" merely because the disqualified person was present, for the special rule concerning a person of defective bodily status and capital law does not transform the qualified judges into a disqualified testimonial group. Nor can one say that there was no valid betrothal because the presence of the relative or disqualified witness nullified the witnesses and betrothal has no legal existence without witnesses. Where betrothal occurs before people whose knowledge would be sufficient in monetary law to establish liability affecting others, the betrothal has sufficient legal establishment. If in monetary law those same people could remove money because they were functioning as judges in a situation where they may constitute themselves without the parties' consent, their knowledge can likewise establish the betrothal. From this we return to the get before us. It appears that where three persons were present and could, if they wished, constitute a beit din regarding the divorce, a relative or disqualified person does not nullify them. Tashbetz, siman 1, writes this explicitly in substance. He explains that the later custom of tearing the get constitutes an act of beit din. Once the beit din performed that formal act, the woman's status as divorced was established through a judicial act rather than through ordinary witness testimony. The judges themselves are believed regarding their status and what occurred before them. The objection that witnesses to a get cannot become judges because a witness cannot become a judge is answered by the ordinary distinction: that rule applies where they already testified in beit din or where they saw the event at night and thereby became witnesses first. Where they saw it in a manner allowing immediate judicial action, the principle that hearing is not stronger than seeing applies. Even according to Ramban's explanation of the rule that the visitors to the sick person may, if they wish, judge because the matter resembles an admission before beit din, the same applies here. The parties acknowledged before them that the woman was divorced. Although a husband's bare statement, "I divorced her," is not always sufficient where no get is produced, in the case before us they are judging the parties' own positions: she claims that she was divorced by this get, and he admits the same. Such an admission has legal force. Therefore the persons who observed the act or before whom the admission was made may themselves function as judges, and their act has the status of a judicial act. Rivash, simanim 390 and 413, similarly writes that beit din may make a formal judicial record concerning a get because hearing cannot be stronger than seeing, and the law does not distinguish this from testimony concerning sanctification of the new month. Even according to the opinion that formal testimony is required in ordinary monetary matters, here there is also the husband's admission that he divorced her. Rivash discusses this at length. One should not insist that people can declare themselves judges only where a formal beit din is inherently required, such as sanctification of the new month or chalitzah, but not in a matter that does not independently require beit din. This is incorrect. The three who enter to visit a sick person likewise need not become a beit din, because if they wish they can instead act as witnesses. Tashbetz, Rivash, and Noda BiYehudah likewise demonstrate that this distinction is not valid. Nor should one object that present day judges lack classical ordination. In common matters such as divorce, present day batei din act as agents of the earlier ordained courts. We even compel the giving of gittin in appropriate cases. Responsa Lechem Rav, siman 26, also supports this framework. He discusses a woman betrothed in the presence of ten people where two disqualified persons were specifically designated as witnesses, and writes that the qualified witnesses who had merely come to observe were not nullified by the disqualified designated witnesses. At first glance this appears contrary to the authorities cited by Shach in siman 36. According to what I have written, however, betrothal is different because the qualified observers can constitute themselves a beit din without requiring the parties' consent, and their knowledge can function judicially rather than as ordinary witness testimony. This also helps explain Tosafot in Shevuot 35. The Mishnah teaches that if one of the group was related or disqualified, the others are exempt from the oath concerning testimony. Tosafot ask that this seems unnecessary, since we already know that the oath applies only to those fit to testify. They answer that the novelty concerns a group of three, and so forth. One might still ask that it is already a Mishnah that one relative or disqualified person can nullify the testimony of the group. According to what I have written, however, the case can be one where the qualified persons could instead constitute themselves a beit din, such as the three who entered to visit a sick person. Even so, they are exempt from the oath concerning testimony, because their possible judicial role does not place them within the category of witnesses obligated by that oath. Accordingly, in our case, since the customary practice gathers ten people at the place where beit din sits and they perform the formal judicial act of tearing the get, as Tashbetz describes, the relative or disqualified witnesses do not invalidate the entire proceeding. Three of the qualified persons can sit and perform an act of beit din establishing that this woman was divorced. This is all the more so according to what I wrote above, that a get does not require witnesses merely because it is a matter of ervah, but also because without signing witnesses, delivery witnesses, or equivalent evidentiary writing it would not have the status of a legal document. Where the judicial framework itself establishes the divorce, that concern is different. There is additional room for leniency because the other observers did not know of the familial relationship among the two witnesses. Tumim writes that where they did not know of the relationship, everyone agrees that in order for the disqualified persons to nullify the others, they must actually give testimony in beit din. Here there was no such testimony in beit din. Although the disqualified witnesses signed the get, one cannot say that their signatures are automatically equivalent to testimony already examined in beit din when they themselves are disqualified. Taz in siman 45 writes similarly. Tosafot and Rosh also hold in many places that a relative or disqualified witness nullifies the others only where both elements are present, observation and later testimony. Here there was no formal testimony by the disqualified persons in beit din. In addition, many authorities hold that certain relationships through the mother or through marriage create only rabbinic disqualification. Where the defect is rabbinic and the woman already married, there are circumstances in which she need not leave the second husband. Although one can distinguish between a get that is rabbinically defective in its form and a divorce carried out through witnesses who are rabbinically disqualified, since the issue is rabbinic there is room to rely upon the lenient considerations. This is especially so in the case before us, where after the second marriage the first husband, immediately upon discovery of the problem, divorced her again with an unquestionably valid get. The remaining issue is therefore only the rabbinic penalty that ordinarily requires her to leave both men. For all the reasons stated above, there is sufficient basis to rely upon the lenient position. I have nevertheless written only what appears correct in my humble opinion.
