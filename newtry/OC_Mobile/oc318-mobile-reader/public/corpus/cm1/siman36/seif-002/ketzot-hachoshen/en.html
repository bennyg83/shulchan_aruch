Where the disqualified witness testified. Mishneh LaMelech cites Maharshal HaLevi that the rule of a relative or disqualified witness nullifying the whole group applies to judges as well. Thus if one of five judges is related or disqualified, the entire judgment is void. Knesset HaGedolah likewise cites this. In my view the rule does not apply to judges in the same manner. Sanhedrin says that by Torah law one learned individual can suffice for monetary judgment, and the Sages required three because otherwise one might have a court of ignorant people. The Gemara answers that with three it is impossible that none knows the law. If the presence of one nonjudge or disqualified person automatically nullified all qualified judges, the presence of the ignorant members would destroy the learned judge's ruling. Baal HaMaor writes that one who is neither learned nor capable is not a judge at all, whether he happens to reach the right result or not. The Gemara's entire solution therefore presupposes that qualified judges are not automatically nullified by the presence of unqualified colleagues. Mordechai at the end of Kiddushin can be read otherwise. He says that where four judges sign a validation and one is disqualified, the validation fails because only two qualified signers remain and validation requires three. This suggests that the disqualified judge does not automatically destroy the others. Rather, the problem is simply that the required minimum of three qualified judges no longer exists. A distinction must therefore be drawn. Where judges sit to issue a legal ruling, the rule of "one related or disqualified witness nullifies all" does not apply. So long as a sufficient qualified beit din remains, their ruling stands. But where judges are also functioning as witnesses, such as signing a certification that a document was validated before them, or issuing a record that testimony was received before them, their signatures carry testimonial force about what occurred. In that testimonial aspect, the presence of a disqualified signer can nullify the instrument. Similarly, if they write a document memorializing a judgment already rendered and one signer is disqualified, a litigant can deny that the ruling was ever issued in that form, because the document itself lacks valid testimonial proof. The substantive judgment, however, is not void merely because a disqualified person sat with the qualified judges. This appears to me the correct distinction.
