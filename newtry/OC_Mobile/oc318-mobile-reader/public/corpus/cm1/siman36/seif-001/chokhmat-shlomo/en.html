Note: See my work on Even HaEzer, laws of kiddushin, siman 28, seventh edition siman 212, where I raised the question whether a witness disqualified because of personal interest invalidates the other witnesses under the rule that if one of a group is found related or disqualified, the entire testimony is void. Perhaps personal interest is not a definitive personal disqualification but only a concern of bias, and therefore does not invalidate the others. I left the matter requiring study there. May Hashem illuminate my eyes to know the halachah. There, Seif 1. See Shach, seif katan 3, and Netivot HaMishpat’s question. In my responsum on Even HaEzer siman 31 I resolved it by distinguishing monetary cases from capital cases and witnesses to kiddushin. In the latter, I wrote, even the Shach concedes that the presence of a disqualified person does not necessarily nullify everything where the valid witnesses themselves did not intend to join him. In my work on Even HaEzer, laws of kiddushin, siman 42, in a responsum to the province of Volhynia, I wrote that the presence of a non-Jew can likewise fall under the rule of one witness found related or disqualified, according to each view’s requirements. This follows a fortiori from an ordinary disqualified witness. It applies, however, only if the non-Jew understands the transaction occurring before him. Even if he did not specifically intend to testify, according to the Shach he can then join the group where the valid witnesses also did not specifically intend to testify. But if the non-Jew, and similarly another disqualified person, does not understand the event at all, his seeing it has no evidentiary significance and is legally as though he saw nothing. He never receives the status of a disqualified witness for that event and therefore does not nullify the testimony. This appears correct for practical halachah. The poskim dispute whom the court asks whether they came “to see” or “to testify,” the valid witnesses or the disqualified ones. It appears to me that the intention of the valid witnesses is decisive. Gittin 81b discusses Rabbi Akiva’s refusal to use a slave in a certain signature arrangement lest people conclude that a slave is valid for testimony, and the Gemara asks why the same concern does not exist with a relative. If the intention of the disqualified witness were decisive, one could distinguish readily. A relative is ordinarily a valid person who is disqualified only by this relationship, so we could presume he intended merely to fill the blank and not to testify. An ordinary slave or robber lacks the same presumption of fitness, so perhaps he intended to testify and thereby nullify the whole instrument. Since the Gemara does not make this distinction, it appears that the disqualified person’s intention is not controlling. Rather, what matters is whether the valid witnesses intended to join him. There is room to reject the proof according to Rabbi Akiva, since Makkot 6 implies that Rabbi Akiva may invalidate the group whenever one witness is related or disqualified even without specific intention to testify. If so, the Gemara’s question concerns Rabbi Akiva’s own broader rule, and the signature case may be exceptional because it is obvious that the additional signer came only to fill the required number. The matter therefore still requires study.
There, Seif 1. I heard a question in the name of a great scholar. Why, when one of a group of witnesses is found related or disqualified, is the entire testimony void? The defendant himself denies the event and says the witnesses are lying. An admission of a litigant against himself is stronger than one hundred witnesses, while his statement in his own favor is ineffective. By denying that the disqualified witnesses were present together with the valid witnesses, he should at least be believed to his own detriment that the disqualified witnesses were not joined with the valid ones. Then the valid testimony would survive. I answered that this is no difficulty in light of Tosafot in Kiddushin concerning an agent who also serves as a witness. Tosafot explain that even if such witnesses swear, their testimony is not repaired where their words alone are insufficient and they need some other factor to make them credible. The Torah says, “By the mouth of two witnesses a matter shall be established,” meaning that the witnesses must be believed through their testimony itself, not only through testimony plus external assistance. Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 35, likewise explains why an additional supporting witness cannot cure certain defects. Here, according to the valid witnesses’ own story, they joined with disqualified witnesses, so the Torah has nullified their testimony. They can become effective only by adding the defendant’s admission that the disqualified people were not actually present in the legally significant way. They are therefore not witnesses whose own words establish the matter. This is unlike one who says, “I never borrowed,” and witnesses testify that he borrowed and later repaid. There the witnesses establish the loan entirely by their own testimony. The borrower’s statement that he never borrowed is treated separately as an admission that he never repaid. Loan and repayment are distinct issues. Here, however, the witnesses speak to one event and their own account gives them the legal status of a group whose testimony has been invalidated. Whether the defect comes from the relative himself or from valid witnesses who joined him, the Torah’s rule makes the group disqualified for this testimony. A litigant’s admission cannot turn testimony that is intrinsically invalid for this event into testimony established by the witnesses’ words alone. This also explains why personal interest may be removable according to some opinions. A personally interested witness is not intrinsically a disqualified person. The interest is an external obstacle. Indeed, several authorities do not require him to have been free of that interest at both the beginning and the end. Once the interest is removed, he is once again an ordinary witness whose words stand by themselves. This differs from a group already invalidated because one member was related or disqualified. The rule can also be illustrated where a defendant says, “I do not know whether I borrowed,” and witnesses testify that he did borrow, but the group includes relatives. The whole testimony is void and there is no admission from the defendant that can cure it. This also answers the approach attributed to the son-in-law of the author of Shitrei Torah in his introduction. I have been brief; examine it carefully.
