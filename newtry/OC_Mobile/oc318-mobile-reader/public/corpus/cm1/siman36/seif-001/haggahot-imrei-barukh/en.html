(Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 10) Likewise, a gentile and a minor do not invalidate the testimony of qualified witnesses. Note: This requires study according to the Raavad and several early authorities, who derive from the law of one witness found to be a relative or otherwise disqualified that a litigant does not invalidate the other witnesses, because a matter is established by those capable of establishing it and a litigant has no status of witness at all. Thus, where one was first a litigant and later removed his interest, he is not considered initially disqualified, because initially he had no status of witness. By the same reasoning, since a gentile and a minor have no status of witnesses at all and do not invalidate qualified witnesses through the rule of one witness found disqualified, perhaps the rule of initial disqualification should likewise not apply to them. A gentile who saw testimony while a gentile and then converted, or a minor who saw it before attaining majority, should then be comparable. Yet Shach, siman 35, seif katan 7, expressly explains their invalidity because the witness must be qualified both initially and at the end. See also above, my comments to siman 35, seif katan 14, and Ritva to Ketubot 28a and Kiddushin 43, who accepts the Raavad's reasoning. According to the Tumim and this author, a woman and a minor do not invalidate qualified witnesses by their presence. One may say the same of a slave, whose testimonial disqualification is derived through a common denominator from woman and minor, as Bava Kamma 88 states. There is some proof from Gittin 73, which rules that a divorced woman may not seclude herself with her former husband except in the presence of witnesses, even a male or female slave, other than her own maidservant because she is familiar with her. Rashi explains that the concern is that he may have intercourse with her for kiddushin and she would require a second get. Tosafot question the Gemara's linkage to the concern for kiddushin, as clarified by the Rashba. According to the Ramah cited by the Ritva in Kiddushin 43, when relatives or other disqualified persons are present at kiddushin they can invalidate all the witnesses under the rule of one found relative or disqualified, unless the groom designates the valid witnesses by saying "you are my witnesses." If so, what concern for kiddushin could arise in the seclusion described here? Even if intercourse occurred for kiddushin, the valid testimony would be nullified by the slaves who also witnessed the event, unless the valid witnesses were designated. This would make even seclusion in the presence of her maidservant harmless with respect to kiddushin, particularly according to the Yerushalmi cited by the Rashba, where the valid witnesses themselves see that the servants are present. It therefore appears to support the Tumim, that a woman, and by extension a slave, is not regarded as a disqualified witness for this rule. Still, the matter is open to extended discussion, because Tosafot's reason there is stated according to Rabbi Yose, and the Ramah may hold that kiddushin testimony is treated only as monetary testimony, while Rabbi Yose may maintain that the rule of one found relative or disqualified applies only in capital testimony.
