Many witnesses who saw the event together, specifically. As I write below, seif katan 9.
But if not all of them intended to testify, etc. This means that if not all intended to testify, the testimony is upheld through those remaining witnesses who did intend to testify, as I explain in the following seif katan.
Beit din asks them, etc. The Sema writes that Rambam's and the Mechaber's wording implies that as soon as a group comes before beit din to testify, even before beit din knows that a relative or otherwise disqualified witness is among them, the judges ask all of them whether they originally came merely to observe or came to testify. According to the Sema, this is done because if they wait until an invalid witness is discovered, there is concern that the witnesses may manipulate their answer, claiming that they intended to testify when that helps the litigant to whom the invalid witness is related, or the reverse. Rashi and the Tur, however, imply that only the relatives or invalid witnesses are questioned, and until it is known that such a person was present there is nothing to investigate. The Sema concludes that this is primary, because we do not ordinarily suspect witnesses of kinship or disqualification until such a defect is discovered. In my humble opinion, Rambam and the Mechaber likewise discuss a case where it is already known that the group contains a relative or disqualified witness. Their words continue the preceding rule concerning many witnesses among whom one was found to be a relative or invalid: "How is the matter examined? They are asked, etc." Their use of the plural, implying that everyone is questioned, is not based on the Sema's concern for manipulation, for that concern would still not be fully removed. Rather, they hold that the testimony must be sustained specifically through the remaining valid witnesses who actually intended to testify. Since a relative or disqualified person who came only to observe does not invalidate the others, it follows that one who came only to observe does not count as a witness at all. The same is therefore true of valid persons who came only to observe, and the judgment is not rendered on their testimony. It would be unreasonable to say that relatives who came to observe are not witnesses, while valid persons who came to observe are witnesses. Rather, anyone who came only to see is not considered a witness, and anyone who came to testify is considered a witness. This is why Rambam and the Mechaber immediately continue: "When does this apply? When there was among them a relative or disqualified witness. But if they were all valid, whether one intended to testify or did not intend to testify, etc., judgment may be rendered on his testimony." They expressly mean that the rule barring reliance on valid persons who did not intend to testify applies when there was also a relative or invalid person in the group. Since the relative or invalid person who did not intend to testify is excluded, the valid persons who likewise did not intend to testify are excluded as well. If all were valid, however, judgment may be rendered even through those who did not originally intend to testify. This is still clearer in Rambam's own language, chapter 5 of Hilchot Edut, law 5: "We ask them, 'Did you come to testify or to observe?' Everyone who says, 'To observe,' is separated, and everyone who says, 'To testify,' is separated. If among those who intended to testify there is found a relative or disqualified person, the testimony of all is void. When does this apply? When there was among them a relative or invalid person. But if they were all valid, whether one intended to testify or did not intend to testify, judgment is rendered on his testimony." Why does Rambam need to say that each group is separated? He could simply have said that if the relative says he intended to testify he invalidates the testimony, and otherwise he does not. Clearly, Rambam means that anyone who says he came to observe is set apart and thereafter is not counted as a witness even if he is valid. His phrase "When does this apply?" refers to that rule, as I have explained. Rambam derives this from the Gemara's plural wording, "What do we ask them?" which implies that the question is addressed to everyone, not only to the relative or invalid person. Likewise, the Gemara continues, "If they say, 'We came to testify,' and one of them is found to be a relative or invalid, their testimony is void." If only the relative or invalid witness were questioned, it should have said simply, "If he says, 'I came to testify,' the testimony is void." Rather, the relative or invalid person cannot alone be asked. Even if he says he came merely to observe, perhaps the valid persons will also say they came merely to observe, in which case their testimony cannot be used under this rule. We therefore ask everyone: "Did you come to observe or to testify?" If those who came to testify include a relative or invalid person, their testimony is void. If the relatives say they came to observe, what are two brothers who merely saw someone commit murder supposed to do? The testimony is then sustained by those remaining valid witnesses who warned the offender and thereby manifested an intent to testify, as the Mishnah teaches. The Tur's language is difficult in my eyes. At first, when paraphrasing Rambam, he says that we examine "them," and that the relative who says he came to testify invalidates all the testimony, while one who came only to observe does not. This sounds as though only the relatives and invalid witnesses are questioned, which certainly is not Rambam's view, as explained. Moreover, Rambam, cited by the Tur himself, continues that this applies when there is a relative or invalid person, but if all are valid, judgment is rendered through both those who intended to testify and those who did not. This explicitly implies that in the first case judgment is not rendered through the valid persons who did not intend to testify, even where the relative likewise did not intend to testify. Otherwise, why distinguish a case where all are valid? It would have been enough to say that when the relative or invalid person did not intend to testify, judgment is rendered through all the valid witnesses, whether they intended to testify or not. And what novelty would there be in saying that when everyone is valid judgment can be rendered through valid persons who did not intend to testify? That would already follow a fortiori from the first rule. Rather, the opening rule means that where an invalid person is present, judgment is rendered only through valid witnesses who intended to testify, even if the invalid person did not intend to testify. Rambam then teaches that lack of initial intent does not inherently remove a valid person from witness status in all circumstances. If everyone is valid, even those who did not intend to testify may be used. Lechem Mishneh, chapter 5 of Hilchot Edut, also appears to have understood Rambam as saying that if everyone says they came to observe, the testimony is nevertheless sustained by the others, but he did not reach the intent of Rambam's distinction. In my view Rambam's and the Mechaber's meaning is clear as I have explained. Rashi, however, explicitly says that the question is asked of the relative or disqualified person in order to examine him, and thus does not appear to agree. Kesef Mishneh therefore should not have cited Rashi as though his words directly explain Rambam. Perhaps Rashi mentions the relative or invalid person because the valid witnesses ordinarily gave the warning and thereby manifested their intent to testify, so only the relatives and invalid persons still require examination. Examine this carefully.
Anyone who says, "I came to testify," etc. Darkei Moshe and the Sema cite Responsa Rashba, siman 1189, that if the invalid persons manifested at the time of seeing the event that they intended to testify, for example by warning the offender, all the testimony is disqualified because of them. There was no need to cite Rashba for this, because it is an explicit Mishnah: "When does this apply? When they warned them, etc." Rashi and all the commentators explain it this way. Nor was there practical need to cite a rule involving warning, because formal warning applies only in capital cases, which are not adjudicated nowadays.
One who says, "I came to testify," etc. Tosafot at the beginning of Makkot asks that according to this rule no one could ever be executed, because relatives could always come afterward and say, "We too came to testify." Rabbeinu Chaim Kohen therefore explains that we ask the valid witnesses whether they came to testify together with the invalid persons, or came merely to observe independently of them. This does not appear correct to me. The primary explanation is Rashi's, that the relative or invalid witness is questioned, as Ramban, the Rosh, Rambam, the Tur, the Mechaber, and the other authorities write. The rule applies specifically where we know that the relatives or invalid persons were actually present when the event was seen. Since they were part of the witnessing group at the time of observation, they are also believed to say that they intended to testify. If their presence is not otherwise known, and relatives later come and claim, "We too were there and intended to testify," they have no power to invalidate established testimony by that assertion. Tosafot's concern that no murderer could ever be executed therefore does not arise. Ordinarily the defendant is executed unless it is established that disqualified persons were present.
If one is found among these, etc. Their testimony is void, meaning even if the relative or invalid person never actually testified in beit din. So explains the Sema. This indeed follows from Rambam's view, and is still clearer to anyone who studies Rambam's own language. It accords with Rashi and Rashbam. The Bach's statement that Beit Yosef incorrectly understood Rambam as following Rashi and Rashbam is itself inaccurate. Beit Yosef correctly understood Rambam to follow Rashi and Rashbam on the point that the invalid person's initial intent to testify alone joins him to the group and invalidates the testimony, even if he never subsequently testified in beit din, contrary to Tosafot. See what I write below, seif katan 8. The Tur writes that Ramah adds the converse rule: if an invalid person testified together with them within toch kedei dibbur, then even if he originally came only to observe, and even if they did not see the event together, all the testimony is void, because the principal verse concerns the stage of giving testimony. The Sema cites this in seif katan 4. Ramah's words are difficult for me. Even concerning conspiring witnesses, the Mishnah immediately afterward teaches that if two witnesses see the act from one window and two from another, and the groups do not see one another, they are two separate testimonies. That Mishnah itself discusses testimony given within toch kedei dibbur, as Rava explains, "provided they all testify within toch kedei dibbur." The latter clause is analogous to the former. Thus, even when they testify within toch kedei dibbur, they must have seen together in order to constitute one testimony. Perhaps Ramah distinguishes conspiring witnesses, but I see no basis for doing so. The primary rule therefore appears as implied by the other authorities: whether or not they testified within toch kedei dibbur, this law requires that they saw together and intended to testify, as I explain below. Tosafot, Makkot 6a at the end of the passage beginning "Rava said," and 6b in the passage beginning "These are one testimony," clearly supports this. The Tur below, siman 38, writes likewise in the name of Rabbi Yeshayah, and the Bach there also interprets him this way. The Bach's additional statement, however, that for the law of hazamah it is certainly enough that they join at the time of testimony, because hazamah depends upon testimony according to everyone and does not require that they saw together, is incorrect. He overlooked the Mishnah of two witnesses seeing from one window and two from another. Rabbi Yeshayah's words there, "provided they all saw together," plainly refer to everything he discussed, both hazamah and the presence of a relative or invalid witness. This is clear. Nor can Rashi's explanation of Rava's statement, "provided they all testified within toch kedei dibbur," be used to support Ramah. Rashi says that the Mishnah's rule that one hundred witnesses are like two applies when they testify within that interval, but if two testify and the others testify later, they are like two separate groups "for every matter." Tosafot already challenged Rashi's language. Moreover, although Tosafot, Beit Yosef, the Bach below in siman 38, and other later authorities understood Rashi as Ramah does, in my humble opinion that is not Rashi's intent. Rava's statement appears earlier concerning the Mishnah of conspiring witnesses, and Rashi explains it there. Only afterward does the Gemara cite Rabbi Akiva's rule concerning a relative or invalid witness. Rashi's phrase "for every matter" therefore means that in the context of hazamah the groups are separate for every consequence: each group is punished independently even if only some were rendered zomemim, and the remaining witnesses are not invalidated merely because one group was found false. One should not infer that the same timing condition governs an ordinary case of a relative or invalid witness. In that ordinary case, toch kedei dibbur is unnecessary because the witnesses are telling the truth and were actually joined by witnessing the event together. Conspiring witnesses are different because their account of joint observation is false. Tosafot itself makes this distinction. This, in my view, is clearly Rashi's position. Rabbi Ovadiah of Bertinoro's position also appears to be so, contrary to Tosafot Yom Tov. Semag, end of negative commandment 213, can likewise be explained, as Maharam Stein does, to refer specifically to conspiring witnesses. Where the witnesses are not zomemim and one is merely a relative or otherwise invalid, there is no distinction whether they testified within toch kedei dibbur or afterward. This follows from the Gemara's question, "Should the victim save him?" and from Tosafot and Hagahot Maimoniyot. It is also compelled by the fact that Rambam, the other poskim, the Tur, and the Mechaber make no such distinction here. This is the primary rule. It follows from all this that where one witness is simply a relative or invalid and the witnesses' joint observation of the event is true, timing of their later testimony does not matter. But if the purportedly invalid witnesses are subsequently shown to be zomemim, they were not truthfully joined in seeing the event and do not invalidate the valid testimony unless their testimony was given within toch kedei dibbur. Mahari Beirav states this in the responsum printed in Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 16, page 24b: A conspiring witness joins a valid witness to invalidate him only when he testifies within toch kedei dibbur of the other. If he testifies afterward, he does not join the valid witness and the valid testimony remains. He writes that this appears to be the view of Rashi, Semag, and Raavad, although Rambam disagrees. Raavad indeed challenges Rambam in chapter 20 of Hilchot Edut. In Mahari Beirav's actual case the conspiring witness was independently wicked as well, as the question and other portions of the responsum show. Maharam Alashkar argues at length that Egypt was a locale governed by Rambam's rulings and that Migdal Oz defended Rambam against Raavad. His words are not correct. The principle of following Rambam in Egypt applies only where his position can reasonably be sustained. Here, in my view, it cannot. Had Rambam himself been present, there is no doubt that he would have conceded to Raavad's proof. I examined Migdal Oz and found that, as often, he confuses the matter. His supposed refutations are actually proofs for Raavad. It is astonishing that Maharam Alashkar relied upon Migdal Oz without first examining whether his defense was sound. Although the Tur in siman 38 also follows Rambam, the truth must guide the law. Maharam Alashkar further invokes Tosafot, a responsum of Rif, and Hagahot Maimoniyot concerning invalidation of testimony, but none of those sources addresses a witness who was shown to be a zomem. They discuss a person who was simply a relative or otherwise invalid. Since such witnesses tell the truth about the event and were joined at the time of observation, no toch kedei dibbur requirement applies. When the invalid witness is shown to be a zomem, Tosafot in Makkot 6a expressly requires testimony within toch kedei dibbur. The same follows from Rashi and Bertinoro, whether one reads Rashi as Tosafot did or as I have explained. The Bach below, siman 38, end of seif 14, likewise writes that Hagahot Maimoniyot concludes that the Gemara explicitly supports Rambam and that Tosafot and the Tur agree. This too is an error. Hagahot Maimoniyot says only that where one is found to be a relative or invalid without hazamah, the Gemara supports Rambam in not distinguishing based on the timing of testimony. In the case of zomemim, toch kedei dibbur is required even to invalidate the other testimony, contrary to Rambam in chapter 20 and the Tur, as I have proved and as Tosafot writes. Maharam Alashkar's other discussion of this issue is likewise confused in my humble opinion. What I have written appears clear.
Even if they did not know of one another. This means that if they later testified in beit din, their testimony is void according to all opinions even though the witnesses were unaware of one another. According to Rambam this is obvious, because the testimony is disqualified by their joinder in seeing the event even if the invalid person never later testifies. According to Tosafot too, once they later testify, the testimony is void even if they did not know of one another, as Tosafot explicitly writes in Makkot 6a. Even according to Rif, whose first opinion below in seif 2 requires that the valid witnesses know of the invalid witnesses' disqualification, this means only that they must know that those persons are legally invalid. They need not know that the invalid persons later testified in beit din. Their original joinder at the time of observation suffices. Moreover, even according to Rambam, who invalidates the testimony from the moment of observation even without later testimony, one can have a case where they did not know one another. Although, as I write below in seif katan 7, the valid witnesses must know that the others are disqualified and all must see the event together, the Mishnah treats witnesses as having seen together when some members of the larger group can see other members. Thus many witnesses can form one group through overlapping lines of sight even though not every individual sees every other. Tosafot, Makkot 6b, expressly states that where some see others, even those who do not see one another join into one testimony, so that if one is found to be a relative or invalid all are disqualified, and likewise for hazamah. Accordingly, if invalid persons were among them, the testimony can be void even though particular witnesses did not know one another personally, so long as the group was connected by some witnesses seeing others. The Sema, seif katan 5, gives another explanation: the invalid person did not know the valid person, and the novelty is that although he did not intend to join his testimony with the valid person's testimony, he nevertheless invalidates it, while the valid witness did know the invalid person's status. This is not persuasive, and the phrase "they did not know one another" does not naturally bear that meaning. I have therefore written what appears correct to me.
Some say, etc. The view that two conditions are required, initial intent to testify and actual later testimony, is the position of Tosafot and the Rosh. Rashba and Ran also appear to hold this way, as Beit Yosef cites them above in siman 7 concerning the rule that a witness does not become a judge. Nevertheless, the primary view in my opinion is that of Rambam, Ramah, Rashi, and Rashbam in chapter Yesh Nochalin: initial intent to testify is sufficient even if the invalid witness never later testifies. Neither the Mishnah nor the Gemara mentions any requirement that he later testify. On the contrary, the plain meaning of the Mishnah is that according to both Rabbi and Rabbi Yose the witnesses are joined by their intent to testify alone. This also follows from the Gemara's question, "Should the victim save the accused?" Tosafot and those who agree answer that once the Gemara concludes that the verse speaks of those who establish the matter rather than those who perform it, the difficulty disappears. That answer does not appear correct to me. The Gemara excludes the actors from the verse, as Rashi explains, but does not retract the assumption that actual testimony in beit din is unnecessary for joinder. The Mishnah's statement, "But when they did not warn him, what are two brothers who saw someone kill a person supposed to do?" also points this way. Tosafot and the Rosh answer that sometimes the brothers do not know of one another and one brother may testify after the other has already testified. This too is unpersuasive. One could simply tell them that only one brother should testify. Why ask, "What are two brothers supposed to do?" They should ensure that only one testifies. If the second later comes and testifies, that is his choice and there is no need to preserve against it. The Mishnah therefore must mean that mere joint observation does not invalidate unless there was intent to testify, as I have explained. Ran argues from the wording, "Did you come to testify or did you come to see?" that the question must concern their arrival in beit din rather than their intent at the event, because perhaps the person had already been present at the scene and did not "come" there. In my humble opinion this is a weak inference. If the question refers to beit din, what sense does "Did you come to see?" make? What would they come to see in beit din? Rather, beit din asks whether they came to the scene to observe the killing or to testify about it. That is why, if they say they came merely to see, the Mishnah asks what two brothers who saw a murder are supposed to do. Tosafot, the Rosh, and those who agree further ask how any public get or kiddushin can be valid when relatives are present. Nowadays the answer is simple. We expressly designate valid witnesses for those acts. The relatives therefore plainly are not part of the testimony. Even if they privately intend to testify, they cannot force themselves into the designated witness group, because the designation of specific witnesses excludes everyone else. Maharam Peretz writes in Hagahot Semak, siman 183, that one must designate witnesses for gittin and kiddushin, because relatives ordinarily stand there and otherwise their participation could invalidate the testimony, and that this is the explanation of the earlier authorities and the universal practice. Perhaps this was also the practice in the Talmudic era, or they ensured that relatives and invalid witnesses were not present when gittin and kiddushin were effected. Alternatively, absent some indication we do not suspect that relatives intended to testify. The Gemara's question about what to ask them arises when they later come to beit din on their own to testify. Until then there is no reason for concern. Ramban, in his final explanation in chapter Yesh Nochalin, writes similarly that everyday kiddushin and ketubot occur in the presence of many people, and one can say that until the relatives come to beit din we presume they did not intend to testify. Because they later came to beit din, they must then be questioned. Ramban adds that the practice is simply to designate two valid witnesses and exclude the rest. This accords with the plain meaning of the Mishnah: only where they warned him or otherwise intended to testify does the joinder arise. Mere observation does not disqualify, and ordinarily relatives are presumed not to have intended testimony. Examine this carefully.
For both are required: he intended to testify, etc. The Bach explains that if the invalid person intended at the time of observation to testify and later came to beit din and testified, he invalidates the testimony even if he testified after the interval of toch kedei dibbur and even if the witnesses did not see the event together, and says that Tosafot and the Rosh hold so. In my view this is inaccurate. No authority can hold that intent to testify helps when the witnesses did not see together. What joined them into one testimony? Tosafot, Makkot 6a, expressly says that where a relative or invalid witness is found, the testimony is treated as one even though testimony was given after toch kedei dibbur because the witnesses are telling the truth, and their testimony began with their observation, which occurred together. This is precisely the opposite of the Bach's statement. Tosafot on 6b likewise says that when some see others they become one testimony so that a relative or invalid person invalidates all. Clearly, seeing together is required. Whenever the poskim say that disqualified persons "intended to testify," they refer to persons who saw the event together. This is clear.
And he came to beit din and testified. If he came and testified but did not originally intend at the time of seeing the event to testify, he does not invalidate the other witnesses according to any opinion. This is explicit in Ramban's novellae to chapter Yesh Nochalin and in the novellae of Rashba and Ran there, cited by Maharam Alashkar, Responsa siman 16, page 23a, and by Beit Yosef above in siman 7. Mahari Beirav therefore correctly writes there that according to all the poskim, if at the time of the event they intended only to observe, then even if they later testify in beit din and one is a relative or otherwise invalid, the valid testimony remains. Rashba and Ran also write so. In the case before him, he writes, we are ourselves witnesses that people who attend a wedding canopy ordinarily do not go there as witnesses. This is contrary to Maharam Alashkar, who attacked Mahari Beirav at length and claimed that Ramban, Rashba, and Ran held the opposite, saying that Mahari Beirav had committed several errors. In truth, Maharam Alashkar is the one who erred. Any careful reader sees that these authorities mean that even after the persons have testified, we ask whether they originally intended at the time of observation to testify. Initial intent must exist at the time the event is seen; otherwise they were never joined as one witness group. Even according to Ran's second explanation of the question "Did you come to testify now?" the later arrival is relevant only as evidence that they probably had an original intent to testify. If at the time of observation they did not intend to testify, all agree that they do not invalidate. The Gemara itself proves this when it says that if they came merely to observe, what are two brothers who saw a murder supposed to do? Clearly, intent must exist at the time the event is seen. Maharam Alashkar further attacks Mahari Beirav's assertion that wedding attendees are presumed not to have come as witnesses, citing Rashba, the Rosh, Ran, Ramban, and Semak as though they imply that even wedding attendees may be presumed to intend testimony. In my humble opinion, here too the law follows Mahari Beirav. Ramban's conclusion in chapter Yesh Nochalin implies that only when the relatives later come on their own to beit din do we presume they may have gone initially to testify. When they never came voluntarily, we presume they did not. Rashba, the Rosh, and Ran can likewise be understood to refer to witnesses who expressly say they intended to testify or who later come to beit din on their own, as I explain below, seif katan 17. In Mahari Beirav's case the witnesses came only because of a public summons, not on their own, so at the event they are presumed not to have intended testimony. Moreover, Mahari Beirav wrote in light of the practice recorded by Semak of expressly designating witnesses at kiddushin. Once special witnesses are designated, all other attendees are presumptively excluded. Semak therefore supports Mahari Beirav rather than Maharam Alashkar.
And he testified. Even after the relatives or invalid persons have testified, they are believed to say that at the time of observation they did not intend to testify. This follows from Tosafot and the Rosh at the beginning of Makkot in their discussion of two brothers who may both come to testify without knowing of one another. It is also explicit from the words of Ramban, Rashba, and Ran cited above.
They may return and testify, etc. This applies when they were not joined together in seeing the event, as I write below in siman 45, seif 13, seif katan 24. Responsa of the Rosh there states this explicitly. According to this, there is no distinction between a written document and testimony in beit din, contrary to Ir Shushan and the Sema, seif katan 7, who distinguish them. See there.
And beit din, etc. Specifically beit din may take the stated action, but the witnesses themselves may not. See what I write below, siman 45, seif katan 25, and see there for additional rules relevant here.
An enactment of the community, etc.; an invalid witness, etc. The Rama's words require explanation. One who studies Rivash, siman 195 and end of siman 168, sees explicitly that with respect to a communal enactment even if all the signers would ordinarily be disqualified, their testimony is accepted because established communal practice has relied upon them. Otherwise, every community would need to bring outside witnesses to prove its own enactments. Rivash additionally validates the particular case before him for another reason: even an ordinary document signed by a litigant himself remains valid with respect to the other signers. The Rama therefore must mean that absent an established custom, a signer is "invalid" because he is himself a member of the community, analogous to a document signed by a litigant. With respect to the prevalent custom, the Rama relies on his unqualified ruling below, siman 37, seif 22, following the Mechaber, that nowadays communities commonly accept members of the community as witnesses regarding their enactments and agreements, and such witnesses are valid even for their relatives. See there carefully.
For if they did not intend to testify. This language is difficult. Even if the person did intend to testify, he does not invalidate the others when he is himself the litigant. Rivash there explicitly says that a litigant does not invalidate the testimony because he is entirely unfit to be a witness in his own case. This accords with the Gemara's answer to the question, "Should the victim save him?" The verse concerns those who establish the matter, not those who perform the act. Rivash further writes there that if a person with a direct financial interest signed the document, he is treated like a litigant and does not invalidate the other witnesses. See there.
And if the claimant designated valid witnesses, etc. Beit Yosef at the end of this siman cites his teacher Mahari Beirav that even if a person summons witnesses indiscriminately, we say that his intent was only to designate those legally fit to testify. The Tur's phrase that "because he designated his witnesses and excluded the others" is not meant to require express exclusion, Mahari Beirav says, because the Tur later writes that when a person needs testimony it is a mitzvah to impose a cherem upon whoever knows it, and such a general summons likewise does not allow invalid persons to spoil valid testimony. Rabbi Shlomo Chacham comments that, with respect, this is not the Tur's meaning. The Tur's first case concerns designation at the time of the event, to observe it for purposes of testimony, and in that setting exclusion of the others is required. This is clear and precise in the Rosh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Nimukei Yosef. In Bedek HaBayit, Beit Yosef later quotes a response, found in Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 66, asking who actually disagreed with Mahari Beirav. Mahari Beirav had cited the Tur without explicitly interpreting every word, and from the rule about imposing a cherem afterward he proved that a mixed summons can be understood as directed only to valid witnesses. Mabit criticizes Beit Yosef for citing Rabbi Shlomo Chacham against his teacher and leaving the matter unresolved. Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit thus retracted and accepted Mabit's defense of Mahari Beirav. In my humble opinion, however, Rabbi Shlomo Chacham is correct. When Mahari Beirav says the Tur's words "and excluded the others" are not literal, that cannot be the proper reading. The Tur is speaking about designating witnesses at the time of the event. In that setting he specifically must exclude the others. If relatives and valid persons are all standing before him and he says indiscriminately, "I designate you as witnesses," his words naturally include everyone present. If he intended only the valid persons, he should identify them by name and say, "I designate you, so and so and so and so, as witnesses." His failure to specify indicates an intent to include all. This differs from a later cherem upon whoever knows testimony. The claimant cannot name the witnesses because he does not know who possesses relevant information, and he presumptively directs his demand to those legally fit to testify. He does not have the relatives before him as a known witness group, and may not even know that relatives were present at the event. The Rosh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Nimukei Yosef all carefully write that he "designated his witnesses and excluded the others," which confirms that express exclusion is required when the designation occurs at the event. This is what Rabbi Shlomo Chacham meant by saying that the point is obvious and exact in their language. The Sema, seif katan 9, also holds that an indiscriminate designation at the time of the event is ineffective, contrary to Darkei Moshe. Darkei Moshe's attempt to explain Rabbeinu Yerucham otherwise does not appear correct. It is well known that copies of Rabbeinu Yerucham contain many textual errors. There is little doubt that the phrase about mixed designation was misplaced in the manuscripts and belongs to the later case of a general cherem, for his words are drawn from the Rosh. Maharam Alashkar, Responsa siman 16, page 26a, writes likewise. This is primary. It further appears that even if he says at the time of the event, "Two of you shall testify for me," this is ineffective. Since he did not identify which two, the phrase "two of you" can include relatives or other invalid persons. If he meant only valid witnesses he should have said, "Two valid persons among you," or, "So and so and so and so shall testify for me." The Sema, seif katan 9, says that a statement of this sort is effective, and Beit Yosef at the beginning of this siman cites Rabbi Shlomo Chacham to that effect, based on their interpretation of Rabbeinu Yerucham. But I have explained that Rabbeinu Yerucham does not mean this. Therefore, the rule appears as I have written.
Likewise, when a person, etc. The statement that the valid testimony is not invalidated cannot mean that it is known that the disqualified persons intended to testify at the time of observation. If so, it would be unreasonable to preserve the valid testimony merely because the person who later issued the general summons intended only legally qualified witnesses. Whatever his later intention, all the witnesses had already joined at the time they saw the event and later testified. Moreover, according to Rambam, whose view the Mechaber cites first above in seif 1 and which I upheld in seif katan 6, the testimony would already have been invalidated at the time of observation even if the invalid witnesses never testified. The Mechaber would then have had to say that the present rule follows only the dissenting opinion. Nor can the case mean that the invalid persons originally intended not to testify. If so, why rely on the later summoner's intent to call only fit witnesses? Even if his summons included everyone, the invalid persons would not spoil the testimony because they had not originally intended to testify, as I wrote above, seif katan 10. Rather, the case concerns ordinary people who witnessed an event without any specific indication of intent. Ordinarily we presume that relatives and disqualified persons did not intend to testify, and they need not be questioned unless they later come to beit din on their own, as I wrote above, seif katan 8. The Rosh's question from gittin and kiddushin can still be understood as asking what happens if those relatives later affirmatively say that they did intend to testify. In the present case, however, those who came to beit din did not come on their own but only because of the public proclamation. One might have thought that the person who ordered the proclamation must have known that they originally intended to testify, for otherwise he should not have summoned everyone without qualification. The rule therefore teaches that his proclamation is presumed to have been directed only to those legally fit to testify. The testimony is not invalidated merely because disqualified persons respond. There is no need even to question them about their original intent, and if they died or went overseas the valid testimony remains. If, however, the disqualified persons expressly state that they intended at the time of observation to testify, then even if the person who issued the later proclamation says that he meant only valid witnesses, once the invalid persons also testify the entire testimony is void according to all authorities. This appears correct. It differs from the implication of Mahari Beirav as cited by Maharam Alashkar, siman 16, page 25b, that even persons who originally intended to testify do not invalidate if they came only because of the proclamation.
