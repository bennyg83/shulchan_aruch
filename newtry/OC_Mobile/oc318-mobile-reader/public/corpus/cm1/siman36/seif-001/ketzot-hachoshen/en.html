When you saw it. The Shach concludes that where the qualified witnesses also did not specifically intend to testify, the qualified and disqualified observers stand equally together. If both intended to testify, or neither did, and a relative or otherwise disqualified person was present, the whole testimony is void. Only where the qualified observers intended to testify and the disqualified ones did not can the qualified testimony survive. Therefore one arranging kiddushin should specifically designate two qualified witnesses at the ceremony and ensure that they intend to testify. Otherwise relatives and disqualified people standing among the assembled crowd may create serious concern. Ritva in Kiddushin writes that in kiddushin, where witnesses are required not merely to prove a fact later but to give the act its legal existence, the danger is even greater. If qualified and disqualified persons are jointly present, their combined witnessing can invalidate the whole matter unless witnesses are designated. Ritva notes that not all commentators agree, but recommends stringency in practice. This is especially important because sometimes the officiant and attendants are relatives, while the general crowd stands at a distance amid noise and does not clearly see or hear the act of kiddushin. Then there may effectively be no valid witnessing at all. Some authorities also questioned whether attendants are disqualified because of special friendship. Therefore two qualified witnesses should be expressly appointed and should hear the groom's declaration of kiddushin. Semak already records that the accepted custom is to designate witnesses at kiddushin, and one should be careful about it.
Who intended to testify. The Tur cites Ramah: the rule that one who came only to observe does not invalidate applies specifically where the witnesses did not testify together within the brief interval counted as one continuous statement. But if they testified together within that interval, even if they say they had not originally come to observe as witnesses and even if they did not see one another, the presence of a relative or disqualified witness nullifies the testimony because the verse's primary formulation concerns the time of testimony. This is difficult from the Mishnah in Makkot. Two witnesses see from one window and two from another, without seeing one another. These are treated as two separate testimonies even though Rava establishes the Mishnah as a case where all testified within the same brief interval. Thus joint testimony in court does not automatically merge people who did not see together. Bach, discussing Tosafot and Rosh, goes even further and says that if a person intended to testify at the time of seeing and later did testify, the presence of a disqualified witness can nullify even if they testified after the brief interval and did not see together. The Shach already objects strongly. In my view, however, the requirement of joint viewing and joint testimony is specific to capital law. Capital testimony cannot combine unless the witnesses saw together and testified together. Therefore the verse equating three witnesses to two for the rule that one disqualified person nullifies the group necessarily operates there only when the witnesses form one valid capital group through joint seeing and joint testimony. Monetary law is different. We rule that witnesses can combine even if they did not see together and did not testify together. Consequently, their combination for disqualification can likewise be broader. There is therefore no proof from the Makkot Mishnah, which concerns capital law. Rashi in Makkot strongly supports this. Rav Nachman says special testimony is valid in monetary law, and the Gemara asks: if so, should it not also "save" in capital law by making all the observers one group when a disqualified witness is present? Rashi explains that because we see that in monetary law even witnesses who do not see one another are treated as one testimony, perhaps in capital law they should at least combine to invalidate, under the mandate to save the accused. This assumes as obvious that in monetary law special testimony can combine also for the rule of a relative or disqualified witness. Ritva cites Raavad, who explicitly reads Rav Nachman's novelty this way: special testimony is valid in monetary cases when all are qualified, but if one is related or disqualified the entire testimony is void because the witnesses still concern one loan. Ritva rejects this as making monetary law stricter than capital law, but I favor Raavad. Its leniency is its stringency: wherever qualified witnesses can combine to impose liability, they can also combine to invalidate when one is disqualified. This also explains another statement in Makkot concerning the biblical source that special testimony is invalid in capital cases. It is not an unrelated teaching, as Tosafot suggests. The Mishnah teaches that in capital cases those who did not see together are separate testimonies. Rav Zutra then supplies the biblical source for that very separation. By contrast, monetary law validates special testimony, and according to Raavad it likewise combines for the rule of relative or disqualified witnesses. This may explain why Shulchan Aruch and Rama do not distinguish throughout this siman between witnesses who saw through one window and those who did not, or between testimony within and beyond the brief interval. Perhaps they follow this broader monetary approach. There remains a difficulty from Sanhedrin 23 concerning two groups of witnesses. Rabbi Meir says one must clarify both groups, while the Sages disagree. If the litigant succeeds in disqualifying one group, should not the other group also be nullified under the rule of a relative or disqualified witness, since both testified to the same loan even though they did not see and testify together? One must establish the case where the group that he seeks to disqualify had not intended to testify in the relevant sense. But then the later question, what if the second group is found related or disqualified, becomes difficult. If they intended to testify, the first group should already be invalid. If they did not, then according to the Shach, where the qualified witnesses also did not intend, the problem still arises. This point requires further resolution.
Their testimony is void. Rambam rules that where a testimony consists of three or even one hundred witnesses, if they testified within one brief interval and some are later shown to be plotting witnesses, no punishment is imposed until all are refuted. But if more than that interval separated their testimony, the groups are divided. Those refuted are punished, while the others are not. Raavad objects: what punishment would ever have been appropriate for the others, and why should their testimony be nullified? The murderer should be executed on the basis of the witnesses not refuted. The Shach says Rambam himself would surely agree with Raavad. A later work, Imrei Tzerufah, tries to defend Rambam by saying that Rambam requires the groups to see one another. If two witnesses are refuted by testimony that they were elsewhere, the remaining witnesses are thereby contradicted concerning having seen them and therefore lose credibility, though they are not punished because only hazamah creates punishment. This cannot be correct. A witness who says, "I saw the event and so and so was with me," is not contradicted merely because that person says he was not there. A witness does not necessarily pay close attention to who stood beside him. Shulchan Aruch states this in simanim 29 and 30. More fundamentally, Ritva already asks how some observers can be refuted when another window saw them. He answers that the Mishnah says only some of the groups saw one another, or they saw generally before the event without enough identification to contradict the plotting witnesses. Thus the case must be structured so that no contradiction arises between the remaining witnesses and the hazamah witnesses. If there were true contradiction, even the plotted witnesses themselves could not be punished because the court could not prefer one group over the other. Tumim offers the same defense as Imrei Tzerufah, but for the reasons stated it does not stand. The Raavad's objection therefore remains strong against that reading of Rambam.
Both are required. I was uncertain about a case where one witness among a group is related or disqualified, but he was qualified at the time of seeing and became disqualified only by the time of testimony. According to Ramah, who says that even where they did not combine at the time of seeing, joint testimony can nullify the whole group because the verse principally concerns testimony, the entire testimony should be void. According to the authorities requiring both joint seeing and joint testimony, perhaps not, because at the time of seeing he was still qualified. The reverse case is less doubtful. If he was disqualified when he saw the event and became qualified before testimony, his testimony itself remains invalid because testimony that begins in disqualification and ends in qualification is invalid. Therefore at the time of testimony he is still legally disqualified. There is proof that even according to the view requiring both seeing and testimony, a witness who was qualified when he saw but disqualified at testimony can nullify the group. Bava Batra and Sanhedrin discuss two witnesses where one says one hundred and the other two hundred. Beit Shammai treats the witnesses as contradicting one another. Tosafot asks that if one of them is then treated as a false or disqualified witness, should not the entire pair be void under the rule that one relative or disqualified witness nullifies the group? Tosafot offers several answers. If a person who was qualified at the time of seeing could never nullify the group merely because he became false or disqualified only through his later testimony, Tosafot could have answered simply that he had been qualified when he saw the event. Tosafot does not do so, even though Tosafot in Makkot requires both seeing and testimony. This shows that where the witnesses saw together and later testify together, the fact that one was still qualified at the first moment does not save the group. The reason is that the testimony was never divided at the time of seeing. The rule that both stages are required means only that if the witnesses did not combine at the time of seeing, later joint testimony cannot recreate a single group. But where they did see together, and later one becomes disqualified before the joint testimony, the unified group persists and is now contaminated by the disqualified witness. This also explains why plotting witnesses require both joint seeing and joint testimony for the special rule. At the moment of seeing, before hazamah, all are still qualified. If they later testify outside the brief interval, the unified group is divided before the disqualification arises. If they testify together, the disqualification produced through hazamah attaches to the still unified group. This gives a better account than the argument that plotting witnesses could never have combined at seeing because their account was false. The Mishnah itself distinguishes whether they saw from one window or separate windows, so even in hazamah the witnesses' asserted joint observation matters for the structure of the testimony. Bach's defense of Rambam is therefore conceptually correct: the plotted witnesses can have a claimed joint seeing for purposes of whether the testimony is one group, even though hazamah later proves that their account was false. Nevertheless, according to Rif's view that the qualified witness must have known of the other's disqualification at the time, a witness who was qualified when they saw the event cannot nullify the group if no one then knew him to be disqualified, because the relevant recognition of the defect did not yet exist.

He needs the testimony. The Shach says one should not explain the case as one where the disqualified observers themselves intended to testify at the time of seeing, because then designating other witnesses later should not save the qualified evidence. In his view, if the disqualified observers intended to testify and later also testified, everyone agrees the whole testimony is void. This is contrary to Mahari bei Rav, cited by Maharam Alshakar, who says that even if the disqualified people intended to testify when they saw the event, if they later testified only because of a general public announcement, they do not nullify the designated witnesses. I do not understand the Shach. The Rosh explicitly supports Mahari bei Rav. Rosh writes that if a lender designated qualified witnesses while relatives or disqualified persons stood there, then even if those disqualified people intended to testify and later did testify in beit din, the qualified testimony is not nullified, because once the lender designated his own witnesses the others cannot destroy his rights. Rosh then adds that when a person needs testimony and announces that anyone who knows testimony should come, and qualified and disqualified witnesses arrive mixed together, his teachers ruled that the qualified testimony is not void because his intent was obviously directed only to persons legally fit to testify. Thus the operative idea is not the Shach's factual intent of the disqualified observers, but the litigant's designation of the relevant witnesses. According to the Rosh, who requires both seeing and testimony, if the litigant designates witnesses at the testimony stage, the disqualified observers cannot force themselves into the legal group. According to Rambam, who allows invalidation already through the moment of seeing, designation must occur then to prevent their inclusion at that stage. Mahari bei Rav is therefore correct. Darkei Moshe likewise writes that even if one says at the event, "Whoever knows anything must later come and testify," his intent is naturally limited to qualified witnesses. This also solves the famous difficulty regarding gittin and kiddushin, where relatives and disqualified people are often present. Kiddushin require witnesses not merely to prove the event later, but to create the legal act. A betrothal without witnesses is ineffective even if both parties admit it, and hidden witnesses whom the parties do not recognize are insufficient because the act must be done in the presence of witnesses. Since the man and woman must direct the act toward witnesses, their intention is necessarily to act before legally qualified witnesses. This is equivalent to designating qualified witnesses at the event. Therefore disqualified onlookers cannot nullify the kiddushin. The same framework explains why explicit designation of two qualified witnesses is the safest practical procedure.
