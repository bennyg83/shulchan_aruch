Many witnesses. The verse states, "By the mouth of two witnesses or by the mouth of three witnesses," comparing three to two. Just as where one of two is found to be a relative or disqualified the testimony is void, so too with three, and even with one hundred, because the verse calls them all "witnesses," Gemara:
Their testimony is void. This applies where they saw the event together and therefore formed one evidentiary group. If they did not see it together, the testimony remains valid, Shach. See Beurim, seif katan 1, that according to this view even if the witness became disqualified between seeing the event and testifying, the testimony is invalidated. According to the Rif's opinion in seif 2, however, where the qualified witnesses did not know of the disqualification, the testimony remains valid. See further in Beurim that a woman, a minor, and a non Jew do not have the status of a relative or disqualified witness for this particular rule:
We ask them. This implies that only the disqualified witnesses are questioned about whether they intended to testify, Sema. The Shach writes that the qualified witnesses are also questioned. If not all of them intended to testify, the law of "one found relative or disqualified" may not apply. See Beurim, seif katan 1, that even the Shach agrees that if all intended to testify, the entire testimony is void even if the disqualified witnesses never actually testified in beit din. But if not all intended to testify, the testimony is not invalidated unless the disqualified witnesses actually testified together with the qualified ones. If the qualified witnesses testified and the disqualified witnesses did not, the testimony is valid even according to the Shach. As a practical ruling, where not all intended to testify, one should invalidate the testimony only if the qualified and disqualified witnesses testified within the same immediate span. The inquiry concerning intent is specifically relevant where one witness is a relative or otherwise disqualified. If all are qualified and some are later contradicted or subject to hazamah, and they all saw the event together and testified within the immediate span, we do not ask about intent. We presume that all came to testify, and the entire testimony is affected. See Beurim, seif katan 1. Where a disqualified person and qualified witnesses were present, but the qualified witnesses intended to testify and the disqualified person did not, the testimony remains valid even if all testified within the immediate span, because their participation was not equivalent, Shach, unlike the Sema:
I came to testify. According to this view, even though mere joint observation can create the disqualifying combination without actual testimony in beit din, the disqualified person must at least have come to beit din. If he never came to beit din, we presume that he attended merely to watch, unless at the time of the event he clearly indicated an intention to testify, for example by warning the offender. See Beurim, seif katan 2. This applies where qualified witnesses know that the disqualified persons were present at the event. Then we presume that they came to testify unless they say that they came only to watch. But if it is not independently known that the relatives were present at the event, those relatives are not believed merely to say that they were there so as to invalidate the qualified testimony. See Beurim, seif katan 3, that if even one qualified witness from the group testifying to the loan says that he saw the disqualified witnesses present at the event, the testimony of the entire group can thereby be invalidated, even if there were many qualified witnesses:
If one of these is found. If the disqualified witnesses were themselves subjected to hazamah, proving that they were not present at the original event, the qualified testimony is not invalidated. See Beurim, seif katan 4. Necessarily, the case is one in which one qualified witness who saw the loan testified that he also saw the disqualified witnesses present, and he is believed to invalidate the entire group as explained above. Therefore, if the disqualified witnesses are later shown by hazamah not to have been present, the qualified testimony remains. But if two qualified witnesses testify that the disqualified persons were present, then even if the disqualified witnesses are later subjected to hazamah, money cannot be extracted from the defendant. Their evidence is stronger than an ordinary case of two against two, and even seizure does not help. If it was never established that the disqualified witnesses were present, then even without hazamah the qualified testimony is not invalidated. See Beurim:
Even if they did not know. This is according to the second opinion. According to the first opinion, the qualified witness must have known of the other witness's disqualification. The Shach writes that the statement applies according to all views in the sense that not every qualified witness must know. It is enough that one qualified witness saw and knew of the disqualified person, because we require only that some members of the group see one another. This is the primary rule. See the responsum cited at the end of the siman in Beurim, where it is concluded that with judges, witnesses to a get and kiddushin, and witnesses to a will, if three qualified persons were present together with one relative or disqualified person, the three qualified participants may still write and act judicially on the basis of what they themselves saw, and they are believed even through their written record:
They remember the testimony. See siman 45 at length:
And beit din shall write. They may write the judicial act, but not restate the testimony itself, because they have already completed their agency with respect to the testimony. See siman 45:
A disqualified witness. This means one resident of the city who is financially interested in the matter. According to the enactment cited by the Rama at the end of siman 37, even if all are residents of the city they are qualified, Shach:
For if they did not intend. The wording is imprecise. Even if they did intend to testify, a witness disqualified because of personal financial interest is not included in the rule of a relative or disqualified witness, because an interested person has the status of a litigant rather than a witness. The principle that partial invalidity of testimony invalidates the whole therefore does not apply, Shach:
He designated. If he did not designate the witnesses by name but merely said, "Two of you," his intent is understood as referring to the qualified witnesses. But if he designated a specific group and said, "You are my witnesses," the presence of a disqualified person invalidates the group. This is unlike a general proclamation under ban, which occurs at the time testimony is given, whereas designation occurs at the time of observation:
Even if they intended to testify. The Shach holds that people who accompany the event and then leave are ordinarily presumed not to have intended to testify. Nevertheless, it is proper to designate witnesses explicitly at kiddushin:
When a person needs testimony and there is a mitzvah, and so forth. Even according to the Mechaber, who holds that joint observation alone can create the disqualifying group when there was intent to testify, coming to beit din is still required.
