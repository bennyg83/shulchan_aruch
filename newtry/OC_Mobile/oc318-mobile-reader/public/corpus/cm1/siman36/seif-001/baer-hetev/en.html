Who intended. See the Shach's lengthy discussion of this law. He also cites Rabbeinu Peretz that in gittin and kiddushin specific witnesses must be designated, because otherwise relatives and disqualified persons commonly stand nearby and their participation could invalidate the testimony. This is the accepted practice. See there.
They say. The Sema writes that Rashi and the Tur imply that the court asks only the relatives and disqualified persons whether they intended to testify. Accordingly, so long as it has not become known that a relative or disqualified person was among those present, no inquiry is necessary at all. This appears primary, because we do not ordinarily suspect witnesses of being related or disqualified until such a fact is discovered.
Is void. The Sema explains that the testimony is void even if the disqualified person never actually testified. The Tur writes that Ramah adds the converse as well: if the disqualified person ultimately testified with them, the testimony is void even if at first he came only to observe, because the principal Scriptural verse concerns the moment of testimony. The Shach disagrees and discusses the matter at length.
Even. The Sema writes that Tosafot imply the witnesses are disqualified even if they did not know of one another. He questions how Rama knows that the Mechaber, who follows Rambam here, holds this way. Perhaps the Mechaber agrees with the Rif that if the valid witnesses were unaware of the disqualified witness, their testimony is not void. The Sema therefore suggests that Rama's gloss should be moved lower and read: if he came to beit din and testified, the testimony is void even if they did not know of one another, because both elements are then present. He adds that perhaps Rama can be read as meaning only that the disqualified witness did not know of the valid witness. The novelty would be that even though the disqualified person did not intend to join his testimony with that of the valid person, he nevertheless invalidates the group. The valid witness, however, knew of the disqualified person, and in that circumstance even the Rif agrees the testimony is void. The Shach rejects this explanation and interprets Rama differently: if they later testified in beit din, their testimony is void according to all opinions even if they did not know of one another at that later stage. Even the Rif's requirement that the valid witnesses know of the disqualified witnesses means only that they knew those persons were disqualified. It is unnecessary for them to know that the disqualified witnesses would later testify in beit din. Once they originally joined at the time of seeing the event, that is enough. See there.
And some say. Their reason is that otherwise testimony could almost always be defeated, since a relative could insert himself at the beginning by intending to testify, or at the end by coming to testify. It is uncommon, however, that he would do both. The dissenters apparently hold that this concern proves nothing, because a litigant can designate valid witnesses and then the disqualified person's intent will not affect them. Thus the Sema.
To return. This rule applies specifically to valid witnesses who signed a document together with disqualified persons. They may later return and testify. But once valid and disqualified witnesses actually testified together in beit din, the valid witnesses may not return and testify anew. Sema.
Intended. This wording is difficult, because even if the litigant himself intended to testify he does not invalidate the testimony, since he is a party to the case. The Rivash writes that if an interested party signed the document, he is treated as a litigant and does not invalidate the other testimony. Thus the Shach.
Designated. Even if he did not designate the valid witnesses by name, but at the time of the event said to a group, "Two of you shall be witnesses to this matter," we assume he intended the valid witnesses. But if at the time of the event he said, "Anyone who knows testimony for me must come and testify," this wording does not distinguish valid witnesses from disqualified ones and is ineffective. This differs from placing a cherem after the event upon anyone who knows testimony, because then he is not addressing any particular person directly. Darkei Moshe rules that even such a general designation at the time of the event is effective. The Sema states that he has written what appears correct to him, and the Shach agrees with the Sema rather than Darkei Moshe. See the Taz for his distinction.
Among those fit. This appears to concern ordinary testimony, where people happened to observe an event. Presumably relatives and disqualified persons did not intend to testify, and there is no need to question them unless they later come to beit din on their own, as explained above. Shach.
