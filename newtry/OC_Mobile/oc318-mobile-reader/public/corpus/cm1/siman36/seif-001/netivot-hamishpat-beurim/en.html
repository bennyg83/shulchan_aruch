See there. At first glance this is difficult. If so, in the Mishnah in Makkot 6, where Rabbi says, "When does this apply? When they warned the offender, but if they did not warn him," and so forth, one could find a case where the testimony is nullified even though the disqualified witnesses did not give the warning, namely where the qualified witnesses also did not warn, since warning can in any event be given even by the offender himself. According to Shach, the Mishnah should have said, "But if the disqualified witnesses did not warn him and the qualified witnesses did." Likewise, the Mechaber writes, "When does this apply? When they intended," and so forth. According to Shach he should have written, "When does this apply? When all intended to testify, or when none intended to testify, but if the qualified witnesses intended and the disqualified did not," and so forth. Moreover, every day testimony is accepted from qualified witnesses who say they saw the loan but did not intend at the time to become witnesses, even though they say that all members of the household were present, and I have never seen such testimony invalidated on that account. It therefore appears that if the disqualified persons never testified in beit din and only the qualified witnesses testified, the testimony of the qualified witnesses is not nullified, even if all saw the event together and none of them intended to testify. Shach invalidates the qualified testimony only where both the qualified and the disqualified witnesses later testified in beit din, they saw the event together, and none originally intended to testify. The reason appears from Tosafot in Sanhedrin 9, in the passage beginning "when they warned." Rashbam holds that immediately upon seeing an event with intent to testify, a person acquires the status of a witness, both with respect to a relative or disqualified person joining and thereby nullifying the testimony of the qualified witnesses, and with respect to the rule that a witness cannot become a judge. Ri, by contrast, holds that a person does not acquire the status of witness at the time of seeing, even if he intended to testify. This is so both regarding a relative or disqualified person, who does not nullify the qualified testimony merely at the time of observation, and regarding the rule that a person may still become a judge so long as he has not actually testified. According to Ri, he becomes a witness only when he testifies. See there. The Mechaber, with respect to a relative or disqualified witness, follows Rashbam. When a person sees the event intending to testify, he is already called a witness and can nullify the testimony of the qualified witnesses, because once they form one body of testimony at the moment of observation, if part of that testimony becomes void the whole is void. Shach therefore correctly learns from Ri's view to Rashbam's. According to Ri, even if all intended to testify, no one yet has the formal status of witness at the time of observation, because we do not follow intention alone, as Ritva writes. Nevertheless, if the qualified and disqualified persons later all testify, Ri holds that once the testimony of the disqualified persons is nullified, the entire combined testimony is nullified. Although at the beginning of the combination, during observation, none yet had formal witness status, their later joint testimony reveals retroactively that they constituted one combined group. So too according to Rashbam, if none intended to testify at the time of observation but all later testify in beit din, then at the beginning no one had witness status, but when they all testify they acquire it. When part of the combined testimony is then void because some witnesses are disqualified, the entire combination is void. For Rashbam, failure to intend at the time of observation corresponds to Ri's case even where they did intend, since under either formulation there was not yet formal witness status at the moment of seeing. If, however, only the qualified persons testify and the disqualified persons never testify at all, the qualified persons acquire witness status through their testimony, while the disqualified persons never acquire witness status because they neither intended at the time of observation nor later testified. In that situation the rule that partial nullification voids the entire testimony does not apply, because the disqualified persons were never part of the body of testimony. Likewise, where the qualified persons intended to testify at the time of seeing and the disqualified persons did not, they were not one combined testimonial group at the outset because their statuses differed from the beginning. Even according to Ri, who holds that intent alone does not yet make anyone a formal witness, if the qualified intended and the disqualified did not, the testimony is not nullified, even if they later testify within the period of immediate speech. The authorities do not distinguish in the law of a relative or disqualified witness based upon whether the later testimony was within immediate speech. Rather, the reason is that there was no true combined body from the beginning where the qualified intended and the disqualified did not. Nevertheless, it appears that even according to Sema, who disagrees with Shach and holds that where none intended to testify the qualified testimony is not nullified merely because disqualified persons were present, if they later all testify within immediate speech and say while testifying that they saw one another, Sema would agree that the qualified testimony is nullified. Proof can be brought from a case where some witnesses are later shown to be plotting witnesses, in which case all are nullified. With plotting witnesses, the Gemara does not say that we ask whether they originally came merely to observe or to testify, even though plotting witnesses too must have formed one group in observation, as Makkot 6 teaches that if some did not see the others they constitute separate testimonies. Nevertheless, we do not ask whether they originally came merely to observe. The reason is that plotting witnesses must testify within immediate speech of one another, and once they combine in the act of testimony, nullification of part of the group nullifies the whole. The argument is all the stronger here. In the case of plotting witnesses, according to Tosafot, there was in truth no operative combination at the time of observation, and nevertheless combination through testimony within immediate speech can cause the whole testimony to be nullified. Where a relative or disqualified witness was also present at the same observation, all the more so should later testimony within immediate speech nullify the entire group, even though none had initially intended to testify. One could distinguish that with a relative or disqualified person, because he knows he is not fit to testify, logic suggests that he ordinarily came only to observe, and therefore we question him. With plotting witnesses all are outwardly qualified, so we assume they came to testify and do not ask. The proof is that we do not ask out of concern that the qualified witnesses might say they intended to testify while those later shown to be plotting might say they did not. Were that so, the testimony of those not shown to be plotting would remain valid, because plotting witnesses are no worse for this purpose than relatives or disqualified witnesses, as Tosafot repeatedly compare the two laws. Rather, with initially qualified witnesses we presume that all intended to testify. Still, as a matter of reasoning, where none intended to testify but they later all testified within immediate speech, since they had equal status at the beginning of the combination during observation and then combined in formal testimony within immediate speech, they are no weaker than a group where some later became plotting witnesses, in which case the entire testimony is nullified even though Tosafot hold there was no effective combination at observation. This explains the language of the Mishnah and the Mechaber. They speak only of combination at the stage of observation without later testimony by the disqualified persons. The Mishnah therefore says, "but if they did not warn," because if the disqualified persons did not warn, even where the qualified persons likewise did not warn, the qualified testimony is not nullified so long as the disqualified persons never testified in beit din. Likewise, the Mechaber speaks before the disqualified persons have testified, and therefore states only that if the disqualified persons did not intend to testify, the qualified testimony is not nullified. When the disqualified persons later testify as well, Shach holds that if none intended originally, the whole testimony is void. Nevertheless, the principal ruling appears to follow Sema, that where none intended to testify the qualified testimony is not nullified merely because the disqualified persons were present, unless they later testified within immediate speech. If at the time of observation a person was qualified, but by the time of testimony he became disqualified through a marital relationship or some other disqualification, the entire testimony is certainly nullified. Tumim proves this, followed by Ketzot HaChoshen, from Tosafot's discussion of one witness saying one hundred and another saying two hundred, where the testimony can be nullified as though one of the witnesses were found to be a relative or disqualified person, even though at the original observation no such later disqualification yet existed. This is also evident in many places among the authorities. Ketzot HaChoshen writes, however, that where the person was qualified at the outset and became disqualified only later, there must also be combination at the time of testimony. This does not appear correct to me. According to Rambam and Rosh, even if the witnesses did not testify together at all, once the originally combined group was later partially invalidated, the whole linkage and combination is nullified under the Scriptural rule that when part of a testimony is nullified the whole is nullified. Tosafot in Bava Batra, in the case where one witness says one hundred and another says two hundred, do not distinguish generally based upon simultaneous testimony. Only in one proposed answer do they write that perhaps the rule of a relative or disqualified witness applies only where they testified together. That itself implies that ordinarily the law is not limited to simultaneous testimony. According to Tosafot's broader approach, the other answers there are needed, and all indicate that even in this kind of later disqualification the testimony is void. Likewise, where one witness says during the examinations, "I do not know," Tosafot write that the entire testimony is invalid as though one witness had been found related or disqualified, even though the defect arises only at the stage of testimony. That case, however, does not by itself prove the point because the witnesses may have testified together. According to Rif's opinion in seif 2, which requires the qualified witness to know of the other person's disqualification, the testimony would certainly remain valid in this type of case, as explained above.

Shach further writes there, in the passage beginning "and it follows from all this," that if the disqualified witnesses were afterward shown to be plotting witnesses, then because their statements are false they do not nullify the qualified testimony. At first glance this is difficult. Shach himself writes in seif katan 5 that a relative or disqualified person is not believed merely to say that he was present unless it is otherwise known that he was there. That knowledge must necessarily come through qualified witnesses. If there are no qualified witnesses establishing that the disqualified persons were present, then even before any contradiction those persons do not nullify the qualified testimony. If there are qualified witnesses establishing their presence, or if the qualified witnesses in this very case say that they saw the disqualified persons there, then testimony purporting to show those persons were elsewhere would seem to create two opposing pairs of witnesses. One group says the disqualified persons were at this location, while the plotting witnesses say they were elsewhere. In an ordinary case of two against two, money cannot be removed from the defendant's possession. Furthermore, here the qualified witnesses themselves should be believed when they say that they saw the disqualified persons more than the later witnesses who seek to establish the contrary, because in the very testimony by which they say that Reuven borrowed one hundred from Shimon, they also said within immediate speech that they saw the disqualified persons present during the event. After immediate speech they would no longer be believed to nullify their own testimony, but within immediate speech they can certainly retract or qualify it, just as witnesses who say, "This is our handwriting, but we were compelled," are believed because of the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. Here too, had they wished they could have refrained entirely from saying that they saw the loan. When they instead say they saw the loan but that disqualified persons were also present, thereby nullifying their own testimony, they should be believed because they themselves supplied the testimony that would otherwise establish liability. It therefore appears that Shach's case is one where only one of the qualified witnesses who testified to the loan says that he saw the disqualified persons. That witness is believed to nullify his own testimony. Once the testimony of one qualified witness is nullified, the whole testimony is nullified, no weaker than a case where one witness says during the examinations, "I do not know," where Tosafot say the whole testimony is void because it resembles a case where one witness was found related or disqualified. Likewise, if the qualified witness says within immediate speech of the examinations that he saw disqualified persons, he is believed to nullify the entire testimony through a migo that he could have said, "I do not know" in the examinations, which according to Shach in siman 33, seif katan 16, would nullify the whole testimony, or he could have contradicted the other witnesses in the examinations and thereby nullified the testimony. So too, his statement that he saw disqualified persons can nullify the whole through that same migo. If, however, qualified witnesses later establish that the allegedly disqualified persons were elsewhere, then although the formal law of plotting witnesses does not apply in exactly the same manner to disqualified persons, the later qualified witnesses are certainly more credible than the disqualified persons, and two qualified witnesses are more credible than the one qualified witness who said he saw them. Once the later witnesses are believed that the disqualified persons were not there, the testimony of the qualified witnesses remains intact. Although the one qualified witness said something false in beit din when he claimed that the disqualified persons were present, he does not become disqualified merely for that mistaken statement, because, as explained at the end of siman 29 in the gloss, a witness does not necessarily scrutinize precisely who was present with him. His substantive testimony therefore remains valid. This appears to me the clear explanation of Shach. Even if they did not know of one another. See Shach, seif katan 7, through his statement that even if they did not know of one another, once some saw the others, and so forth. At first glance his words are difficult. Those witnesses who neither saw nor knew that qualified witnesses saw the others are analogous to qualified witnesses who did not know of the disqualification of the disqualified witnesses. Why should such witnesses be invalidated? It therefore appears that Shach distinguishes as follows. In the ordinary case where one member is found to be a relative or otherwise disqualified, Rif holds that because the disqualified person never truly had legal witness status, one cannot simply say that part of a testimony became void and therefore the whole is void. The qualified witness is invalidated only because he linked himself to disqualified persons. Therefore, if the qualified witnesses did not know of the others' disqualification, so that no blame attaches to their joining with them, their testimony is not nullified. Where all were initially qualified witnesses, however, and the testimony of one qualified witness is later nullified, the testimony of all is nullified under the direct rule that when part of a testimony is nullified the whole is nullified. The proof is that if one qualified witness says, "I do not know" during the examinations, all are invalidated, even though the other witnesses certainly committed no wrongdoing by joining themselves to a person who later said he did not know. Thus, according to Rif there is a distinction between a group containing someone who was never legally a witness because he was related or disqualified, and a group of qualified witnesses where one witness's testimony later becomes void. According to Tosafot, who hold that even in a case of a relative or disqualified person the qualified testimony is nullified despite lack of knowledge of the disqualification, there is no such distinction. Whether the testimony of the other person is nullified because he was a relative or disqualified person, or because some later defect arose in his otherwise qualified testimony, the principle that partial nullification voids the whole applies equally.

See Shach, seif katan 17, who asks that if they intended to testify, their testimony was already nullified from the moment of observation, while if they did not intend to testify there is no need for the rationale stated by the Mechaber. From this he derives that the case is one of ordinary presence without explicit intent. When a person ordinarily comes to beit din, we infer that he had intended to testify, whereas where he comes only in response to a public proclamation we do not. Tumim questions this because the Shulchan Aruch expressly says, "even if he intended to testify." I am surprised by Tumim's question. The phrase "even if he intended to testify" appears in the first clause, concerning a case where witnesses were designated at the time of the event. The latter clause, concerning one who ordered a public ban so that anyone with knowledge should come forward, concerns the later stage of testimony and can indeed speak of people who had not originally intended to testify. Nevertheless, Shach's inference is not necessary according to what I wrote above. If none intended to testify, their later testimony can still be nullified if they testify within immediate speech, because they then combine at the stage of formal testimony. The rationale stated by the Mechaber, that the party intended only persons fit to testify, remains necessary to prevent that later combination. When he designated only qualified witnesses, later formal testimony by others does not join them, just as the observation itself does not join them when qualified witnesses were specifically designated. Even so, Shach's practical ruling is correct as a matter of reasoning. Tumim's statement that one who comes only because of the public proclamation is not among those who establish the matter is strained and self defeating. Nor is there any difficulty from a rapist or killer who comes to beit din and might otherwise supposedly preserve testimony by his presence. A litigant or perpetrator is not within the legal category of witness, and those who perform the act are excluded from the category of those who establish it. There is also proof for Shach that even according to Rambam, in order for a relative or disqualified person to nullify the qualified testimony, he must ordinarily come to beit din on his own to testify. Otherwise one could find a case where relatives become liable for an oath concerning testimony, namely where someone administered an oath to relatives demanding that they testify whether they came merely to observe or to testify. If their answer could affect the validity of the qualified testimony and thereby affect money, their statement would have monetary effect. Yet the Mishnah in the chapter Shevuat HaEdut teaches that the oath concerning testimony does not apply to relatives. Rather, where they do not come to beit din on their own but only because of the oath, they do not nullify the qualified witnesses' testimony. This is correct.
