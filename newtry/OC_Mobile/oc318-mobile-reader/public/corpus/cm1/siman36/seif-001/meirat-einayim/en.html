Many witnesses, etc.; their testimony is void. This is derived from the verse, 'by the mouth of two witnesses or three witnesses.' If testimony is established by two, why did the Torah write three? To compare three to two: just as if one of two is found related or disqualified the testimony is void, so too with three, and likewise with a larger group.
Beit din asks them: when you saw it, etc. See the Perishah. This wording implies that Rambam and the Mechaber hold that whenever one group comes together before beit din to testify, even before it is known that one is related or disqualified, beit din asks all of them whether initially they came to see or to testify. The Gemara likewise appears this way. The reason would be to prevent fraud if a related or disqualified person is later found among them. Were beit din to wait until discovering such a person and then ask only him, he might intentionally say that he came to testify so as to invalidate testimony favorable to his relative, or the reverse. Rashi and the Tur, however, imply that only the relatives or disqualified persons are asked. Accordingly, until one is found among them there is no need to ask at all. In my opinion this is the more correct approach, for we do not suspect witnesses to be related or disqualified unless evidence of that appears.
Anyone who says. Rashba, Responsa siman 1189, writes that if at the moment of seeing they demonstrated that they intended to testify, such as by warning the offender, the entire testimony is disqualified because of them.
If one of these is found, etc., their testimony is void. Meaning, even if the related or disqualified person never actually testified. The Tur writes that Ramah adds the reverse: if he testified with them, even though at the time he saw the event he came only to observe, the testimony is void, because the main verse concerns the act of stating testimony in beit din. This is derived from 'by the mouth,' which refers to testimony.
Even if they did not know, etc. See Tosafot, Makkot 6a, s.v. 'Shmuel said the halacha follows Rabbi Yose.' They write that two conditions are required: the disqualified person intended to testify, and both he and the valid witness actually testify in beit din. They ask: if so, why does the Gemara say that if he came only to observe he does not disqualify? What would two brothers who happened to see a murder do, since there is no concern as long as they do not both testify? Tosafot answer that sometimes one does not know of the other's presence and testifies after his brother has already testified in beit din. It follows that disqualification can occur even when they did not know of each other. But it is difficult to know the source for attributing this to the Mechaber, who here follows Rambam. Perhaps Rambam follows Rif, who, as the Tur and Mechaber state below, holds that if the valid witness did not know the related witness was there, the valid testimony is not void. Only according to Tosafot and the Rosh, who require both intent and testimony, is it proven from the Gemara that lack of mutual knowledge does not save the testimony. According to Rambam, who invalidates through intent to testify alone, the Gemara proves no such thing. Thus this gloss perhaps belongs later, after the Rosh's view, and should read: 'and he came to beit din and testified, even if they did not know of each other, for two conditions are required,' etc. One can nevertheless force an explanation here by saying Rama means that the disqualified witness did not know of the valid witness. Even though he did not intend to join his testimony with the valid one, he still disqualifies, and all the more so if he knew. The valid witness, however, knew of the disqualified witness. In such a case Rif too could agree that he disqualifies.
Two conditions are required, etc. Their reason is that otherwise testimony would generally never stand, because a relative could always insert himself at the beginning by intending to testify, or at the end by coming to testify and thereby invalidate everything. But it is uncommon for him to do both. Those who disagree apparently hold this is no proof, because a divorcing husband, one performing kiddushin, or any party can designate valid witnesses, and then a disqualified person's intention does not invalidate them.
If the valid witnesses remember the testimony through seeing the document, they may return and testify, etc. This law was written by the Tur and Mechaber themselves in siman 45, seif 13, and siman 51, seif 5. In my glosses to Darkei Moshe there I wrote that this applies specifically where they are signed on a document. If valid witnesses already testified together with invalid ones in beit din, the valid ones may not return and testify anew. I also discussed this in the Derishah to siman 45.
Or a document signed by the litigant himself, etc. Rivash explains that no law of testimony applies to such a signature, so one cannot ask whether he came with the intent to testify. See Darkei Moshe.
If the claimant designated valid witnesses, etc. In the Perishah and Derishah I explained that he need not designate them by name. If he tells the group present at the act, 'Two of you shall be witnesses for me,' we say he intended the valid witnesses, for otherwise he could have remained silent or said to everyone, 'You are witnesses.' But if at the time of the act he says, 'Whoever knows testimony for me will have to come testify,' he has not distinguished valid from disqualified witnesses. Only later, after the event, when he places a ban on anyone who knows testimony, do we say his intent is only toward valid witnesses because he is not directly addressing any particular individual. Darkei Moshe rules that even a designation of this type may suffice; what I consider correct is written in the Derishah.
