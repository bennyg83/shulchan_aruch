Or any item for safekeeping. Tosafos at the end of HaChovel, s.v. "and he challenged it from the law of safeguarding," write that one who deposits an item without specification does not impose liability upon the custodian until he says, "Guard this for me." Rashi likewise writes in Beitzah 40, s.v. "he said to him," that the case there of deposited produce is not an unspecified deposit, but one in which the recipient accepted responsibility for guarding it. Ramban likewise writes at the end of HaChovel that the custodian is not liable until the owner says, "Guard this for me," whereas for an unspecified deposit he is certainly exempt. It appears that this is precisely the Mechaber's intent in writing, "one who deposited with him money or utensils for safekeeping," implying that the custodian is not liable until he was asked to guard them. At first glance this is difficult, for even one who merely allows another's ox to enter his premises is liable according to the Sages, since permission to enter is understood as permission to enter and be guarded, and according to many authorities this applies even to damage coming from elsewhere. The Shach, seif katan 8, likewise writes that when one brings his property into the owner's house with permission, ordinarily the owner has accepted responsibility to guard it even according to Rabbi. How, then, can there be an unspecified deposit for which the recipient is not liable, when an ordinary deposit is placed in the recipient's house? In my view the answer follows Ramban in Milchamos, who writes that a custodian is not liable until the owner says, "Guard this for me," or the circumstances themselves ordinarily imply an undertaking to guard. Thus, where the item requires the owner's protection when it is handed to another, the circumstances show that it was delivered for guarding. Tosafos and Ramban, who require an express statement of "guard this," refer to a case where the depositor designates a place that does not require the recipient's personal guarding. In such a case the circumstances do not show that the recipient accepted custodial responsibility. Ran similarly writes at the beginning of Pesachim, regarding one who designates a house for another, that designating a place is comparable to saying, "Bring your ox into your own domain," and he is exempt. A case came before us during wartime unrest. One person gave property to another so that it could be placed in a hidden cache in the ground of the recipient's house. After the danger had passed, the recipient dug up his own property and informed the depositor that he had already removed his own. The deposited property was later stolen. We ruled that the recipient was not even required to take an oath, because he had never become a custodian at all. The depositor had not said, "Guard it for me," the designated place did not require the owner's personal guarding, and the circumstances did not demonstrate an undertaking to guard.
He is an unpaid custodian. If one purchased merchandise from another through a kinyan sudar or another binding acquisition and the merchandise remained with the seller, Beit Yosef rules in siman 198, in his discussion of the Tur, that the seller is not even an unpaid custodian. He writes that this is the primary ruling. The reason is as stated above: the buyer never said, "Guard it for me." This applies, however, where the buyer has already paid, or where the seller does not insist on immediate payment and is willing to extend credit. If the seller refuses to release the merchandise until payment is made, he is a paid custodian, like a lender holding collateral. If the sale is of the type from which the seller derives a particular benefit, the seller is in any event a paid custodian because of the benefit of retaining the goods as security for the money owed. Tosafos limit that reasoning in the case of collateral because a lender does not derive such a benefit merely from lending his money, but here the seller does derive a commercial benefit. If one confers an item as a gift upon another through a third party and the item remains with that intermediary, Beit Yosef cites Rashba in siman 235 that where the gift is conferred upon a minor through another person and the minor is present, it is clear that the item was delivered to the intermediary for guarding, and this is equivalent to saying, "Guard it for me." If the minor is not present, however, perhaps the item was delivered merely so that the intermediary could acquire it for the minor, not so that he should guard it. This distinction appears applicable only to a gift for a minor. If the recipient is an adult and is absent, the intermediary certainly becomes a custodian. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of the chapter Arba'ah Shomrim in Shevuos: when one says, "Acquire this as a gift for so and so," the item's safeguarding rests upon the intermediary as it had rested upon the owner. The reason is straightforward. Once the gift is acquired for an absent adult, the intermediary is no worse than a finder of lost property. Maharit goes even further in Choshen Mishpat 116 and writes that he is a custodian because the object is in his possession while its owner is absent and cannot guard it. Nevertheless he is not a paid custodian by virtue of the benefit associated with Rav Yosef's perutah. As I explained in siman 72, that principle applies when the Torah itself imposed the mitzvah that created the custodial duty, such as one who lends upon collateral and thereby becomes obligated in the mitzvah related to caring for the collateral. Here, however, the Torah did not obligate him to acquire a gift for his fellow, so Rav Yosef's perutah does not apply, although he remains an unpaid custodian. In the case of one acquiring for a minor, by contrast, custodial liability cannot ordinarily arise from the minor himself. Therefore, according to Rashba he is exempt unless the minor is present and the person conferring the gift effectively appointed the intermediary as custodian and guardian. That case resembles a guardian appointed by the father of orphans or by beit din, who is liable for negligence because the duty of care originates in the appointing adult authority, as Ran writes in the name of Ramban in Shevuos. Rashba follows his own view that a custodian for a minor is exempt from negligence. According to the Mechaber and Rambam above in siman 96, however, who impose ordinary custodial liability regarding property of a minor, it appears that even one who acquires a gift for a minor through an intermediary becomes like a finder guarding the minor's property and is liable. In Rambam's view, a minor is excluded only from the double payment rules, while ordinary custodial liability does not require delivery by an adult. If the adult donee himself is present, however, it appears that the intermediary does not become his custodian. Regarding a minor, who is not capable of guarding for himself, one may say that the donor appointed the intermediary to guard for him. An adult, however, can guard his own property, and the donee himself never said, "Guard it for me."
"Place it before you." See Shach, seif katan 5. His meaning is that according to one side of the unresolved question in Bava Metzia 81, an unspecified statement of "place it" in the marketplace leaves the recipient exempt even according to the Sages, including from damage arising from the recipient himself. According to the other side of that question, an unspecified statement of "place it" in the marketplace creates liability even according to Rabbi, and the same rule would apply elsewhere.
An unspecified "place it." The same applies where he remained silent even after the owner said, "Guard it for me," and then placed it before him. Silence is not treated as assent except where remaining silent serves the silent party's interest, as Maharik writes in shoresh 155, or where a claim is made before beit din and he remains silent. But if the owner said, "Guard it for me," and the recipient drew the item into his domain while remaining silent, his affirmative act certainly creates custodial liability. Maharit writes that if the recipient tells the owner, "Throw it on the ground," intending thereby to avoid responsibility, this does not release him from liability. Ketzos HaChoshen already questioned this ruling. The discussion of damage coming to the property through a custodian, mentioned by the Sema and Shach, does not belong here.
"The house is before you." See Shach, seif katan 6. Even though the recipient accepted payment for guarding when he would guard, meaning that the owner expressly promised payment if he guarded, once the recipient says, "The house is before you," it is as though he explicitly stated that he does not wish to become a custodian and that the payment is only for use of the house. If the owner then pays him, or even obligates himself to pay after that statement, it is as though he agreed that the entire payment is solely for the house. He therefore owes the full payment, while the recipient is exempt from custodial liability.
And the shoes were lost. This ruling derives from a responsum of the Rosh and follows his view in seif 5 that a custodian does not require meshichah. According to the view that custodial liability requires meshichah, however, there seems to be no acquisition here, because drawing an animal does not acquire the utensils carried upon it, and the recipient cannot acquire them through his courtyard because the courtyard is moving. It is therefore surprising that the Mechaber and Rama rule here without qualification. It appears, however, that here all agree that he must pay. The requirement of an acquisition applies where the person committed no affirmative act upon the item itself and merely failed to guard it, such as failing to guard his house. A stranger who never accepted guardianship would not be liable for such omission, for even one who breaches a fence before another's animal is exempt, whereas a custodian is liable. Thus, without a custodial acquisition he is treated as a stranger. But where he performs an affirmative act upon the item itself, such as Reuven taking Shimon's utensil from Shimon's house and placing it at the roadside in a wholly unguarded place, he is certainly liable even without an acquisition. Bava Metzia calls property deliberately left in an unguarded place a knowingly abandoned loss. Once he places another's property in a place so unguarded that it is legally considered lost, he has effectively destroyed or damaged it with his own hands. Therefore he is liable even if he never became a custodian, and even where the ordinary exemption of guarding while the owner is present would apply. So too here, where he moved the shoes from a guarded location to a completely unguarded one, he is treated as one who directly damaged them. Nevertheless, according to the view that custodians require meshichah, he still does not acquire the status of a custodian for purposes such as taking the custodians' oath if he claims that he guarded them properly.
But on the road. See Sema, seif katan 5, who compares it to a house and writes that with respect to damage coming from elsewhere a house does not create liability. His meaning follows the side of the unresolved question in Bava Metzia 81 that when one says, "My house is before you," perhaps he merely grants the owner permission to bring the item in and does not undertake to guard it. The same reasoning may apply to the donkey in this case. Yet the Sema's explanation of the Rosh's responsum cited by the Tur is not linguistically precise when one reads the Tur itself. It appears that the Rosh initially wished to compare the case of the shoes both to "my house is before you" and to an unspecified "place it," and then distinguished based on whether the location is guarded. His distinction is needed primarily from the case of "my house is before you." In the case of an unspecified "place it," the distinction is simpler because that case concerns a marketplace where both parties remain present, so the recipient can say, "I meant: sit and guard it yourself," whereas in the case of the shoes the owner leaves. But the case of "my house is before you" in Bava Metzia is indeed difficult for the Rosh, because there the recipient remained and the owner said, "Let the deposit money remain with you," and then left. This closely resembles the shoes. Nor can we say regarding a house, "Sit and guard it yourself," because the essential distinction between a courtyard and a house, cited by the Shach in seif katan 8 from Hagahos Ashri, is that people do not object to another walking through a courtyard, so permission to enter can mean, "Enter and guard for yourself." With a house, however, people do object to another's entry and use, as explained above in siman 154 in the Sema, seif katan 7. Therefore permission necessarily means, "Enter and I will guard." The Tur accordingly explains that in the case of "my house is before you" he does not become an unpaid custodian because the house itself is a guarded place. The recipient may therefore say that his intention was not to undertake personal guarding, but only that the property would be protected by the house itself. In the case of the shoes, by contrast, they were placed in an unguarded location and the owner then left. In such circumstances, the ordinary implication is an undertaking to guard, as Ramban writes in seif 3.
