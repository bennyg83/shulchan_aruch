Where property deposited with a third party was placed before one of the litigants in his home, relying on the presumption that it would not be snatched from his house, this would seemingly be like a custodian placing an item in the place where he normally keeps his own property. Although one might argue that property held in escrow requires additional protection from either litigant, one cannot impose liability upon one who acted innocently and relied on the presumption of proper conduct, provided he intended only to act properly. Even under the laws of Heaven he is exempt. If the first custodian gave it to a second against the depositor's interests, and the second was an unpaid custodian, different rules may apply. If the second was a paid custodian or borrower and must pay, even according to those who hold that an oath regarding negligence arises only by extension, a supporting witness can exempt the first from an oath, since after payment there is no independent oath resting upon him while the deposit still exists. See the Rosh, who rejects the objection raised there. Where no supporting witness exists, although ordinarily one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay, here one may say that no oath ever took effect because the second custodian will certainly testify. Still, there may remain some possibility of an oath if the second is a borrower and claims that the object died through its work, or if it was entirely lost and an oath that it is not in his possession would be required. The latter oath is, however, rabbinic according to all opinions and therefore does not create the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. By contrast, an oath concerning death through its work may indeed do so. This requires further study. Regarding a claim of 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' when the second custodian is a renter or borrower who pays, if the deposit is no longer extant the second is a witness who himself requires a rabbinic oath that the object is not in his possession; if that oath is waived he may become interested testimony. Where the first says, 'I do not know whether I repaid,' perhaps even the depositor's silence when the deposit was placed with the second, or the fact that the first deposited it with someone with whom he regularly deposits, does not suffice, because the depositor may trust the second under oath only concerning the depositor's own doubtful claim, not against a definite claim. Still, perhaps the first is exempt because he had no reason to know, once the depositor authorized placing it with the second. Regarding negligence at the outset followed by unavoidable accident, if the first was midway on the road and handed the goods to the second, and witnesses establish that rain ruined the merchandise and it could not be saved, he may perhaps argue that he too could not have saved it and that the accident was not caused by his negligence in transferring it. Even if returning the merchandise home would have avoided the rain, if continuing on the originally intended route would not have saved it, the liability is doubtful. Since a worker may retract, perhaps an accident should not be attributed to negligence merely because returning the goods home would have prevented it, particularly where remaining at the destination would still not have enabled him to save them. One may need to distinguish between a matter involving irretrievable loss and a contractor, and between actual damage and loss of anticipated profit. Even if returning the goods would have caused loss of profit, and there may be liability under the laws of Heaven for withholding another's capital from use, where completion of the journey would not have saved the goods it appears difficult to impose liability merely because he could have returned them home. This requires further study. If two persons were custodians and one transferred the item to a third in his stead, as where a wagon driver and merchant share responsibility, although the wagon driver watches when the merchant is absent, the merchant may remain the primary custodian. The merchant's absence therefore diminishes the level of guarding, and he may not transfer responsibility to another wagon driver without the depositor's authorization unless it is customary to hire such drivers even without personal acquaintance, or to rely on a broker who recommends them. Where both were custodians together, perhaps the depositor cannot say, 'I do not trust him,' since the custodian whose mode of guarding was not changed can swear the three custodial oaths. Although he cannot swear that the third custodian did not misappropriate the item, liability for that point may arise only by extension. On the other hand, because each custodian could be required to swear, and neither is interested testimony, one witness may support the other; perhaps the depositor can insist that he trusts neither one's oath alone and requires the oaths of both. The same may apply to a wagon driver. Even if it is known that at certain times the guarding is in practice solely in the driver's hands, one may say that a rule based on necessity cannot establish consent for ordinary situations, and the depositor accepted the driver's oath only for those limited times when the merchandise cannot be transported otherwise. This is not necessarily equivalent to depositing with one with whom the depositor regularly deposits.
