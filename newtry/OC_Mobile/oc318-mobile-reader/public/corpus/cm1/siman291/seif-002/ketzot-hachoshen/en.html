But if he said to him, "Place it before yourself." In that case he accepted no custodial responsibility at all and is not even an unpaid custodian. He is therefore exempt from the Torah oath of custodians. Rambam nevertheless rules that if the depositor says he entrusted the object to him and the other says, "I merely told you to place it before yourself," the latter takes a hesset oath and includes in it that he did not misappropriate the property. This should not be understood as applying the special law of a custodian's unauthorized use. That special liability exists only for a person who has actually become a custodian. If a person who never accepted custody takes the property for himself, he is liable only under the ordinary law of theft or robbery, like any stranger. The distinctive law of misappropriation by a custodian, including liability even where he intends eventually to pay, does not apply. The same distinction matters for agency. Scripture specially makes a custodian liable for misappropriation performed through another person. One who said merely, "Place it before yourself," is not a custodian and therefore does not come within that special rule. If he caused an ordinary theft or robbery through an agent, the ordinary principle that there is no agency for transgression applies.
Or if he said, "The house is before you." Maharit discusses one who was asked to carry a deposit to another city and expressly refused custodial liability. He told the owner to place it on the ground so that he would pick it up only as though it were a found object and carry it as a favor. Maharit ruled that once he took it for carriage he nevertheless became a custodian, comparing the case to one who undertakes to carry shoes on a journey and thereby necessarily accepts ordinary care. In my view the common practice of persons who expressly refuse custody and tell the owner to place the object down is effective. The decisive question is intent. The rule that saying, "The house is before you," does not make one a custodian reflects the fact that no custody was accepted. If the owner wishes the other person to transport the object but the transporter expressly conditions that he accepts no responsibility, there is no reason to impose custody upon him against his will. The owner cannot manufacture the law of a lost object and thereby force another person to become a custodian merely by putting his property where it will otherwise be lost. A deliberately exposed loss does not impose an unwanted custodial office upon the person who clearly refused it. The responsum of Rosh, which says that one who agrees to carry another's shoes on a journey becomes an unpaid custodian, speaks where he accepted the task without such an express disclaimer. Since the shoes would otherwise be lost on the road, ordinary acceptance of the carriage includes reasonable safeguarding. But where the carrier repeatedly and explicitly states that he will transport only if exempt from all custodial liability, that condition is effective. The common device of having the owner place the object down is merely a way of demonstrating that clear intention.
