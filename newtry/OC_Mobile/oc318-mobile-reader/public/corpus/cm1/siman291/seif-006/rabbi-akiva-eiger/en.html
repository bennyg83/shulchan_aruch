[Shulchan Aruch] But only with silver. Note: At first glance the law appears to be as stated, except that he must swear that the gold is not in his possession, as is the law for every custodian who pays. Although we rule below, at the beginning of siman 295, that where an item of the same kind is readily available he need not swear, here, since he profits by paying the value of silver in place of gold, he should have to swear. However, it appears to me that there is room for analysis in light of the ruling below, siman 301, that with servants and documents he need not swear the oath that the item is not in his possession. The reason must be that the basic rabbinic enactment requiring a person to swear, despite the claimant's merely uncertain assertion, that the item is not in his possession was instituted only because it is common for a person to set his eyes upon another's property, since people understand the prohibition of coveting as applying only where one takes without payment. With servants and documents, however, since he does not pay for them, we do not suspect that they remain in his possession and that he seeks to take them, and the Sages did not enact the oath in such a case. This is also precise in the language of Nimukei Yosef in chapter 3 of Bava Metzia, and I found the same in Pnei Yehoshua, chapter 4 of Bava Metzia, in the sugya concerning the townspeople who contributed their shekels. Accordingly, here too we do not suspect that the gold remains in his possession while he seeks to take payment. The practical ruling requires study.
