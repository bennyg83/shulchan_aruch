Even if he is an unpaid custodian. See Baer Heitev. The wording that even if the first custodian borrowed while the owner was working for him, the second remains liable, is apparently a scribal error, as explained above. The source in Yam Shel Shlomo states that if the first custodian is working for the second custodian, the second does not receive the exemption of guarding while the owner works, because the property belongs to the original owner. The Torah exempted only where the owner of the property is working for the custodian. Thus the correct text is that even if the second custodian borrowed while the first custodian was working for him, the second is liable. The law of the first custodian's own exemption is not addressed here. See below, seif katan 19.
Even if it is known to everyone that the second person, etc. Sema explains that the owner may say, "You should not have trusted someone with whom I have no dealings." See Nachalat Tzvi regarding whether an admission by the depositor before beit din that he too trusted the second custodian is sufficient to exempt the first, as in the case of one to whom the owner regularly deposits.
Before the depositor. Dagul MeRevavah writes that if one custodian transferred the item to another not in the depositor's presence, but later informed the depositor, see above siman 176, seif 10, and Sema there, seif katan 34.
To deposit with him regularly. Ritva, cited by Beit Yosef, limits the exemption to a second custodian who has not meanwhile become poor or lost his trustworthy status. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 17, explains that formal suspicion is not required. If his established reliability deteriorated, the owner may say that he is no longer trustworthy. He further rules in the case of Levi who sent silk through Reuven to Shimon for repair, and Shimon later returned it through Reuven, that Shimon is exempt because Levi had already demonstrated trust in Reuven with that very item. Even if one prior entrustment would ordinarily be insufficient to establish regular practice, all agree in such a case where the same object was sent through him and no change occurred. He cites Hagahot Ashri concerning returning a deposit through the same messenger. According to Rabbi Simcha, the bailee may even say that the depositor is not his direct litigant where they never dealt with one another. According to Or Zarua, although the bailee becomes the depositor's litigant, he is still exempt because the item was returned through one whom the depositor had trusted. If the messenger cannot pay, the matter depends on the earlier dispute and money cannot be extracted from the possessor. Responsa Maharam of Brisk, siman 7, cites an analogous case from the Geonim and rules for the sender, so the matter requires study.
For example, where the first was a paid custodian, etc. Shach, seif katan 44, rejects the Tur's position because even an unpaid custodian who transfers to a paid custodian may be liable on the ground that the owner says, "I trusted you to swear, but not him." See Netivot HaMishpat, Shaar Mishpat, and Shemen Rokeach, volume 1, siman 83. Shaar Mishpat cites Mabit concerning wool given to Shimon to weave and transferred without permission to Levi. Reuven alleged that the completed garment consumed more wool than comparable garments and perhaps Levi retained some. Mabit held Shimon liable because a custodian who transfers to another custodian bears responsibility and the owner may say that he trusted Shimon, not Levi. Shaar Mishpat strongly disagrees where the owner's claim is itself uncertain. If Reuven certainly claims that some wool remained and Shimon does not know whether all the measured wool entered the garment, this can be treated as "I do not know whether I returned your property," creating liability. But if Reuven too is uncertain, both parties are uncertain and there is no Torah oath, so money cannot be extracted merely from suspicion. He therefore concludes that practical law should not follow Mabit in that uncertain case.
And he pays even though he borrowed or rented while the owner was working for him. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 94, writes that the same applies to a custodian of documents who transfers them to another custodian. If the first is a paid custodian and gives them to an unpaid custodian, he is negligent even where the owner regularly entrusts property to the second. Although regarding documents both paid and unpaid custodians are monetarily liable only for negligence, the paid custodian's level of guarding is intrinsically superior, and reducing it to unpaid custody is a reduction in guarding. See there, and Tosafos, Bava Kamma 11b, s.v. de'iluy.
Even though he borrowed, etc., while the owner was working for him. See Chatam Sofer, Even HaEzer, volume 2, siman 163, on the difficulty from Bava Metzia 96b, and see Shev Yaakov, siman 11, and Netivot HaMishpat.
While the owner was working for him. Baer HaGolah, letter 200, cites Kesef Mishneh that the reason for liability is that transferring the property is more like direct damage. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Sechirut, chapter 1, law 4, questions this, because the first custodian may actually have increased the protection by transferring the item to a person who is liable in circumstances where the first custodian would be exempt. See Netivot HaMishpat. Shev Yaakov, siman 11, writes that the same rule applies earlier in the seif where the first custodian did not reduce the formal level of guarding but transferred the item to a person to whom the owner does not usually entrust property. The first remains liable even if his own custody began while the owner was working for him. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch state the rule here as the greater novelty. Earlier, liability is obvious because the owner can say that the second person was not trusted at all, which resembles direct damage by handing the property to an untrusted person. Here, by contrast, the owner may trust the second person's honesty, and the defect is only that the first reduced the level of guarding, such as from paid to unpaid custody. One might have thought that this is no worse than the first custodian's own negligence, from which guarding while the owner works would exempt him. The ruling therefore teaches that even here he is liable, and all the more so in the earlier case. See there, and what I wrote above in siman 176, seif 10, seif katan 13.
And if there are witnesses. See Baer Heitev and Shach, seif katan 46. Shach concludes that even Rambam and Semag agree that an oath by the second custodian can sometimes suffice. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Sechirut, chapter 1, law 5, similarly asks why witnesses are needed where the owner regularly deposits with the second person, since the owner cannot say, "I trusted you to swear but not him." It should be enough for the second custodian to swear that an unavoidable accident occurred. He explains that Rambam mentions witnesses because ordinarily the second custodian will be willing to swear only that he was not negligent, whereas the first paid custodian needs proof of an actual unavoidable accident in order to be exempt. If the second is willing to swear to the actual unavoidable accident, that oath is equivalent. Machaneh Ephraim, laws of custodians, siman 31, writes similarly. Shach therefore concludes that even where the first reduced the level of guarding, such as by giving the item to an unpaid custodian, if the owner regularly entrusts property to that second person, the second person's oath that an unavoidable accident occurred exempts the first. Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat disagree and rule according to the plain implication of Rambam and Mechaber that only witnesses exempt the first in such a case, not the second custodian's oath. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 15, also disagrees with Shach and explains that when the level of guarding was reduced, the second custodian's oath cannot exempt the first even if the owner regularly trusts him. The reason is that a false oath that changes liability to exemption is a deposit oath and carries an asham gezelot, whereas an oath that changes one exemption to another or one liability to another is a less severe ordinary oath. A paid custodian who transfers to an unpaid custodian is liable for theft and loss, while the second is exempt from them. If the item was stolen or lost and the second falsely swears that an unavoidable accident occurred, his oath is only the lighter type because he himself was exempt either way. Such an oath cannot discharge the first custodian's more serious oath of deposit. Therefore the owner may say that although he trusted the second person for the severe oath involving denial of money, he did not necessarily trust him for the lighter oath. This may also explain Ritva and Rashach, who limit the presumption that a person deposits with his wife and children to an unpaid custodian. If a paid custodian transfers to family members who are unpaid custodians, their oath cannot exempt him for the same reason. See there for the full analysis and additional distinctions.
