For example, where the first was a paid custodian and the second an unpaid custodian, etc. The Beit Yosef cites the students of the Rashba. According to the Ramban, where a custodian entrusted the item to his wife, children, or another person with whom the owner ordinarily deposits, the first custodian remains liable unless the loss is clearly established as an accident exempting him. Thus, even if the second custodian swears that he was an unpaid custodian, did not act negligently, and that the item was stolen or lost, the first cannot say, 'Perhaps it died through an accident and I am exempt.' He must clarify the exempting circumstance. Likewise, if the second refuses to swear that the animal died naturally, or has gone overseas, the first is liable. He cannot argue that perhaps it died through an accident, exempting him, or through negligence, making the second liable. Rabbi Shimon, my teacher, rules so. Rabbeinu, however, holds that a paid custodian who entrusted the item to an unpaid custodian is exempt where the second refuses to swear or has departed, even if the owner claims with certainty that it was stolen. The first says that he does not know whether it was stolen, in which case he would be liable, or whether it was lost through an accident or through the second custodian's negligence, in which cases he would be exempt. Rabbeinu compares this to one who says, 'You owe me one hundred,' and the other answers, 'I do not know whether I ever became liable,' who is exempt. This view does not appear correct. The Gemara states that even an unpaid custodian who entrusted the item to a paid custodian is liable because the owner may say, 'I trusted you to swear, but I do not trust the other.' Thus, even when the second is willing to swear, the first is liable because he himself cannot swear. Certainly he is liable when the second refuses to swear or is unavailable. Perhaps Rabbeinu interprets the Gemara's phrase to mean that the item may still be in the second custodian's possession, whereas if it is known to be gone, the first is exempt. This is not the primary interpretation. Rashi and the other authorities imply that whenever the first cannot establish clearly that the item was lost through an exempting accident, he is liable. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 333, goes further and rules that even where the item remained with the original custodian, if it was lost and he does not know whether through negligence or accident, he is liable, because every custodian must swear clearly and cannot satisfy his obligation by saying that he does not know. This is the primary ruling. It is unlike the Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Raah, who distinguishes between a borrowed donkey used by the borrower's agent and one that the borrower himself then gives to his agent. The first case, he says, resembles uncertainty whether the initial obligation arose, whereas the latter involves negligence. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this at the beginning of siman 340. It appears that the Rama omitted that view there because the Rosh and Tur disagree. The Ir Shushan, who cites both rulings without qualification, contradicts himself. Maharshal also cites the Nimukei Yosef, but then independently disagrees, distinguishing between a certain and an uncertain claim by the lender. He apparently did not notice that the Rosh, Tur, and Terumat HaDeshen reject the premise. The law therefore is that the custodian is liable even where the lender's claim is uncertain. This is not comparable to uncertainty whether repayment occurred. Once he became a custodian, his uncertainty is comparable to saying, 'I do not know whether I returned your property.' Although a borrower's assets become encumbered only from the time of the accident and not from the moment of borrowing, that concerns the heirs' liability. A loan is given for expenditure, so the debt immediately rests upon the borrower's assets. A borrowed object, by contrast, must be returned in kind while it exists. When the borrower does not know whether it died naturally, his claim is therefore equivalent to uncertainty whether he returned it. This distinction is correct and clear. Tosafot in Perek HaShoel, 56b, also support this distinction. Accordingly, the view of the Ramban and Rabbi Shimon, followed by the Rosh and Tur, is primary.

And if there are witnesses. This implies that even if the first custodian reduced the level of care, he is exempt when witnesses establish the exempting accident. The Bach writes so plainly, and this is explicit in the Rambam and Semag. Maharshal disagrees and holds that where the first custodian diminished the guarding, he is liable despite witnesses because his initial conduct was negligent. It appears to me that even an oath should suffice to exempt him where the owner customarily deposits with the second custodian, as Rabbeinu Yerucham writes. Most authorities questioned the Rambam on this point. In my view, the Rambam and Semag also agree that an oath suffices. Their statement that the first must not reduce the guarding means that if the unpaid second custodian merely swears that the item was stolen, the paid first custodian has been negligent, because he himself would have been liable for theft or loss. Their subsequent statement regarding witnesses also applies to a case in which an oath would suffice to exempt the first. They mention witnesses because the opening clause also addresses a person with whom the owner does not ordinarily deposit, where witnesses are specifically required. This resolves Maharshal's objection that the Rambam and Semag call the first custodian negligent and nevertheless exempt him. In any event, the Rambam and Semag state that if the second custodian brings proof that the animal died, the first is exempt. As Mahari Ashkenazi and Maharshal explain, this is because the death did not result from the earlier negligence. In such a case, even if the owner never deposits with the second custodian, the first is exempt. This is correct as law. Nevertheless, the Rosh explicitly states that a paid custodian who transfers an item to an unpaid custodian is not deemed negligent merely because he entrusted it to a competent person. Therefore, Rabbeinu Yerucham's ruling appears primary. The Tur's wording below, siman 305, seif 5, likewise omits the phrase that the first was negligent and speaks only of death or another accident.
