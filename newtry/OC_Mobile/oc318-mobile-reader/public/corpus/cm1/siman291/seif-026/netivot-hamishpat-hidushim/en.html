That the first was a paid custodian. The first cannot say, Perhaps an unavoidable accident occurred in the second custodian's hands and I am exempt, or perhaps it died through negligence and the second custodian is liable. Every custodian must swear with certainty, and since the first does not know and therefore cannot swear, he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must pay. If the opposing party makes a definite claim, he is also liable because this resembles one who says, I do not know whether I repaid you. Therefore, where one borrows a donkey to ride and stipulates that his agent may ride it, and it dies on the road, and the borrower does not know whether it died naturally or through negligence, he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must pay. See Beurim: the primary rule is that if he transferred it without permission to a person with whom the owner ordinarily deposits, he is liable because he was negligent in placing himself in a position where he would not know the facts. But if he transferred it with the owner's permission, so that he was not negligent in failing to know, he is exempt. See Beurim: where a custodian must pay because he is obligated to swear but cannot swear, his heirs are exempt. This applies specifically where it is known that the item died, but they do not know whether through negligence. If, however, there is a possibility that it remains in the second custodian's possession and the second misappropriated it, and the second is not someone with whom the owner ordinarily deposits, the first is liable as a damager, and even his heirs are liable:
Although the first borrowed or rented it. The reason is that the exemption of borrowing while the owner is present applies only to a custodian who receives the item directly from the owner, not to a second custodian, while the first custodian becomes liable for the transfer into the second custodian's domain. See Beurim: this applies specifically where he lowered the quality of the guarding by transferring it without authority, and for that reason he becomes liable for the second custodian's domain. But if, when the first custodian borrowed from the owner, he explicitly stated that he was borrowing or renting it in order to rent it onward to others, and the owner was then with him, he is exempt. No new kinyan is made when it is later rented to the second person; the second person's rights derive from the first borrowing or rental, and since the owner was present at that original time, the first custodian is exempt:
And if there are witnesses. The Shach concludes as practical law that even where the first custodian worsened the guarding, if the second custodian swears that an unavoidable accident occurred, the first is exempt. See Beurim: the first custodian is exempt specifically where there are witnesses to the unavoidable accident, but the second custodian's oath alone is insufficient:
And he can swear. Meaning, he can swear that he was not negligent. Although there remains a concern that the second custodian may still have misappropriated the item, which could make the first liable, we do not apply the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, because the oath concerning misappropriation is imposed only through the mechanism of a supplemental oath. A paid custodian who redeposits the item with the owner remains liable if it is stolen from the owner's house. If he transfers it to a deaf mute, minor, or mentally incompetent person, he is liable even if an unavoidable accident later occurs in their possession, because the situation began with negligence.
