If he is an unpaid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 41, who compares the case to stolen property after the owners' despair. At first glance the comparison is weak. With stolen property, the object remains the owner's and the second person physically damages it, so ordinary tort liability applies. A second custodian, however, is liable for negligence only if he accepted custody. Since the owner never entrusted him directly, he can say, "You are not my litigant." The owner's claim against the second custodian should therefore arise through the rule of R. Natan, because the second accepted responsibility toward the first custodian. A better comparison is the case where one rents a cow and then lends it to another. The second person becomes a borrower from the first, and payment can ultimately flow through that relationship.
For example, the first was a paid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 44. He rejects those who treat the first custodian's uncertainty as "I do not know whether I became obligated," and says it is more like, "I do not know whether I returned what I owed." His statement assumes the owner makes a definite claim. If the owner is also uncertain, even one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," can be exempt in comparable circumstances. Shach instead grounds liability in the rule that one who cannot take the required oath must pay. The earlier authorities, however, including Ir Shushan and Nimukei Yosef, exempt the first custodian where the owner expressly permitted the transfer, such as where he stipulated that an agent could ride the animal. This can be explained by the rule I developed in siman 75. One who says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," can be exempt where he had no reasonable way to know, unless his inability to know results from his own negligence. If he transferred the object to another person without permission, he was negligent in placing himself in a position where he could not know what occurred. He is therefore liable both under the rule of uncertain repayment and under the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. If he transferred it with permission, his lack of knowledge was not negligent. He is exempt. Shach holds more broadly that "I do not know whether I repaid" creates liability even where there was no negligent failure to know. That is why he requires payment when the depositor makes a definite claim even where the transfer itself was authorized. In siman 75 I proved that the primary rule follows Nimukei Yosef: where the person could not reasonably know and was not negligent in that ignorance, he is exempt. There is also a difficulty from Shach's own ruling in siman 72, seif katan 51, that if the claimant admits the defendant genuinely does not know, we do not apply the rule that one unable to swear must pay. Here, when the owner expressly authorized the transfer to the second person, the owner himself knows that the first custodian may be unable to know what later occurred. Why should he be liable? Therefore the primary rule is that authorized transfer exempts when the first custodian's ignorance is non-negligent. Unauthorized transfer creates liability because his own negligence caused the evidentiary uncertainty. The rule concerning a suspect custodian in siman 92 is different. A suspect person does not fall under the ordinary rule that one who cannot swear must pay, because the inability to swear results from his legal disqualification. The Sages instead enacted that the opposing party swear and collect. If the opposing party dies, the special enactment may not apply. Where an uncertain claim is supported by one witness, the suspect defendant can be liable because the witness is treated like two witnesses so long as the defendant does not rebut him with the required oath. Accordingly, if the first custodian dies, his heirs are exempt where his only liability would have arisen from the rule that one unable to swear must pay, since heirs do not ordinarily pay under that rule. But if the second person was someone to whom the owner did not ordinarily entrust property, so that the second person's oath would never have been accepted and the object is therefore treated as still in the first custodian's responsibility, the first custodian's heirs can remain liable on a direct damage theory. Where the second person was someone the owner regularly used, or where witnesses establish the accident and the only uncertainty is whether negligence occurred, the heirs can be exempt if the first custodian's liability depended only on inability to swear. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that in siman 340 he resolved the matter in favor of exemption where the second was a regular depositary. I do not understand this. There he himself acknowledges that according to Rabbeinu Tam, who treats an uncertain denial as sufficient to create the rule of "unable to swear, pays," liability can remain. The better distinction is the one stated above: non-negligent inability to know can exempt, while negligent creation of uncertainty can obligate.
And the second is a paid custodian. The text should read "a renter," not literally a paid custodian. If it meant an ordinary paid custodian and the animal died, the original borrower would in any event be liable for accidents. If it died from ordinary work, "death through work" does not apply to a paid custodian in the same way. The case must therefore concern a renter, who has the law of a paid custodian, where the animal died through the permitted work. The original borrower can then be liable because he reduced the legal structure of the owner's guarding by transferring the item into a different custodial category.
Even though he borrowed or rented while the owner was with him. Why is transfer to the second person considered a reduction of guarding when the first custodian had shemirah b'baalim and was entirely exempt even from negligence, while the second is at least liable for theft? There is certainly a reduction when a paid custodian gives the object to an unpaid custodian. The paid custodian must continually sit and guard and protect from theft, while an unpaid custodian need only use ordinary guarding. Even a borrower who transfers to a paid custodian can reduce the legal protection. Although their only formal difference is liability for accidents, some accidents can in fact be prevented, such as armed robbery if the property could have been kept within a fortified enclosure. One might therefore think that, under the view which makes the first liable for the second's responsibility, even transfer from one paid custodian to another should obligate the first where the second cannot pay and the first had been exempt through shemirah b'baalim. But Bava Metzia 96 asks whether a husband has the status of borrower or renter regarding his wife's property, and asks what practical difference there can be since his relationship is shemirah b'baalim. If liability on a second person's custody applied even without reducing the level of guarding, there would be a practical difference if the husband later rented the property to another. Shav Yaakov raises this difficulty. The explanation appears to be that liability for the second person's use arises when the transfer itself constitutes a new acquisition beyond the authority of the first arrangement. Taz in siman 176 writes that if, during an existing borrowing period, the borrower enters a new rental arrangement for a longer term, the later kinyan can displace the first arrangement. Siman 363 teaches that where one has no authority to rent another's property, the rental proceeds belong to the owner. Thus, when the first borrower had no authority to reduce the guarding by renting or lending onward, the second person's meshichah creates a new acquisition that was not contained within the first one. That new acquisition can displace the original shemirah b'baalim exemption. The same applies to a paid custodian who was not authorized to give the object to an unpaid custodian and thereby free himself from continual guarding. Although siman 346 says a later borrowing not in the owner's presence does not necessarily displace an earlier borrowing with the owner present, that is where the object remained within the first person's legal possession. Here the second person's separate permission and meshichah create the new transaction. Bava Metzia 41 derives shelichut yad without the owner's knowledge from the case of a borrower acting with permission. If a new authorized transaction would create liability, all the more so an unauthorized one can. Accordingly, if the first borrower or renter originally received the object expressly on terms permitting him to lend or rent it onward, the later transfer creates no new acquisition. It operates under the original arrangement that was made with the owner present, and the shemirah b'baalim exemption continues. This also resolves the Gemara's question concerning the husband. From the moment of marriage he acquires his rights in his wife's property in the ordinary manner that includes the ability to rent it onward. The later rental is therefore part of the original relationship and creates no new transaction displacing the exemption.
If there are witnesses that the second guarded it. See Shach, seif katan 46. The cross reference there is misplaced and should be attached to seif katan 48 before his citation from Rosh. According to that rule, when the first custodian reduced the level of guarding, liability exists specifically where there are no witnesses. If witnesses establish that the second guarded properly, the first can be exempt. Why is the second custodian's own oath not sufficient when he is someone to whom the owner regularly entrusts property? The reason is that the exemption for transferring to a regular depositary rests on presumed consent of the original owner. We estimate that had the owner been present at the moment of transfer, he would have agreed and accepted the second person directly as his own custodian. Ramban, the source of this law, therefore holds that the first custodian can be exempt even if the second lacks means to pay. The second has become the original owner's own legal custodian. That is why the second person's oath can ordinarily exempt the first. But where the first custodian reduced the guarding, the presumed consent collapses. If the owner had been present, he plainly would not have agreed to replace a paid custodian, obligated to sit and guard, with an unpaid custodian who bears a lower standard. The second person therefore never becomes the original owner's own custodian. He is only the custodian of the first custodian. His own oath cannot by itself release the first from the original owner's claim. If he was negligent and cannot pay, the first remains liable. Witnesses are different. They do not rely on the second custodian's personal legal status. If witnesses establish the actual fact that proper guarding occurred and the loss was through an exempt accident, the first can be released on the facts themselves. Ketzot HaChoshen approaches the matter similarly, though I do not accept his formulation that the second custodian entered only for negligence and that we fear theft or loss. If the animal died, he plainly must swear or prove the actual event, and there is no reason to reject his oath about the manner of death merely because the issue is not negligence. The real problem is that he is not the original owner's litigant once presumed consent is absent.
And he himself can swear. See Sema, seif katan 49, who says he must also swear that the second did not commit shelichut yad. Ritva asks why the first custodian should be liable for the second person's shelichut yad at all. In my view the basic liability can be explained simply. Once the second person commits shelichut yad, he becomes a robber. If the object then dies, he remains liable because it had already been removed from the owner's domain through robbery. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Kamma explains that robbery causes payment according to the time of robbery because the robber removed the object from the owner's control for purposes of sale and consecration. The first custodian can therefore become liable from the moment the second person turns the property into stolen property. There is no difference whether a thief stole it from the first custodian's house or from the owner's house. If an ordinary thief steals from a custodian and the item later dies, the custodian can be liable because, even before the death, the item was already in the thief's possession. Mechaber says similarly in seif 9. Nevertheless, Shach is correct that the first custodian need not swear that the second did not commit shelichut yad. The central custodial oath concerns whether the object was lost through the custodian's negligence. Where the first custodian cannot swear to that necessary matter, the rule that one unable to swear must pay may apply. But the second person's shelichut yad is not an ordinary element of the first custodian's custodial oath. It is more like theft by an outsider. We do not require a custodian, whenever he says an animal left to pasture and died naturally, also to swear that perhaps a thief had already acquired it before the death. If shelichut yad were an independent principal custodial oath, the Torah's special suspicion of custodians would create a basis to require it. Here it arises only as a possible gilgul concerning a third person's new act. We do not invent such an event without evidence. The general rule is that the party asserting a new act bears the burden of proof.
