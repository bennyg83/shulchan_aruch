Therefore, if the owner is accustomed to deposit with that person. Chacham Tzvi was asked about the shopkeeper's ledger case, where workers say to the employer, "You trusted the shopkeeper." Does their own practice of depositing with that shopkeeper defeat the complaint that the employer trusted him? He answers that it does not. In the ordinary rule of a custodian who transfers to another person whom the owner regularly trusts, the second custodian enters the place of the first. It is as though the owner himself deposited with him. In the shopkeeper case, however, the workers never made the shopkeeper their litigating counterpart in this debt. Even if he is generally trustworthy to them, they can still say, "What dealings have we with him in this transaction?" Therefore their general trust does not eliminate their claim against the employer.
He regularly deposits this particular matter with the second person. Ritva adds that the rule applies only so long as circumstances have not changed. If the second person became poor, untrustworthy, or otherwise unsuitable in the interim, the owner's former practice of depositing with him no longer establishes present consent.
The first custodian is exempt from payment. Beit Yosef cites students of Rashba that according to Ramban, if the second custodian refuses or is unable to swear that he was not negligent, the first remains liable. The first cannot say, "Perhaps it was lost unavoidably and I am exempt," because the burden lies on him to establish the custodial defense. Another view says that where the owner regularly entrusts the second person, the first is exempt even if the second is absent or refuses to swear. The case resembles one who says, "I returned the item to the person whom you yourself trust." Hagahot Ashri goes further: even if the second says it was lost through negligence, the first can say, "You trusted him; I did not undertake to accept his accusation against me." Only if negligence is independently established does the first become liable under the view that he remains responsible for the substitute's negligence. I explained in siman 340 that this latter approach is persuasive. Once transfer to the usual custodian was authorized, a mere inability to produce the second person's oath does not turn the first into a definite debtor. The first can say that he lawfully transferred the object to the person the owner himself normally trusted. Therefore, unless the manner of loss that would create liability is actually proved, the first custodian remains exempt.
Provided that he did not reduce the level of safeguarding. Rambam's qualification is correct. If a paid custodian transfers to another paid custodian, or a borrower to another borrower, the second enters the same level of custodial responsibility and his oath can discharge the first. But if the first reduces the level, such as a paid custodian transferring to an unpaid custodian, the second has not entered the first custodian's full legal position. Therefore, if the first custodian's liability includes theft and loss while the second is liable only for negligence, the second's oath that no negligence occurred does not establish that the event was one for which the first is exempt. The item might have been stolen or lost, categories that do not obligate the second but do obligate the first. Because the second never became the owner's opposing party regarding those categories, his oath cannot discharge the first from them. This explains why the owner's prior practice of depositing with the second person is not enough when the first has reduced the level of custody. The prior practice shows willingness that the object physically be in that person's hands, but it does not enlarge the second person's legal liability beyond the level he actually assumed. The same logic applies where a borrower transfers to a paid custodian. The second can swear about negligence, theft, and loss, but ordinary unavoidable accidents remain outside his liability while they obligate the borrower. His oath therefore cannot resolve the first borrower's exposure to those accidents. If, however, witnesses establish the actual event in a way that exempts the first custodian under his own higher standard, the first is exempt. The difficulty exists only where the second person's oath is being used to establish a fact concerning a category for which he never entered the same custodial relationship.
If there are witnesses that the second custodian guarded properly. A paid custodian who transfers to an unpaid custodian is not automatically considered one whose negligence began at the moment of transfer. Entrusting the object to a competent person is not itself negligence. Tosafot writes that even if the first person transferred to an unpaid custodian, he can be exempt where the later loss was truly unavoidable. Thus if witnesses establish proper care and an exempting accident, the first custodian is discharged.
And he is able to swear concerning this. Sema explains that the first custodian must be able to swear that the second was not negligent and did not misappropriate the deposit. Shach objects that according to Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch, the principal custodial oath is that the item is no longer in the custodian's possession, while absence of negligence and misappropriation can arise by gilgul. If witnesses establish the principal loss, the collateral oaths fall away. Sema is nevertheless substantially correct where no witnesses establish the loss. If the first custodian must take the principal oath, the ancillary issues can be rolled into it. If he cannot swear concerning facts that are essential to his exemption, the rule that one who is obligated to swear and cannot do so must pay can become relevant. The more difficult issue is unauthorized use by the second custodian. Ritva writes that the first should not be liable merely because the second misappropriated the object, unless the object was actually lost through the consequences of that act. Ordinary negligence is different: the first accepted an obligation to guard and failed by placing the object with someone who was negligent. But unauthorized use by the second resembles an independent transgression for which agency ordinarily does not operate. Rosh, however, appears to imply that if the second definitely misappropriated the deposit, the first can become liable. Scripture specifically extends the law of a custodian's misappropriation to an act done through another person. That makes the issue more severe than ordinary agency for transgression. Even so, where the first merely does not know whether the second misappropriated the object, the doubt is not the same as uncertainty whether the item was lost through negligence or unavoidable accident. Uncertainty about negligence concerns whether an already existing custodial liability was discharged, and can resemble "I do not know whether I repaid." Uncertainty whether a later misappropriation ever created the special additional liability is more like "I do not know whether I ever became liable." Accordingly, the Shulchan Aruch's phrase that the first custodian "can swear concerning this" should primarily be read as requiring that he be able to swear the actual exempting loss. A mere inability to know whether the second secretly misappropriated the object does not automatically create payment liability under every view, especially because that matter ordinarily appears only by gilgul.
