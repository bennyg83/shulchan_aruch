A depositor deposits on the understanding that the custodian may entrust the item to his wife, children, and household. Shach cites a responsum questioning the view that this applies only to an unpaid custodian. Bedek HaBayit cites Ritva with that limitation, but Ritva stated it only under the Gemara's preliminary assumption that a paid custodian who transfers to an unpaid custodian has worsened the guarding and is liable. Under the accepted rule, transferring custody to a competent person is not itself negligence. Therefore the principle that a depositor relies on the custodian's household can apply even to a paid custodian. Ritva himself adds that members of the custodian's household may in practice guard with the seriousness of paid custodians because of their relationship to him. Nevertheless, according to Rambam's view, which I discussed earlier, a paid custodian who transfers to an unpaid custodian may remain liable unless the manner of loss is established. The second person's oath cannot necessarily discharge the first regarding categories for which the second never assumed equivalent liability. In the actual case cited by Maharash Kohen, the merchandise was given to the paid custodian's servant. Since the servant was himself part of the paid custodian's household and served for wages, the transfer was effectively from a paid custodian to another paid custodian. Therefore the first custodian was exempt under all views.
