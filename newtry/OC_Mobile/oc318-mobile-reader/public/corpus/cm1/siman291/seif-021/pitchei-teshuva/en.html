One might infer that if it actually had been under his head and was stolen, even a paid guardian would be exempt. The full text of Maharik shows otherwise. Reuven hired Shimon to travel to Venice on an errand. Shimon slept in a room with other respectable men. Night thieves entered and stole clothing and purses from several of them, including Shimon's purse containing both his own money and Reuven's. Maharik rules that Reuven is not liable for Shimon's own loss, but Shimon, as a paid guardian of Reuven's money, is liable. According to the view that a paid guardian is liable even for theft of money buried in the ground, liability is obvious. Even according to those who exempt a paid guardian for unavoidable theft after money was buried in the ground, this theft was not a complete accident because Shimon could have protected the purse better by placing it under his head, tying it to his arm, or hiding it where a thief would not find it. Maharik adds that even a gratuitous guardian might be liable because such conduct approaches negligence. Thus, exemption after placing the money under his head would follow only according to the authorities who exempt a paid guardian for unavoidable theft, not according to the view accepted by the Rosh, Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and apparently Sema that a paid guardian remains liable even for such unavoidable theft. Moreover, even under the lenient view, placing money under one's head may suffice only where one sleeps in a room with respectable companions. If the companions are not reliable, beginning one's sleep in such circumstances may itself be negligence. Baer Heitev's citation of Sema also appears to omit a word and should read that if the money was lost during transport, the guardian is exempt where he kept it in the place where he normally keeps his own money, as the Shach explains. Responsa Galya Masechta 13 discusses a wagon driver who was given money to purchase salt and transport it. He lodged in a field with a caravan, placed the money inside his outer robe, put the robe under his head, and slept on it. The robe and money were stolen. Galya Masechta holds him liable. Choosing to sleep at the normal sleeping time is not an unavoidable accident, and placing money inside a visible robe under one's head is not adequate safeguarding of cash on the road, where passersby and thieves are common. Maharik's case involved a room and respectable people, and Maharik spoke of placing the purse itself under the head so that no one would know anything valuable was there. The responsum adds that in such cases it may nevertheless be proper to go beyond the strict law in accordance with the principle at the end of Bava Metzia. It also explains why Darkei Moshe and Sema cited Maharik even though they seem elsewhere to follow the stricter view that a paid guardian is liable for unavoidable theft: Maharik is useful at least to establish that a gratuitous guardian can also be liable where the conduct itself is negligent. Finally, Baer Heitev cites the Shach's criticism of Rashach for limiting the rule that one who deposits with a person is presumed to deposit with that person's wife and children to a gratuitous guardian. Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit also cites Ritva in that direction, but the Shach elsewhere disagrees, relying on Nimukei Yosef and students of Rashba that the rule applies to a paid guardian as well. Ketzot HaChoshen reconciles the views by suggesting that even Ritva and Rashach agree that when a paid guardian transfers the item to his wife or children, who are gratuitous guardians, the first guardian is exempt if there are witnesses to the accident or he himself can swear to it. Their stringency is only where there are no such witnesses and the wife or children seek to exempt him by their own oath. A similar distinction appears in Responsa VeShav HaKohen.
