See Netivot HaMishpat and Shaar Mishpat, and Responsa Shemen Rokeach, volume 1, siman 83. Shaar Mishpat further cites Responsa Mabit, volume 2, second set, siman 23, concerning Reuven who gave spun wool to Shimon to weave. Shimon, without Reuven's knowledge, gave it to Levi, another weaver. After Levi completed the garment, Reuven claimed that when Shimon had woven comparable garments, they did not consume as much wool as this one, and perhaps Levi had retained some wool. Reuven said, "I trusted you, but not Levi." Mabit ruled that because all artisans are paid custodians and a custodian who transfers to another custodian is liable, Shimon bears responsibility even though Reuven's claim does not create the ordinary custodian's oath. Shimon is nevertheless subject to a heset oath on other claims. If Reuven had asserted with certainty that Levi retained some of his wool, Levi would have to swear, and Reuven could say that he trusted Shimon's oath but not Levi's, obligating Shimon to pay and then litigate with Levi. Likewise, where Reuven claims that comparable garments woven by Shimon used less wool, even though this is not a certain claim and Shimon would merely have had to accept a communal ban that he retained nothing, Reuven may say that he trusted Shimon to accept that ban but not Levi. Shaar Mishpat finds this extremely difficult. Since the underlying claim is not certain, how can the rule of a custodian who transfers to another custodian create monetary liability? Even where there was clear negligence, if the depositor does not know the value of the deposit he cannot extract money merely on estimation and doubt. All the more so here, where there is no Torah oath and the claimant himself is uncertain. If Reuven were certain that wool remained with Levi, even though Shimon does not know, one might accept Mabit's ruling. This differs from a case where the depositor says that some item from the deposit was stolen but cannot identify the amount, because there the custodian can say, "I do not know whether I ever became liable." Here, if the wool was delivered by count or weight and Reuven certainly claims that some remained with Levi, while Shimon does not know whether all of it entered the garment, Shimon's position is "I do not know whether I returned your property," which creates liability. But where Reuven too is uncertain, that principle does not apply. Both parties are uncertain, and Shimon has no Torah oath, so the rule that one who cannot swear must pay does not apply. Shaar Mishpat therefore concludes that the matter requires great study and that the practical ruling should not follow Mabit. See there.
