This indicates that had he actually placed it under his head and it was stolen, he would be exempt according to all views, even as a paid custodian, because the case there involved a paid custodian. In truth, however, Darchei Moshe and Sema greatly abbreviated Maharik. Maharik's case was Reuven, who hired Shimon to travel to Venice on an errand. Shimon slept in a room with other respectable people, thieves entered at night and stole clothing and purses from three of them, and Shimon's purse contained both his own money and Reuven's. Reuven demanded payment from Shimon as a paid custodian liable for theft and loss, while Shimon counterclaimed that Reuven should reimburse his personal loss because it occurred on Reuven's errand. Maharik rules that Reuven is plainly exempt from Shimon's loss, but Shimon is liable for Reuven's money because he was a paid custodian. Certainly according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who holds that a paid custodian is liable even when money buried in the ground is stolen, Shimon is liable here. Even according to the dissenting view that a paid custodian is exempt for theft of money properly buried in the ground, the exemption applies only where the money was buried, the highest available form of guarding, leaving nothing more he could have done. Here it was not a complete unavoidable accident. He could have placed the purse under his head, tied it to his arm, or hidden it in a place a thief would not find. Maharik adds that even an unpaid custodian might be liable in such a case because it resembles negligence. From this it appears that exemption for placing the money under his head applies only according to the view that a paid custodian is exempt from theft occurring through unavoidable accident. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak and those who follow him, including Rosh, Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and apparently Sema in siman 303, even placing it under his head would not exempt a paid custodian, no more than burying it deep in the ground. It also appears that even according to the dissenting view, placing it under his head would exempt him only in Maharik's case, where he slept in a room with respectable people. If they were not respectable, the custodian would be liable because the situation began with negligence toward those sleeping with him. See Rama in this seif. Baer Heitev's citation of Sema concerning loss on the road appears to omit a word. It should read that if the money was lost while being carried on the road, he is exempt, meaning because he placed it where he ordinarily keeps his own money, as Shach states. Baer Heitev's reference to siman 292, seif 10, concerns the case where some money is lost and it is unknown to whom it belonged. See also Responsa Galia Masechta, siman 13, concerning one who gave money to a wagon driver to buy salt in the market and paid him to bring it to his city. The driver camped with a caravan in a field, put the money in his outer garment, laid the garment beneath his head, and slept on it. The garment and money were stolen. Galia Masechta rules at length that the wagon driver must pay. Going to sleep at the normal sleeping time is not an unavoidable accident, and he is liable even according to those who disagree with Rabbeinu Yitzchak. Placing money inside an outer garment and then under one's head is not proper guarding for money. Maharik's case was different because he slept indoors among respectable people. Moreover, Maharik said the purse itself should have been under his head, without the garment, making it less visible. Here the garment protruded visibly from beneath his head while he slept on the road in a field, where travelers and thieves are common, especially at night. Strict law therefore requires payment, though in such cases it is proper to act beyond the letter of the law, as stated at the end of Bava Metzia, chapter HaOmanin, and by the Tur at the beginning of siman 304. He further notes that Darchei Moshe and Sema's citation of Maharik is puzzling because in siman 303 they did not cite the dissenting view and appear to accept Rabbeinu Yitzchak that a paid custodian is liable even for theft through unavoidable accident. Their purpose here must therefore be to teach that even an unpaid custodian may be liable because the conduct constitutes negligence. Baer Heitev also cites Shach's objection to Responsa Mahari ibn Lev, who limited the rule that one deposits with his wife and children to an unpaid custodian. In Bedek HaBayit, Beit Yosef also cites Ritva that way. But Shach above in siman 72, seif katan 134, cites that Bedek HaBayit and disagrees, noting that Nimukei Yosef explicitly applies the rule to a paid custodian as well, and that the students of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef imply the same. Ketzot HaChoshen explains that even Ritva and Rashach agree that a paid custodian who transfers the deposit to his wife or children is exempt if there are witnesses that an unavoidable accident occurred in their possession, or if the first custodian himself saw it and can swear to it. Their point is only that the wife or children's own oath cannot, by itself, exempt the first paid custodian. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, end of siman 15, makes a similar distinction, cited below in seif 26, seif katan 21. See Nachalat Tzvi for an extended discussion of these laws.
