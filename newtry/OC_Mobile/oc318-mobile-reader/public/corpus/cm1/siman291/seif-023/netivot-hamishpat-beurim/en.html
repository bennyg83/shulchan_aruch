All the more so she would have been careful. See Shach, seif katan 33, who writes that a paid custodian is liable because he should have explained the matter to his mother. At first glance that reason seems unnecessary. A paid custodian who hands the item to his mother, who is only an unpaid custodian, and it is ultimately stolen, should plainly be liable because he reduced the level of guarding, as explained below in seif 26. If Shach means he is liable even for a later accident, while mere reduction of guarding would exempt from accidents, that also seems incorrect. If an unpaid custodian is exempt on the ground that the failure to inform the mother is not full negligence but only comparable to theft, then a beginning comparable to theft followed by a true accident remains exempt. The correct reading appears to be that Shach means a case where both the first custodian and the mother are paid custodians. Then the first custodian can be liable because his failure to explain the special circumstance caused the loss. This can be so even according to the opinion in seif 24 that the first custodian is otherwise exempt where the second has no means to pay. Shach also says that the mother's assumption was not that she simply did not wish to trouble herself. He is explaining why every custodian cannot make such an excuse. Ordinarily a depositary is personally obligated to guard and cannot say he assumed someone else would do it. A mother dealing with her son's property is different because she may reasonably assume that her son does not insist on strict procedures with her and habitually allows her to handle matters in the ordinary family manner. Thus she can say she thought he would not object.
For he did not tell her. See Shach, seif katan 34, who says the mother must swear a hesset oath to the son even though he is a relative. In my view, according to Rama's first opinion in the next seif, the son may actually be liable to pay. According to his own account, the mother was negligent, and she denies it. That resembles a second custodian who lacks means to pay, as explained in siman 176, seif 31. On that view the mother must swear to the son. Shach's discussion is therefore best understood according to the second opinion, or in a case where the son lacks means and the depositor seeks to impose an oath on the mother based on the son's claim. Shach concludes that the depositor can impose the matter through gilgul shevuah. At first glance this is difficult because siman 94 rules that one does not use gilgul to impose an uncertain claim unless there are grounds supporting the suspicion. But as I explained in siman 94, a custodial oath is itself imposed on an uncertain claim. Once the principal oath is of that type, other uncertain matters can be included through gilgul. Shach also says the son is not interested testimony because even if he says he did not inform his mother, he remains exempt. Although the case may be one where witnesses establish the accident and he would otherwise be exempt from an oath, this does not make him interested merely because he would prefer that his mother swear and perhaps choose to pay. If the truth is that he never told her, why should we assume she will refuse to swear truthfully?
The custodian shall swear that these were the coins. This is the d'oraisa custodial oath, as Rosh writes. Whether he must also include that he did not commit shelichut yad will be explained in seif 26.
And his mother shall swear that she hid them. In my view this oath is not imposed on the mother as an ordinary custodial oath. Tosafot at the end of HaChovel and Ramban in Milchamot write that merely depositing an item with another person, or saying, "Hold this utensil for me," does not make him a custodian until the owner expressly says, "Guard it for me." I discussed this at the beginning of the siman. All the more so, members of one's household do not automatically become custodians merely because one's personal property is left with them. Thus the mother does not ordinarily bear a custodial oath. According to Rabbeinu Tam, however, who holds that the first custodian must pay if the second was negligent and has no means to pay, the mother's oath can exempt the son. He entrusted himself to the reliability of his household, and her oath can establish that the event occurred in a manner that exempts him. According to the view that the first custodian is independently exempt, the mother's oath must instead be for her own exemption. In that case we must establish that the son actually told her, "Guard it for me." This resolves Tosafot in Bava Metzia 42. Tosafot cite Rabbeinu Tam that the first custodian is liable when the second was negligent and lacks means, and then say, "Therefore here his mother also swears." At first glance their statement implies that without Rabbeinu Tam's position the mother's oath would be inexplicable. According to what I have written, that is exactly so. Tosafot follow Ramban's principle that family members are not automatically custodians over one's personal effects. Only if the son disclosed that these were deposited funds, thereby showing that this particular item required guarding, is that like saying "Guard it for me." Under Rabbeinu Tam, however, her oath is needed to exempt the son because the depositor entrusted himself to the son's household.
A specified object. Elsewhere, if one sues two borrowers and each denies liability, the claimant can require both to swear; the oath of one does not suffice for the other. There the claimant says with certainty that the one who swore falsely denied his own debt. Here the claimant is uncertain which household member was responsible, and by depositing with a person who ordinarily entrusts property to his household, he effectively accepted the reliability of each. Therefore the oath of one can suffice in the relevant circumstance. One should not object that Rashi's reason for exempting the second custodian regarding money is that he may say he did not bury it because he needed ready access to the money, while that rationale does not apply to an ordinary object. The case of an object must therefore be one where the family member can likewise say that he reasonably needed access to it, in circumstances where such placement is considered ordinary guarding for the son's own property.
