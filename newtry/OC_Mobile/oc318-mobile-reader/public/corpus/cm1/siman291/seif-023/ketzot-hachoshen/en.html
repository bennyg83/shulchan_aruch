But if it is a specific identifiable object, the second custodian swears. Maggid Mishneh, citing Ran, says that when the second custodian knows the object, his oath can establish the loss. Ritva disagrees and requires the first custodian himself to swear that he actually delivered the object to the second. The dispute turns on how to characterize the first custodian's plea. If giving the item to the second person, where the owner customarily entrusts that person, fully removes the first custodian's responsibility, then saying, "I delivered it to that authorized person," resembles saying, "I returned it." It is not itself the Torah oath of custodians, and the second person's testimony concerning what he received can suffice. But according to Rosh and Tur, the first custodian can remain liable if the household member or usual substitute is insolvent and negligently loses the item. On that view, delivery to the second person is not complete return. It is part of the first custodian's performance of his custodial duty, namely placing the deposit in an appropriately guarded location. He must therefore swear that he actually made that delivery. Only after that does the second custodian enter the relationship and become competent to swear concerning the later accident. Thus Maggid Mishneh's approach fits the view that the first custodian is completely discharged by proper transfer to the authorized substitute, while Ritva fits the view that the first custodian's own custodial oath must first establish the transfer. There remains difficulty in Maggid Mishneh's distinction between money and a specific object. If the authorized transfer completely discharges the first custodian even if the second later acts negligently, then once the first credibly states that he delivered the money to the authorized substitute, why should lack of identification by the second matter at all? The point requires further study.
