And he accepted responsibility to guard it, etc. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 23, concerning a woman who took her sister's small child in her arms to go with him into the street. The child's mother placed a coin in the child's hand to play with, and the coin was later lost. The mother demanded that her sister pay because she had relied upon her to guard it. Tzemach Tzedek ruled that the woman is exempt, because when the mother gave the coin to the child she said nothing to the aunt. This is worse than merely saying, "Place it down," and the aunt never accepted custodial responsibility. Even if the aunt later took the coin from the child's hand and returned it to him, she remains exempt, because from the outset the mother knowingly exposed the coin to loss. Shaar Mishpat, above at the end of siman 96, questions this ruling because Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and Sema in siman 349, seif 2, imply that a husband must take the oath of a paid custodian even though he never expressly accepted responsibility, because he is presumed to accept it. So too here, when the sister placed the coin in the hand of a child lacking legal understanding, she presumably relied upon the aunt to guard it, and the aunt presumably accepted that responsibility. Shaar Mishpat therefore leaves the practical law unresolved. In my opinion there is no difficulty, because Bach in siman 349 explains the husband's oath on the basis of Rambam, cited by Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 346, that when a wife informs her husband that an item is borrowed, he steps into her place because a husband has the status of a purchaser with respect to his wife's property. That reasoning does not apply here. In particular, the author of Tzemach Tzedek was a student of Bach and generally followed his rulings. See what I wrote on this in siman 349.
