And he is not liable to pay. All custodians pay according to the value of the item at the time of the negligent act. See Ketzot HaChoshen, who cites Ramban and Ritva distinguishing a robber, who pays according to the value at the time of the robbery, from a custodian, who pays according to the value at the time of the negligence. Ketzot questions them and writes that even according to the view that a borrower becomes liable from the time of meshichah, this means, as Ritva explains, that his assets become retroactively encumbered, but the actual payment obligation does not arise retroactively. This explanation helps only when the item fell in value. But if it rose in value, the difficulty remains: why should a custodian be better off than a robber, who, where the item breaks on its own, can have to pay according to its current higher value? This is why Ramban and Ritva labored to distinguish the cases. In my view the matter can be resolved simply. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 56, s.v. "obvious," write that a robber is not a custodian. If part of the stolen goods rots, he can be exempt. His liability in a case of change arises because the change effects acquisition, making restoration "as he stole it" impossible, and he must therefore pay money under the verse concerning what he stole. Thus the robber's liability for later accident does not arise from custodial responsibility. It arises from the original robbery. We therefore assess him from the time of the robbery. A custodian is different. He accepted responsibility specifically to pay if negligent. His legal liability therefore arises at the time of the negligence, and he pays according to the item's value then.
