The owners withdrew from guarding. Wagon drivers are custodians when the owners of the goods have withdrawn from guarding them. If the owners of the goods themselves travel with the wagon, there is ordinarily no complete withdrawal by the owner. Nevertheless, at inns, especially in winter, it is well known that travelers often do not attend to the wagon and horses at all, while the wagon driver guards his own property there and therefore effectively guards all the goods. He may sleep outside on the wagon and ensure that the inn gates are locked. Siman 291 contains a dispute where the owner was initially present. Perhaps that case concerns a guardianship that actually began while the owner remained present. Here, while the owner is still attending the wagon there may have been no beginning of custodianship at all, and each time the owner later leaves and distances himself from the wagon a new guardianship may begin. This requires further study. If the owner changes the inn and the driver ordinarily lodges elsewhere, perhaps the driver never accepted guardianship at the new inn. If it is uncertain whether the theft occurred before arrival there, the driver can say, "I do not know whether I ever became liable." If the owner lawfully seized money before the doubt arose and knows with certainty that the theft occurred at the inn, the result may be different. Those who lease a mill generally may not refuse to grind grain brought there, particularly grain of local residents. Their practical power is usually only to choose the order of customers. Consequently, merely leaving grain at the mill does not place ordinary custodial liability upon the lessees, even if it remains for a long time and even if they employ a watchman to protect their own interests, arrange the grinding order, and maintain the mill. If they say "leave it before me" and the owner departs, that phrase ordinarily means that the grain will not be rejected from grinding, not that the lessee personally accepts full custodianship. Even according to the authorities who sometimes impose guardianship through the words "leave it before me" without a formal transfer into the custodian's property, known commercial custom controls the meaning of the words. The actual miller who handles the grain may be different. If he expressly says "leave it before me" and the owner departs, he may become a custodian according to the view that neither meshichah nor placement in his courtyard is required. Yet the ordinary conditions of a mill define the standard of care. The miller is constantly occupied feeding grain to the stones and removing flour. The premises are open to many people and he ordinarily cannot exclude them. He sleeps according to the normal intervals of his work, and on Shabbat he may not be present. A custodian need ordinarily guard only in the manner that people guard their own property under comparable conditions, and depositors are presumed to entrust subject to the ordinary household or business routine. The fact that the miller is paid for grinding does not automatically make him a paid custodian for the grain in the strongest sense. His paid status may arise only from the ancillary benefit that artisans receive from being entrusted with work. At times when the mill is constantly busy and there are more customers than he can serve, that special benefit may not exist at all, perhaps leaving him only a gratuitous custodian. It is possible that the general dispute concerning artisans as paid custodians rests precisely on whether that incidental benefit suffices. There is no need to apply a rigid rule where the factual benefit is plainly absent. If, however, he receives extra compensation precisely because a customer's work remains pending or occupies his facilities, he may become a genuine paid custodian according to all opinions. Even then, his obligation is defined by the known custom of the mill unless an express condition imposes a higher standard. Thus, if grain sacks must be moved to make room for later arrivals, the miller is not necessarily liable because other customers moved them. He is occupied with flour and grinding, people act quickly, and ordinarily he cannot prevent every person from moving sacks even if he is physically present. If a sack tears during such ordinary movement, it is difficult to say that he alone should have moved it. Protection from mice may likewise be limited by the practical conditions of a water mill. The Shach notes that a principal method of protection from mice is the placement of water, and at a mill the mice and the water arrangements are part of the environment of the grinding process. If grain remained unground for an extended period and the miller told its owner that he would pay any loss caused by the delay, it still requires study whether this statement creates custodianship. The phrase "leave it before me" may apply primarily to the initial placement of the object, or at least to the owner's act of abandoning his own supervision in reliance on the custodian. Where the grain has already been lying there without the owner's supervision for many days, a later promise may not create guardianship without a new kinyan or act of transfer. A protected courtyard can serve as a mode of acquisition for purchasers and correspondingly for custodians, but the place where grain stands in a public mill may not be the miller's rented private courtyard at all. Any customer may have a right to place grain there. At best it resembles a jointly used courtyard, which does not readily effect acquisition between users, and the premises are functionally available to all customers according to the daily rental arrangement. One might alternatively seek liability from the law of guaranty. Some authorities hold that if one says, "throw a maneh into the sea and I will pay," he becomes liable as a guarantor. If the owner leaves grain in an unguarded place because the miller promises to pay the resulting loss, perhaps that promise creates similar liability. Yet ordinary guaranty generally arises at the moment the money is given. Even after a debt becomes due, a statement to leave the money with the borrower can fail to create guaranty if no new consideration is supplied. If the miller never told the owner to leave the grain there, and both knew that the owner would not remove it even if grinding were delayed, while the owner merely urged him to grind promptly and the miller said he would pay for delay, the words do not resemble "leave it before me" at all. If the place is not the miller's courtyard, there may be neither custodial liability nor guaranty, because the owner did not newly deliver or abandon anything on the strength of the promise. This requires further study.
