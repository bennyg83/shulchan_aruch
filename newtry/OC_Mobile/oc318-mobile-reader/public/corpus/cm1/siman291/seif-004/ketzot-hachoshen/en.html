I accepted only the safeguarding of a silver dinar. The Gemara distinguishes one who was told that a gold coin was silver. If the custodian negligently loses it, he pays only the value of silver because he accepted only that level of custody. If he actively damages it, he pays its true value, since he had no right to damage another's property and should have considered that the owner may have understated its value precisely so that he would agree to guard it. The distinction is not that an active tortfeasor is always subject to a stricter standard merely because he acted with his hands. Rather, custodial liability itself rests upon acceptance. The Torah imposed the various levels of liability upon custodians because they voluntarily accept those responsibilities. If the owner concealed the true nature of the object, the custodian never accepted responsibility for that extraordinary value. This explains why even Rabbi Yehudah, who can impose liability for hidden items in fire damage, agrees that a person who accepted storage for barley need not pay for concealed wheat. In the storage case liability depends upon the scope of the accepted guardianship. In fire damage, by contrast, the Torah itself imposes a duty not to cause harm, independent of consensual acceptance. Therefore, where the owner acted negligently by misleading the custodian, the custodian can say, "I accepted only the ordinary safeguarding of what you told me it was." But where the object was deposited without such misleading conduct and its value later rose, the custodian bears the value at the time of negligence. A custodian who accepts an object without qualification accepts the normal possibility of appreciation. The same is true of valuable stones, whose very nature is commonly expensive. Accordingly, where a sword or other object was deposited without any misleading statement by the owner, the better rule follows Maharshal: the custodian is liable according to its actual value at the time of the breach. Only where the owner himself created the misunderstanding, as in the gold coin described as silver or wheat concealed under barley, may the custodian limit liability to the lower level of custody he actually accepted.
A further question concerns the finder of a lost object who reasonably thinks the object is silver although it is actually gold, or a thief who steals golden objects coated with silver without knowing their value. In those cases there was no owner who misled the finder or wrongdoer. If the limitation in the gold coin case rests on the owner's fault in misleading the custodian, then a finder, thief, or robber should not have that defense. The Torah imposes the finder's custodial duty without bargaining, and the owner did nothing to narrow its scope. Still, there is another issue. Acquisition based liability often requires an act of meshichah or another legally meaningful taking. If the person did not know that the hidden gold existed, perhaps his hand did not acquire that hidden value. Rama rules that one who acquires what he thinks is tin but which contains unknown gold may not acquire the gold because he had no intent to acquire it. This consideration is especially strong for theft and robbery, where liability for unavoidable accidents follows from the wrongful acquisition. It may also affect a finder according to Rambam, who requires the ordinary modes of acquisition for custodial responsibility. Even according to Tosafot and Rosh, who generally impose custodial responsibility once the owner has withdrawn from guarding, a finder seems to require actual gathering under the verse, "You shall gather it into your house." Merely seeing the object does not make him custodian. Therefore, where the hidden nature of the valuable object was truly unknown, the applicability of full custodial or robbery liability requires study. A separate issue is an object whose market value is ordinary but whose owner faces an extraordinary claim from a gentile if it is lost. In my view a custodian is liable only for the object's own monetary value, not for a special personal exposure of the owner. Such additional loss is only indirect damage. Custodial laws apply to property whose body is money. Even documents are excluded because their bodies are not money. Therefore an exceptional outside claim is not part of the custodian's ordinary liability, and even direct tort liability for that additional exposure is doubtful.
