Only the guarding of a silver dinar. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if a custodian learns before the loss that the coin was really gold, there is a distinction between an ordinary custodian and a borrower. The custodian accepted only the responsibility of silver and remains exempt for the greater value, while a borrower who continues using it after learning the truth becomes liable for the gold. In my view even the borrower is exempt for the added value. The lender granted permission to use the object while the borrower accepted only the liability of silver. Learning later that it is gold does not automatically cancel the permission to use. The owner had already accepted the arrangement under which the borrower would bear only the silver-level obligation. Ketzot also questions a finder-custodian: since the Torah places guarding upon him involuntarily, perhaps one cannot say he accepted only silver-level custody. The answer depends on the broader dispute. According to Ramban, the dispute between R. Yehudah and the Sages, and Rav Ashi's question in Bava Kamma 62, concern primarily the special enactment for a victim of theft or damage. As to ordinary payment, one who physically burns another's property is liable even where he did not anticipate its true value. Only a custodian may be exempt because he accepted no greater level of responsibility. On that approach, a finder-custodian can indeed be liable for the full value because the Torah imposed his custodial duty. According to Tosafot and Rosh, however, the dispute concerns the actual obligation to pay, and even a direct damager can be exempt where he had no reason to anticipate the hidden value. When one is told that an item is silver and damages it, liability follows because he should have understood that the statement may have been made for purposes of limiting or defining custody. But a custodian remains exempt beyond the level he accepted. Likewise, one who gave permission to stack wheat but the other concealed it with barley can be exempt because the permission itself implied only the ordinary anticipated risk. On this approach, even a finder-custodian is exempt for hidden value that he had no reason to anticipate. The Torah's involuntary duty to guard cannot be broader than the Torah's ordinary liability for direct damage in a case where the hidden value was unforeseeable. Mechaber in siman 418 rules explicitly like Tosafot, that even one who burns another's property can be exempt where he had no reason to know its true value. See Shach there and Rama in siman 388, seif 1. All the more so, a finder-custodian is exempt for the unanticipated excess value. Ketzot also questions one who steals gold covered with silver and the item is later lost through accident. He suggests that because robbery requires an acquisition and siman 232, seif 18, rules concerning tin containing hidden gold that the first purchaser did not acquire the gold when he did not know of it, perhaps the robber likewise never acquired the gold-value and is not liable for it. In my view a robber is certainly liable. At minimum he acquired the body of the object as an act of robbery to the extent of its apparent silver value. He became a robber of that physical object. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 56 explain that a robber's accident liability follows because the object becomes his through the robbery framework and subsequent changes. Once the object is changed, the robber acquires even involuntarily, without any intent to acquire the change. The same is true where a stolen lamb becomes a ram. Since no special intent to acquire each hidden component is required, lack of knowledge that it contains gold does not remove robbery liability for the body of the object. Moreover, siman 232 itself concerns a different rule. The hidden gold was not something ordinarily expected to be found, and Tosafot in Bava Metzia 26 compare such hidden treasure to something effectively lost. A lost object requires that it actually reach the finder's hand in the relevant legal sense. Thus, after the first buyer had already transferred the tin onward and the second person discovered the gold, the second person could acquire it. But so long as the original holder physically retains the object, the hidden component remains legally associated with him. One who takes the entire object from him unlawfully is therefore a robber of it and liable accordingly.
