If they have no means to pay. Even according to the opinion that exempts the first custodian, this applies specifically to an unpaid or paid custodian. A borrower who transfers the borrowed item to another borrower whom the owner regularly allows to borrow, although such transfer is permitted under siman 342, remains liable to the lender if the second borrower has no means to pay. The Gemara's reason for exempting a custodian who transfers to another custodian is that he handed the object to a competent adult. That rationale fits ordinary custody. An unpaid custodian's obligation is only to place the property in a guarded location. Once he gives it to another competent custodian, he has completed the guarding required of him. Machaneh Efrayim therefore writes that an unpaid custodian who later borrows an object from the depositor does not thereby create shemirah b'baalim merely because he still has the earlier deposit. Once he has placed the deposit in proper guarding, he has no continuing labor obligation. By contrast, a paid custodian must continually sit and guard and can therefore be considered engaged in the owner's work. Even a paid custodian can be exempt when he transfers to another paid custodian, because he can appoint a competent substitute to perform the guarding. If the first custodian's inability to guard arose through accident, this is certainly sufficient. Even according to authorities who impose liability where the paid custodian himself ceased guarding because of accident, appointing another paid custodian can be treated as though he continued guarding personally. A borrower is different. His liability extends even to an unavoidable death of the animal, something no degree of human guarding can prevent. His obligation is therefore not merely a duty to guard but a personal payment responsibility imposed by Torah. Handing the object to a competent second borrower does not discharge that personal obligation. All the more so, if the borrower hands the object to his wife or children, the lender cannot be assumed to have substituted their personal liability for that of the original borrower. The lender relied on the original borrower's own obligation. Thus an unpaid or paid custodian can be exempt even if the second custodian was poor from the outset, because the first performed his own guarding duty by transferring to a competent person. A borrower remains personally liable if the second borrower cannot pay.
