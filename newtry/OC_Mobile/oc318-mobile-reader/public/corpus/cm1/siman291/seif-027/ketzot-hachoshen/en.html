Shach cites Mahara Sasson, who was uncertain whether an unpaid custodian who conditionally accepts a borrower's liability must also take the ordinary custodial oath. See what I wrote on this issue in siman 301, seif katan 3.
He becomes liable through words alone, without a kinyan. The reason an unpaid custodian can accept a borrower's liability merely by verbal stipulation is the benefit he receives from the owner's trust. That benefit supplies the seriousness needed to create the obligation. This explains the incident where Rav Sheshet asked that garments be brought to him, and after the bearers had already received them they were asked to accept responsibility for road accidents. They wanted a formal kinyan. Why was verbal acceptance not enough? Because the benefit of being trusted had already been received. The goods were already in their hands. The new assumption of liability therefore lacked the present benefit that ordinarily supports a verbal enlargement of custodial responsibility. The same applies where a custodian initially receives an object for a term and later enlarges his liability while the owner still could have reclaimed it. The continuing benefit of being trusted can support the new verbal obligation. But where the possessor has a fixed right to keep the object for the term, such as a renter, the owner cannot simply repossess it. The original benefit has passed, and a later verbal enlargement of liability should require a kinyan. This distinction explains the sources.
