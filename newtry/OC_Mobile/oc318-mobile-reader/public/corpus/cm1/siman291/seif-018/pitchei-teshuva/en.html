And in a protected place. Responsa Shivat Tzion 99 discusses a guardian who placed government paper notes in a paper wrapper and then in the inner pocket where he kept his own money. The responsum rules that this is negligence because common practice was to place such notes in a leather wallet or portfolio and then place that wallet inside the breast pocket. Thin paper notes placed only in a paper wrapper can easily slip out when a person repeatedly reaches into his pocket, without his noticing. This is not the enhanced level of care required of a guardian. A person may take such a risk with his own property, but not with another's. Therefore even a gratuitous guardian is liable for this negligence.
And according to the time and place. Responsa Zichron Yosef, Choshen Mishpat 1, concerns Reuven and his prospective son-in-law, who deposited a sealed package of dowry money with Shimon for thirty days. Shimon undertook to store it and placed it in a locked chest containing expensive clothing and some silver utensils. When the deposit remained with him beyond the agreed period, soldiers entered at night and plundered the chest. The questioner argued that Shimon was negligent because he should have placed the money in his iron strongbox with his own funds, because he or his wife sometimes permitted a servant or other household member to open the ordinary chest, and because the chest was insufficiently protected against fire. He further argued that once enemy forces were known to be approaching, even a place normally sufficient for storage was no longer adequate. Zichron Yosef rejects most of these grounds as insufficient to extract money. Earlier authorities recognize many secure storage places even where the guardian's own money is not kept there, and contemporary custom itself determines reasonable safeguarding. A locked chest used for valuables could therefore qualify. The fact that household members sometimes opened it does not by itself make the guardian negligent, since a depositor is presumed to entrust property with awareness that the guardian's wife and household are involved, though they may need to take the appropriate guardian's oath and show that minors were not allowed access. Nor is an ordinary chest comparable to a highly flammable hut merely because it is not fireproof. Nevertheless, Zichron Yosef agrees that Shimon is liable on the final ground. Once it became publicly known that soldiers and plunderers were approaching, the governing standard of care changed and he was required to move the money to a more hidden and protected place. Although it had been reasonably guarded when first deposited, failing to relocate it after learning of the danger was negligence. The expiration of the thirty-day term did not terminate his custodial duty automatically. Until he told the depositors, before witnesses, to take back their property because he no longer wished to guard it, he remained obligated to protect it. Since he did not do so, he must reimburse the full dowry. See the responsum for the related claim that if Shimon refused to pay, the father-in-law should provide a replacement dowry.
