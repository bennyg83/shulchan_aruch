Seif 26. A custodian who transfers an entrusted item to another custodian is liable. Note: It is uncertain whether the first custodian may impose a ban requiring the depositor to affirm that he genuinely does not trust the second custodian's oath, rather than fabricating distrust merely to make the first custodian pay. The first custodian cannot exempt himself simply by saying that he does not know whether the depositor believes the second custodian. Since he certainly accepted the duty of custody and became liable for it, while it is uncertain whether he was discharged by the second custodian's protection and oath, this resembles 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' If the depositor truly does not trust the second custodian, the first must pay. This supports the Shach's view here that even a custodian can fall under the rule of 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' Yet where a defendant says, 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' we rule that the claimant must first accept a ban. Does the depositor likewise have to accept a ban that he genuinely distrusts the second custodian? Likewise, if in his heart he does believe the second custodian, may he nevertheless say, 'I do not believe you'? In Yoreh De'ah 197, where one inwardly believes another, he may not claim disbelief. Perhaps the same should apply here. I have not found an explicit ruling. I also question a finder of lost property, who has custodial status although the owner did not appoint him. If he transfers the lost item to another custodian, does the rule of one custodian transferring to another apply? The principal rationale is that the owner may say, 'I trusted you to swear, but I do not trust him.' Yet the owner never voluntarily entrusted the item even to the first person; the Torah made him a custodian. Why should the second be different? A similar doubt arises where a member of Reuven's household, or a complete stranger, took Reuven's item and gave it to Shimon to guard, and only afterward was it discovered that the item belonged to Reuven. If Shimon then gave it to another custodian, is Shimon liable? Reuven seemingly cannot say, 'I trusted you,' because he never knowingly entrusted it to Shimon. Even if one argues that Reuven steps into the rights of the person who entrusted it to Shimon, there remains a case where that intermediary commonly entrusts items to the second custodian but Reuven does not. Do we follow the true owner or the person who delivered the item? In all these cases, it seems to me that since the first custodian's liability rests on his being obligated to swear and being unable to do so, whenever he does not know that the depositor himself knows for certain that the item was lost, and it is likewise unknown whether the depositor would have trusted the second custodian, the first custodian remains obligated to swear that it was lost. He cannot swear, because it was not lost in his hands, and therefore he must pay as one who is obligated to swear but cannot. Accordingly, in the case of heirs, where the depositor died before litigation, in the case of a finder of lost property, and in the case where the person who delivered the item regularly entrusted items to the second custodian but the true owner did not, the first custodian must pay because he cannot take the required oath. However, if the depositor genuinely trusts the second custodian in his heart, it would be theft for him to claim otherwise. It therefore appears that he must first accept a ban affirming that he truly does not trust him. The matter still requires study, because the poskim are very terse.
Regarding the case above where another person delivered the item. I believe there is a distinction. If Shimon knew from the outset that the item belonged to Reuven, he became Reuven's custodian, and if Reuven does not regularly entrust property to the second custodian, Shimon is liable even if the intermediary does. But if Shimon did not know when accepting the item that it belonged to Reuven, he can say to Reuven, 'I never became your custodian; I accepted it only for the person who gave it to me, and that person regularly entrusts property to the second custodian.' Relative to that intermediary Shimon need not swear, and since he never accepted custody from Reuven, he has no Torah oath toward Reuven and the rule of an oath obligation that cannot be fulfilled does not apply. If he did not know initially but later learned that the item was Reuven's and nevertheless retained it, I remain uncertain whether he thereby becomes Reuven's custodian.
