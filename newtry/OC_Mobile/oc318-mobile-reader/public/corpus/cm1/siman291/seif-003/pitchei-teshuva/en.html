Netivot HaMishpat explains that people do not ordinarily object to mere passage through a courtyard, so permission to place property there can mean only, 'sit here and guard it yourself.' In a house, where entry itself is normally subject to objection, granting permission more naturally means, 'enter, and I will guard it for you.' Responsa Zichron Yosef 1 discusses a custodian who claimed that when asked to store a dowry he had said in German, in substance, 'Why should I not take it in? It costs me nothing,' intending that he accepted no law of a guardian but merely allowed use of his home as a kindness. Zichron Yosef rejects the claim. The case is at least like 'leave it before me,' and in context it is stronger: the parties said they were depositing the dowry with him until the wedding and then would return home to another town. Plainly, if he did not guard it no one else would. His expression that he would 'take it in' naturally means that he would keep it. Nor did the statement that it would cost him nothing constitute a condition exempting him from liability. It was merely an expression of confidence that no loss would occur. A true condition would have required explicit conditional language such as 'on condition that I incur no loss.' Therefore he is unquestionably a gratuitous guardian and is liable for negligence.
