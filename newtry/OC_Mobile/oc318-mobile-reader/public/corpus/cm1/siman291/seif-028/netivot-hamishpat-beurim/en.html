The custodian is entirely exempt. Nevertheless, he retains the legal status of custodian for shelichut yad. Scripture excluded shemirah b'baalim from payment liability, not from the very category of custodian. Bava Metzia 41 derives that shelichut yad is liable even where the owner was with him. The Gemara says that one might otherwise argue: just as a borrower is exempt when the owner is with him, perhaps shelichut yad should also be exempt. This cannot mean merely that the owner happened to be present at the later moment of shelichut yad, for then once the custodian unlawfully takes the item he is a robber, and robbery is plainly not exempted by the owner's presence. Rather, the Gemara's initial thought is that an object originally received under shemirah b'baalim might never create ordinary custodial status. The verse teaches that the exemption is only from payment duties arising from the guarding, not from the status needed for shelichut yad. This differs from land, slaves, and documents. Those are excluded from the Torah's custodial framework itself, and therefore shelichut yad can likewise be excluded there. It follows that even under shemirah b'baalim the custodian should have to swear the principal oath that the object is not still in his possession. That oath reflects his underlying status as custodian, and if the object is still present he must return it. Ketzot HaChoshen cites Tosafot in Bava Kamma 57, who question whether double payment applies to a false theft claim under shemirah b'baalim, and Tosafot at the beginning of Merubah, who connect double payment and oath. This does not prove exemption from the oath. Tosafot merely note that both are written in the same statutory context and may be excluded together by a particular verse. There is no identified exclusion here removing the principal oath. Ketzot also cites Yerushalmi at the end of Shevuot, where R. Zeira says he swears and R. Chanina says he is exempt. That too does not prove a general exemption from oath. The Yerushalmi is discussing the oath that he was not negligent, and the view exempting follows the position that negligence under shemirah b'baalim is itself exempt. Indeed, the sugya supports the opposite conclusion. It implies that if negligence would create liability, an oath would be required. Thus the statutory exemption is not from oath as such. Accordingly, the principal oath that the item is not in his possession remains required even where shemirah b'baalim applies, while the oath against negligence follows the substantive liability for negligence. After writing this I found the same principle explicitly in Beit Yosef, siman 60, quoting a responsum of Ramban: even with shemirah b'baalim there is liability for shelichut yad and an oath concerning it. This is exactly the rule stated here.
