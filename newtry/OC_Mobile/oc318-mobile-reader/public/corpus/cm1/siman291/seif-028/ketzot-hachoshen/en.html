If the owner was with him, he does not pay. I was uncertain whether a custodian who enjoys the Torah exemption of "the owner was with him" must nevertheless take the custodial oath. Tosafot appears to assume that such an oath can exist. They discuss the possibility of double payment where a paid custodian claimed armed robbery while the owner was with him. Double payment for a false claim of theft ordinarily requires an oath taken in beit din, which suggests the custodial oath still applies even though payment liability is exempt. On the other hand, Tosafot elsewhere teaches that the laws of the custodial oath and double payment are closely linked. Where Scripture excludes a class from the oath, it also excludes the false theft double payment. If the "owner with him" exemption removes double payment, it may likewise remove the oath. The verse itself says only, "If the owner is with him, he shall not pay," which sounds like an exemption from payment rather than from oath. The matter therefore remains uncertain.
I later found that the Jerusalem Talmud in the fifth chapter of Shevuot expressly records this dispute. Rabbi Zeira says that where the owner was with the custodian, the custodian still swears; Rabbi Chanina and Rabbi Ulla say he does not swear. It is surprising that the early and later authorities did not cite the passage decisively. The practical law therefore remains unresolved.
Tosafot at the end of Perek HaZahav says that when Scripture excludes land and similar property even from the custodial oath, a person cannot obligate himself to the full ordinary custodial law merely through words. A kinyan is required. In the usual case of an unpaid custodian accepting borrower's liability, the public treats him as though he truly borrowed the object, and that benefit of trust creates the obligation. If, however, the custody was with the owner present, and if we accept the view that there is no custodial oath in such a case, neither rationale applies. Even an actual borrower with the owner present is exempt. The public therefore cannot regard the arrangement as an ordinary borrowing that naturally entails liability, and there is no underlying oath structure. Accordingly, a custodian exempt because the owner was with him should not become liable as a borrower merely by words; a formal kinyan would be needed. See what I wrote in siman 340.
