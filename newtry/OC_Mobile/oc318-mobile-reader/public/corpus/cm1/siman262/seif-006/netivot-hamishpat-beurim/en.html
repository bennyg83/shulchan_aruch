Presumably the owners became aware, so this is not despair without knowledge. Ketzot HaChoshen, seif katan 1, cites Beit Yosef and Taz that in matters of mitzvah we can say, "Since he is pleased now, he was pleased from the outset." On that basis he writes that if one forgot to nullify chametz before the prohibition and tried to nullify after the sixth hour, it should work retroactively because now he is clearly pleased that the chametz not belong to him. This must not be accepted. Even without this principle we know the person does not desire ownership of prohibited chametz. A person does not desire to acquire a prohibited object, and after the prohibition takes effect the chametz is not ordinarily within his legal possession at all because it is forbidden in benefit. The Torah nevertheless places it against his will within his responsibility for purposes of the prohibition, as Chazal say: two things are not within a person's possession, yet Scripture treats them as though they were. What difference, then, does it make that we know he was never pleased to own it? The Torah itself places it in his responsibility against his desire until he actually nullifies it in the legally required manner, by speech or in his heart before the prohibition makes such nullification ineffective. Therefore the Gemara's rule that nullification after nightfall is ineffective cannot be displaced by this satisfaction doctrine.
