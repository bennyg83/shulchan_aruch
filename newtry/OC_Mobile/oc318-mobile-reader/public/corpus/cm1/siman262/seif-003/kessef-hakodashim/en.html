It requires great study whether knowledge of the loss by a wife who regularly conducts the household's business can create despair on behalf of her husband when the husband himself knew nothing of the missing household property. It appears that her status as one who conducts the household does not help. Where her act gives her husband no benefit, such as where she causes damage, her business authority does not make him liable merely because of her act. Here too there is no benefit to the husband in her saying, "alas for the loss," so as to empower her to remove his property from his ownership through despair. Although he presumably wants her to know of the loss so that she can search for it, he certainly does not want her to stop searching and despair. Even in a place where most people are gentiles and despair ordinarily follows immediately upon knowledge, he may prefer continued searching, since some gentiles return lost property and it may not even be known where the item was lost. Thus, until the husband's own despair is established, the item should remain the property of the original owner. More fundamentally, the doctrine that a husband is presumed pleased with his wife's business acts applies where her act brings him benefit from another's money. It should not be extended to an act that simply destroys his own ownership. There is also room to say that the special halachic acquisition through despair in a lost object does not apply to a ben Noach. Despair itself is not full hefker. In other contexts it does not transfer ownership without an additional change of possession or similar factor, whereas in a Jewish lost object the Torah's laws of aveidah permit acquisition after despair. A gentile therefore may not acquire through mere despair in the same fashion, and certainly a gentile who finds a Jew's lost object does not acquire it through the Jew's despair unless the owner made an explicit complete abandonment. Even in a case rescued from the tide of the sea, which is treated as complete abandonment in Jewish law, the parties may still be governed by their civil law where that law recognizes the original owner. Dina demalchuta operates according to the practical and public reach of the government's law. Where the matter is entirely hidden from enforcement, there may be a different consideration. This requires further study.
