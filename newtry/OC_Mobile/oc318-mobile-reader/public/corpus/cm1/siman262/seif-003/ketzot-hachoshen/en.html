The Gemara challenges Abaye from the law that where an unauthorized person separated terumah and the owner later said, "Go to the better produce," the terumah can be valid. Rava answers for Abaye that the case must involve prior appointment as agent, since terumah requires the owner's knowledge. Beit Yosef therefore questioned Rambam, who rules that even one who entered another's field without permission, gathered produce, and separated terumah can have his act validated by the owner's later statement, "Go to the better produce," when better produce was indeed available. Beit Yosef concludes that Rambam follows the Kiddushin sugya, which implies that where the later response shows satisfaction, we infer that the owner was pleased from the outset. In matters of mitzvah, prior actual knowledge is not always required because a person is presumed pleased that a mitzvah be done with his property. Taz reaches a similar conclusion. The sugya of Elu Metziot is displaced by later sugyot showing that mitzvah based satisfaction can be inferred retroactively. There is strong proof from Eruvin. Beit Hillel permits cancellation of courtyard rights after dark and compares it to the statement, "Go to the better produce." Rashi explains that from the person's present satisfaction we infer that he was already satisfied earlier but had merely forgotten. This clearly works even though no prior agency appointment existed. The same principle explains why authorities permit use of another person's etrog or sukkah without permission when there is no damage, because a person is presumed pleased that a mitzvah be performed with his property. Tosafot says that ordinary property may not be taken without the owner's knowledge merely because one knows he would later agree, since that resembles unknowing despair. Mitzvah benefit is exceptional. I once used this principle as an additional ground in a case where a person, through unavoidable circumstances, forgot to nullify chametz before the prohibition began and remembered only afterward. Since the accepted practice is to nullify and people ordinarily intend to do so, there was room to say his later nullification reveals that he wished the nullification earlier but forgot, like cancellation of courtyard rights after dark. One might thereby distinguish the ordinary rule that chametz cannot be nullified after the prohibition begins, which speaks where the owner knowingly failed to nullify earlier. Nevertheless, because the practical authorities in Orach Chaim do not rule this way, I did not rely upon it except as a secondary consideration in a case containing other grounds of leniency.
Even Beit Yosef's explanation of Rambam through mitzvah based satisfaction does not fully solve the wording where a person entered another's field, gathered produce for himself, and then separated it. In such a case the mitzvah of separating terumah from the owner's produce does not rest upon the taker, and his taking creates a monetary loss. The general presumption that a person wants mitzvot done with his property does not extend to material loss. Taz proposes that the case means he took the entire amount solely in order to separate it, leaving nothing for himself, so his later conduct shows that his original gathering was for terumah. I find this insufficient. If he separated terumah for his own produce using another's produce, the owner's mitzvah is not being performed, and a person is not presumed to consent to a monetary loss merely for another's mitzvah. Shach in siman 358 adopts a different approach. He disputes Tosafot's comparison to unknowing despair and says that if it is certain the owner would consent, one may use the property even without present knowledge. Unknowing despair is different because if the owner knew where the lost object was, he would not despair. Terumah and ritual impurity require actual knowledge for separate statutory reasons. On Shach's approach, taking for oneself can be permitted where the owner's consent is certain even without a mitzvah rationale. Yet Shach's view conflicts with Tosafot, Hagahot Ashri, and Mordechai. A better solution follows Ramban's analysis of a thief or robber whose terumah and consecration can take effect through change of name after despair. Ramban explains that the real acquisition is the change of name itself. Despair is needed only so that the owner's continued pursuit does not prevent that new legal name from taking hold. Once the owner would certainly consent and would not pursue the property, the change of name can operate. Thus where the unauthorized taker gathered produce for himself and made it terumah for his own produce, the acquisition can be completed by the change of name. Although the owner did not yet know, once it is certain that upon learning he would consent and would not pursue the property, there is enough basis for the new legal status to attach. This differs from one who merely separates terumah for the owner, where no acquisitive change of name in the taker's favor exists. The reasoning also accords with Tosafot regarding myrtle branches taken from gentile land, where later awareness of the cutting is not required once the essential despair already exists. So too here, once the owner is certain to consent, the change of name can perfect the taker's acquisition.
