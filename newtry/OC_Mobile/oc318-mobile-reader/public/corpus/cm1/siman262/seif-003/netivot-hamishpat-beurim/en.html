Since it came into his possession before despair. See Shach, seif katan 1, who writes that the same is true when an object enters one's courtyard before despair. He says similarly in siman 268. At first glance this is very difficult from the rule later in this siman concerning a merchant who finds money after enough time has passed for it to become mixed with his own produce. There the owners have already despaired, yet the money entered his courtyard before despair. If courtyard acquisition before despair prevented later acquisition, it should remain despair without prior knowledge. This is especially difficult for the authorities who do not accept Tosafot's special distinction that a courtyard does not acquire something not expected to be found there. They explain a found treasure in a wall through despair without prior knowledge, which implies the courtyard could otherwise acquire before despair. In my view Shach's rule applies only when the person now seeks to acquire by virtue of the courtyard itself. If another finder discovered the object and the courtyard owner claims, "My courtyard already acquired it for me earlier," then he necessarily treats the courtyard as his legal hand and agent. Since it came into that legal hand before despair, he cannot acquire through it. But if the owner expressly does not want courtyard acquisition and intends to acquire only by later lifting, the courtyard does not acquire for him. A courtyard is no stronger than acquisition through another person, and where a person protests against an attempted acquisition made for him, he does not acquire, as Bava Batra 137 teaches. Thus the courtyard need not become his agent or hand against his will. It is as though it never acquired at all, and he can subsequently acquire by lifting after despair.
