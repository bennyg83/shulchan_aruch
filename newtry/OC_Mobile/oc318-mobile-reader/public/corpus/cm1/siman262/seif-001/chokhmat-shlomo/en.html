Seif 4. One saw an object fall from two or three people, and if it has no identifying mark it shall remain until Elijah comes. Note: The wording is imprecise. It begins with both two and three people, yet then says that if there is no identifying mark the object should remain, apparently without asking them. Why not ask them and perhaps determine ownership? The case must be one where enough time has elapsed for the owner to have checked his pocket. If the object belonged to only one of three companions, he would ordinarily despair because the other two could have taken it, so the finder could keep it. It must remain only because perhaps the three were partners, in which case the owner would not despair. Therefore the finder should not ask them, because they could collude, falsely claim partnership, and divide the object among themselves in order to remove it from the finder. If there is an identifying mark, the truth can be established through the mark. Without one, their joint statement is not reliable. This reasoning applies to three people. With two people, however, even if the object belongs to only one, he does not despair because he can bring a claim against the other. The finder should therefore ask them. If one admits that it belongs to the other, it must be returned, since there was no despair. The Tur, source of this ruling, clearly distinguishes the case of two from that of three. The Mechaber's language is compressed, but necessarily must be understood in this manner.
