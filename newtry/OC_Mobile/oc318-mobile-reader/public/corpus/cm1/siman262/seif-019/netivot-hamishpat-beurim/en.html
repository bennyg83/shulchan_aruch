If the circumstances indicate. Ketzot HaChoshen asks how near or far scattered produce must be from the vessel in order to be attributed to it. In my view the measure is practical: any distance to which the produce could reasonably scatter when the vessel fell is considered near. Ketzot compares this to Avodah Zarah concerning stones near a Markulis, where seven cubits are called near. That is not a strong comparison because stones are thrown and can land farther away. Ketzot also challenges Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 17, who writes that a purse or ring found near a corpse is not sufficient proof of identity because perhaps the object lay there from before. Ketzot says this is just like fruit found near a vessel. The cases are not comparable. In a lost-property case, the claimant says, "This particular vessel with this fruit was lost from me." We already know he had this fruit in that vessel at the time of loss. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 22 say we do not suspect him of inventing the claim; the only concern is perhaps these fruits fell from someone else. Since the fruits are located where they could naturally have spilled from the identified vessel, we attribute them to that vessel rather than suppose that his actual fruit disappeared and unrelated fruit happened to be there. The circumstance operates somewhat like a place sign. How else would he know to describe precisely this type of fruit as having been inside precisely this vessel? A corpse with a purse lying nearby is entirely different. We do not know this corpse ever had that purse. Two unrelated events can occur near one another: this person was killed here and another person lost a purse here. Nearness alone does not identify the purse with the corpse. The same is true if the owner of the vessel died and the heirs merely prove the vessel belonged to their father but know nothing about the fruit. They cannot claim the fruit merely because it lies nearby. Nor, if two lost items are found near one another, can proof of ownership of one automatically establish ownership of the other. Only where the claimant himself says this fruit was inside this vessel, and the opening of the vessel points toward the fruit, do the circumstances corroborate his account. Moreover, one cannot freely compare monetary identification rules with matters of personal-status prohibition, as Bava Metzia says, and Even HaEzer similarly requires stronger linkage, such as an item actually tied to or contained with the relevant object.
