An object whose owners have despaired may be benefited from. It appears that despair does not remove an item from the owner's domain until it reaches the hand of an acquirer. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 116 discuss Rav Safra, who each night sent a donkey to a lion. When his turn came and the lion did not eat the donkey, Rav Safra rushed and reacquired it. The Gemara asks why he needed a new acquisition, since he abandoned it only for the lion and not for everyone. Tosafot ask from the rule that one who rescues from a lion or bear acquires because the owner despairs. The distinction becomes clear from Tosafot in Bava Kamma 66, who say despair is not complete ownerlessness. If it were, even a robber who received the item unlawfully before despair could later acquire it from ownerless status. The difference is this. True hefker removes the object from the owner's domain even before anyone else acquires. We rule against R. Yose that hefker is effective even without a later acquirer, and the former owner himself would need a new act of acquisition to regain it. Ye'ush is different. It does not fully remove the object from the owner's domain until another person acquires it. If the owner despairs from an aveidah and the despair ceases before anyone else acquires, he need not reacquire it; his ownership automatically resumes in practical effect. This explains Tosafot's proof. If despair alone fully removed the item from the victim's ownership, then after the victim despaired from stolen property the robber should at least be able to say that the body no longer belongs to the victim, even if the robber himself cannot acquire because it came to him unlawfully. The victim could then claim only value, not the object. Since that is not so, despair plainly requires a later lawful acquirer. Scripture itself supports this. Hefker derives from the verse concerning abandonment of the field in the sabbatical year, which does not depend on acquisition by another. Ye'ush, by contrast, is derived from the lost-property verses, where "you find it" implies that the object comes into a finder's hand. This resolves Tosafot's question about a slave whose owner says, "I have despaired of so-and-so, my slave." The slave is already in his own possession and therefore functions as the later acquirer. It also resolves their question why despair does not exempt produce from tithes according to the view that despair alone does not transfer to a robber. While the property remains only in the robber's unlawful possession, no lawful acquisition has occurred, and the produce remains legally the owner's, just as under R. Yose hefker before acquisition would remain the owner's for certain purposes. Likewise, there is no contradiction between rescue from a lion and Rav Safra's donkey. In a rescue case, the owner's despair remains in force until the rescuer lawfully acquires, so the rescuer gains title. In Rav Safra's case, the lion did not take the donkey and the basis of despair ended before anyone acquired it. Rav Safra therefore remained owner and did not truly need a new acquisition if the matter was only ye'ush. The Gemara therefore explains that the case involved a form of intentional abandonment directed specifically to the lion, which can have a different rule. The same distinction resolves the case of a donkey swept by a river that later came up by itself. Where the property is utterly beyond rescue, it can become genuinely ownerless. But where rescue was possible with difficulty and the owner's conduct showed despair, the status is only ye'ush. If the donkey comes up by itself before another person acquires, the despair has ended and the property remains the owner's. This is why the Gemara compares that case to Rav Safra and requires the additional reasoning that the owner's affection remained upon the donkey. Thus the practical principle is that ye'ush is unlike complete hefker. Until another person lawfully acquires, the property remains legally associated with the original owner.
