Who stole it. The Jerusalem Talmud excludes theft from an unsecured rooftop but treats theft from a protected roof like theft from a house. It further says that an object stolen from a borrower, paid custodian, or renter is still regarded as stolen from the owner because the custodian's possession is legally the owner's possession. The Bavli and the practical authorities, however, do not appear to adopt the distinction concerning an unprotected rooftop. They exclude primarily theft from a thief, where the original owner cannot presently consecrate the object because it is outside his legal control. If the owner's property is merely sitting in the public domain or in an unsecured place, he can still consecrate it. Therefore theft from such a place remains theft from the owner for double-payment liability. An object standing in a meadow can likewise be subject to the custodian's rights. An unsecured rooftop should be no worse. The Jerusalem Talmud's limitation therefore does not appear to govern the accepted law.
