If it is no longer extant. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 177, discusses a minor sent from a festive gathering to bring wine. He was given money to pay for it, but found the cellar open, took a large quantity without paying, and much wine was also spilled and lost. When the owner later learned what happened, he sued the minor after he had grown up for the damage and value of the wine. At first glance siman 349 indicates that if the stolen item is no longer extant, the minor is exempt even after reaching adulthood. Shevut Yaakov explains that Rambam's source states specifically that where the stolen object was lost, the minor is not liable for the principal even after he grows up. Where, however, he personally benefited from another's money, he certainly must repay it, as explained at the end of siman 235 seif 15 and in Sema there. Moreover, even for what was lost, if he wishes to satisfy his obligation in the heavenly court he should pay something, because Orach Chaim siman 343, based on Terumat HaDeshen siman 62, teaches that a person who committed a transgression as a minor should undertake repentance after reaching adulthood. In matters between people, the essence of repentance is to appease the injured party and compensate the loss, although beit din does not compel this. Shevut Yaakov rejects Beit Yaakov's criticism of Terumat HaDeshen and Rama. Nevertheless, even for heavenly purposes the former minor need not necessarily pay the entire amount. Terumat HaDeshen and Rama speak of accepting some measure of repentance. The injured party should therefore not be cruel and should accept a reasonable appeasement, in keeping with the enactment encouraging wrongdoers to repent. Sefer Chasidim, siman 692, appears to require full repayment after adulthood, but perhaps that is only an act of piety or a case in which the stolen property still remained in the person's possession. See also above siman 96 seif 3 and Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, end of siman 147.
