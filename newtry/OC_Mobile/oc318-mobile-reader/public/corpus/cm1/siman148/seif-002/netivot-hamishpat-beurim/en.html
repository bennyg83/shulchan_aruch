One person who purchased them from four. There are several serious difficulties here. Tosafot in Yevamot 36, s.v. i shatket, ask in the case of four people who purchased from one seller why the single seller cannot simply refund the value of the lost path. Their answer is difficult in my view, especially in light of Sema, seif katan 2. More fundamentally, where one purchaser acquires from four sellers, land cannot be stolen by acquisition, and among the purchaser's fields there is certainly land that does not belong to him. Why should he not be obligated to restore it merely because he acquired whatever legal right each seller possessed? If one bought animals from several people and witnesses established that one animal was stolen, although they could no longer identify which animal after it became mixed with the others before ye'ush, surely the buyer could not retain everything merely because property not his own had become mixed with his. Nor can the buyer be exempted on the ground that he originally acquired under a beit din ruling. If Reuven stole land without witnesses and denied the claim successfully before beit din, and Shimon later bought it from him, but after the purchase Shimon learned conclusively that the land was not Reuven's, Shimon must return it. Bava Batra 30, with the statement, "Are you not admitting," shows this. The purchaser cannot answer, "Had you remained silent, I could have kept it." Indeed, if it were known that among the four sellers there was definitely stolen land, purchasing from them would itself be prohibited because the forbidden item is fixed among the group. Why, then, does the Mishnah speak only of a person who lost a path? It could have taught a stronger case where a person lost his actual field among other fields, such as where he traveled overseas and one of four surrounding owners absorbed his field into his own, but it is no longer known which one. The Shach himself writes at the end of his book Tekafo Kohen that if one of two people with the same name stole a field from Reuven, and the witnesses do not know which namesake was the robber, and afterward one namesake sold all his fields to the other, Reuven may recover one field from the remaining holder. This is so although Reuven cannot prove which person stole or which field was stolen. The Shach is correct, because Bekhorot 48, concerning two people with identical names against whom a promissory note cannot be collected, explains that if one such person bought a field from the other, the creditor still cannot proceed by a dilemma only because a person's assets are merely guarantors for his debt. This implies that where the claimant can proceed directly against the actual property by a dilemma, he may do so. Accordingly, one purchaser from four sellers is exactly analogous to the Shach's case of one namesake who stole and sold to the other. The purchaser cannot say, "I bought all the rights of each seller." The reason the claimant could not extract the property from any individual seller was only his inability to identify which one was liable. Once all their property is held by one person who necessarily has the claimant's property, that evidentiary obstacle should disappear. A seller cannot transfer what is not his, and the property remains in the chazakah of its original owner. The rule of an established judgment does not change this. Terumat HaDeshen explains that the principle that a judgment remains in place rests on a form of ye'ush, because the claimant has diverted his attention and despaired of recovery. As I explained at the end of the rules of seizure, this concerns a matter unlikely ever to become clarified, where ye'ush can apply. Even there, however, an established judgment applies only when the holder originally obtained the property permissibly, as in the case of doubtful sons of a yavam, or the case in the gloss to siman 268 where one acquired permissibly under a beit din ruling. Where property came into a person's possession through robbery or prohibition, the principle does not apply, because ye'ush alone is ineffective when the recipient obtained it unlawfully. It therefore appears that the present law applies specifically to a lost right of passage. The body of the land belongs to someone else; the claimant merely had an obligation imposed on the landowner to allow him passage. Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot Shekhenim explains that this is only an encumbrance. Since the claimant cannot determine which seller personally bore that obligation, he cannot enforce the personal obligation against any of them, and the purchaser likewise bears no asset lien for it. Once the primary obligation cannot be enforced, the lien on the property also falls away. The land itself belongs entirely to the purchaser; the only obligation would have been to permit passage when needed. This resembles the rule at the end of siman 49 that when the creditor cannot sue the seller, he cannot sue the purchaser either. This also resolves Tosafot's question in Yevamot why, where four people bought from one, the seller need not repay the value of the path. According to the opinion in siman 107 that one who sells to a non Jew land encumbered to a Jew is exempt for damaging the lien when he performed no act in the land itself, the seller here is certainly exempt. Even according to the other view there, he may still be exempt because a right that has no independent market value and is worth money only to this particular person is not treated as property for which a tortfeasor must pay. Bava Kamma concerning a wife's contingent financial interest indicates this: if that interest belongs to the husband, the defendants can say to the wife, "What loss did we cause you, since had you sold it your husband would have taken the money?" One might ask that the contingency still has subjective value to her equal to what a buyer would pay. The conclusion must be that damage to something that cannot itself be sold for money does not create ordinary monetary liability. Tosafot in Ketubot 30, regarding one who forces food into another's mouth, likewise write that the item is not worth a perutah, although it has value to the eater who is not repulsed by it. The reason is that something for which money cannot be obtained is not considered marketable value. A private path right likewise has value only to the owner of the dominant field and may therefore not generate damages. If the path right would pass to a purchaser of the claimant's field, one can establish the case where the servient owner could refuse to permit a subsequent purchaser to use the route. Tosafot may have found such an establishment forced and therefore answered differently. Where the body of land itself was lost, however, the law follows what was stated above. The Gemara in Ketubot 109 concerning the declaration, "a palm tree to my daughter," says that the palm tree is among the heirs' property, and Rashi explains that its identity is unknown. This does not imply that if the particular tree given as a gift had once been identified the heirs would be free to keep it after its identity was forgotten. Rashi merely explains the wording of the Gemara. If its current holder were known, the daughter would proceed against that holder alone. If the tree was identified when given but its identity was later forgotten and the heirs divided the grove, all heirs would have to return the value proportionately, because each took a share of the daughter's tree. For example, if four brothers divided twenty palms and each took five, each took one quarter of the daughter's palm in the aggregate, since had she first removed her tree each brother would have received five less one quarter. Likewise, if the heirs sold the palms to four purchasers, received the proceeds, and divided the money, where the gifted palm had been identified initially but its identity was later forgotten, the heirs must reimburse the daughter because each received a share of its proceeds. If they cannot pay, she proceeds against the purchaser who bought last, under the laws of collecting from purchasers. If all purchased simultaneously, she proceeds against them like a creditor, as in siman 111, seif 15. If the palm was never identified at the time of the gift, however, the last purchaser must return an actual palm while earlier purchasers are exempt. The rule is that a transferee receives no less and no more than the transferor's right. Since the seller could have directed the daughter to whichever palm he wished, the purchaser can direct her to a palm remaining with the seller, as explained in siman 119, Sema, seif katan 10, concerning intermediate and inferior land. If all purchased simultaneously, she may recover the palm from whichever purchaser she chooses, and that purchaser may seek reimbursement from the seller, just as a creditor may choose among simultaneous purchasers under siman 111, seif 15. Here the assets were encumbered to provide the body of one palm. The same analysis yields a practical rule. Suppose Reuven had a right of passage through Shimon's house, and Shimon had the option to designate the route anywhere he chose, without having fixed a particular location. Shimon then sold the eastern half of the house to Levi and the western half to Yehudah. Levi wishes to build from the south up to the courtyard and tells Reuven to use a route through the northern side, while Yehudah wishes to build from the north to the middle and directs Reuven to the southern half. Reuven would otherwise be left without a route. If Levi and Yehudah purchased successively, the obligation of providing the passage falls only on the later purchaser. The rule that a transferee acquires the transferor's rights allows the earlier purchaser to direct the claimant toward the area remaining with the seller, just as in the intermediate and inferior land case. If they purchased simultaneously, Reuven may obtain a passage through the house and both purchasers share the obligation. They must then reach an arrangement between themselves about where that passage will lie, just as Rashi explains in the case of the unspecified palm tree, where all bear the obligation because its particular identity is unknown. This differs from the lost path case in our seif. There the path had once been specifically fixed, so a person whose field did not contain that route owed the claimant nothing and derived no benefit from the claimant's property. Here the original route was never fixed; there was simply an obligation on the seller to provide a route wherever he chose. When both purchasers acquire simultaneously, that obligation falls upon them jointly. If one already built permanently on the southern side while the other remained silent, leaving Reuven's practical route on the north, and the other later demands demolition so that the passage be supplied partly from both properties, the matter appears to depend on the dispute in siman 153 whether such servitude rights require three years and a claim or whether immediate acquiescence constitutes waiver. Since the first purchaser performed a permanent act whose obvious purpose was to place the passage on the other side, silence may constitute waiver. This is even stronger after a sale to a third party, because we plead for a purchaser that the arrangement was established through a sale. The case here of one purchaser from one seller must also be understood in circumstances where we cannot plead that the seller previously acquired the place of the path from the claimant, for example where three years had not elapsed.
If I return it. See Shach, seif katan 2, concerning the death of the four sellers. There appears to be a textual error. It should read that the purchaser is not required to give him a path, and that this is not comparable to siman 119. According to the text before us the comparison is difficult. In siman 119 the reason is not that a transferee acquires all the transferor's rights, since the seller himself could not originally direct the creditor to inferior land. The purchaser's power there rests on his ability to return the document of purchase for the inferior land. If the sellers die, he can no longer return it in a way that restores the former legal position, because returning it to the heirs is effectively a new transfer, as explained there. Here, however, the principal reason is that stated by Nimukei Yosef in Perek HaCholetz: the purchaser acquires all the rights of his seller. Since each seller could have deferred the claimant, the purchaser can do so as well, even after the sellers die. This is clear. I later found that Ketzot HaChoshen rules likewise for another reason, and the result is certainly correct.
And if he took possession of a route. See Shach, seif katan 3, who disagrees with the Sema and holds that seizure is effective against all of them because the claimant says with certainty that this is his path while they are uncertain. In my view the Sema is correct. When the dispute first arose, the landowners were in possession and the claimant could not extract property from them because of their monetary chazakah. A later seizure in the presence of witnesses does not help. Even with movable property, where seizure can sometimes be effective, it is ineffective where the holder says, "I do not know whether I ever became obligated to you." Certainly the same is true where the claimant asserts definite ownership of identified movable property while the holder says he does not know. All the more so with land, where ordinary seizure does not change chazakah. The Shach's statement should therefore be limited to a case of one person who purchased from several sellers, where seizure of one route can be effective. Although seizure ordinarily does not help with land, this resembles Bava Metzia 116 concerning the upper story of a house. Tumim writes in the rules of seizure that where one item must necessarily leave a person's possession, and the uncertainty concerns only which item, the ordinary principle that land remains in its owner's chazakah does not apply. Here too the purchaser is certainly obligated to provide one route; seizure can therefore identify the claimant's path when he says with certainty, "This is my route." If the doubt arose before either party had established possession and both make definite claims concerning the route, the rule is that the stronger party prevails. If one claims with certainty and the other is uncertain, the definite claim prevails.
