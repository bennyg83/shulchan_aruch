The Shach writes that if the four original owners died, the purchaser must now provide the claimant a path, analogizing to the law where returned property to heirs changes the collection structure. I do not accept the analogy. There, if the property is returned to heirs, the rule barring collection from encumbered assets while free assets exist no longer applies in the same way because the relevant assets were acquired only after the father's death. Here, even if the land were returned to the heirs of the four sellers, they still would not have to give the claimant a path, because the uncertainty among four parcels remains. The Shach's conclusion can perhaps be supported by another route. Tosafot distinguishes one purchaser who bought from one owner from one purchaser who bought from four owners. A purchaser who bought all parcels from one owner cannot manufacture a new defense by later selling them to four people, because before his purchase the claimant could have sued the one owner. But a purchaser who bought from four owners inherited a position in which the claimant already could not identify which owner owed the path. Such a purchaser can preserve that inherited defense even through later transfers. One might therefore argue that he need not return specifically to the original sellers. Yet I later found Rama's responsum stating explicitly that in the lost path case there is no distinction whether the original sellers are alive, and the purchaser's defense remains. This confirms that the Shach's limitation was unnecessary.
Sema says that where a person can gain by returning each deed to its seller, he may argue as though he had returned them even without actually doing so. Perishah likewise says the doctrine applies where there is some benefit. The benefit that matters, however, is not the returner's own benefit. The principle works where the opposing party would actually be better off if the deeds were returned, and therefore cannot insist, "Return them first." In the classic superior and inferior land case, if the purchaser returns the inferior land to the debtor, the creditor would collect only inferior land; while the purchaser keeps it, the creditor can collect medium land. The purchaser therefore says, in effect, "If you accept my position, I will save you the worse result." So too in the lost path case the purchaser can say, "If you cooperate, I will sell you the path at a reduced price." The present nonreturn creates a benefit for the claimant. Sema's explanation focusing on benefit to the person holding the deeds therefore misses the point.

Kesef Mishneh explains "if he took possession of the path" as forcible seizure against the field owner. Sema limits the effect chiefly to the case where one owner always surrounded the claimant and certainly owes one path, though he can ordinarily choose which path. If the claimant seizes one, possession can determine the choice. Sema says that where several owners derived from one or four sellers and no one necessarily owes a path, seizure does not help. Responsa Maimoniyot indicates more broadly that the defense, "I could return the deed to the original owner," ordinarily works only to maintain possession, not to extract. If the claimant has already seized the path, the field owner may therefore be unable to use that hypothetical return argument to remove it. On that theory, seizure can help even where one purchaser came from four sellers. Rosh's responsum, however, suggests that the hypothetical return doctrine can sometimes be used even to extract. He discusses a Jewish holder of a debt instrument transferred from a gentile and says the court can adjudicate as though the instrument were returned to the gentile, whose substantive rights would then be asserted. That is an extraction case. Thus the scope of the doctrine is disputed. Still, where the claimant has actual possession of the path, there is substantial support for leaving it with him if the owner's only response depends on a hypothetical return of deeds.
