The king. Even here, the King, the Holy One, blessed be He, decreed that just as the assailant acted and severed his fellow's limb with a sword, so should be done to him. We have learned, however, that the Torah's required equivalent act is monetary payment. Therefore, even after paying the value of the victim's lost hand, he must still pay for the pain caused by severing it. In principle he should pay the value of the pain of having a hand severed by a sword exactly as he caused it. But since the victim's hand has already been severed, while no person would accept an ordinary sum to have his own hand severed initially, the Sages instead estimated how much a person condemned to have his hand cut off by a sword would pay to have it removed painlessly by medication. From the verse, 'and he shall consume in another's field,' where the Sages teach that one assesses the damaged garden bed as part of the entire field, as explained in siman 394, we learn that the Torah had compassion on the damager and lightened the method of assessment. Thus writes the Rosh. Sema.
