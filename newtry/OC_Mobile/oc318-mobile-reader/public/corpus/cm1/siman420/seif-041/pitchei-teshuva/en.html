He receives forty lashes. See Tosafot, Kiddushin 28a, who explain that this is measure for measure: he said that his fellow had violated a prohibition, and therefore he himself is punished accordingly; see there. Magen Avraham, siman 610, seif katan 6, explains that the relevant prohibition is “A mamzer shall not enter” the congregation; see there and Machatzit HaShekel. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 181, writes that one should not ask what this means when the person shamed is unmarried, because the slanderer at least seeks to bring him to lashes once he marries. This is also explicit in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 8, siman 44; see there. Yam Shel Shlomo further writes that the rule of lashes is not limited to one who calls another a mamzer. Rather, it applies to any defamatory accusation that alleges violation of a prohibition. Chatam Sofer comments that, but for those words, he would have said that if one tells another, “You ate forbidden fat,” or the like, perhaps the person has already repented; likewise, the ancestors of a mamzer may have acted under compulsion or repented. The status of mamzer, however, is an enduring blemish, and the slanderer thereby suspects him of being destined continually to violate the prohibition against entering the congregation whenever he marries. Chatam Sofer further writes that the lashes are customarily redeemed with money. Mahari Weil states that the amount is forty gold coins, and the Rama likewise rules at the end of siman 2. Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 8, siman 49, writes that he found in the name of earlier authorities that the lashes are redeemed for eight Rhenish gold coins, and that this custom has the force of law even against the wishes of the person who was shamed. The printed text there reading five Rhenish gold coins is in fact a scribal error and should read eight Rhenish gold coins, as is also evident from Responsa Maharshal, siman 101, where he counts eight gold coins. See also Responsa Nachalat Shivah, part II, end of siman 66. Yam Shel Shlomo further protests the custom of distributing the fine to yeshivah students or the like. He rules that anyone who sees his work should tell the person who was shamed that the money belongs to him and that he may do with it as he wishes. Mahari Weil, however, at the end of siman 147, rules that the offender should give forty gold coins to charity, while the person who was shamed chooses how they are to be distributed. It appears to me that there is no dispute. When the offender impugned the honor of the person himself, the money belongs to that person, and he may even spend it on drink, as Maharshal writes. But where the offender impugned the honor of his ancestors, the descendant should not eat and rejoice from the degradation of his forebears. Rather, so that the payment should serve as atonement for the offender, the money should be distributed to charity for the benefit of the souls of those who repose in the earth. Nevertheless, the right to designate the recipients belongs to the son, who stands as their blood-avenger. Mahari Weil is explicitly discussing such a case. See further there. See also Atzmot Yosef on Kiddushin there, who cites a responsum of Ri Migash: if one called another “mamzer, son of a mamzer,” he incurs two sets of lashes; but if he said, “Your father and you are mamzerim,” he incurs only one set. The reason is that it is known that if a man is a mamzer, his son and descendants remain mamzerim through all generations. Thus, once he first calls the father a mamzer, he has already necessarily characterized the son and descendants as mamzerim. But if he first calls the son a mamzer, it remains possible that the father is fit; when he then adds the separate insult that the father too is a mamzer, he incurs a second liability. See there.
