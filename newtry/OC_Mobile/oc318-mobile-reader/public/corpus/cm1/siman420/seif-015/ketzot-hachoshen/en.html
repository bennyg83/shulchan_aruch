As though he were a slave sold in the market. Rashi explains the bodily-injury valuation as though the injured Jew were sold as a Hebrew servant. Nimukei Yosef follows him. Rosh instead says the person is appraised like a Canaanite slave. Rashi's view has consequences for the parallel law of one who says, "My value is upon me," where Sanhedrin says he is assessed as a slave. If Rashi consistently means a Hebrew servant, why does that valuation require nine people and a kohen as in land appraisal? A Hebrew servant is not compared to land in the same way as a Canaanite slave. This indicates that Tosafot, at least, understands the Sanhedrin valuation as a Canaanite slave, in accordance with Rosh. That explains why the valuation is governed by the law of land and requires nine Israelites and a kohen. Rashi can maintain his own position because he holds elsewhere that every human being is compared to land for the relevant valuation law. Therefore even valuation as a Hebrew servant can require the land-appraisal procedure. The two approaches are thus internally consistent.
