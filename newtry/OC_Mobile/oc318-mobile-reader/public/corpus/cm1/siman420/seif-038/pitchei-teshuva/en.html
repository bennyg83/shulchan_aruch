Some say that he may offer an explanation. See Responsa Chavot Yair, simanim 62 and 65, and Responsa Shvut Yaakov, part I, siman 179, which discuss this matter at length.
If he called him a slave or a mamzer, and it is true, he is exempt. See Responsa Shvut Yaakov, part I, siman 179. He writes that this applies specifically to calling someone a slave or a mamzer, a status that does not depend upon repentance and cannot be remedied; indeed, there is even a mitzvah to publicize it so that others will not marry into that status. This differs from disqualification because of a transgression, for which repentance provides a remedy. One should not shame transgressors, as stated in Sotah. Although Maharshal, chapter HaChovel, siman 47, appears to imply that whenever the humiliating statement is true no fine is imposed, Shvut Yaakov writes that he does not understand his words on this point. He found in the Agudah, chapter HaZahav, that greater stringency applies when one speaks the truth than when one lies, citing the prohibition against saying to the child of converts or to a penitent, “Remember your former deeds.” Although that proof is not conclusive, since one could say that it applies specifically where we know that he repented, or to a Torah scholar, who is presumed to have repented, but not to everyone else, Shvut Yaakov concludes that the principal rule is that even a truthful statement is subject to the law of humiliation whenever the speaker intends to shame the person rather than to prevent future wrongdoing or to bring him to judgment. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 62, likewise states this plainly; see there.
