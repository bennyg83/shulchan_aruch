If he spat at his fellow, he is liable. This is because of humiliation. If he spat on his clothing, or merely humiliated him verbally, he is exempt, because the act must be done to the person's body, comparable to the verse "and she seized him by his private parts."
They excommunicate him, for there is speech that is like sword thrusts, and there is no greater humiliation than slander and spreading an evil report. So writes Ashri. See also the end of siman 1. The Mechaber likewise wrote this without qualification there in seif 6, whereas here he writes it in the name of "some say." I have written many times that the phrase "some say" does not necessarily prove a dispute. When the Mechaber or Rama finds a view stated by an individual authority and it is not widely stated among the other decisors, they often cite it as "some say."
They give him disciplinary lashes. Because he violated the prohibition of verbal oppression. Since it is a negative commandment that involves no physical act, however, Torah lashes are not imposed.
"I am not an apostate," etc. Hagahot Mordechai writes that if one called another an apostate after the latter had repented, he violates the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom. If he did not know of the ban, he is not called a willful transgressor. Regarding a man who insulted a woman, Hagahot Mordechai to Kiddushin writes that he should fast Monday, Thursday, and Monday, sit barefoot before the synagogue, and request forgiveness at the bimah. See below, siman 421, seif 13, regarding a woman who insulted another.
Some say he may explain his words, etc. Mahariv further writes there that if he called him "one who humiliates women," he may explain that he meant one who humiliates them verbally. Likewise, if he called him "son of a zonah," he may explain that he meant "son of an innkeeper." Darkei Moshe adds in the name of Nimukei Maharam that if one witness says he called him a mamzer and another says he called him "son of a zonah," the testimonies combine with respect to the latter insult, because the lesser statement is included in the greater. Nevertheless, he does not receive the legal treatment associated with being called a mamzer, for perhaps "son of a zonah" referred to a woman who engaged in relations while unmarried.
Some disagree. Meaning, they hold that he cannot reinterpret his words. There, this was written regarding one who called another "Canaanite." Maharam writes there concerning a woman about whom a rumor spread that she had committed adultery in Tiberias, and someone shouted at her, "Tiberias, Tiberias," that this is not considered public humiliation, etc. See Darkei Moshe.
He should send his agent there. It concludes that the agent should take two people from the city where the deceased is buried, go to his grave, and request forgiveness on behalf of the person who insulted him.
But not for other humiliations. Meaning, there a ban is imposed following testimony, but even for insulting one who lies in the dust no oath is required concerning it. So it is there. See there.
One who calls an informer "son of an informer" is exempt. In chapter Arba Mitot the Gemara says: in accordance with whom do we call the wicked son of a righteous person "wicked son of a wicked person"? As it is stated regarding the daughter of a Kohen who profanes herself through immorality, "she profanes her father." See there.
One who calls his fellow a slave, etc. Maharam of Rothenburg writes that one who calls another a mamzer, slave, wicked person, adulterer, disqualified person, or vile person receives lashes. See what I wrote in the gloss to Sema in the name of Rosh. If he calls him one who violates a communal ban, an informer, or a thief, it is like calling him wicked, and he may descend against him. Rabbeinu Yitzchak explains that it is permitted to strike him because he violated the prohibition of verbal oppression. But if he calls him impure, a dog, or uses other insults, there is no fixed judicial penalty, though he must appease his fellow. Darkei Moshe. See the beginning of siman 421 concerning one who complains that his fellow informed upon him or stole from him. Darkei Moshe further cites Mahariv, siman 28, who ruled that one who injured another should be flogged in the synagogue between Minchah and Maariv, because one who raises his hand against another is called wicked and is liable to lashes. He also had to request forgiveness, and was ordered to give a silver zekuk to the injured person if he was poor; if he was wealthy, the money should be given to those who labor in Torah. A great authority similarly ruled concerning one who pulled out another person's hair that he should give him a silver zekuk. If there is a local custom, however, everything follows the custom. It appears to me that all of this was an emergency ruling based on beit din's assessment of the offender's sin. By strict law he is not simultaneously liable to both lashes and money, as explained above. Darkei Moshe.
