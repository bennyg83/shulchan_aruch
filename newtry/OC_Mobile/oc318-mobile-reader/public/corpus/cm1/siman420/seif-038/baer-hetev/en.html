To explain his words. Mahari Weil further writes that if one called another 'one who humiliates women,' he may explain that he meant one who humiliates them verbally. Darchei Moshe. It also cites Nimukei Maharam that if one witness testifies that he called the person a mamzer and another testifies that he called him 'son of a harlot,' the testimonies combine regarding the latter insult, because one hundred is included within two hundred. Nevertheless, he does not acquire the legal status of a mamzer, because perhaps he called him 'son of a harlot' where the mother was unmarried. Ibid.

A ban. But he need not take an oath, even regarding humiliation of those who dwell in the grave. So writes the Sema. See Maharshal, chapter HaChovel, at length concerning these laws. See also Responsa Maharit, Yoreh Deah, siman 16, who writes that language spoken in a rhetorical or witty manner does not necessarily constitute actionable humiliation. Thus, calling a group 'sons of Greeks,' or saying of someone who came from another place and became a judge, 'the stranger who is in your midst shall rise above you,' does not constitute humiliation even though the verse speaks of a gentile. The woman who described Rabbah by reference to his large belly was at fault, because she could have identified him by another sign. See there for his proof. Gloss in the Shach.
