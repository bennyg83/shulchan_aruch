Like an idle worker, etc. The Tur explains this law more fully. We assess what a worker in the victim's occupation would accept in order to sit idle rather than perform that work. The amount depends upon the nature of the work. A difficult occupation with low wages may require only a small payment to induce the worker to remain idle. An easy occupation with high wages may require much more. The Rambam adds that if the person finds idleness especially difficult, like the people of Mechoza, he receives his full wage. If an organ was permanently lost, the tortfeasor has already paid damage for the reduction in the victim's capital value caused by that organ. For example, if his hand was severed, he has already received compensation for the decrease in his market value. Yet after recovery he can still guard cucumbers. Therefore during the period of illness he receives loss of work according to the wages of a cucumber guard. If one leg was broken he can likewise later guard cucumbers. If both legs were broken, after recovery he cannot circulate through gardens as a cucumber guard, but he can sit in one place and guard an entrance, and his temporary loss of work is valued accordingly. The less work he will be able to perform after recovery, the greater the permanent damage award. If one eye was blinded, he may still be able to return to his original work after recovery, so temporary loss is based on that work. If both eyes were blinded, after recovery he may be fit only for grinding at a mill, and the temporary loss is valued accordingly. The Rosh writes that these examples concern ordinary people without specialized professions. If he is a skilled worker, we assess the temporary loss according to the work he can still perform after recovery. Thus a teacher who loses a hand or leg can still teach after recovery, and a pearl driller who loses a leg can still drill pearls. There is no reason to value his temporary loss as that of a cucumber guard. This explains the Mechaber's wording. When he says "he cut off his leg," he means both legs in the relevant clause, and when he says "he blinded his eye," he means both eyes. The Rambam's phrase that we calculate all the days of illness and pay him means that the entire estimated amount is paid immediately in one payment, as explained in the following seif.
