If he called him a slave or mamzer and it is true, he is exempt. See Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 179, who writes that this exemption applies specifically where he called the person a slave or mamzer, a status not dependent upon repentance and not correctable, and where there may even be a mitzvah to publicize the status so that others will not marry into it. This is different from insulting a person by publicizing a sinful act from which repentance is possible. One should not shame sinners unnecessarily, as indicated in Sotah. Although Maharshal, chapter HaChovel, siman 47, appears to imply that whenever the insulting statement is true no monetary penalty is imposed, Shevut Yaakov says he does not understand that position. He cites Agudah, chapter HaZahav, who writes that telling a harmful truth can be more severe than lying, and brings proof from the prohibition against reminding a convert's child or a penitent of his past. Although that proof is not absolute, because perhaps it applies only where repentance is known or where a Torah scholar is presumed to have repented, Shevut Yaakov concludes that truthful speech can still create actionable humiliation whenever the speaker intends to shame rather than to prevent future wrongdoing or to carry out lawful discipline. Chavot Yair, siman 62, states this plainly as well. See there.
