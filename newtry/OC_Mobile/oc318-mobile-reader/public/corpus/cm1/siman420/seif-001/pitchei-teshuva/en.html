To release him so that he may be counted in a minyan. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 182, concerning a wicked man who struck a Torah scholar with his fist until blood was shed. Unfortunately, the assailant had many supporters and no one sought to bring him to account. He was also in mourning for a parent and led the prayers. The questioner asked whether one should answer Amen, Yehei Shemei Rabbah, and Kedushah after him. Chatam Sofer answered that if the facts were as stated, the man was subject to the ancient communal ban until he accepted the authority of beit din and stood judgment for what he had done. Nevertheless, his blessings and prayers were not like the prayers of idolaters to which no Amen is answered. His blessings were directed to Hashem. The ancient ban was imposed as a punishment and disciplinary measure, excluding him from participation in sacred communal matters, so if he forcibly led the prayers the congregation should ordinarily refrain from answering Amen in order to discipline him and bend his stubbornness until he repented. In the case before Chatam Sofer, however, no one was prepared to punish or separate him and the congregation supported him. If he learned that a few individuals alone refused to answer Amen after his blessings, he would merely mock them. Those individuals would then lose the opportunity to answer Yehei Shemei Rabbah and Kedushah, while he would remain rebellious and add sin to transgression. In such circumstances, Chatam Sofer writes, we lack the power to restore religious discipline to its proper standing. If in the present generation we were to treat every person technically liable to excommunication under the enactments of the Sages as actually excommunicated, we would be unable to deal with much of the generation. He cites an even stronger precedent from Sanhedrin 60a and Rashi there, s.v. ba-zeman hazeh. See there.
