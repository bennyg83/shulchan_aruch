For it is stated, "lest he add," etc. At the end of Parashat Ki Teitzei it is written, "and it shall be, if the wicked man deserves lashes... forty he shall strike him, he shall not add, lest he add to strike him," etc. The Tur and Mechaber derive the prohibition from "lest he add," alluding to the principle that wherever Scripture says "beware," "lest," or "do not," it is a negative commandment. Rambam, in chapter 5 of Hilchot Chovel, derives it from the explicit phrase "he shall not add," and Rashi likewise explains on the Torah that this is the warning against striking another person. Rambam preferred the explicit prohibition "he shall not add." The Tur and Mechaber did not derive it from that phrase because it refers directly to flogging a wicked person who has already received thirty-nine lashes, where adding another blow may be dangerous. One might therefore think the prohibition applies only there. They instead derive the broader prohibition from the additional phrase "lest he add." See Perishah for further discussion.

Even though he did not strike him. For Scripture says, "Wicked one, why will you strike your fellow?" It does not say, "Why did you strike?" but "Why will you strike?"
There is an ancient ban upon one who strikes his fellow, and he must have it released, etc. The wording implies that there is no need to place him under a new ban now. Rather, the person who strikes another is already under the standing ban imposed by the early authorities, and one who violates it must now obtain release from that ban. It appears to me that the same applies wherever later authorities mention that communities imposed a ban concerning a matter, or that it is subject to the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom. It means that the violator is already under the existing ban and does not need a new declaration of excommunication. Since Rama states simply that "it must be released for him," this apparently means an ordinary annulment before three, as the Tur and Mechaber write in Yoreh Deah siman 334. This is unlike the view of one scholar who thought that whenever later authorities write that communities imposed a ban, release requires one hundred persons from three congregations in three countries. We find that severe requirement only for one seeking permission, because of necessity, to marry another wife in addition to his first wife. Apart from that exceptional case, we do not find such a requirement for other communal bans or for the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom.
