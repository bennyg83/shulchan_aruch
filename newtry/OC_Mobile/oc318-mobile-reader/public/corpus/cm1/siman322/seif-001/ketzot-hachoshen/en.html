If this occurred to most of the fields. Maharam bar Baruch was asked about a tenant who prepaid two years' rent and then fled the city with the Jewish population because of mortal danger. Maharam treated it as a regional calamity but divided the loss, because perhaps another Jew from the minority who remained could have rented the house. This differs from devastation of most fields, where the calamity struck the field itself and any other tenant would have suffered the same loss. In Maharam's case the house remained usable and the calamity struck the people. Still, his rule of dividing is difficult. If the question is merely whether another tenant could have been found, the landlord already holds the prepaid rent. Under our accepted law, property remains with its possessor in unresolved monetary doubts, even where the Gemara leaves a question unresolved. Accordingly, the landlord should not have to return half merely because of doubt.
