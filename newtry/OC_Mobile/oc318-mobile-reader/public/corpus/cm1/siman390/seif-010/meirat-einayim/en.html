Roosters are considered forewarned, etc. Until, "and it rolled and broke something, he pays half the damage." This is Rambam's wording, and it is obscure. See Rambam there, where he writes further details concerning a rooster with a cord tied to its leg, distinguishing who tied it and who owned the cord. Raavad objects to most of those rulings. Perhaps for that reason the Mechaber did not copy them. It is nevertheless difficult, because then he should also not have copied this clause, "if it rolled and broke something, he pays half the damage," for Raavad objects to that as well. Nor should Rama have described it as unexplained, since Rambam himself explains it there by writing, "When does this apply," etc.; rather, the other authorities disagree with him. It appears that the Mechaber copied the beginning of Rambam's words because they can be reconciled with the accepted halacha by explaining that the case concerns an ownerless cord and damage caused by throwing. In that case the other authorities also agree that the owner of the rooster pays half the damage, as Rama concludes. Although Rambam himself continues in a way showing that this was not his case, the Mechaber nevertheless chose to quote the portion of his wording that can be sustained. Rama's statement that "it is unexplained" therefore means that the Mechaber copied Rambam's unexplained wording, though this explanation remains forced and requires study.
He pays full damages. Because this is classified as regel.
It threw it and broke something. This is classified as tzerorot.
Only when it propelled an ownerless object. Like tzerorot, which are ownerless.
The owner of the cord is liable. Meaning, for the entire damage if the rooster is ownerless, as Rama concludes, because the owner of the cord should have guarded his cord so that an ownerless rooster would not become entangled in it.
A rolling pit. Meaning, although the owner of the cord did not place it at the location where it caused damage, it nevertheless has the law of a pit, because his cord has the status of a pit that rolled from the place where he left it to the place where it now came to rest through the rooster, and it caused damage there. The Tur writes that if it caused damage after coming to rest, the owner is exempt for damage to utensils, as explained in siman 410 under the law of a pit, where we expound "a donkey" and not utensils. The exclusion of "an ox" and not a person applies specifically where the person dies by falling into the pit, whereas a person will not die from this cord. By contrast, a utensil that breaks has thereby suffered its equivalent of death. If the damage occurs while the object is being dragged or thrown, liability is based on regel or fire, and one is liable for all categories of property.
Both pay together. Because the owner of the rooster was also negligent, since he should have guarded his rooster.
The owner of the rooster pays one quarter. Throwing by a rooster is like tzerorot of an ox, for which only half damages are paid. Since the owner of the cord must share the payment, the rooster's owner bears only half of his own half-liability, namely one quarter of the damage, while the remaining three quarters fall upon the owner of the cord. Had the owner of the cord been the only responsible party, such as where the rooster was ownerless, he would have been obligated for the entire damage.
If it caused damage after coming to rest. We expound, "If a man shall dig a pit," excluding a pit created by an ox. Therefore there is no basis to impose liability upon the owner of the rooster, even if the cord was ownerless and caused damage after coming to rest.
Even if the cord has an owner. This is also the Tur's wording. He could seemingly have stated an even greater novelty, that this applies even if the rooster also has an owner. One must say that he saw no additional novelty in that, because any damage attributable to the rooster certainly falls upon the person who tied the cord to it and not upon the rooster's owner. Once he took the rooster and tied the cord to it, he assumed responsibility for that act. The novelty is only that he is also liable in the capacity of the cord's owner, as owner of the obstacle, even where the liability is not attributed to the rooster itself.
