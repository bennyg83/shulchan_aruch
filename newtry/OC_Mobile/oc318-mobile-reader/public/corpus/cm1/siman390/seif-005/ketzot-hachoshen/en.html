It rolled to another place. Tosafot distinguishes a thrown vessel from a thrown stone or arrow. If one throws a vessel from a roof, the vessel can already be treated as doomed, so another person who breaks it is exempt. But if one shoots an arrow and another person breaks the object before the arrow reaches it, Tosafot says the second person is liable; otherwise the law of half-damages for pebbles would largely disappear. Rosh appears not to accept this distinction. In discussing an animal that kicked pebbles in the public domain and the damage occurred in the injured party's domain, he says we do not follow the beginning of the act there only because Scripture specifically requires the consumption to occur in another's field. If arrows were generally different from thrown vessels, he could have answered simply that pebbles resemble arrows rather than thrown vessels. Tur and the Shulchan Aruch concerning fire likewise imply that where the first person's fire was already sufficient to reach the destination, a second person who adds fuel is not liable. Fire can operate as one's arrows. This suggests that once the first causal force is sufficient, the damage is attributed to that first act. Nimukei Yosef also says that fire as one's arrows is treated as though the entire act occurred when the fire was first released. If the person dies before the fire reaches the stack, his estate remains liable because the act is legally complete from the outset. Tosafot's concern for the law of pebbles can be answered differently. Even if the breakage is regarded as beginning at the first moment, the animal's act remains only damage caused by its force rather than by its body, and the halachah of pebbles can still impose half-damages. This distinction also explains the dispute whether one who actively spreads a fire through an entire building after the first ignition is liable for the additional burning. If fire is treated as one's arrows, the initial ignition can make the later damage legally complete from the outset. Yet Tur says that where a camel spreads the fire through the building it can incur full damages for that spreading, which remains difficult under the strongest version of the initial-act theory. The matter requires study.
