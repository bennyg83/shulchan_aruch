[Seif 5] And it rolled to another place, the Mechaber says, and so forth. We evaluate the act from its inception, so it is not treated as pebbles but as direct bodily damage. The Rosh writes in the chapter Keitzad HaRegel that if an animal kicked an object in the public domain and it caused damage in the injured party's domain, the owner is liable, because we follow the place where the damage was completed. Although where it stepped on a vessel and the vessel rolled elsewhere and broke we conclude that the law follows the initial act, here we follow the place where the damage occurred, because the verse concerning foot says, “and it consumed in another's field,” and the destruction occurred in the injured party's courtyard. Maharshal understands the Rosh to mean that only where the entire event occurs in an authorized domain do we follow the initial act, which was likewise in the injured party's domain. He therefore asks how the category of pebbles could ever arise according to the Rosh, since whenever everything occurs in an authorized domain we should follow the initial act and treat the damage as direct, as in the case where it stepped on a vessel and the vessel rolled elsewhere. He answers that there is a major distinction. When it stepped on the vessel, or when one threw a vessel from a roof, an act was performed at the outset upon the damaged object itself, and it was already destined to break, as Rabbah says, “he broke an already broken vessel.” With pebbles, however, the projectile was not yet fit to damage at the moment it was kicked until it reached another place. Maharshal therefore concludes that if it stepped on a vessel in the public domain, causing it to fly into the injured party's domain and break there, the owner is exempt because the initial act occurred where the vessel was not protected. In my opinion, this is also the correct understanding of the Rosh. It is obvious that the Rosh would exempt where the animal stepped on the vessel in the public domain and it flew into the private domain. Since the owner would have been exempt had it broken where it was stepped upon, the fact that it ultimately broke in the injured party's domain cannot increase liability, because the vessel was lying in a place where foot is not liable. This is precisely the Rosh's distinction between an object kicked in the public domain and a vessel that rolled and broke elsewhere. In the first case the object that was ultimately damaged was lawfully situated in the injured party's domain, and the Torah imposes liability because the destruction occurred there. In the second case, the animal stepped on a vessel in the injured party's courtyard and it rolled elsewhere within that courtyard. Since the initial act also occurred in the injured party's domain, we follow the inception and impose full damages. But if it stepped on the vessel in the public domain and it flew into the private domain, the owner is exempt because the vessel was initially in an unprotected place. Maharshal's distinction can be understood consistently with the Rosh as well, and Tosafot in Keitzad HaRegel 17b likewise state that it is a simple logical distinction between throwing a stone and throwing a vessel. Although siman 391, seif 7, rules that if an animal takes food in the public domain and eats it in the private domain the owner is liable, that case is different because the animal physically carried the food into the private domain and is considered to have taken it anew there. Here the object merely flew there.
