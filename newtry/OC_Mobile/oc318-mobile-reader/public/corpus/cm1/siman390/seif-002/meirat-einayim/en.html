It swished its tail, etc. The Tur writes: If it swished its tail in the ordinary manner of animals and broke a utensil through that ordinary movement, this is normal conduct and has the law of foot. The owner is exempt for damage in the public domain and liable for full damages in the injured party's domain. If it swished excessively and the utensil would not have broken from ordinary swishing, the Gemara asks whether that remains normal conduct. The Rambam holds the question was not resolved. The Rosh holds it was resolved as normal conduct and therefore a derivative of foot. If it swished with its genital organ and thereby broke a utensil, everyone agrees the Gemara's question remains unresolved. From this one can understand the Mechaber and Rama. The Mechaber follows the Rambam and treats excessive tail swishing and ordinary genital swishing alike as unresolved questions whether they are derivatives of horn or foot. The burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Therefore, if the injured party did not seize and the damage occurred in the public domain, he receives nothing because the tortfeasor can say it was a derivative of foot. If it occurred in the injured party's domain, the tortfeasor pays only half damages whichever way one looks at it. But if the injured party seized, then according to the Rambam, who allows seizure in a legal doubt, his hand is on the upper side. If the damage occurred in the injured party's domain he can say it was a derivative of foot and demand full damages. If it occurred in the public domain he can say it was horn and retain half damages. Rama writes that some say seizure does not help in a doubt of law. He also writes that according to the Rosh, where the tail swishing was not excessive, the question was resolved and it is certainly normal conduct and a derivative of foot. Therefore damage in the public domain is fully exempt even according to one who generally allows seizure in legal doubts. Correspondingly, in the injured party's domain full damages are payable even according to Ri and the Rosh. Rama could have stated the affirmative liability as well, but it follows directly from the exemption.
In every doubt of law, seizure does not help, etc. This refers to an unresolved Talmudic question, as explained above. These authorities nevertheless agree that where the damage occurs in the injured party's domain, even half damages are not collected without seizure, because perhaps the conduct was abnormal and therefore a derivative of horn. Horn pays half damages only from the body of the damaging animal. Thus the injured party must seize the damaging animal itself in order to collect half damages from it. The Tur concludes that even if the same animal damaged one person once in the public domain and once in the injured party's domain, we still rule leniently in both directions. The injured party cannot say: whichever way you characterize the conduct, if it is horn then pay me for the public domain damage as well, and if it is foot then pay me full damages for the damage in my domain. We treat each doubtful penalty leniently. Although in siman 1 the Tur and Mechaber rule that where there is a doubt whether the buyer's utensils acquire in the seller's domain, and the parties made two transactions under opposite circumstances, one of them must have effected acquisition whichever way the law is resolved, that is different. Here we are dealing with a penal obligation, since the owner's own body did not cause the damage but his animal did. Therefore in both doubts we follow the lenient result. See the Derishah.
