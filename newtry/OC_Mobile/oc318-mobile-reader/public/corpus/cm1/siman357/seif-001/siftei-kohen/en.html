If witnesses came, etc. The Sema and Bach interpret the Rambam as speaking independently of a public report of theft, but that cannot be correct. If so, the Rambam would hold that with articles ordinarily lent or rented, the owner recovers them even without a report of theft and even when he commonly sells his utensils. They therefore invoke a migo, yet the Rambam never mentions one, and the distinction matters where there are no witnesses that the object is presently seen in the possessor's hands. Moreover, the Rambam expressly rules in the laws of claims that when the owner says an article was stolen and the possessor says others sold or gave it to him, the possessor retains it, even if it is ordinarily lent or rented, unless a report of theft had circulated. The Tur and Nimukei Yosef therefore understood the Rambam correctly: his reference to articles ordinarily lent or rented is joined to the earlier condition that a report of theft circulated. The Rambam always requires two favorable factors, either a report of theft together with an owner who does not ordinarily sell his utensils, or a report together with utensils ordinarily lent or rented even though the owner does sell. In those cases the owner recovers even without a migo and even where the possessor could have claimed that he returned the object. Conversely, where the owner does not ordinarily sell and the object is ordinarily lent or rented, the same two favorable factors exist even without a public report. The Rambam's ruling in the laws of claims, that no recovery is made without a report despite the article's type, refers to an owner who ordinarily sells. The Tur's shorthand statement selected one side of this structure but did not deny the other. The Rosh, however, disagrees in law. In his view, once the owner claims theft, the presumption created by the article's being ordinarily lent or rented is weakened; without a genuine migo it adds nothing. Where there is a migo, such as the ability to claim that the article was lent or rented, the owner may claim that another thief stole and sold it, because he is not accusing the present possessor of theft. The Rosh's refusal to presume a person a thief applies only when the claimant accuses that very possessor. Accordingly, the Rambam and Rosh truly disagree, contrary to the Beit Yosef and Maharshal's claim that they do not. The Rama's alternative opinion is therefore well placed: according to the Rosh, articles ordinarily lent or rented do not help when the owner claims theft and lacks a migo; when he has a migo, they do. The Ran agrees. The Levush's explanation that recovery here rests simply on a migo is mistaken, because this entire siman includes cases in which the possessor could have claimed return, yet recovery is ordered when a public report of theft and the other favorable factor are present. See siman 133 for a concise statement of these rules.
