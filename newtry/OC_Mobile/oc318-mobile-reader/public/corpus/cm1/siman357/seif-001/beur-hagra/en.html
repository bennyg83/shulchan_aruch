(Collected note) The owner, etc. The Tur writes that, according to Ri, this case concerns before despair, and Tosafos there say the same. The Raavad, however, writes that it applies even after despair, because in his view despair together with change of possession acquires only to the extent that the recipient need not return the stolen object itself, but must pay its value. Beit Yosef objects that here the purchaser returns the very object, and Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and the Derishah labor to reconcile the Raavad. There is also a further difficulty, for according to the Tur the Raavad would agree with the Rambam in the laws of despair and change of possession, yet the Tur does not cite him that way elsewhere. In truth the text contains a scribal error and should read “except for the money.” Rashba quotes the Raavad this way in his novellae, explaining the statement in Bava Kamma 114a that the purchaser returns, meaning that he returns value, as in other cases of theft. Rashba nevertheless rejects the position from the merchants in the beginning of chapter 3 of Sukkah, and also discusses it in his responsa.
And a report emerged, etc. He explains the Gemara's statement that “there is no need to return,” etc., according to the plain meaning of the Mishnah, unlike Rashi there; see Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh.
Or he was, etc. This is comparable to one who ordinarily sells such objects. Tosafos in Shevuos 46b and the Rosh there explain the case similarly.
If witnesses came, etc. As stated in siman 90, seif 12, siman 72, and siman 133.
Some say, etc. The Tur and Rama understand the Rambam's phrase “or he was accustomed,” etc., to apply even where no report of theft emerged, and write that the Rosh disagrees. Although the Rosh follows Tosafos, that is only where a report of theft emerged; otherwise he does not accept the claim, as stated in siman 90 and siman 133. Beit Yosef, however, understands the Rambam as speaking only where a report of theft emerged, and accordingly the Mechaber's ruling here is consistent with his ruling in siman 133. The Tur's reading of Rambam's structure is nevertheless compelling. As a matter of law, there may be no practical disagreement between the Mechaber and Rama in the cases addressed elsewhere. It appears to me, however, that the Raavad's position is more persuasive, because the rationale that we do not presume a person to be a thief is weak where there could have been any of many thieves. Also, when the text says “if witnesses came,” it requires that they also saw the object presently in his possession, as explained in the cited simanim; the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch often abbreviate this requirement. The Shach offers another interpretation, that a report of theft can substitute for witnesses who saw the object, but his explanation does not appear correct; see siman 133, seif 7.
And all, etc. According to the conclusion it is unnecessary that the witnesses identify the specific book or other article; see Lechem Mishneh.
