Articles ordinarily lent or rented. The Shach correctly proves that Rambam does not remove such articles from a possessor merely because they are ordinarily lent or rented. Rambam requires an additional factor, such as a public report of theft, or that the owner is not one who normally sells his possessions. Rambam defines "articles ordinarily lent or rented" narrowly: objects manufactured primarily for lending or renting, such as large banquet vessels or bridal ornaments. Such an object is intrinsically not intended for sale. Therefore, when joined with a report of theft, it resembles an owner who does not ordinarily sell his possessions. Rosh uses a broader definition, namely an object that this owner frequently lends or rents despite being saleable. Under his view the characteristic is relevant chiefly where the owner claims he lent it. If he claims theft, the fact that he often lends it does not help. Accordingly, Rambam's category has force because the object itself is not ordinarily sold. But that factor alone is still only one supporting circumstance. It requires combination with a theft report or another independent reason. Combining it merely with the fact that the owner does not usually sell gives only one substantive circumstance and should not suffice without a theft report.
