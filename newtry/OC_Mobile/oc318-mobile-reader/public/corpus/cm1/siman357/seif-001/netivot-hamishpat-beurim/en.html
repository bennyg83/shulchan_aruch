See Shach, seif katan 20, whose general rule is that where there are two favorable factors, such as a prior publicized theft together with an owner who does not normally sell, or a publicized theft together with utensils normally lent out, or an owner who does not sell together with utensils normally lent out, we can remove the objects from the possessor even without witnesses who saw them recently in his hands. At first glance this creates many difficulties. If there are witnesses of a prior deposit or loan and the owner does not normally sell, why should that not be at least as strong as utensils normally lent out? Likewise, with animals that move about on their own or with an artisan, which are treated similarly to lent-out utensils for certain presumptions, we should be able to remove property without current viewing whenever the owner does not normally sell. Yet no major authority states such rules. Bava Batra 45 regarding an artisan likewise could have answered several questions by establishing the case where the owner does not normally sell, if Shach's general combination principle were correct. The answer is that Shach's rule is specific to Rambam's definition of utensils "made to be lent or rented." Rambam describes items whose very type is not ordinarily sold but is intended for lending. That creates two cumulative factors: the owner is not a seller, and the object itself is not a normal sale object. Ketzot HaChoshen uses a similar idea when combining a publicized theft with utensils normally lent out. Other factors, such as witnesses of a deposit, an artisan's possession, or freely roaming animals, do not make the object intrinsically non-saleable. They merely weaken the presumption arising from present possession. Therefore they do not combine in the same way. Still, I find it difficult to read Tur as creating a major rule, under Rambam, that where both the owner and the type of object are normally not sold, recovery is allowed without a recent-viewing condition, when neither the Gemara nor Rambam states such a rule explicitly. In my view Tur's words are simpler. At the beginning of the siman, "publicized theft" means witnesses saw a forced entry or other objective signs of theft. Tur then cites Rambam that if there are witnesses identifying the objects as the claimant's, witnesses need not also have seen the physical break-in. Nevertheless, the owner must have created some public indication of theft himself, such as previously crying out that his utensils were stolen. Mechaber, Perishah, and Sema imply this. Tur then says that with utensils normally lent out, Rambam does not require the objective break-in proof even if the owner is someone who sells utensils. But he still requires the owner's prior public claim that these items were stolen. Rosh disagrees. According to Rosh, utensils normally lent out have no special advantage for a theft claim where there is no current-viewing evidence, because the entire siman lacks the migo that would support a loan claim. Therefore, if the owner normally sells, he needs the full objective theft circumstances listed by Rambam, such as evidence of forced entry. This is also the intent of Rama: according to Rosh, the simple "publicized theft" mentioned at the beginning, meaning only the owner's own prior outcry, does not suffice when the owner is someone who normally sells, even if the utensils are normally lent out.
