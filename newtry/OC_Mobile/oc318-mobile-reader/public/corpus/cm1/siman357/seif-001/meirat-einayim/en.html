If the householder was accustomed to sell his utensils, etc., until "but if people came and lodged there." See the Tur. He states these rules regarding a person who is not ordinarily accustomed to sell his utensils, where no witnesses identify the utensils themselves as belonging to the claimant and no one saw the exact utensils when the thieves carried them out. He also writes that where the householder is accustomed to sell utensils, the claim can still succeed even though the witnesses do not personally recognize the items as his, provided there are witnesses who saw utensils being carried from the breached house and the following three facts join together: first, witnesses saw the people who had lodged in his house leave through the breach; second, they saw them carrying utensils matching the signs the owner later gives; third, we now see in the defendant's possession an article with those same signs. Together these circumstances establish that the utensils are his. The Tur then cites the Rambam's language reproduced by the Mechaber as though it were a differing view. In the Perishah and Derishah I proved that they do not actually disagree in law. Each explains a different aspect of the same Gemara according to his own reading. Darkei Moshe also writes that they do not disagree. The Mechaber omitted the Tur's formulation because, in the Beit Yosef, he understood the Rambam to disagree with Rashi, Tosafot, and the Rosh. I answered the Beit Yosef's objections in the Derishah. The Mechaber also omitted the Tur's statement that according to the Rambam and Raavad the rule can apply even after the owner's despair. That omission likewise follows the Beit Yosef's understanding. In the Derishah I explained that the Raavad's position presents no difficulty, and Darkei Moshe alludes to the same answer.
