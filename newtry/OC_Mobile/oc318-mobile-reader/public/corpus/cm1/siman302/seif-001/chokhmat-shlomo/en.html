Seif 2. A minor who deposited or lent an item to an adult. Note: Bava Batra 51 states that one should not accept deposits from slaves or minors, lest the property actually belong to the slave's master or the child's father. If one did accept it, he should preserve it for the minor's benefit. Rashbam explains that one may not return it directly to the minor, because doing so is like knowingly exposing property to loss, and if he does return it he must pay. At first glance this suggests that Rashbam agrees with Rambam and the Mechaber that the laws of custodians apply to property entrusted by a minor. According to those who hold that a minor cannot create custodial liability, why should returning it to the minor make the adult liable? Perhaps the case is different because, even if ordinary custody does not arise through the minor, the adult has the duties of one guarding lost property and must protect it rather than hand it to the minor. This must be so, because in siman 186 we rule that where a father sends a minor to a shopkeeper with a container for oil, the shopkeeper is liable if he returns the item through the minor. According to the opinion that minors do not create custodial status, why is the shopkeeper liable? He cannot be liable by virtue of the child's delivery, nor can we treat it as though the father himself appointed the child as an agent, because a minor cannot serve as an agent. It must be that the liability arises under the laws of guarding lost property. It remains difficult why the Mechaber omitted the Gemara's rules here, especially the instruction that one who accepted a deposit from a minor should make a protected investment for him, concerning which the Gemara debates whether this means a Torah scroll or palm trees. This requires study.
