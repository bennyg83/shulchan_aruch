See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 33, who cites his uncle, the eminent Rabbi Sender, as having ruled that the recipient did not acquire the cellar and underground chambers even in a gift, because the giver did not write that the transfer included the depth and height. Rabbi Sender brought proof from Radbaz, quoted by Mabit, regarding a roof. His apparent reference is to Mabit, part 2, siman 15, where a responsum of Radbaz concerning a gift by a deathly ill person is quoted. Radbaz writes that if the roof has a parapet ten handbreadths high and an area of four cubits, the recipient does not acquire the roof or the water that falls upon it. Radbaz gives no reason. Rabbi Sender apparently understood that the reason is that the deed did not state "depth and height." He held that the Mishnah at the end of the fourth chapter of Bava Batra, which says that the limitations apply to a seller but one who gives a gift gives everything, refers back to the opening Mishnah of that chapter, where one who sells a house does not sell the annex, the roof when it has a parapet, the pit, or the cistern, even if he wrote "depth and height." Most commentators other than Rashbam explain that the phrase "even if he wrote depth and height" applies also to the roof. On that reading, the later Mishnah teaches that a gift includes all those things specifically where the giver did write "depth and height": in a sale that language still does not include them, while in a gift it does. But without such language, even a gift would not include them, and all the more so a pit or cistern. Beit Ephraim questions his uncle's ruling at length. First, it is not clear that Radbaz held this at all; perhaps he excluded the roof for another reason. Second, even if Radbaz did hold so, the straightforward language of the poskim implies that an ordinary gift includes a pit and cistern without any special wording. We should not abandon that clear implication because of uncertainty in Radbaz's language. Moreover, in my humble opinion, all of the earlier discussion concerns a pit or cistern, whose ordinary function is holding water. A modern cellar, by contrast, is used to store objects, movable goods, food, and drink. Its law therefore resembles an inner room. What difference is there whether the room is beyond the house or below ground? Indeed, a cellar is stronger than a separate room, because concerning a room the poskim hold, as Rama writes, that because it is not physically within the house it is not included in the gift. A cellar, however, lies beneath that very house and opens into it. Even an ordinary room, although structurally distinct, would be included in the house were it not located outside it. This also appears from Rama, siman 171, seif 14, who writes that from Responsa Rosh it appears that one may divide a house against a shop or a cellar, and that the same applies against a room or upper story, because these are considered uses of similar value. Clearly no one would say that a house is divided against a pit or cistern. It follows that a cellar is different and has a use somewhat comparable to that of the house.
