But one who gives a gift, etc. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 33. He cites his uncle, the gaon R. Sender, who ruled that even in a gift the recipient does not acquire a cellar and underground chambers unless the donor expressly conveyed the depth and height. R. Sender supported this from a responsum of Radbaz, quoted by Mabit, concerning a roof. Beit Ephraim questions that ruling at length. First, it is not clear that Radbaz based his decision on the absence of the words conveying depth and height; another reason may have applied. Second, the plain implication of the major authorities is that a gift made without qualification does include a cistern and underground chamber, and one should not abandon that straightforward reading because of an uncertain inference from Radbaz. Beit Ephraim further argues that even if the usual rules exclude a cistern or reservoir used primarily for water, a modern cellar used for storing household goods, food, and drink is different. Such a cellar is functionally comparable to an inner room. Its location below the house rather than adjacent to it should not change its classification. Indeed, it may be more readily included than a separate room, because the authorities who exclude an outer room do so largely because it is a distinct structure outside the house, whereas a cellar lies directly beneath the house and opens into it. He also points to the Rama in siman 171:14, citing the Rosh, that a house may be divided against a shop or cellar because their uses are sufficiently comparable. No one would say the same of a house as against a water cistern. This demonstrates that a cellar has a household use resembling that of the house itself. See there further.
