The document does not state that he died from that illness, etc. The Tur's wording is: the heir says he recovered before dying and the gift was therefore automatically void, even if he later fell ill again and died, as the Tur and Mechaber wrote at the beginning of the preceding siman; the recipient says he never recovered and the gift therefore remains valid. The Mechaber abbreviates because even if the heirs do not raise the claim themselves, beit din raises it for them. The Tur states the heirs' claim to teach a greater novelty according to Rashbam, who holds that even when they make the claim, the fact that he was ultimately buried proves that he died from the illness, and the heirs bear the burden of proof.
To rely, etc. The text should read through "and died from it, so that the witnesses may..." The Nimukei Yosef writes that the witnesses may rely on women and slaves who attended him. They need not remain with him continuously. It is enough to ask the household and people who regularly attended him, and if they say that so-and-so's condition worsened and he died from that illness, the witnesses may testify that he died from the original illness. The Nimukei Yosef continues at length. It does not say there that they may rely on the sick person's own statement. Rama follows the Beit Yosef's wording. Accordingly, Rama's phrase "to testify so" does not mean that they testify in beit din merely that they heard it from him or his attendants. Rather, they may testify affirmatively that he died from the first illness, as though they themselves saw and knew it.
Even though this person who made the instruction has died. Meaning, one might have said that since he died before us and we know of no interval of recovery, we should preserve the original presumption and assume he certainly never recovered in between.
Until he brings proof, etc. If the recipient brings no proof, the property remains with the heirs without an oath even though the recipient asserts certainty. The Tur does say in the following ruling, cited by Rama, that when the giver recovered and wishes to retract, and the recipient must prove the circumstances of the gift, if the recipient lacks proof the giver takes a hesset oath and is exempt. That applies where the giver himself is alive. Heirs, however, do not take a hesset oath merely to exempt themselves, as the Tur writes in siman 69:14 and siman 75:19.
The recipient says that he was healthy. The case is where the document expressly records a kinyan but does not state that "while sick and lying in bed so-and-so gave to so-and-so." The giver claims that it was a gift of all his property as a shechiv mera and seeks to retract. Even if the kinyan was written with enhancement language in the manner used for a full-property shechiv mera gift, the recipient may still claim the giver was healthy, because healthy people too sometimes use enhancement language, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 250:23. See the Perishah.
The recipient bears the burden of proof. If he has no proof, the giver takes a hesset oath and is exempt, as the Tur writes. This differs from heirs in the preceding law, as explained above. Rama's phrase "the same law applies" means they are equal in the core question of who bears the burden of proof.
Likewise, wherever there is a dispute among the authorities. Meaning, for example, in this law the Amoraim dispute the point, as explained in the Perishah: one opinion says that as he is now healthy or ill, so we presume he was originally. Rashbam follows that view, as cited by the Tur. Rama similarly writes at the end of siman 139 and below at the end of siman 240: if both parties are in possession, or neither is in possession and there is no prior-owner presumption, they divide. This is from Terumat HaDeshen.
