To ask. The Tur states: The heir says that the giver recovered before he died and the gift was therefore void, while the recipient says he did not recover and the gift remains valid. The Mechaber abbreviates because even if the heirs do not make this claim, beit din makes it for them. The Tur states the parties' claims to teach the greater novelty according to Rashbam, who holds that even when the heirs themselves expressly make the claim, the burial proves the giver's condition and the heirs bear the burden of proof. See there. Thus far the Sema. See Responsa R. Aharon Sasson, siman 165.
Proof. If the recipient brings no proof, the property remains with the heirs without an oath, even though the recipient makes a definite claim. The Tur writes in the law cited by the Rama that if the sick person recovered and wishes to retract, and the recipient has the burden of proof, then if the recipient lacks proof the giver swears a shevuat hesset and is exempt. That applies where the giver himself is alive. Heirs, however, do not swear hesset merely to be exempt; they swear only when collecting, as the Tur writes in siman 69, seif 14, and siman 75, seif 19. Sema.
Seized. The same applies where the person dangerously ill recovered and says, 'I gave it to you while I was dangerously ill and I now retract,' while the recipient says, 'You were healthy.' The recipient must bring proof. If the movable property is already in the recipient's hands, then because he could claim ownership independently, he swears hesset that the giver was healthy. Rambam, chapter 9 of Hilchot Zechiyah. The Maggid Mishneh writes that even if there is no migo, where witnesses testify that the property came into his hands because of this gift, since the movable property is physically in the recipient's hands, the giver should again bear the burden of proof. This distinction requires study. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The Kesef Mishneh likewise writes that the Rambam's view is simple, and the Tur and Rama here agree. Ordinarily, when witnesses saw an item enter the possessor's hands, he cannot simply say, 'You gave it to me as a gift.' Here too, because the witnesses do not know whether the recipient acquired through a valid gift and because we rule that a deathbed gift takes effect only after death, if it was indeed a deathbed gift the property never left the giver's ownership during his life. The recipient must therefore prove that it left the giver's ownership. Otherwise, in every ordinary case we could likewise allow a possessor to claim that he bought or received it as a gift. Where witnesses saw it in his possession, the property remains legally in the original owner's domain unless the recipient proves the transfer. This is clear. It differs from siman 72, seif katan 109, where witnesses know that property came into the creditor's hands as collateral but do not know for how much. There it was given to him specifically as collateral, so he is believed as to the amount secured. Here there was no unquestionably effective transfer. Shach.
That he was healthy. The case is where the deed expressly states that a kinyan was made, but does not state that 'while sick and lying in bed, so-and-so gave to so-and-so.' The giver claims it was a gift of all his property while dangerously ill and seeks to retract. Even if the kinyan was written with strengthening language customarily used for gifts of all property by a person dangerously ill, the recipient may still claim that the giver was healthy, because healthy people too sometimes use strengthened kinyan language, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 250, seif 23. Sema.
A dispute. Meaning, a law disputed in the Gemara, such as this one, where one opinion says we presume the earlier condition matched the person's present condition, whether healthy or sick. Rashbam explains this, and the Tur cites him. The Rama likewise rules above in siman 139 and below at the end of siman 256, seif 1, that if both parties are in possession, or neither is in possession and there is no established prior owner, they divide. This follows Terumat HaDeshen. Sema.
