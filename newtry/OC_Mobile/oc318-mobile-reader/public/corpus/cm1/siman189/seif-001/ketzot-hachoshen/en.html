The Tur says that if the buyer already had the seller's deposited property in his possession and merely intended to acquire it for an agreed price payable to the depositor, he does not acquire. At first this is difficult, because a person's courtyard ordinarily acquires property for him. Taz answers from Rashbam that the custodian's premises, with respect to the deposited object, are treated as the depositor's premises. Maharit challenged this. How did the depositor acquire a right in the custodian's premises without money or hazakah? What if no specific place was designated? And if the depositor's right ends when he transfers the deposit, why not say that the courtyard and gift become the custodian's simultaneously, just as a get and a woman's courtyard can come to her simultaneously? In my view Rashbam can be defended. Even according to authorities who do not treat mere use as acquisition of land itself, rights of use can be acquired through the very use for which the premises were lent. A path intended for walking can be acquired by walking. So too, when the custodian authorizes the depositor to place his goods in the premises, the depositor acquires the relevant use right by placing the goods there. No fixed location is needed if the original permission extended to wherever the goods would be placed. Partners are treated similarly: because they do not object to one another's placing items in the common area, each one's possessions can be regarded as resting in a jointly usable courtyard rather than exclusively in the other's courtyard. The principle of "his courtyard and his gift arrive simultaneously" does not solve the problem. That principle works where the legal instrument itself effects the transfer of the courtyard together with the gift, as with a get placed in a courtyard whose ownership changes through the same get structure. But where the depositor's use right continues until the deposited object is actually returned, a mere oral declaration, "It is yours," does not itself transfer the use right back to the custodian. A slave likewise does not free himself merely through an oral statement even though, after emancipation, his own hand would become his. A document or other valid acquisition is still needed. Maharit's proof from the law of leasing a place also does not undermine this. Where one wishes first to acquire a courtyard so that a new object can then be acquired through it, a formal land acquisition is required. Here the deposited object already belongs to the depositor, and his placement of his own goods in the authorized area itself constitutes use sufficient to establish the limited right. Therefore the custodian's courtyard does not automatically acquire the deposit back for him merely through his intention.
There remains a difficulty from the case of a prostitute's payment discussed in Avodah Zarah, where the animal stands in her courtyard and the Gemara treats the courtyard as capable of acquiring it. If the animal had been deposited there, why is the courtyard not considered the depositor's? One can answer that the animal entered without her intentionally granting the depositor a continuing right of use. In such a case no depositor's courtyard right was created. The Mishnah concerning one who merely thinks of misappropriating a deposit likewise supports the distinction. If the custodian's courtyard automatically reacquired every deposit when he intended to appropriate it, mere thought should make him liable as a thief through courtyard acquisition. Yet liability for sending a hand into a deposit requires an act. Rashi implies that even acquisition of stolen property through a courtyard requires conduct such as locking the animal in. Mere thought does not suffice. A more serious question comes from the law that a custodian who pays rather than swears can acquire the double payment right, because the owner is treated as saying from the time of deposit, "If it is stolen and you choose to pay me, my animal shall be acquired to you immediately before the theft." The Gemara says that if the animal stands in the custodian's courtyard at that time, the courtyard can acquire it. Why is the courtyard not still the depositor's courtyard? The answer is that the implied condition was created at the original time of deposit. The owner is treated as saying from the outset that immediately before the theft the object's status will change from deposit to sale. This resembles a deposit made only for thirty days: once the agreed period ends, the special use right can revert automatically. But if, after an ordinary indefinite deposit has already been established, the owner later merely says, "Let it become yours," the courtyard remains subject to the depositor's use right until a valid transfer or return occurs. Rashi's language, that the owner is treated as saying this "from the time of meshichah," is therefore precise. The future change of status was built into the original custodial arrangement. A final difficulty remains from kiddushin with a deposit already in the woman's hands. If some of the deposit remains, she can be betrothed with it. How can her courtyard acquire it if it is treated as the depositor's premises? This question remains difficult according to the present analysis.
