Rabbeinu Yonah says a migo works only where the possessor could have obtained the very same money or property through the alternative plea. If he admits the mortgage term has ended, the land returns to its original owner. He cannot then use a migo that he could have claimed purchase in order to keep eating fruits for a monetary debt, because his present claim is no longer ownership of the land but merely a demand for money. Sema and Taz explain this as a migo to extract. The Shach argues that Rabbeinu Yonah rejects such a migo even with movables. He cites Rosh in the two against two case, where Rabbeinu Yonah held seizure ineffective, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. In my view that proof is not sound. Where two witnesses stand against two, an ordinary migo cannot add anything. A migo is no stronger than witnesses, and two witnesses are like one hundred. Tosafot explicitly rejects using a migo to strengthen one contradictory pair because the migo cannot surpass testimony. Thus Rabbeinu Yonah's position in a two against two case does not establish his view about ordinary movables. It is also possible that seizure without witnesses is stronger because it occurred in a manner that, from the possessor's perspective, was lawful and unobserved, while witnessed seizure openly appears unauthorized. But the main point is that the land and movable cases differ, as explained next.
The distinction between land and movables is clear. Rav Yosef himself does not allow an ordinary migo to extract land from the prior owner's presumption, while he would allow migo concerning movables held in one's hand. We rule like Rabbah concerning land only where the possessor's present claim is to ownership of the body of the land. If he admits the land belongs to the challenger and claims only that money is owed to him, he has no possession in the land at all. His migo is therefore one to extract money from the original owner. Movables are different. A person physically holding movables is a possessor even if his present claim is only that they secure a debt. A creditor acquires a pledge, including in some circumstances a pledge taken by force. His possession therefore supports a defensive migo rather than an extractive one. This also answers the Shach's proof from Rava bar Sharshom. He held a debt instrument. Although he could not collect from minor heirs in the ordinary way, the document itself supplied an evidentiary strengthening. A migo can sometimes extract where there is an independent strengthening such as a document. Tosafot says similarly in the case of a collateral payment allocated to another debt: although a pure migo to extract would fail, the outstanding document allows the migo to preserve the document's force. Moreover, against heirs or purchasers who make only an uncertain plea, a certain claimant can sometimes use a migo even to extract, as explained in siman 133. Rava bar Sharshom was certain while the heirs were uncertain. The Shach also asks that if Rava bar Sharshom had already eaten fruits, he should be believed simply through a migo that he had repaid whatever he consumed. That is correct and has nothing to do with Tosafot's rule that where a person lacks a migo concerning the land he also returns the fruits. Tosafot speaks where the possessor claims the fruits only because he says the land itself is his. Once the land claim fails, the fruits fail with it, even if he could have denied eating them. But if he makes an independent monetary claim to the value of the fruits and has a migo regarding those fruits themselves, he can be believed. If his alternative plea would require hesset against an administrator making a certain denial, he likewise must swear. By contrast, where the heirs merely make an uncertain plea and his alternative would be unavoidable accident, the migo can exempt him even from hesset. This preserves the distinction.
