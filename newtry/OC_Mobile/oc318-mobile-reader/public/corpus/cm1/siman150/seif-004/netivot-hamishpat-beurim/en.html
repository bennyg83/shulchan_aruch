Shach argues that otherwise the Rosh's second answer gains nothing from the possessor's migo. The Rosh asks why Rabbah bar Sharshom would in any event not have to take a heset oath. He answers that Rabbah bar Sharshom meant as follows: had the Sages not enacted an oath for one who comes to collect, I would not need to swear if I waited until the orphans became adults and collected on my document. I could not have collected from them while they were minors in the ordinary way, because beit din does not collect from minors' assets. But because I am already in possession and have a migo that I bought the land, I can collect even while they are minors, as explained in siman 110. Yet collection based on the migo of purchase requires a heset oath. I would therefore have preferred to wait until they matured, when I could collect under the document without an oath. Once the Sages enacted that even one collecting with a document must swear, there is no advantage to waiting, so it is better for me to consume the produce before they mature. From this Shach proves that the Rosh refers to produce the possessor will consume in the future. If the discussion concerned produce already consumed, why was the migo necessary? It is true that had Rabbah bar Sharshom come before beit din while the orphans were minors, even produce already consumed would have to be returned absent a migo, because possession before witnesses does not ordinarily help against minor heirs. But he could simply have refrained from informing the orphans until they matured. At that point, according to the Rosh's hypothetical assumption that the Sages had not enacted an oath for one collecting on a document, he would collect without an oath even without the migo. The fact that the Rosh needs the migo shows that the issue is produce he wishes to continue consuming before coming to beit din. Ketzot HaChoshen correctly writes that the other poskim who understand Rabbah bar Sharshom as speaking of future produce may nevertheless agree with Rabbeinu Yonah's rule. Rabbah bar Sharshom possessed a written debt instrument. With a written lien, where the lien on land is d'oraisa, he has a present claim against this very land and the migo of purchase therefore concerns the same property. Rabbeinu Yonah's ruling can concern an oral loan, where the Sages removed the ordinary property lien. Ran writes that according to the view that liens are not d'oraisa, collection from property exists only because beit din coerces payment. In that setting a migo from possession of the land to establish the underlying oral debt is a migo from one item of property to another. As for Shach's further proof that if the case concerned produce already eaten, the possessor should be believed through a migo that he paid the debt, there is another resolution. Siman 137 establishes that regarding produce already consumed, one who says, "I descended only for the produce," is believed even without a migo because he is presently the possessor. Tosafot in Bava Batra 33 likewise say that even where he has only two years and no migo of purchase, he is believed that he descended for produce. The present case can therefore concern one who earlier told witnesses that the land itself was his and consumed the produce under a claim of ownership. He can no longer say that he descended only for produce. Once he made the earlier purchase claim before witnesses, he likewise cannot later say, "I paid the loan," so the migo of payment is unavailable. This answers Shach's question.
