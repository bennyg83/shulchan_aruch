But if he comes to claim from him before he has consumed the produce, etc. Since the Tur records this ruling without qualification in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, yet in my humble opinion it is not correct, I must discuss the matter at length. At first glance Rabbeinu Yonah, as cited by the Tur, appears to distinguish land from movable property because land remains in the presumptive possession of its owner. The Sema likewise explains that even if the mortgagee remained in possession of the land for three years after the mortgage term ended, this is not comparable to holding movable collateral. A holder of movable collateral may claim another debt against it up to its value through a migo that he could have claimed ownership. Here, however, once he admits that he did not purchase the land, it remains in the owner's possession, and his only support is a migo of one property to another, which is not accepted. Rabbeinu Yerucham similarly writes that the Geonim ruled that a creditor holding collateral may collect from minor heirs only with an oath, even on a doubtful claim and even if he took possession during their father's lifetime. He adds that with land, even against an ordinary person, one cannot say, “He owes me such and such upon it,” through a migo that he could have claimed that the land was purchased. He derives this from the incident of Rabbah bar Sharshom. Even if one possessed the land during the father's lifetime and claims that the father owed him a certain amount, the land is returned to the heirs, and he may litigate with them when they mature. If he claims that the land came to him as a mortgage and circumstances would have permitted him to claim that he purchased it, he is believed concerning the amount owed, whether the loan was documented or oral. If he could not have claimed purchase, the land is returned and he must litigate with the heirs when they mature. In truth, however, there is no sound basis to distinguish land from movable property. Land held for three years is no different in this respect from movable property, as is evident from many passages in the Gemara and the authorities. Since we rule in siman 72 and elsewhere that one who holds movable property may retain it for a different debt and is believed with an oath up to its value, the same should apply to land after three years of possession. It therefore appears that Rabbeinu Yonah also ruled this way concerning movable property, following the Baal HaMaor and the authorities cited by the Mordechai and Hagahot Mordechai, who hold that even movable property may not be retained for a different debt because that would be a migo from one property to another. Rabbeinu Tam is cited as ruling that a migo is effective only concerning the very same object. Thus, one who holds land from Reuven as a mortgage and has consumed its produce for the period needed to establish a presumption cannot claim that a different house occupied by Reuven belongs to him merely because he could have suppressed the mortgage document and claimed ownership of the mortgaged land. The Hagahot Mordechai likewise explains that where the claim is for money corresponding to money, such as payment for clothing or produce, the defendant could have denied the transaction entirely. With other property, however, he may not seize one object for a debt arising from another, for who authorized him to appraise that object as payment? The incident of Rabbah bar Sharshom proves this. Had he openly said that he was holding the land for a separate debt, the migo would not have helped him. The goats that ate barley are different because the goats themselves were fattened by the barley and were therefore treated as its equivalent. These authorities plainly do not distinguish land from movable property. Their rule is based upon rejecting a migo from one item of property to another. Accordingly, the Tur's citation of Rabbeinu Yonah, that one is believed only regarding produce already consumed but may not retain the land in order to consume future produce, is not limited to land. Rabbeinu Yonah's reason applies equally to movable property. Once the holder admits that the object belongs to another, it is legally regarded as standing in its owner's possession, and he cannot support a different claim through a migo that he could have claimed ownership. The Tur referred to land only because that was the case under discussion. There is no reason to distinguish movable property simply because it is physically in his hand, for land held for three years is likewise in his control. This understanding is reinforced by Rabbeinu Yonah's position concerning seizure. The Rosh records that Rabbeinu Yonah did not recognize seizure as effective merely because the holder asserts a certain claim. Rabbeinu Yonah derived this from the rule concerning a widow who appraises property for herself, where the court asks, “Who appraised it for you?” The Bach sought to distinguish a seizure made without witnesses because there the holder has a migo. This does not appear correct. The very authorities cited above reject that argument because it is a migo from one property to another. Since the holder cannot appraise the object for himself, seizure is ineffective even with movable property. Rabbeinu Yerucham also indicates expressly that according to Rabbeinu Yonah, seizure is ineffective even where a migo exists. Although I demonstrated elsewhere that one statement of Rabbeinu Yerucham is erroneous, his understanding of Rabbeinu Yonah on this point is correct. Thus Rabbeinu Yonah follows his own consistent view. We, however, rule that seizure of movable property is effective and is not rejected as a migo from one property to another, because the holder may retain the object until his debt is paid even though he may not appraise it for himself. The same rule should therefore apply to land held for three years. Moreover, whatever Rabbeinu Yonah's exact position may be, the Rambam, Semag, Raavad, Maggid Mishneh, Baal HaTerumot, Rabbi Yosef Halevi, Ramban, Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef clearly disagree, and their view is primary. Rabbeinu Yonah and the Hagahot Mordechai understand the incident of Rabbah bar Sharshom as concerning produce already consumed, and hold that before consumption he could not say that he was retaining the land for his money. The other authorities do not accept that interpretation. The Ramban asks why, when the mortgage term ended, Rabbah bar Sharshom did not simply come to beit din, state the matter properly, and claim his debt before returning the land. He should have been believed up to the value of the land through a migo that he could have claimed purchase, just as in the cases of the goats and the funeral knife. The Ramban first suggests that the heirs were minors and their property could not be collected until they matured, though in my view Rabbi Yosef Halevi's explanation is preferable. Even against minors, a person who has a valid migo is believed, as is also explicit in the Rambam, Raavad, Maggid Mishneh, Semag, and Baal HaTerumot. Rabbi Yosef Halevi explains that Rabbah bar Sharshom admitted that the land belonged to the heirs and that the mortgage term had ended. His concern was that if he returned the land and then sued, he would have to swear before collecting from heirs. He therefore retained the land. Since he held it, the heirs could not remove it from him because he was believed through a migo. His intent was that he could continue consuming produce up to the amount of his debt, and if the heirs challenged him he would be believed because the land remained in his hand. It follows from Rabbi Yosef Halevi and both explanations of the Ramban that one may consume future produce when he has a migo that he purchased the land. The fact that Rabbah bar Sharshom possessed a document for the debt does not alter this. Since the heirs were minors, he could not collect by the document alone and depended upon the migo. The Ramban also compares the case directly to movable property. Nor can it be said that Rabbah claimed that the land remained mortgaged for the additional debt. The Gemara states that he held the land under one mortgage and had a separate debt against the father. The Rambam accordingly rules that where one consumed the land for the period of presumption during the father's lifetime, he may say, through the migo that he could have claimed purchase, that the father owed him money, and he may collect that amount from the produce. The Semag and Baal HaTerumot rule likewise. The Raavad disagrees only as to whether he may collect without an oath, comparing the case to one who swears and collects upon collateral. He nevertheless agrees that the migo permits future collection. The Maggid Mishneh defends the Rambam's ruling that no oath is needed. The Nimukei Yosef and other authorities similarly indicate that he may continue collecting. The Rosh also plainly disagrees with Rabbeinu Yonah. His second resolution of the incident concerns future produce, for otherwise the migo would add nothing where the creditor already possessed a document. His wording shows that the migo serves to permit future consumption. I am therefore surprised that the Tur records Rabbeinu Yonah's view, since the Rosh clearly disagrees. Perhaps the Tur intended only to report it, not to adopt it. The Tur first speaks of retaining the field and consuming its produce, which indicates future produce. He later rules that one who claims a separate debt and wishes to retain the land until that amount has been consumed is not believed only where the force of the migo has been weakened by a public report. This implies that absent such a report, the migo is valid even for future produce. The Bach also understands the Tur as speaking of future produce, though his distinction between past and future produce where the migo has been weakened does not appear correct. The Rosh and Nimukei Yosef indicate that when the migo is impaired, even produce already consumed may have to be returned. The plain meaning of the Gemara also shows that Rabbah bar Sharshom wished to keep the land and consume future produce. Abaye told him that since he could no longer claim purchase, he must return the land. This implies that had the purchase claim remained available, he could have retained the land and continued consuming its produce. Furthermore, if the case concerned only produce already consumed, Rabbah could have claimed that he had already repaid what he consumed. The fact that the heirs were minors does not prevent such a claim, for one may assert that he paid their guardian. The correct explanation of the Gemara is therefore that of Rabbi Yosef Halevi. As for the oath, it may be said that in Rabbah bar Sharshom's time the rabbinic oath of denial had not yet been enacted, or that one does not swear upon a doubtful claim. The Rambam and Semag rule that, as to future produce, the migo permits collection without the special oath imposed upon one who comes to collect from heirs. The Raavad's objection based upon the oath required of one holding collateral was addressed above. According to the Rambam, that oath does not apply to land. As a practical matter, however, I concluded in siman 149, seif katan 22, that the Geonic oath applies even to land, whereas an ordinary oath of denial does not apply because the heirs have only a doubtful claim.
