And they do not know for how many years, etc. The Tur cites Ramban’s view, which is the first opinion here, that where it is known that one entered the field as a mortgagee, he can never establish chazakah. It then cites Ramah’s dissenting view, which is the second opinion here, and says that from the words of his father the Rosh it appears that the law follows Ramban. The Tur then writes that Rabbenu Yonah says that if witnesses testify that this field was mortgaged to him for a number of years but they do not know how many years, etc., which is the third opinion stated here. Perishah asks why Rabbenu Yonah is placed after the statement that the Rosh appears to agree with Ramban, since Rabbenu Yonah disagrees with the Rosh and his position inclines toward Ramah. The Tur should have placed Rabbenu Yonah next to Ramah, and should not have introduced him with the expression “and Rabbenu Yonah wrote.” See there, where Perishah answers with difficulty. In my view there is an additional question. Why does the Tur say only that it “appears” from the Rosh that he agrees with Ramban, when the Rosh explicitly writes, in discussing Rabbah bar Sharshom, the same rule as Ramban, and Tosafot write likewise? We must therefore understand Tosafot and Rosh carefully. They write concerning Rabbah bar Sharshom’s statement that he would suppress the mortgage document that this applies specifically because by suppressing the mortgage document it would not be known that the property had originally come into his hands as a mortgage. Had it been known from the outset that it came to him as a mortgage, Rabbah bar Sharshom could not mistakenly think that he could claim, “I purchased it from him,” for this would be like the case of a craftsman or sharecropper where it is known that he originally entered in that capacity. End quote. This requires explanation. Rabbah bar Sharshom surely did not consume the field beyond the years of the mortgage during the debtor’s lifetime, for on what basis could he wrongfully retain the debtor’s property while the debtor remained silent? Rather, the mortgage term necessarily ended when the debtor died, and thereafter Rabbah bar Sharshom continued to possess the property against the orphans, as the Gemara explains, in order to avoid the oath required against orphans. If he had not suppressed the mortgage deed, that deed would itself have stated after how many years the land was to return without payment. There would then be no chazakah at all, because during the father’s lifetime he possessed the field only for the mortgage term, and after the father died there can be no chazakah against minor orphans. Tosafot therefore did not need the separate rule that one who originally entered as a mortgagee can never establish chazakah, as Ramban holds. Even if Tosafot and Rosh independently accepted that rule, this passage would not be the place for it because the difficulty can be resolved without it.
However, much is taught here. In his Chiddushim, Ramban discusses the Gemara’s statement that Rabbah bar Sharshom could not claim purchase because a public report had gone out. Ramban asks why a mere report should prevent him from asserting purchase in the face of that report. He cites others who explain that the “report” was actually supported by witnesses who testified that he entered the field as a mortgagee. See there. We must say that those witnesses did not know for how many years it had been mortgaged. Had they known, their testimony would be equivalent to the mortgage deed itself, and it would make no sense to speak of suppressing that deed, since the witnesses would know its contents. We can now understand Tosafot and Rosh. Their purpose was to establish that even a mere public report destroys the migo, as Tosafot explicitly write in the passage beginning “purchase,” and likewise the Rosh. One might otherwise explain, like those cited by Ramban, that the report consisted of actual witnesses, while suppression of the mortgage deed concealed only the number of years of the mortgage. Tosafot therefore write that suppressing the mortgage deed must mean that because of the suppression it would not be known at all that the property had entered his hands as a mortgage. Had it been known that it came to him as a mortgage, meaning as those interpreters in Ramban explain, while suppression concealed only the number of years, Rabbah bar Sharshom could not have thought that he could claim purchase, because that would be like a craftsman and sharecropper whose original entry in that capacity is known. How could he have contemplated claiming purchase? We must therefore conclude that by suppressing the mortgage deed the entire fact of the mortgage became unknown. It follows that there were no witnesses at all, only a public report, and nevertheless the migo was lost. This is what Tosafot and Rosh sought to prove, and their words are precise and clear. Accordingly, Tosafot and Rosh may not agree with Ramban at all, but rather with Rabbenu Yonah, who distinguishes between witnesses testifying merely that a mortgage was “for years” and witnesses testifying simply that it was a mortgage, which by default means one year. We must then explain the comparison to a sharecropper and craftsman. A sharecropper and craftsman have no fixed termination point. [The text is deficient here.]
