Sema writes that if there was no coercive protest but the owner merely used a device, first giving the property to another and then selling it to this person, the resulting debt is treated like one documented by a written instrument. Taz disagrees. Tumim asks that this resembles one who sells property he does not own and later acquires it from the true owner, where the first purchaser acquires because the seller is presumed to want to maintain his credibility. For that reason the resulting obligation is also treated like one documented by a written instrument, as explained in Bava Metzia. In my view the cases differ. There, the seller plainly wants to keep faith with the first purchaser, and when he initially sold the property he intended to acquire it from the true owner so that his sale would stand, as the Gemara explains. Here, by contrast, his conduct itself demonstrates the opposite. He deliberately structured the transaction as a device to undermine the later sale. The Gemara's language that he intends to evade the other party applies precisely here, because the entire arrangement was one of artifice and circumvention.
