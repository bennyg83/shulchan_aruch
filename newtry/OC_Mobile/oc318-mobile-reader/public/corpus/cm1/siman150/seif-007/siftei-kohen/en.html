Ravina's challenge concerning a Sura mortgage cannot refer only to Rav Yehudah, as Rashi explains, for then the Gemara should have placed the challenge immediately after Rav Yehudah's statement. It must refer also to Rav Pappa, and all the more so according to Maharsha's reading that it refers specifically to Rav Pappa. In any event, Tosafot plainly do not understand it as referring solely to Rav Yehudah. The passage should instead be explained as follows. A document “intended for collection” means a document through which the creditor will ultimately collect his debt, not merely a document governing consumption of produce. The creditor is therefore careful to preserve it. Once he admits that the land is not purchased property, we tell him to produce his document. His claim that it was lost is not credible, because a person certainly guards a document by which he is to collect when the mortgage ends. We suspect that he has concealed it temporarily so that he may consume another two years of produce. If, however, he claims that he later purchased the land and that the original mortgage document was lost, he may be believed, because after the purchase he no longer needed to preserve the mortgage document. Alternatively, one who admits the mortgage is not believed through a migo that he could have claimed purchase because he may presently be concealing the document to consume two additional years, intending later to produce it, say that he found it, repay the unauthorized produce, and still collect the original debt. Had he claimed purchase, he could never later use the mortgage document. His proposed alternative claim is therefore not a true migo. The Gemara then asks from a Sura mortgage, which states that when the stated years end the land returns without payment. Such a document is not intended for collection. If the concern depends upon a document intended for collection, then in a Sura mortgage the holder should be believed to say that he had a document and lost it, and all the more so to claim purchase. Ordinarily, one who first admits that the land came to him by a written mortgage and later claims purchase would not be believed without producing the mortgage document. The presumption created by three years applies where he claims purchase from the outset, but not where he admits an earlier mortgage and claims a later purchase. Yet once the distinction is made between documents intended and not intended for collection, the question arises why the loss claim should not be accepted where the document was not intended for collection. The Gemara did not simply ask why he could not claim that he purchased the land from the outset, because Sura mortgages are publicly known. This also resolves why the Tur cites Rabbeinu Yonah here but rules without qualification in siman 317 that a tenant is believed through a migo. A lease document is not intended for collection, and even Rabbeinu Yonah agrees in that setting. I later found this explanation stated expressly by the Baal HaIttur at the end of the laws of apotiki. He disagrees with the Rif and rules like Rav Zevid and Rav Avira. He also cites Rabbi Moshe bar Chanoch as ruling like Rav Avira and explains that a document not intended for collection includes a Sura mortgage, which provides that the land returns without payment when the years end. In such a mortgage the holder may claim that he purchased the land, because the owner was required to protest. If he says that he had a document and lost it, he is believed through a migo. By contrast, an ordinary mortgage with deduction from the principal is intended for collection. The creditor carefully preserves the document, and if he suppresses it we do not accept a migo. He must produce the document. Although this interpretation appears clear to me, I do not have the confidence to rule outright against the Rif, Rambam, Rosh, and those who follow them. Yet Rabbi Moshe bar Chanoch, the Baal HaIttur, Halachot Gedolot, Raavan, and Rabbeinu Yonah disagree, and they are major authorities. Their reading also appears more convincing in the Gemara. I therefore rule at least that the matter is a doubt in law. In an ordinary mortgage with deduction, if the creditor has not yet consumed the produce of the disputed two years and wishes to do so, the land is removed from him and returned to the borrower, because land remains in its owner's presumptive possession and the burden of proof lies upon the claimant. In a Sura mortgage or a lease, however, the holder is believed through a migo to retain the land for another two years. If he already consumed five years and the owner claims that only three were authorized, the creditor is in possession of the produce already consumed. Even where he now lacks a migo, such as where he consumed in the presence of witnesses, and the document's witnesses have died so that he apparently cannot prove his claim, the produce is not removed from him. According to the Rif, Rambam, Rosh, and their followers he had a valid migo that he purchased the land, and this is sufficient to prevent recovery of the produce already consumed, even in an ordinary mortgage with deduction.
