I had a mortgage document and it was lost. Since the poskim do not distinguish here between a case where there was a public report and one where there was not, as they do in siman 149, seif 20, it appears that here he is believed even where there was such a report. Shitah Mekubetzet, in the name of Rashba and many poskim, writes that a Sura mortgage normally has publicity, and the entire sugya deals with a case where there is such publicity. Necessarily the report emerged only after the three years, for otherwise there would be no chazakah, as explained in siman 149. Even so, there is a difficulty, because a migo does not ordinarily prevail against a public report, as explained there. The answer is that this case is different. Ramban in Sefer HaZechut, in the chapter HaKotev concerning a bundle of documents, asks why a mortgage claim itself is not independently a substantial claim. A pledge does not require writing and delivery. He answers that because the holder seeks to collect money from the other person's property, he needs a migo. According to this, just as one can claim outright purchase, one can also claim a mortgage for whatever period he asserts. There is no reason that a claim of total purchase should be accepted without a migo while a claim of partial purchase for a fixed term should require one. A Sura mortgage, in which the body of the produce is transferred, is like a partial purchase. The claimant's admission that he acquired only part of the right is comparable to returning a lost object concerning the remainder. Since the public report does not contradict the mortgage claim itself, he is believed even where there is publicity. There is an additional reason. Rashba, cited in Choshen Mishpat siman 72, seif katan 18, with Shach there, seif katan 94, rules that if there are witnesses that an object is held as collateral but they do not know the amount of the debt, the lender is believed under oath because he is the possessor. Shach in siman 278, seif katan 6, writes that this applies both to a pledge taken at the time of the loan and to a land mortgage. Even though the pledge is not acquired in those forms, the holder is nevertheless called the possessor. Thus the migo of purchase here is unlike the ordinary migo of the Gemara, where the migo functions as evidence that the claimant speaks truth because he could have made a stronger false claim. Here the meaning is that because, had he wished, he could have claimed purchase and would have been believed, he is therefore deemed the possessor, like any pledge holder, and he is believed to state how much is owed on the collateral. This also resolves Rashi's difficult words in Bava Metzia 106. Rashi explains that Ravina's question, "If so, what remedy is there for a Sura mortgage?" is directed only against Rav Yehudah. This seems very difficult, because everyone agrees that a purchase claim is believed, and Rav Pappa disputes only the mortgage claim. According to what I have explained, however, Ravina had no difficulty at all from the possibility of a purchase claim itself. As Shitah Mekubetzet and Shach explain, a Sura mortgage has publicity, so there is no serious concern that one will brazenly claim outright purchase against the report. Ravina's problem is specifically with Rav Yehudah, who holds that the possessor is also believed to extend the mortgage term for as long as he wishes. Even if there is no concern of an outright purchase claim, there remains concern that he will wrongfully extend the mortgage term. This also explains the Gemara's statement that Rav Zevid does not agree with Rav Yehudah because a document intended for collection is carefully guarded. The implication is that a weakness in the missing document defeats the mortgage claim. Yet with an ordinary debt instrument, which is certainly intended for collection, the borrower can sometimes claim that the document was lost and rely on a migo, as seen in Bava Batra concerning the whispered claim before Rabbah. According to what I have explained, there is no difficulty. In the mortgage case, because of the public report, he is not believed by ordinary migo. His credibility rests on the strength of the mortgage claim itself and his status as possessor. Once there is a weakness from his failure to preserve the mortgage document, he is no longer believed. Shach explains the sugya at length and with considerable difficulty. His question on Maharsha, however, is indeed strong. Maharsha's own statement that Ravina's question is directed only against Rav Pappa is also difficult, because Rav Yehudah expressly says that the possessor is believed through a migo of purchase. It appears that Maharsha can be explained as follows. Tosafot write that a protest is effective only if the owner says, "So and so is consuming my land as a robber." That requirement makes sense only if chazakah and protest are understood as a rabbinic enactment because a person cannot preserve his deed forever. The Sages then required a specific form of protest, and they did not enact the same form regarding a mortgage claim. But according to the rationale that chazakah is learned from an ox that becomes forewarned, as Rashi explains, once it has been established that the owner remained silent, the matter is d'oraisa. On that approach there is no distinction between a protest saying, "He is a robber," and a protest saying, "I mortgaged the land to him and will sue him tomorrow." In either case the owner's silence has not been established, because he is in fact protesting daily. The core function of protest under every explanation is to warn the possessor to preserve his deed. Accordingly, Rav Zevid's statement that a document intended for collection is carefully guarded can be understood only according to the approach that chazakah is a rabbinic enactment. Where the possessor must preserve his document anyway, the enactment of chazakah falls away. Rav Yehudah, by contrast, can hold that chazakah is d'oraisa and operates even in a setting where one would otherwise be expected to preserve his document. On that understanding, a mortgage protest is also effective. Ravina therefore could not ask Rav Yehudah what remedy exists for a Sura mortgage. The question is only against Rav Pappa, who holds that protest is a rabbinic device requiring the specific wording enacted by the Sages. Ravina had proof that chazakah must be effective even against a mortgage claim, for otherwise even a claim of robbery would never be defeated by chazakah, because the possessor would always need to preserve his deed against the possibility of a mortgage claim. Ravina therefore asks what remedy the owner has to prevent chazakah in a Sura mortgage, since the Sages must have provided some means to defeat it. Otherwise they would have enacted something that inevitably causes loss. This also explains why Tosafot had to say that Ravina was asking what remedy was available rather than that the possessor should simply not be believed. According to both the rationale of established silence and the rationale of inability to preserve a deed, chazakah itself must remain effective even against a mortgage claim. Ravina therefore asks only what form of protest the Sages established to protect the owner.
