Even if the money thrown in repayment landed far from the creditor, there can still be a question of discharge where the debtor placed it within an area the creditor could guard. Maggid Mishneh reads Rambam through the Jerusalem Talmud as limiting the debtor's liability to money sufficiently near the creditor, but Tosafot in Gittin suggests the rule can apply even when the money is physically farther away so long as it is within the creditor's effective guarded domain. The decisive issue is not geometric distance but control. Rabbi Yochanan defines "near to him" and "near to her" by the ability to guard the object. Thus where the creditor can guard the money, payment may be effective even if the coins are not literally adjacent to him. Where he cannot guard them, the debtor remains responsible unless the creditor explicitly accepted the risk.
The Gemara explains "half to half" by two possible structures. Rabbah and Rav Yosef establish contradictory pairs of witnesses, one saying the object was nearer to him and the other nearer to her. Rabbi Yochanan defines nearness by the ability to guard, and "half to half" as a situation where each can guard it equally. The practical principle is that legal possession in these cases depends on effective control, not simple physical measurement. Where two equal evidentiary or guarding claims remain, the law follows the relevant rules of doubt rather than treating one side as having exclusive possession.
