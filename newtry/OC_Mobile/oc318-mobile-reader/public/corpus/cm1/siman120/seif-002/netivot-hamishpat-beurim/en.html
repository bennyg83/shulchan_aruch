Here is your money, tied up and ready. See Sema in siman 70, seif katan 17, and siman 74, seif katan 8, and see Taz. The matter requires explanation in light of Baal HaTerumot, Gate 50, who distinguishes between a craftsman who says, "Take what is yours," in which case he becomes an unpaid custodian, and the case of sesame at the end of siman 198, where the person does not even become an unpaid custodian. The distinction appears to be that when a craftsman says, "Take what is yours," he merely states that he will no longer retain the article as security for his wages; he does not necessarily withdraw from custody of it. But where money is concerned, saying, "Take your money," plainly means that the owner should actually take it, and the statement is therefore intended to terminate the speaker's responsibility for custody. In the case of a loan after its due date, the borrower's statement, "Take what is yours," clearly terminates his liability as borrower. During the term, however, it may mean only that he waives his right to retain the money until the due date, while the money remains a loan until it is actually repaid. Since the money remains available to him for business use, he would then continue to bear even liability for unavoidable loss. The Rama in siman 74, who exempts him in the law of a deposit, is discussing theft or loss of money that the custodian had been permitted to use. By saying that the money is tied up and ready, he withdraws from his permission to use it and thereby loses the benefit that made him a paid custodian. He cannot be compelled to continue using the money merely so that he will remain subject to that level of responsibility. The ruling does not address negligence during the agreed term, for which he remains liable. Full exemption arises where the lender refuses to accept repayment and the borrower says that because of that refusal he will take the money home; then the law of forced repayment applies. If the lender says to keep the money as a loan and the borrower remains silent, the loan remains in force and the borrower remains liable. The Sema's question from siman 198 can therefore be answered: there the person merely says, "Take what is yours," while the other remains silent, and that does not constitute repayment. It is also possible that the poskim do not disagree at all. The authority who exempts speaks after the lender has refused to accept the money, whereas the authority who imposes liability speaks of the bare statement, "Take what is yours." If the lender consents and tells the borrower to leave the money in a particular place, and it is then stolen, the borrower is exempt, as stated in the gloss to siman 126, seif 9.
