Not by way of inheritance. Maharit writes that explicit exclusion of inheritance helps only where the recipient is one son among several sons. The additional amount he receives over his brothers is not his by Torah inheritance unless the father chooses to assign it to him. Therefore the father can say that this additional amount is being given only as a gift and not by inheritance. But if there is only one heir, who inherits everything automatically by Torah law, Maharit argues that saying "not by way of inheritance" should accomplish nothing. The inheritance comes to him automatically, without need for the father's words. How can the father uproot it merely by saying the gift is not through inheritance? Even saying, "My son shall not inherit me," ordinarily has no effect. Nevertheless, the poskim make no such distinction, and their general language implies that explicit gift language excluding inheritance works even with a sole heir. This is readily understandable according to Ramban, who says inheritance without interruption applies specifically where the decedent himself begins the transfer by placing the heir into the property with special protective status. Here, by saying explicitly that he is not giving by inheritance but only as a gift, he did not begin with the Torah inheritance structure at all. He began with gift language, which can be interrupted. Maharit himself explains Ramban along those lines. Even according to the great commentators cited by Nimukei Yosef, the result can be the same. Once he explicitly gives through gift language and says "not by inheritance," he has uprooted the inheritance aspect within the transfer he is making. There is no difference whether inheritance is displaced by giving to another person or by giving the heir himself a gift with an express structure that the later recipient is to follow. Rosh, responsum kelal 81, siman 1, writes that if one gives property to an heir on condition that the heir perform a specified act, inheritance without interruption does not apply if the condition fails. Because the gift was conditional and the condition was not fulfilled, the gift is retroactively void and the property never left the father's estate under that transfer. If he gives to an heir on a condition and says that if the condition is not fulfilled the property shall go to another person, the result again depends on the dispute cited above from Nimukei Yosef. According to Ramban, since the donor's initial placement of the heir fails retroactively when the condition fails, inheritance without interruption does not arise, and the later recipient can acquire much like one who says the property shall go to another person after a period. According to the great commentators, who hold that even a delayed later gift can be defeated by inheritance without interruption, the matter differs. They do agree that where the condition itself is one the later recipient can immediately fulfill, inheritance without interruption may not apply. But where the condition is not under the later recipient's control, the case resembles an ordinary delayed transfer. Mordechai, chapter 13, siman 598, writes concerning one who gave property to his son on condition that if the son die without viable offspring it revert to his daughters. He rules the condition ineffective because inheritance has no interruption. Although failure of the condition would retroactively defeat the first son's gift, the later recipients do not control the condition, and Mordechai treats it like a delayed transfer under the view of the great commentators. Rosh's responsum cited by Tur, involving defined gifts to several sons, should be understood consistently with these distinctions.
If he gave the money through a third party. See Shach, seif katan 5. The reference is mistakenly printed under "and he said, not by inheritance"; it belongs under the words "and he said, give." Beit Yosef states explicitly that if the money was placed with a third party, even ordinary gift language is effective. Maharit questions this reasoning: what difference does placement with a third party make? Wherever the property is, it ultimately stands to fall into the heir's domain. Ketzot HaChoshen explains the rule through the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. In my view that principle does not apply here for the reason stated in responsa Maharam Padua, siman 50: wherever the second recipient acquires only through the first recipient, the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words is not the mechanism. There is a proof from Bava Batra 129. The Gemara proves that gift language can assist inheritance language even between two different persons from the rule, "their sons shall inherit in their place." If Ketzot were correct, that proof could be rejected by saying the effectiveness there rests merely on the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words. Rather, there is no such objection. Since the second acquires only through the first, the mitzvah principle is not independently doing the work. Indeed, it appears that if the property was deposited with a third party and the donor said in inheritance language, "So-and-so shall inherit this amount," that too can be effective in the relevant setting, because the issue of a stipulation contrary to Torah law does not arise in the same way when the second recipient takes only through the first. Ketzot himself essentially recognizes this. Therefore the Gemara can validly prove that gift language strengthens inheritance language even for separate persons. Sema, seif katan 7, implies rejection of Ran's first answer, and from that it appears he also rejects Ran's second answer. The underlying reason is necessary to explain. Ran had suggested that the heirs received only a weekly shekel while the balance was given to others, so perhaps the heirs never acquired the whole. Yet because the heirs' right nevertheless extends throughout the whole fund, in the sense that if part is lost the loss is borne by the later recipients rather than by the heirs, their acquisition spreads through the estate sufficiently for us to say inheritance has no interruption.
