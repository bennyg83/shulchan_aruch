Even if the second is also fit to inherit. Ramban states that if the first is an heir, the rule that inheritance has no interruption applies even when the second is also an heir. Once inheritance vests in the first it remains his, like an ordinary Torah inheritance. He criticizes authorities who distinguish where the later recipient is also an heir. There is, however, a rationale for that distinction based on Ramban and Ran's treatment of "after you to myself" and "after you to my heirs." When a donor reserves the property for himself after the first recipient, the reservation is effective from now and the first receives only the fruits for his lifetime. Ramban extends this to "after you to my heirs": the heirs' future right is reserved from the present. Where the second is himself an heir, one can therefore read the phrase "after you" as a present reservation for an heir, similar to "after you to myself." If the later right exists from now, the first never receives full title to the body and the rule of uninterrupted inheritance does not apply. Rama follows the rule that an explicit "from now" in favor of the later recipient prevents the first from entering full inheritance of the body. The Talmudic challenge from Rav Acha can be understood under the opposite premise. The baraita says that if the second dies during the first's lifetime, the property returns to the first's heirs. That result proves the first possessed the body, not merely lifetime fruits. Once that is established, Rav Acha's rule applies and the inheritance cannot be interrupted. Under our ruling like Rav Papi, however, a reservation "after you to my heirs" can be treated as present, and the first may possess only the fruits. On that analysis there is room for the second heir's later right despite Ramban's own rejection of the distinction.
