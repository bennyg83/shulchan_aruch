An inheritance has no interruption. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, end of siman 132. He writes that he is uncertain about one who says, "My property to my brother's son, and after him to so and so," where at the time of the gift the donor's brother was alive but the brother later died before the recipient, his son. Likewise, where one says, "My property to so and so, after him to my brother's son, and after him to so and so," and the donor's brother dies before the first recipient, so that when the property later comes to the nephew he is then an heir entitled to inherit it because his father, the donor's brother, has died. Does the rule that inheritance cannot be interrupted apply in such a case? Rivash, siman 167, writes that one who says, "My property to so and so, and after him to my son," cannot interrupt the son's rights. But perhaps that is only where, at the time of the original disposition, the later recipient was already fit to inherit, so he acquires through inheritance. Here, at the time of the disposition, the nephew was not yet fit to inherit and the transaction already had the character of a gift, because had his father remained alive he would have taken only by gift. Perhaps therefore the gift can be interrupted. Alternatively, since at the time the property actually reaches his hands he is then fit to acquire it by inheritance, perhaps he takes by inheritance and it cannot be interrupted. Even according to Rambam, whose view is codified in seif 2, that where the donor expressly says the heir is to take not by inheritance but only by gift, the donor may interrupt his interest, our case remains uncertain. True, when the gift was first made the nephew's father was alive, so the donor could not then bequeath the property to him and must have intended a gift. Yet perhaps Rambam permits interruption only where the donor expressly uproots the recipient's inheritance rights. In an unstated case, the donor may be understood as intending the recipient to acquire through every legal right available, so that once the brother dies the nephew also acquires by inheritance. Rambam writes that where a healthy person gives property to his son and after him to another person, the later gift is effective. Maggid Mishneh explains that a healthy person cannot confer inheritance and therefore the son takes only by gift. This might seem to prove that even if the son later has inheritance rights upon his father's death, the gift can nevertheless pass to the later recipient. But Maharik, responsa part 2, simanim 6 and 74, explains that Rambam speaks only where a healthy person gives to one son among several sons. If he gives to his only son, the later recipient does not acquire. Thus there is no proof. In the multiple son case, even at the father's death that son possesses the specific property only by gift, because as inheritance all brothers would share equally. In our case, however, when the nephew ultimately receives the property he can then acquire it by inheritance, and perhaps inheritance cannot be interrupted. The matter requires study. See there also his challenge to the Maggid Mishneh's explanation.
Where they were accustomed to litigate in gentile courts. See Baer Heitev. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that this ruling is from Rivash, while Responsa Tashbetz, part 1, siman 61, disputes Rivash and writes that Heaven forbid one should thereby extinguish and destroy a Jew's legal right. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, agrees with Tashbetz. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 142, who also cites Tashbetz and writes that had the Rama seen his words, he plainly would not have disagreed, for they are persuasive. Chatam Sofer further suggests that Rivash himself spoke only in the circumstances of his own case. The expression "gift" does not itself mean inheritance. It is only Torah terminology that treats a gift to one who is entitled to inherit as also an inheritance. If it is clear that the deathly ill donor did not intend Torah terminology, then even under Torah law the disposition is a gift rather than inheritance and can be interrupted. Since the matter turns on intention and language, and those people were unfamiliar with Torah terminology because they conducted their affairs through gentile courts, Torah law itself construes their words as a gift rather than an inheritance. But Heaven forbid that one imagine uprooting Torah statutes and laws even by a hairbreadth. He adds that this is close to the Sema's intent in siman 369, seif katan 20. The Rama there discusses one who says, "and after you to so and so from today," where "from today" is effective because the second recipient acquires retroactively, so the first recipient's interest can be interrupted. Here, however, the issue is that the first recipient himself was given the property "from today," and one might say that inheritance cannot be interrupted.
