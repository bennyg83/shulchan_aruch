Fit to inherit from him. See Sema, seif katan 1, who writes that the same applies if he said, "my property shall be yours," and so forth. If he did not say at all, "my property shall be yours," but only said, "my property is given to so and so after my son's death," the authorities disagree, as cited by Nimukei Yosef, chapter 13, page 214. Ramban maintains that only where the testator himself placed the heir into possession of the property, such as by saying, "my property shall be yours," do we say that inheritance cannot be interrupted. See Responsa Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 74, for the explanation of his words. But where the heir takes by ordinary Torah inheritance, a gift of a shechiv mera can be effective for the later recipient even after a period of time, although the donor is no longer alive. The reason is explained in Responsa Rosh, rule 84, siman 1: every gift of a shechiv mera takes effect when the donor is no longer alive, whether according to the view that it takes effect at the completion of death or after death. Therefore, in a case of "after you," what difference is there whether the acquisition takes effect immediately after death or only later? See there. According to the great commentators cited by Nimukei Yosef there, the distinction is different. A gift of a shechiv mera subject to a condition that delays the recipient's entry into the gift is effective, but where the gift itself is not to take effect until later because the inheritance first passes to an heir, we say that inheritance cannot be interrupted. As to the practical ruling, see what I write in seif katan 5. See also Sema, seif katan 2, where there is a scribal error. It should read: "then we do not say that inheritance cannot be interrupted except where the recipient was a son," and so forth.
Even if he consecrated it after him. Meaning, he said, "my property to my son, and after him to hekdesh." See Sema, seif katan 4, who writes that if he stipulated that when his son leaves the house the poor shall acquire it, then immediately upon the son's leaving, the poor acquire it. See Beit Yosef, who strongly questions this, and Darkei Moshe, who resolves their words, though his explanation is forced. In my humble opinion, this ruling depends upon the dispute above in seif katan 1. According to Ramban there, whose view is primary as I write in seif katan 5, it depends upon the wording. If he said, "this house to my son, and if my son leaves the house it shall belong to the poor," or, "after he leaves it shall belong to the poor," we say that inheritance cannot be interrupted. But if he did not first say, "my house to my son," and merely said, "if my son leaves the house," or, "after my son leaves the house, it shall belong to the poor," as in the incident cited in the responsum of the Geonim, the poor acquire it.
If he made the will. See Sema, seif katan 5. There is a scribal error there, and it should read, "see the end of siman 369." The essential reason is that since this has become the accepted practice, it is as though he expressly stated that he gives it as a gift and not by way of inheritance.
But a healthy person. See Sema, seif katan 6, who states that a healthy person cannot give using the language of inheritance. See Maggid Mishneh for the reason: since a healthy person cannot transfer by the language of inheritance, his statement must be construed as a gift. At first glance, the same should apply even to a shechiv mera who has both a firstborn and an ordinary son and says, "my property to my ordinary son as a gift, and after him to so and so." We should not say that inheritance cannot be interrupted, because he cannot give to the ordinary son through language of inheritance, only through the language of gift, as explained in siman 281. Even if there are several ordinary sons and he gives to one ordinary son in the language of inheritance, he has said nothing, as explained there in seif 6. Since he can transfer only through a gift, and a gift can be interrupted, the later recipient should acquire. Nevertheless, it appears that we do say here that inheritance cannot be interrupted. Maharit asks why ordinary language of gift is insufficient here unless he explicitly says that he did not intend inheritance, while with a firstborn, where Scripture says that one may not give the firstborn's portion to another, an ordinary gift to the regular son is effective. He answers that with a firstborn, even if the statement has both the character of inheritance and of gift it is effective, because he has placed it upon two legal tracks. Even if he did not uproot inheritance, that is immaterial, since he added and strengthened the regular son's right by a gift. By contrast, with "after you," even if he gives by way of gift, so long as he has not removed the status of inheritance, the language of gift does not negate inheritance. Since he has the Torah power to direct inheritance among his sons, the language of gift includes inheritance unless he explicitly uproots it. Once the property has fallen before heirs, the later recipient receives nothing. According to this, it appears that even here we say that inheritance cannot be interrupted. Since he used language of gift, we say he placed the transfer upon two legal tracks: against the firstborn it is effective by virtue of the gift component, while against the later recipient it is effective by virtue of the fact that he never uprooted the status of inheritance, as Maharit writes. This appears correct, and is also indicated by the authorities' unqualified formulations, which do not distinguish whether a firstborn is present. Know further that where a healthy person says, "and after you to so and so," without saying "from now," the later recipient acquires only if land was transferred by deed and the deed still exists at the first recipient's death. With other forms of acquisition that no longer exist when the first recipient dies, the acquisition is void, as explained in siman 191 regarding one who transfers property to take effect later. Likewise the donor must still be alive when the first recipient dies, for if the donor dies earlier, the acquisition is void since the donor no longer exists when the later acquisition is to take effect. Proof is Responsa Rosh, rule 84, siman 1, which states that with a shechiv mera's gift in an "after you" arrangement, it is effective even though the donor is not alive when the acquisition takes effect, because every gift of a shechiv mera takes effect when the donor is no longer alive. See what I wrote above in his name. Thus, for a healthy person who did not say "from now," the instrument of acquisition and the donor must both exist when the later acquisition takes effect. It follows likewise that the donor may retract before the first recipient dies, since the acquisition has not yet taken effect, just as one who gives property to take effect later without saying "from now" may retract. Therefore, even if language of inheritance could be effective for a healthy person, the principle that inheritance cannot be interrupted would have no application. If he says, "from now, and after him to so and so," the Rama rules in seif katan 7 that we do not apply the rule that inheritance cannot be interrupted. If he does not say "from now," the donor must still be alive when the later acquisition takes effect, namely after the first recipient's death. Since the later recipient acquires while the donor is still alive, the first recipient's heirs plainly have nothing to inherit. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise writes this, contrary to Maharit, and it is correct.
