If the first recipient is fit to inherit him. Rav Acha son of Rav Avya says that if a deathly ill person says, "My property to you, and after you to so and so," and the first is an heir, the second receives nothing because the language operates as inheritance, and inheritance once vested cannot be interrupted. Rambam limits this to language that can be read as inheritance; if the donor expressly says he gives by way of gift, the later recipient can take. Other authorities hold that even express gift language becomes inheritance when given to one who is his heir. Ramban discusses the baraita, "Give my sons a shekel each week, and if they die others shall inherit after them," where the others can receive after the sons. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi answers that the sons inherited only the weekly shekel, not the entire estate. Ramban objects that the later recipients nevertheless acquire only after the sons die. If Torah inheritance has already vested in the sons, how can any later gift interrupt it? The same problem arises in every conditional deathbed gift. He suggests that the Torah's power allowing a father to designate inheritance includes the ability to make it arise later or conditionally; the rule that inheritance has no interruption applies specifically when the donor begins by giving the entire property to the heir as inheritance. An alternate answer is that the assets were placed from the outset with a shalish. In that case the mechanism can be not only deathbed gift but also the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. Beit Yosef understands Rambam to say that when the assets are in a shalish's hands, even inheritance language need not allow the first heir to defeat the later recipient. Maharit and Maharashach object that if the first has truly inherited, placing the property with a third person cannot erase the Torah's rule that inheritance has no interruption.
Beit Yosef's position can nevertheless be sustained by distinguishing acquisition from the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. If the first recipient is an heir, the later recipient cannot acquire the property through the ordinary deathbed gift, because inheritance has no interruption. But the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's instructions does not itself transfer title. Rabbeinu Yerucham says that if heirs violate the instruction and sell, the intended recipient cannot remove the property from the purchaser. The heirs remain owners; they are merely obligated to carry out the deceased's direction. Therefore there is no contradiction with "inheritance has no interruption." We do not interrupt the heir's title at all. We say only that the heir, as owner, is under a mitzvah to deliver the property to the later recipient. That mitzvah applies where the property was deposited from the outset for fulfillment of the instructions, as required in siman 252. Accordingly, where such a shalish arrangement existed, Beit Yosef is correct that the heirs must deliver to the later recipient even though the first acquired as heir.
