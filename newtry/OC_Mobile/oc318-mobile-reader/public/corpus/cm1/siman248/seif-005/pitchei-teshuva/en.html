If he said, "my property," and so forth, but a Torah scroll, and so forth. See Baer Heitev and Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 43. He writes that he sees no basis to doubt whether a Torah scroll is included in a gift stating that the donor gives "all my books." On the contrary, our printed books are called "books" only by borrowed usage from a Torah scroll. The argument that a Torah scroll might be excluded because it is forbidden to sell it, by analogy to the uncertainty in the chapter Mi Shemet concerning "my property to so and so," is mistaken. The Gemara's question there is not that because a Torah scroll may not be sold the donor did not intend to include it. If it were linguistically included in "property," the prohibition against sale would not affect the scope of the gift. Rather, the Gemara's doubt is whether something that one may not sell is itself called "property." Here the document expressly gives "all the books," and a Torah scroll is certainly a book, so there is no doubt that it is acquired by the gift. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 143, concerning a similar case in which a man distributed his estate and gave all his books to two of his sons, leaving a Torah scroll worth several hundred gold coins. The heirs disputed whether it was included. Chatam Sofer cites Noda BiYehudah and discusses the issue at length. He concludes that according to the authorities who explain the Gemara's uncertainty as arising from the exceptional importance of a Torah scroll, comparable to gold and precious stones that may not be included in an unspecified gift of "property" absent express wording, there is likewise room to doubt whether a Torah scroll is included in the unqualified term "books." Since the heirs are in possession, the Torah scroll cannot be extracted from them. This remains so although there is a double doubt: perhaps the correct explanation is that the issue in the Gemara concerns the prohibition against selling a Torah scroll, which does not apply here in the same way; and even according to the view based on importance, the Gemara itself leaves the matter unresolved. We nevertheless do not extract money on the basis of a double doubt. Moreover, Bach in Choshen Mishpat 97, cited by the Shach there, seif katan 13, explains that the earlier rule concerned generations who actually studied from Torah scrolls. This applies all the more to an especially valuable Torah scroll worth several hundred gold coins. And if the two sons are greater Torah scholars than the other heirs, the donor's purpose in giving them the books is evidently that they study Torah from books intended for study, whereas there is no reason to prefer them over the other heirs with respect to the Torah scroll. Clear proof is therefore necessary to remove it from the heirs' possession. See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, part 6, siman 53, concerning a will stating that all the testator's books should go to his son, except that books of which the son already possessed copies should be given to the testator's son in law. The son already owned many books purchased with his own money and some books previously given to him by his father. The son argued that his father intended only books matching those he had earlier received from his father, so that only a few books would go to the son in law. The son in law argued that the clause covered every book of which the son owned a copy, however acquired, which would leave most of the library to him. Chatam Sofer ruled for the son. First, assessment of the father's intent indicated that he did not mean to divert so much inheritance from his son; the small number was sufficient, and there was no reason to increase the impropriety of diverting inheritance contrary to the spirit of the sages. This is especially so with books, concerning which there is an especially strong presumption in favor of the son. For this reason earlier authorities established the practice in a half male inheritance deed of excluding books and land, because the owner's name remains associated with them and they should not pass to another family. See what I wrote below, siman 281, seif 7. Second, the language of the document itself also favors the heir. He concludes that the son prevails not merely because the claimant under a document has the weaker hand, but under the substantive law as well, based on the donor's presumed intent and the language of the instrument. The son in law receives only those books in the estate of which comparable copies had already been given to the son by his father during the father's lifetime. See there.
