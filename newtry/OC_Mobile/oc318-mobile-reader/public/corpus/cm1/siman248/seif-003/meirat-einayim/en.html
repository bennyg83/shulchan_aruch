If the second recipient died during the lifetime of the first, etc. The Tur writes: he gave the third recipient rights only through the second, and since the property never came into the second recipient's possession, the third cannot acquire it. As long as it remains with the first recipient, both principal and produce belong entirely to the first, for he can sell it, etc.
The same is the law of the third recipient relative to the second. Meaning, if the property already came to the second after the first recipient's death, and the third recipient died during the second recipient's lifetime, the property remains with the second recipient's heirs.
The first recipient is forbidden, etc. This is so that the giver's words be fulfilled, for his desire was that the property come to the second recipient after the first dies. Nevertheless, if the first recipient violated this and sold it, the sale is valid.
He is called wicked. In the Perishah I cited Rashbam's explicit language that if the first recipient himself sells the property, although he commits a prohibition he is not called wicked on that account, because it was given to him as a complete gift for his lifetime and he sold or gave it away for his own benefit. The one who advises him to do so, however, derives no personal benefit and is called wicked. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who writes that all the more so the seller himself is called wicked.
If the first recipient freed them. He committed a transgression, violating the command, "You shall leave them as an inheritance to your children."
Or made them shrouds for a corpse. The Nimukei Yosef explains that he threw upon the corpse more shrouding material than the corpse required, so that there was no aspect of mitzvah. There is therefore a transgression, as the Tur and Mechaber write in Yoreh Deah siman 349, because he violates bal tashchit, disgraces the corpse, and increases decay upon it. One might have thought that the first recipient's act should not be effective, because the giver surely did not intend that his property be used to perform a transgression and gratuitously create a prohibition of benefit. The ruling teaches otherwise.
The second recipient removes it from the purchasers. Meaning, after the ten years. During those ten years the purchasers stand in place of the first recipient. This is obvious.
