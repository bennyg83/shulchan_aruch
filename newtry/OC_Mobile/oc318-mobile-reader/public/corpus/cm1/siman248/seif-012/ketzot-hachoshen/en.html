But if he said, "after you to me or to my heirs." Rama treats "after you to my heirs" like "after you to myself," and therefore a sale by the first is ineffective. Ramban explains that when the donor says "after you to myself," he has not removed himself from the property. He reserved a present right in the body and gave the first only lifetime fruits. The same, according to Ramban, applies to "after you to my heirs": the heirs' right is reserved from now. This creates tension with Rama's rulings in Even HaEzer that a wife's right under "after you to my heirs" can be considered prospective rather than presently possessed, so her husband does not inherit it. If the later heir right exists from now and the first cannot sell, it should resemble a Jubilee reversion and count as presently possessed. Beit Shmuel tried to resolve this by saying that according to our ruling "after you" is not ordinarily equivalent to "from now." Therefore even if the first cannot sell where the later beneficiary is the donor or his heirs, the later heirs still have only a future right. But that does not explain why the first cannot sell. Ramban's own reason for invalidating the sale is precisely that the donor or heirs retained a present right. Shach therefore proposed emending the Even HaEzer text so that the case there concerns "after her to her heirs," not "after her to my heirs." That would remove the contradiction, but another difficulty remains in Rama here. He says that if a daughter is the later heir and dies childless, more remote heirs do not stand in her place unless the donor said "from now to my heirs." If every phrase "after you to my heirs" already creates a present right, the explicit words "from now" should be unnecessary. I therefore suggest a different explanation of Rama. A bare phrase "after you to my heirs" is not automatically "from now." The reason the first recipient cannot sell is instead that the donor is particularly insistent that the property remain for himself or his heirs. Even in an ordinary "after you to another" arrangement, the first should not sell initially, but if he does, the donor is presumed not to have intended the first gift to fail retroactively. Where the donor says "after you to me" or "to my heirs," he is presumed to make preservation of the remainder an actual condition of the first gift. If the first sells, his gift is void retroactively and the property reverts to the donor or passes to his heirs. Rosh uses a similar structure concerning an etrog given "to you and after you to so and so." Because the first recipient must own the body in order to fulfill the mitzvah, the later clause can operate as a condition rather than as denial of bodily ownership. If the condition is violated under Rabbi's view, the first gift itself fails. On this approach, the first recipient can have full body and fruit ownership during his lifetime, while a sale violates a condition favoring the donor or his heirs. The later heir is therefore not presently possessed unless the donor expressly says "from now." This reconciles Rama's civil and marital rulings. Still, this explanation is offered chiefly to reconcile Rama. Ramban, from whom the doctrine originates, explicitly grounds "after you to my heirs" in a present reservation. Under Ramban, the later heirs are presently vested, a husband should treat the wife's right as possessed, and remote heirs can stand in the designated heir's place without an added "from now." The authorities therefore do not all fit Rama's synthesis, and the matter remains difficult.
