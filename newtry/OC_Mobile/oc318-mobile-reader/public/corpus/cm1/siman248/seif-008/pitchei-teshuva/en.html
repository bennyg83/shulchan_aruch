Everything that is called a garment. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 171, concerning one who directed on his deathbed that all his garments and clothing be given to a relative. The estate included several cubits of camel hair fabric purchased for making a new garment, and the question was whether this too was included in "his clothing." He cites Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 3, concerning a ketubah condition providing that the wife should take all her clothing, where the estate contained ten cubits of silk that the husband had purchased for her. Tzemach Tzedek ruled that she acquired the silk, relying on the rule above in siman 97, seif 25, that hekdesh and a creditor do not collect from items purchased for her. The same, he reasoned, applies when the husband dies, and she takes the material like her other garments. Shevut Yaakov disagrees. The cases are not comparable. There, because he purchased the material for her, he immediately conferred a right in it upon her even before it was made into clothing, so hekdesh or a creditor cannot displace her right. But where the husband dies, the Gemara in Ketubot 54 concludes that he did not transfer clothing to her with the intention that she take it and leave the marriage. Thus she has no independent right to the material and can claim only under the ketubah condition that she take "all her clothing." We therefore follow ordinary speech, under which an item is not called her clothing unless it has already been made and is ready to wear. The same applies to the present will. The relative does not acquire the camel hair fabric, because it is plainly not included in ordinary usage within "his garments and clothing" while it is not yet fit to wear at all. He adds that even if a garment had already been made from the material but the testator never wore it even once during his life, it is not entirely clear that it is included in "his clothing" so as to extract it from the heirs. See there. Ateret Tzvi cites this briefly and writes that in his own responsa, siman 23, he proved that once the garment has been made, even if the testator never wore it, the heirs must give it to the recipient. See also Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 11, concerning one who obligated himself by document to give another person "good garments." He may give garments of intermediate quality, because those too are called good. He adduces proof from Bava Batra 69b, where "a good palm tree" is explained by Rashbam as one of intermediate quality, and from Rosh Hashanah 16b, "and let them not be written with the righteous," referring to the book of intermediate people. Only if the document says "choice good garments" or "genuinely good garments" must he provide garments of truly superior quality rather than intermediate quality. See there.
