(Her offspring takes her place.) In the earlier editions it states that the husband does not take her place, because the property is merely prospective for him, etc. The Beit Yosef asks: since the donor said, "and after her, to my heirs," what difference does it make if the daughter has no offspring, seeing that the donor's brothers or nephews would then inherit him? This requires study. I am astonished at his question. If so, why does the Gemara say that if the grandmother had a son, he would inherit? In any event she would have some heir, so why should it matter whether she has a son? The wording in the earlier editions is correct. When the donor says, "and after her, to my heirs," he means the person who is his heir through the daughter, namely his daughter and anyone descending from her. Therefore her offspring takes her place. This excludes those who would inherit the donor only after the daughter's death, for they do not inherit through her, but would then be called the donor's own heirs, and they were not included in his wording. The husband, however, inherits through the daughter, and one might have thought that he too is included in "my heirs." We are therefore taught that he is excluded because the property is prospective for him. This also accords with the Rama's wording below, which refers only to the heirs he had at the time of the gift.
