But a healthy person who gave. Maggid Mishneh explains that a healthy person cannot redirect inheritance by using inheritance language. To transfer he must use gift language, and an ordinary gift can certainly be interrupted by a later interest. Maharit objects that the Talmudic question whether the rule of uninterrupted inheritance applies to a healthy donor was not conclusively resolved according to our text. According to Rabbeinu Chananel it may even have been resolved without distinction. Thus, he argues, where doubt exists between the heirs and the later recipient, the later recipient should not take by doubt. Maharit therefore limits Rambam's case to one son among several heirs. If the healthy father used language that might operate as inheritance, the favored son cannot by doubt remove the other heirs' presumptive shares. Once the first recipient himself has not certainly acquired, the later recipient cannot derive a right through him. But where that son is the sole heir, Maharit's view is that even a healthy gift can remain subject to the unresolved rule that perhaps it is inheritance and therefore cannot be interrupted. He concludes that the later recipient should receive nothing in that case.
Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch do not indicate that the healthy donor rule is limited to one son among several. Had that been intended, it should have been stated. Their language implies that the later recipient can take even if he is not an heir at all. The distinction is this. A deathbed transfer takes effect only at death. When property is given to an heir in that framework, even express gift language can operate as inheritance, because the Torah itself granted the deathly ill person power to designate his heir at death. Ramban says that what belongs to an heir by inheritance cannot truly be converted into a gift merely by changing the wording. A healthy person's gift is different. A valid gift by a healthy person transfers the body from now. A purported gift only after death would not be a gift at all. Once the father gives the body during his lifetime, this is not Torah inheritance but an ordinary gift, and ordinary gifts can contain later interests. Moreover, if the father transferred the body to his son during life and said "after him to so and so," the father has removed himself from the property. At his death the son does not newly inherit the body from his father, because the father no longer owns it. This differs from a Jubilee field, where the father retains a future reversionary right and the son can inherit that right at death. Here the donor has no remaining body to be inherited. There is a further decisive point. A healthy donor cannot practically make an "after you" gift that first takes effect only after the first recipient's death unless the later right is created from now. If the donor dies before the first recipient, no new acquisition can arise after the donor's death, just as a get cannot take effect after the husband's death. If the first recipient dies while the donor is still alive, the later recipient can acquire from the living donor and there is no inheritance issue at all. Thus the only workable form is a present later interest, and an explicit present interest prevents the first from receiving the entire body as inheritance. Therefore the rule that inheritance has no interruption does not apply to an ordinary healthy gift even according to Rabbeinu Chananel.
