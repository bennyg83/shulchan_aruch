A husband has the status of a purchaser. Maharashach discusses a will giving houses to Leah and, after her, to heirs born from her body. Another scholar argued that because Leah was unmarried when she received the gift and later married, her husband became a purchaser in his wife's property and acquired the houses. Maharashach sharply rejected this, reasoning that the testator's very reference to heirs born from Leah necessarily contemplated her later marriage, and therefore the testator plainly intended to exclude the husband from acquiring the houses. In my view there is no compelling difficulty. One who gives property to an unmarried woman and says, "to you, and after you to so and so," has created the ordinary law of "after you." Initially she may not transfer the property to her husband, just as the first recipient may not intentionally sell away the remainder interest. But marriage itself is not prohibited. She can marry while arranging an effective removal of her husband's rights from this particular property, as can be done during betrothal or through another effective disclaimer. If she nevertheless violates that duty and brings the property fully into the marriage, the husband has the law of a purchaser and can acquire. Therefore the testator's phrase, "after her to heirs born from her body," can refer to whatever remains after her, as it ordinarily does in every "after you" gift. If she preserves the property from her husband, it passes to those later heirs. If she instead places it completely into her husband's ownership, he is a purchaser and the remainder may be lost. The fact that the testator expected marriage does not itself eliminate the husband's purchaser status.
There is, however, a more fundamental point in this case. The gift after Leah was directed to "heirs born from her body," meaning people who did not yet exist. Such recipients do not acquire. Even a deathbed gift cannot be made to a person not yet in existence, because every rule ineffective for a healthy donor remains ineffective for a deathly ill donor unless the Sages specifically enacted otherwise. Rosh states expressly that "my property to you and after you to so and so" works only when the later recipient is already in existence. The special rules of a deathbed gift relax the need for the donor himself to remain alive at the moment the gift takes effect, but they do not create acquisition capacity in a recipient who does not yet exist. The Gemara likewise limits a deathbed gift to a fetus to special cases such as the donor's own child, because a person's intention is especially close to his child. Accordingly, the later heirs described here never acquired. Once the "after you" interest fails, the property remains with the first recipient and those who acquire through her. Even if Maharashach is correct that the phrase showed an intention to exclude the husband so long as the remainder gift could operate, the exclusion was only in favor of that remainder interest. Chacham Tzvi explains similarly that where a later recipient dies before the first recipient and the remainder therefore fails, the property returns to the first recipient's heirs, despite the original donor's wording. So too here. Even if Leah was already married and the donor said, "to Leah and after her to heirs born from her body," the husband would be excluded only for the sake of the valid remainder. Since the later unborn heirs cannot acquire, the remainder collapses, and the husband, as purchaser in his wife's property, can acquire. This is the clearer basis for the result.
