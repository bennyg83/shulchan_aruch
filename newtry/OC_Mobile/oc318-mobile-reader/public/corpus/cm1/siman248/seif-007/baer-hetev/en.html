Her descendants. The same applies to the descendants of her descendants for all generations. Whenever a person says, "after him, to my heirs," his intent is directed to the heir who at that time stands first in the legal order of inheritance. Therefore, if at the time of the declaration and gift he had a son or daughter, his intent was toward them and all their descendants, since those descendants would take precedence even over the giver's father, and certainly over his brothers and nephews. Because we rule that "after you" does not mean "from now," if the daughter dies leaving no descendants at all, the remaining relatives are considered strangers for this purpose, since the giver did not have them in mind. It is then comparable to one who says, "My property to So-and-so, and after him to So-and-so," and the second person dies during the first recipient's lifetime, in which case the property remains with the first recipient's heirs. If at the time of the declaration and gift the giver had no son or daughter but did have a brother, that brother and his descendants have the same status as the daughter and her descendants. We always say that when he said, "after him, to my heirs," his intent was toward the person who was then fit to inherit him and that person's descendants. This resolves the Beit Yosef's difficulty.
