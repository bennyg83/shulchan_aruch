After you, to me or to my heirs, etc. In the Derishah I wrote that according to the version of Rashbam, the Rosh, the Tur, and other authorities in Bava Batra 125b, in the incident of one who said, "My property to the grandmother, and after you to my heirs," and the donor had a daughter, one must conclude that if a person says, "My property to you, and after you to my heirs," and the first recipient sells it, the sale is effective. The Gemara explicitly says that if the grandmother sold it, her sale stands. Under that version, one must distinguish between "after you, to me" and "after you, to my heirs." Other versions read "and after you to her heirs." According to that version one can say that a reservation for one's heirs is like a reservation for oneself. Rivash likewise equates "to my heirs" with reserving to oneself. Rama, who also equates the two, evidently had the version "and after you to your heirs." His introductory words, "one who says my property to you and after you to so and so," are not limited to an unrelated person. Even if he says "after you to your heirs," he treats it like an ordinary later beneficiary, as will be explained immediately below.
He said, "After you to my heirs," and he had a daughter, etc. The Mechaber states the same matter below in seif 9. It is the Gemara's incident just mentioned. Rama's novelty here is that the other heirs do not stand in her place. The Mechaber's novelty below concerns whether her husband inherits the right. It is somewhat difficult that Rama made this an independent gloss rather than adding his point briefly to the Mechaber's seif 9, as he ordinarily does. The Tur combines the rulings.
Her descendants stand in her place. The same applies to descendants of descendants indefinitely. When one says "to my heirs," his intent is directed to the nearest legal heir then eligible to inherit him. If at that time he has a son or daughter, his intent is upon that child and all of that child's descendants, because they precede even the donor's father, certainly the donor's brothers and nephews, in the order of inheritance. Since we rule like the Rif, Rambam, Rashbam, and Tur that "after you" is not equivalent to saying "from now," if the daughter dies leaving no descendants, the donor's other relatives are treated like unrelated later persons because the donor's intention was not directed to them. The case is then like one who says, "My property to so and so, and after him to so and so," where the second person dies during the first recipient's lifetime. The property remains with the first recipient's heirs. If, when the gift was made, the donor had no son or daughter but did have a brother, then the brother and his descendants occupy the same position that the daughter and her descendants occupied in the earlier example. The general principle is that when a person says "after him to my heirs," his intent is directed to the person who was then first in line to inherit and to that person's descendants.

And after you, to your heirs, etc., the heirs of the heirs receive nothing. The Beit Yosef likewise writes this according to the authorities who hold that "after you" is not the same as "from now." They are forced to explain the version of the incident that reads, "and after you to your heirs." Why was it necessary to say "and after you to your heirs"? Who else would inherit from the grandmother? The Beit Yosef answers that the words come to exclude her heirs if the immediate heir dies during the grandmother's lifetime. Since the donor said, "and after you to your heirs," he stipulated that the grandmother's direct heir, namely her daughter, should acquire not through the grandmother but directly through the donor. Once the daughter dies before the grandmother, she never acquired, and therefore the daughter's son likewise does not acquire. The donor effectively made the daughter like an unrelated second beneficiary, and when an unrelated second beneficiary dies during the first recipient's lifetime, neither he nor his heirs acquire. The property therefore returns to the grandmother's other heirs. In the Derishah I wrote that this is contrary to the Gemara and cannot be reconciled with either textual version. It is also unlike the case of an unrelated later beneficiary. When a person says "after you to your heirs," his intention is not limited to the daughter then alive, but extends to all of her descendants, as explained above, as though the donor had expressly said from the outset that the later right shall belong to that daughter or to her descendants. Although the Beit Yosef appears to attribute his view to the Ran, I did not find it stated there. On the contrary, the Ran expressly indicates that specifically the father's other heirs do not inherit, implying that the daughter's own descendants do. The Beit Yosef himself cites this. I likewise proved the point from Nimukei Yosef in the Derishah. The Beit Yosef's question, why the donor needed to say "and after you to her heirs," has another answer cited by Nimukei Yosef and the Ran and brought by the Beit Yosef. Had he not said that, the grandmother could initially have sold or given the property through an ordinary gift. Even a dangerously ill person's gift or a transfer to a creditor might have been effective. By saying "and after you to your heirs," her power to dispose of the property is restricted in accordance with the donor's intention. The Ir Shushan follows Rama and the Beit Yosef, but their words remain difficult and require study.
It is merely for embellishment of the document. The source concludes that this is like other standard embellishments written into documents, such as giving the recipient authority to sell, mortgage, and do whatever he wishes with the property. The practical meaning is that the recipient may sell or give it away initially and may use it to satisfy a creditor just like his other property.
Reuven gave a gift, etc. The Beit Yosef in siman 241 cites a responsum of Rashba concerning Reuven, who lent one hundred to his sons Shimon, Levi, and Yehudah, taking their houses as collateral. Reuven later died. Before the estate was divided, Shimon gave Levi a deed of gift covering everything Shimon inherited from his father. Levi then sought to collect from Shimon the very debt that Shimon had owed Reuven. Rashba ruled for Shimon, because at the instant of the father's death Shimon inherited his own debt to the father and that obligation was extinguished before the later gift to Levi. Darkei Moshe cites the responsum and discusses it at length.
He acquired the property by virtue of Shimon. Although at the time Shimon gave Levi all the rights he had in the property the original gift had not yet physically come into Shimon's hands, Reuven had written the gift to Shimon in language effective from now if Levi would violate the condition. Once Levi did violate it, the right is treated as having belonged to Shimon from the earlier time. Therefore Shimon's prior transfer to Levi can operate. The Tur cites this from the Rosh.
