There is no distinction whether he said "from today." See Sema, seif katan 22, who says that even the authorities who disagree in some respects concede that when "from today" was said, the first recipient has no power to sell. Nimukei Yosef and Baal HaMaor do write explicitly that even with "from now" the first can sell, because the meaning is treated as, "from now, whatever you leave after yourself shall belong to the later recipient." Sema appears to understand that this applies only according to the interpretation of Bava Batra 125 that "the reasoning of the Westerners is sensible because if the grandmother had sold, her sale would stand," even assuming "after you" operates from now. On that interpretation, "from now" does not prevent the first sale. But our halachic interpretation is the reverse. We say that the proof from the grandmother's power to sell demonstrates that ordinary "after you" is not equivalent to "from now." That implies that where "from now" was explicitly said, the first cannot sell. At the same time, inheritance without interruption can still apply even when "from now" is used. Bava Batra 133 shows that Rav Huna holds that if the first recipient is an heir, he takes as inheritance, with the resulting rule that inheritance has no interruption. Yet Rav Huna in Bava Batra 125 holds that "after you" is equivalent to "from now." Rashba proves from this that inheritance without interruption can coexist with "from now." There is a sound conceptual distinction. For inheritance without interruption, once the donor has granted the heir a present body-right for the fruits during his lifetime, as explained in siman 209, the inheritance aspect has attached to the body. A mere future interest of the later recipient cannot cut off that inheritance, because the heir's inherited right has no interruption. But for the first recipient's power to sell, the matter is different. If the second recipient already acquired the body from now, with only the fruits delayed until the first recipient's death, the first recipient cannot sell away the body already acquired by the second. This resembles a father who says, "The property shall belong from now to my son after my death." The father cannot later sell away the son's body-right. It follows that if a shechiv mera says, "After you, from today, to so-and-so," the later recipient's right can still be characterized as a shechiv mera gift despite the words "from today." Siman 203, seif 9, says generally that where a shechiv mera says "from today," the gift has the law of a healthy-person gift. Why should this case differ? The reason that "from today" ordinarily shows an intention for a healthy-person gift is that otherwise the phrase is unnecessary. A shechiv mera gift would take effect after death without it. We therefore interpret the extra words as showing that the donor intends a present transfer. Here, however, the donor has an additional reason to say "from today": he wants to prevent the first recipient from selling. Therefore the words do not necessarily show that he intended a full healthy-person gift. He may still intend to delay the later recipient's practical enjoyment until the latest possible moment of his life, while using "from today" only to establish the body-right against alienation.
"After you to my heirs," and he had a daughter. See Sema, seif katan 25 and 26. His words imply a reading "after you to your heirs," because he is troubled by Rama's earlier ruling that when one says "after you to my heirs," even if the first sells, the heirs can remove the buyer, based on the principle that "after you to my heirs" is like "from now." If so, the daughter's right should be presently vested and therefore not considered merely expectant property. Shach, seif katan 8, reads "after you to my heirs" and rules that even the husband inherits, contrary to Sema. This is very difficult, because Rama explicitly rules that even other heirs do not always stand in her place, and all the more so the husband should not. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 90, seif 2, answers that even if the first recipient cannot sell, the property can still be considered merely expected because it never came into the daughter's possession. This is also difficult. If "to my heirs" is equivalent to "from now," Ramban explicitly says such property is presently held, not merely expected. Ketzot HaChoshen raises this. The matter can be resolved as follows. Ramban in Bava Batra 137 explains that when one says, "My property to you for your lifetime," the first recipient's gift itself is limited to life. The donor retains the residual body-right for himself and his heirs. The same applies when he says, "My property to you, and after you to my heirs." He did not create a new gift to the heirs. Rather, he retained the remainder of the estate beyond the first recipient's lifetime. Therefore the first cannot sell the residual right. This differs from saying, "My property to you, and after you to so-and-so." There the donor divests himself fully. The later recipient's right does not yet vest under ordinary "after you," so the first recipient can sell. Now distinguish a shechiv mera from a healthy person. A shechiv mera giving because of impending death does not ordinarily contemplate living and having new children later. When he says, "after you to my heirs," it is natural to interpret him as reserving the remainder to whoever are his heirs at the time of his death. His use of "my heirs" identifies the residual inheritance and not a specific class existing at the moment of speech. That is why even later-born heirs can be included. Moreover, the main proof that "my heirs" might mean the current heirs is that otherwise he could have said, "and after you to me." But a shechiv mera giving in contemplation of death cannot sensibly say "after you to me," because he expects not to be alive then. Therefore his words necessarily refer to heirs at death. Thus, after the first recipient dies, the residual property falls to the donor's heirs as ordinary inheritance. For that reason even a sale by the first recipient can be removed, as Ramban explains. A healthy person is different. If he says, "My property to you and after you to my heirs," and at the time he has only a daughter, while Rama rules that even if sons are later born the daughter takes, then the language cannot be interpreted as a simple reservation to whoever will be his heirs at death. If it were merely a residual inheritance, the later-born sons would inherit instead. We must therefore say that in the healthy-person case he intended an actual gift to the daughter whom he then called "my heir." Such a gift requires a normal kinyan, such as kinyan sudar. Once we understand it as a gift to that specific current heir rather than a residual inheritance, it has the same law as ordinary "after you to so-and-so." The first recipient's sale can be effective, and the daughter's interest is merely expectant for purposes of the husband's inheritance. If she dies during the first recipient's lifetime, the later right goes to the first recipient's heirs, except that her descendants can be included in the "after you" designation because they were within the intended class present at the original statement in the manner Sema explains. Accordingly, Rama's earlier rule, that "after you to me or my heirs" prevents the first recipient's sale, concerns a shechiv mera context. The later case, "after you to my heirs," where he had a daughter and later sons were born, necessarily concerns a healthy person and is a true gift to the then-existing daughter. There is another possible explanation based on Machaneh Efrayim's citation from Kesef Mishneh. Even authorities who do not generally treat "after you to my heirs" as "from now" can agree that the phrase "after you to me or my heirs" is different. Because the donor expressly includes himself and his heirs in one statement, just as he retains the body-right for himself, he retains it for those heirs named in the same reservation. On that approach both clauses of Rama can concern a healthy person. In the first, "after you to me or my heirs" creates a retained residual right that defeats sale. In the second, "after you to my heirs" alone is a gift to the current heir and does not have that effect. This also explains why Rama states the first rule broadly without entering the dispute among Tur and other poskim whether "after you to my heirs" by itself equals "from now." The combined phrase "to me or my heirs" is stronger according to all. The practical conclusion is that "after you to my heirs" is treated as a present residual inheritance only in a shechiv mera setting, or where he said "after you to me or my heirs." In a healthy-person gift saying only "after you to my heirs," the then-existing heirs are treated as gift recipients, and the rules of ordinary "after you" apply.
Her descendants stand in her place. This appears to mean specifically descendants already in existence at the time of the statement. A child born afterward does not acquire, because one cannot transfer property to a person not yet in existence. Rosh, responsum kelal 87, writes explicitly that "after you to so-and-so" applies only to one who was already in the world.
"And after you to your heirs," some say it is as though he said... See Sema's question. In my view descendants are included here as well, as stated in seif 9. Rama's point is only to exclude the idea that the words "after you to your heirs" are mere boilerplate. He teaches that they have substantive effect. Sema, seif katan 27, explains that this resolves Beit Yosef's question why more remote heirs should not inherit, since they are heirs of the grandmother. In my view the primary interpretation is that the phrase refers to heirs at the time of death. Darkei Moshe also questions this, but the cases can be distinguished. When a person says, "after you to my heirs," Rama understands the phrase in a healthy-person context as referring to the heirs he has now. The reason is that if he intended whoever will be his heirs at death, he could have said, "after you to me," and left the ordinary inheritance rules to operate afterward. But when he says, "after you to your heirs," why should we assume he means the recipient's current heirs rather than those who will legally be heirs when the time comes? There is a proof. The poskim labor to identify practical consequences of the words "after you to your heirs," such as whether the first recipient may sell initially and the law of a shechiv mera gift. If the phrase referred only to the heirs existing at the time of the gift, the obvious difference would be that later-born children are excluded. The fact that they do not give this as the principal novelty implies that the ordinary meaning of "heirs" is heirs at death. Accordingly, not only direct descendants but all persons who are legally heirs at that later time can be included, subject to the particular law of the case.
