He said, “My property,” etc., but a Sefer Torah, etc. See Baer Heitev. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 43, writes that where a gift expressly includes “all my books,” he sees no basis to doubt whether a Sefer Torah is included. On the contrary, our printed books are called sefarim only by extension from a Sefer Torah. The fact that one may not ordinarily sell a Sefer Torah does not mean that a donor who says “all my books” excludes it. The Gemara's doubt concerning whether a Sefer Torah is included in “my property” is a question whether an object that may not be sold is itself called property. Here, however, the language is “all my books,” and a Sefer Torah is certainly a book. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 143, considers a similar case where a testator distributed his estate, gave all his books to two sons, and left a Sefer Torah worth several hundred gold coins. He cites Noda BiYehudah and analyzes the question. According to authorities who explain the Talmudic doubt as arising from the extraordinary importance of a Sefer Torah, comparable to gold and precious stones, a Sefer Torah may perhaps not be included in an unqualified expression of “books” unless expressly stated. Since the heirs are in possession, one cannot extract it from them. This remains so even though there are two doubts in favor of the sons who received the books, for money is not extracted by a double doubt. Bach also writes that the Talmudic issue reflected earlier generations who studied directly from a Sefer Torah. This strengthens the distinction where the particular Sefer Torah is especially valuable. If the two sons are greater Torah scholars than the other heirs, that too shows that the donor's purpose in giving them “books” was to provide works actually used for study, which does not necessarily distinguish this Sefer Torah. Clear proof is therefore required to remove it from the heirs' possession. Chatam Sofer, part VI, siman 53, discusses a testament giving all the testator's books to his son, except that any books of which the son already had copies were to go to the son-in-law. The son already owned many books, some purchased and some previously given by his father. The son argued that his father meant only duplicates of books that the father himself had previously given him; the son-in-law argued that every duplicate of any book the son happened to own was included, which would give most of the library to the son-in-law. Chatam Sofer rules for the son. First, there is a strong presumption that the father did not intend to divert so much inheritance from his son, especially with books, concerning which earlier custom specifically sought to preserve the owner's name and prevent their passing to another family. Second, the language of the testament itself favors the son. Thus, not merely because the holder of a document bears the burden of an ambiguous text, but from the donor's presumed intent and the wording itself, the son-in-law receives only those books found in the estate of which the father had already given the son another copy during his lifetime.
Everything is included in “property.” Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 68, discusses a dying man who said, “All my property to my minor sons from my second wife.” His estate included an oral loan secured by pledged synagogue seats. The question was whether the loan was included in “all my property.” Mishkenot Yaakov concludes that it was not. In Bava Batra 150b the Gemara separately asks whether money is called property and whether a document is called property. If “money” included an oral debt, there would be no need to ask separately whether a document is property, since every debt instrument represents a debt. Rather, an oral loan itself is not called “property”; the statement that money is property refers to cash in hand. This also appears from Bava Batra 152a, where the Gemara speaks of a deathbed gift that includes a kinyan and uses the explicit expression “my loan to so-and-so,” implying that “all my property” alone would not include the loan. There is some room to argue that a secured loan differs because the pledged property is physically held by the creditor, but even that is not sufficiently clear to remove the debt from the heirs. At minimum the issue is disputed, and it is especially difficult to extract property from heirs on the strength of a rabbinic deathbed gift. See there.
But concerning a Sefer Torah there is a doubt. Magen Avraham, siman 153 seif katan 22, writes that this refers to a Sefer Torah that was given to the synagogue for public reading, in which case the owner may not sell it. See there, and see Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 43, together with the gloss by the author's son there.
Anything that is called clothing. Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 171, discusses one who directed before death that all his garments and clothing be given to a relative. Among the estate were several cubits of camel-wool fabric purchased to make a new garment. He cites Tzemach Tzedek, siman 3, concerning a ketubah stipulation that a wife should take all her clothing, where ten cubits of silk purchased for her were found in the estate. Tzemach Tzedek ruled that she acquired them, reasoning from siman 97 seif 25 that consecrated property and creditors cannot collect from items a husband purchased for his wife, and therefore she should likewise take such material after his death as part of her clothing. Shevut Yaakov disagrees. There, because the husband purchased the item specifically for her, he immediately vested a right in her even before it was made into a garment, and therefore a creditor cannot displace her. But after the husband's death the Gemara teaches that he did not give her garments with the intention that she take them and leave him. Her right then depends solely on the ketubah language “all her clothing,” which follows ordinary speech. Unsewn fabric is not called her clothing. Likewise in the testamentary case, camel-wool fabric not yet made into a wearable garment is plainly not included in “my garments and clothing.” Shevut Yaakov adds that even if the garment had already been sewn but the deceased never wore it once, it is not entirely clear that it can be extracted from the heirs as “his clothing.” Ateret Tzvi cites this briefly but writes that in his own responsum, siman 23, he proved that once the garment has been made, even if never worn, the recipient is entitled to it. Tzemach Tzedek, siman 11, further rules that one obligated by document to give another “good garments” may give medium-quality garments, because those too are called “good.” He adduces proofs from Bava Batra and Rosh Hashanah. Only if the instrument says “choice good garments” or “truly good garments” must he give superior rather than average garments.
