Their sons have chazakah, etc. This applies even to the son of a robber while his robber father is still alive. We do not say that because of fear of the father the owner is afraid to protest against the son. Darkei Moshe writes this explicitly. See also the Perishah at the end of siman 134 for the reason underlying Rama's gloss there, as I explained in the Sema.
And he claims that the owners sold it to him, etc. The Tur writes that even if the son admits the property originally came into his father's hands as an artisan's property, as sharecropping property, or through robbery, but claims that his father later purchased it, the son can establish chazakah.
But if he claims that he inherited it from his father, etc. Even if the father ate the produce and afterward the son also did so for the years of chazakah, it does not help, because this is a chazakah unaccompanied by a valid claim.
If he says he inherited it from his father, he has chazakah. We advance for an heir every claim his father could have made. Had his father been alive, he would have been believed to say that he purchased it, provided the father was not a robber and had possessed it for three years, or had lived there one day and the son who stands in his place then lived there for three years, as explained above in siman 146. This is also the intent of the Mechaber later in this seif: 'A grandson of the robber, even if he comes with his father's claim, has chazakah, but if, etc.' Rama's statement here is therefore seemingly redundant, since the Mechaber himself writes it immediately afterward. Rama likely wrote it here incidentally because the Tur also does so, while the Mechaber followed Rambam's order in chapter 14 of Hilchot To'en, first discussing the robber's son and afterward the robber's grandson. One can also say that Rama teaches here that the same applies to the son of an artisan and similar cases.
Gloss: Some say that even if he did not bring witnesses, etc. Meaning, the sons have no witnesses to the admission, but they themselves claim, 'He admitted before us that the property was purchased.' They are believed through a migo. This excludes the Mechaber's preceding requirement of witnesses, which follows Rashi. Rama cites the Tur's wording, following Tosafos.
My father counted out the money to you in my presence, he is believed through a migo. It appears this applies specifically according to the Rosh's view cited by Rama in the gloss to the preceding siman, that even a robber's purchase is valid if witnesses saw the payment and the seller did not deliver a protest. According to Rambam, who holds that when dealing with a robber no protest is required, even if the son saw money counted out there is no chazakah; at most the money must be returned. See the Perishah.
And the grandson of the robber, even if he comes, etc. The correct text is 'the grandson,' not 'and likewise.' The meaning of 'even if he comes' is: not only if he asserts his own claim that the claimant sold or gave it to him does he acquire it; even if he relies on his father's claim he also acquires it.
You admitted in my presence that you sold it to my grandfather. Meaning, after the property was already in my possession, you admitted before me that you had sold it to my grandfather. Such an admission does not help the robber's son, even if the claimant admitted before the son outside the robber father's presence, because he may have feared the father would learn of it. The Rosh writes this explicitly. See the Perishah.
