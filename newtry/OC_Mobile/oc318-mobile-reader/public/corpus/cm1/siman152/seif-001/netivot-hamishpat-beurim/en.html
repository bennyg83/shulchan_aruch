Their children can establish chazakah. See Sema, seif katan 1, who writes that although a person may fear protesting against a robber, we do not say that he is afraid to protest against the robber's son. This does not contradict the rule that an admission made before the robber's son is ineffective because we say that he admitted out of fear of the father. There, he fears that the son will report the admission to his father, as Sema explains. A protest, however, is made outside the son's presence, and he does not have the same fear.
He counted money before you. See Sema, seif katan 6, who writes that according to Rambam there is no chazakah here, but the buyer returns the property and receives back the money. At first glance this is difficult. The case concerns one in which the dispossessed owner denies that money was counted before him. Otherwise Mechaber would not need to say that the son is believed by virtue of a migo. Since a sale under duress has only the status of an oral debt concerning return of the money, as explained in simanim 70 and 106, the dispossessed owner should be believed to say, "I repaid," and thus should have a migo against the son's claim. There is an additional difficulty that the son's migo appears to be a migo used to extract property, as Ketzot HaChoshen asks. We must say that this case is different. Even though Rambam voids the sale, the land remains in the buyer's possession as collateral until the money is repaid, as explained in siman 194, Sema seif katan 10. A claim concerning collateral is considered a migo to retain property, as explained above in siman 106, seif 6. The son's migo is therefore stronger. His migo is one of retention, while the dispossessed owner's migo would be one used to extract, similar to the principle Tumim records in his rules of migo in the name of Knesset HaGedolah.
