Their children. Even where the robber's son possesses the property while his father is still alive, we do not say that fear of the robber causes the owner to refrain from protesting against the son. Darkei Moshe writes likewise. See the end of siman 134. Sema. Maharshal, Responsa 33, questions this. Knesset HaGedolah.
Sold it. The Tur writes that even if the possessor admits that the property initially came into his father's hands as a craftsman, sharecropper, or robber, but claims that his father purchased it afterward, he may establish chazakah. Sema.
That he consumed it. Even if the son subsequently consumes its produce for the full years of chazakah, this does not help because it is a chazakah without an accompanying claim. Sema.
From his father. We advance for an heir every claim that his father could have made. Had the father been alive, he would have been believed to say that he purchased the property, since he was not a robber and had held it for three years, or had occupied it for one day and his son, who stands in his place, then occupied it for three years, as explained in siman 146. The Mechaber likewise intends this in writing that the grandson of the robber may establish the right even when he comes through his ancestor's claim. The Rama writes similarly here. Alternatively, the Rama may be teaching that the same applies to a craftsman and similar persons. Sema.
Except. See Responsa Rama 12, page 36b. Shach.
Through a migo. This appears to follow specifically the Rosh's view, cited by the Rama at the end of siman 151, that even with a robber, if witnesses saw the payment of money and the seller did not issue a moda'ah, the sale is valid. According to the Rambam, who holds that in the case of a robber the seller need not issue a moda'ah, there is no chazakah here and the possessor merely returns the money. Sema.
And so. The Sema writes that the text should read "and a son," not "and so." The point is that not only when the robber's grandson comes with his own claim that the challenger sold or gave it to him does he acquire it; even if he comes with his father's claim, he can acquire it. See Rivash 252. The same applies to the robber's son-in-law who inherits from him. Mishpat Tzedek I, 69. For the rule concerning the son of a partner, see Responsa Ma'aseh Chiya 2. Knesset HaGedolah.
Before me. Meaning that after the property came into my hands, you admitted before me that you had sold it to my grandfather. Such an admission is ineffective for the robber's son even if it was made before the son and not in the robber father's presence, because the owner may have feared that the admission would become known to the robber. The Rosh states this explicitly. Sema.
