But if the grandson claims that he inherited it from his father, he has chazakah. Sema in seif katan 4 asks that the Mechaber’s own ruling concerning a robber is already the same, namely that the grandson of a robber has chazakah when he comes with a claim through his father, and all the more so should this be true in the other cases. It appears that Rama teaches that this is true even according to the Rosh. The baraita states that a grandson has chazakah, and the Rashbam explains that this means he comes with a claim through his father, because we make claims on behalf of an heir. The Rosh, however, explains that the grandson still comes with a claim through his grandfather, but says that the original owner admitted before him that the property belonged to the grandfather, and there is no fear that the owner made such an admission to the grandson because he feared the robber. According to the Rosh, then, the baraita provides no proof for the present rule where the grandson claims through his father. We might have said that he is presumed to have inherited from his grandfather through his father, because a son stands in his father’s place, and therefore the taint of the grandfather’s lack of chazakah continues. Since the Mechaber did not adopt the Rosh’s interpretation, the present rule is obvious according to him, because the baraita itself states it. But according to the Rosh, who interprets the baraita differently, there is no textual proof for this rule. Rama therefore records that the rule is nevertheless true and sound by logical reasoning. Alternatively, the Mechaber’s words concerning the robber’s grandson may themselves refer to the baraita’s framework. The baraita says that the robber’s son has no chazakah, meaning even where the original owner admitted that the property belonged to the robber, and then says that the robber’s grandson does have chazakah, likewise meaning where the original owner admitted before witnesses that it belonged to the grandson’s father. We do not say that the owner’s fear of the robber caused him to make an admission to the robber’s son. The Mechaber’s words can be understood as referring to such an admission. But where there was no admission at all and the issue is simply whether we make a claim for the heir, one might have thought that we do not, because ordinarily his father inherited from his own father, whose possession lacked chazakah. Rama therefore teaches that we do make the claim. There is no difficulty.
And if he brings witnesses that the owners admitted, etc. These are Rambam’s words, because this was his reading of the Gemara. Tosafot, and likewise Maggid Mishneh, already ask that if the owner admitted before witnesses, why is this rule stated specifically regarding the son of a craftsman or sharecropper? Even the craftsman or sharecropper himself should prevail through such an admission. They give no answer. It appears that they were troubled by the Nimukei Yosef’s question that the owner should be able to say, “I was merely jesting,” or, “I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy.” Nimukei Yosef answers that because the property is in the other person’s possession those defenses do not apply. Perhaps Rambam holds that this does not apply to land, since land remains in the legal possession of its owner, and the person making the admission is therefore still regarded as the possessor. The Gemara accordingly establishes the case as one where the owner admitted before witnesses in the presence of the son of the sharecropper or craftsman. The defense of jesting does not apply, because the admission was not made to the actual litigant. The son did not himself claim, “I purchased it from you,” such that we could say that just as he was jesting with the owner, the owner was jesting with him. The son does not know whether his father’s claim was true. Nor does the defense that the admission was made only to avoid appearing wealthy apply, because there was at least some existing claim by the litigant. The admission is therefore effective. According to this, the case may be one where the son did not possess the property for three years. This distinction is specifically needed with the son of a craftsman. With the craftsman himself, the owner could claim that the admission was made in jest, since the craftsman had not held the property for three years and the land remained in the claimant’s legal possession. But after three years of possession, the owner plainly cannot claim that the admission was made in jest, because we do not advance such a defense for one seeking to take property from another, as Nimukei Yosef writes. This also explains why the Mechaber, following Rambam and Rashba, need not disagree with Rama’s ruling that the son may claim, “You admitted before me,” by a migo that he could have claimed, “I purchased it from you.” Such use of migo is common throughout the laws of chazakah. Rambam’s Gemara reading establishes the case with witnesses in order to teach a greater novelty, namely that even where the son has no chazakah at all and the property therefore remains in the first owner’s legal possession, the defenses of jesting and avoiding an appearance of wealth do not apply to such an admission. That is not a novelty where he has already possessed the property for three years. A migo, by contrast, can operate only where there is a chazakah. Therefore the Gemara states that there are witnesses.
