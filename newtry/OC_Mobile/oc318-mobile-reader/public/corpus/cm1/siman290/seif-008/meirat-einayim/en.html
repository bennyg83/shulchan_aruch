For he will exert himself over them more than beit din. Meaning that the guardian will make the effort to find a trustworthy person fit to receive the money for investment. The guardian should nevertheless deliver the money to that person through beit din, because beit din has authority to condition the arrangement so that it is close to profit and remote from loss for the orphans. This is the language of Rashi and the Tur.

That has no identifying sign. The Rambam concludes: Why should they not take a pledge consisting of gold utensils? Perhaps it belongs to others, and after the borrower's death someone will identify it by a sign and take it, if the judge knows that the borrower was not wealthy enough to own such items. The source is the Gemara. During the recipient's lifetime the owner cannot recover the object from the orphans merely because the recipient was not wealthy, because the recipient could make a definite claim that he purchased it, and perhaps he found it or became wealthy secretly. After his death, however, beit din does not advance for orphans an unusual claim of that kind. Tosafot hold even more broadly that if their father dies and someone brings witnesses that he deposited utensils with him, they are removed from the orphans even if the father had been wealthy. We do not argue for them that perhaps their father later purchased the utensils, although the father himself could have claimed ownership with a migo that he had returned them. See siman 297.
They fix his compensation at one third or one half. All these fractions refer to the recipient's share of the profit. They may agree that he receive one third of the profit and the orphans two thirds, or that each receive one half, or even that the orphans receive only one fourth and the recipient three fourths. Read in this way the listed fractions are arranged progressively. See the Perishah.
So too he may receive the money himself. Meaning he may conduct business with it under an arrangement for one third or one half of the profit, as explained above. Although above at the beginning of siman 72 the Tur and Mechaber rule that one who holds a hoe or axe as a pledge may rent it to others for the benefit of the owner when the rental income is substantial and the depreciation small, but may not rent it to himself because of suspicion, and a similar rule appears below regarding a custodian, the case of orphans is different. Beit din is their legal protector. When the guardian informs beit din and receives the money through them, it is as though the owners themselves placed the funds in his hands as an investment. That mechanism is unavailable regarding ordinary adult owners, whose private affairs beit din does not administer in this manner.
