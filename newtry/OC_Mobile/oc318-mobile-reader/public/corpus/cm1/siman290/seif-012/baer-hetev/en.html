Is believed. The Mordechai writes: If the guardian lent money to another, and the borrower says, "I repaid," while the guardian says that he did not repay, then if the borrower admits that the money belongs to the orphans, the guardian is believed without an oath. But if the borrower does not believe that it belongs to the orphans, he must swear. Although one does not swear on a claim of uncertainty, he nevertheless swears that he did not repay. It seems clear to me that this refers to a case where the guardian held collateral or some other property of the borrower. This also appears from the Mordechai's language, where he compares the guardian to a third-party depositary. See there carefully. Accordingly, where he has no collateral, the borrower must take a Torah oath to contradict him, as stated in siman 123 regarding an authorized agent; see the Beit Yosef there. See also Sefer Imrei Emet, seif 37, and above, siman 108, seif 6. Shach.
Known. This is the wording of the Mordechai: if the borrower admits, etc. Meaning, he admits that the guardian lent him the orphans' money. It follows that the borrower himself made the guardian a third-party depositary. Otherwise, even if it is known that the money belonged to the orphans, who says that the borrower trusted the guardian? As stated in siman 56 in the name of Maharam, where he does not admit that he made him a third-party depositary, the latter is not believed. Accordingly, the Rama's wording, "if it is known that the money belongs to the orphans," is difficult. Perhaps his intent is as I have written. This requires study. See Sefer Imrei Emet, p. 77, col. 3. Ibid.
A claim. This is one of the reasons we appoint a guardian. In this respect the orphans' position is stronger, because the guardian commonly raises every possible claim based on uncertainty that their father, were he alive, might have raised, provided he does not deliberately make a claim he knows with certainty is false.
