The guardian is believed. The Mordechai writes: If the guardian lent money to another person, and the borrower says, 'I repaid,' while the guardian says that he did not repay, then if the borrower admits that the money belonged to the orphans, the guardian is believed without an oath. It appears obvious to me that this concerns a case where the guardian held collateral or some other property of the borrower. This also appears from the Mordechai's wording, for he compares the guardian to a third-party stakeholder. See there and examine carefully. Accordingly, where the guardian holds no collateral, the borrower must take a Torah oath to contradict him, as in the case of an authorized agent in siman 123. See the Beit Yosef there. This is clear. See also Imrei Binah, siman 37, on this matter, and see below, siman 108, seif 6.
If it is known, etc. The Mordechai's wording is: 'If the borrower admits that the guardian lent him the orphans' money, etc.' It appears to me that this means that the guardian himself lent him the orphans' money, so that the borrower himself appointed the guardian as a third-party stakeholder. Otherwise, even if it is known that the money belonged to the orphans, who says that the borrower trusted the guardian? This accords with what I wrote above, siman 56, in the name of Maharam, that where the party does not admit that he appointed him as a third-party stakeholder, the latter is not believed. Accordingly, the Rama's wording, 'if it is known that the money belonged to the orphans,' is imprecise. Perhaps his intent is as I have explained, but the matter requires study. See Imrei Binah, folio 77c.
