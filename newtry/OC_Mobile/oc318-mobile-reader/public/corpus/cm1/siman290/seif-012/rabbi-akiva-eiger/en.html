[Shulchan Aruch] It is not negligence. Note: From the words of Nimukei Yosef one may learn that this applies specifically where the seller was initially present and, had the cattle keeper informed the guardian, it would have been possible to seek recourse against the seller. In such a case the guardian was not negligent, because he can say, "I relied on the cattle keeper to inform me, and I would then have sought recourse against the seller." But where recourse against the seller is impossible, failing to inspect the animal immediately constitutes negligence. The view of many Rishonim cited in Shitah Mekubetzet appears to be that the guardian is never considered negligent, because he need not suspect such a defect. Rather, the paid custodian is liable for it, and the orphans collect from the cattle keeper, who is a paid custodian, the reduced value of the meat, since in any event they would have had to slaughter the animal. An exception is where it had been possible to return the animal to the seller, but the seller subsequently went overseas. In that case the cattle keeper must pay the orphans the full amount they paid. If the orphans later locate the owner of the ox and recover their money from him, the owner of the ox cannot recover from the cattle keeper, since presumably he knew the condition of his own ox. If, however, he was merely a broker, he may recover from the cattle keeper after first swearing that he did not know of the defect. This is what emerges from our sugya. Examine it carefully.
