And a guardian is believed. It appears that this applies even where, had the guardian not said that the debt remains due on the pledge, the orphans themselves could not have brought a claim against him. They do not know at all how much money he expended on them or how much he earned with their funds. Since he could have said that he had already paid out the amount for the orphans and thus discharged himself, he is likewise believed when he says that the borrower did not repay. He is not considered interested testimony. This resembles Kiddushin 43, where an agent can become a witness and is believed to say, "We repaid the lender," through a migo that he could have said, "We returned the money to the borrower." But without such a migo the guardian would be directly interested. One could say that he wishes to retain for himself money that the borrower actually repaid, while exempting himself toward the orphans by presenting an accounting in which that amount appears to remain due from the borrower. The case must also be one where, even had the guardian not said that the borrower failed to repay, the orphans would have had no evidentiary basis to suspect him of two silver pieces and thereby require the guardian's oath. As I wrote in seif katan 1, a guardian's oath requires some basis for suspicion. But if without his statement the orphans would have had grounds to suspect him and make him swear, while by saying that the borrower did not repay he removes that basis for suspicion and thereby exempts himself from the oath, he is again an interested party. He is saying the borrower did not pay in order to exempt himself from swearing against the orphans. See Shach, seif katan 16, who writes that the guardian must have informed the borrower at the time of the loan that the money belonged to the orphans. Only then does the relevant right attach in the manner of an interest in property not yet in existence. The reason appears to be that even if the guardian is not interested, how is he stronger than an ordinary witness, who likewise is not interested but is not believed by himself to extract money? True, a pledge is in hand. But the claim on the pledge itself is believed only through a migo that it was purchased or that it was returned. The orphans cannot assert that migo because they do not have a definite claim. The guardian himself likewise has no migo of "I purchased it" or "I returned it," because had he made such a claim he would have had to swear. Since the pledge is not physically in the hands of the lenders, namely the orphans, the case resembles siman 72, seif 18, in the gloss, where one is not believed when the object is not in his possession. Therefore it is necessary that the borrower was informed that the loan was orphan money. Then the guardian is believed through the law of a shalish, a third party holding property for others, who is believed when he is not interested even if the recipient does not know the facts, provided the recipient does not deny the third-party arrangement, as explained in siman 56, Shach seif katan 5. Agudat Ezov, cited by Shach, writes that this applies only to a guardian who does not actually hold the orphans' money but was appointed merely to litigate for them; such a guardian can testify. But the language of Mordechai, which states the case with a pledge and a claim that repayment was made to the guardian, proves that even where the money is actually in the guardian's hands he can be believed. This supports the explanation I have given. Agudat Ezov brings proof from Mordechai in the chapter Zeh Borer. There, however, the guardian testifies about a matter where, if the property had been in his hands and the facts were not as he says, he himself would have been liable to pay because of negligence, as is evident from the text there. See also Shach, seif katan 22, who says that we do not apply a migo that the debtor could have said, "I returned it to the guardian." Maharam gives several reasons, one being that beit din appoints only a person known to be fit, so this is a weak migo. According to that reason, where the orphans' father appointed the guardian himself, the rationale does not apply. The practical halachah therefore still requires study in light of Maharit there.
