He is exempt from theft and loss, etc. I am astonished at the Mechaber's ruling that the guardian is exempt from theft and loss. Although the Rosh and Tur agree with this, they follow their own view above, simanim 72 and 267, where they do not rule in accordance with Rav Yosef. The Mechaber, however, rules there in accordance with Rav Yosef. Here, therefore, the guardian performs a mitzvah and is deemed a paid custodian. Although the Rashba and Rav Hai agree with the Rosh that every guardian is liable for negligence, their point is merely to reject the Ramban's view that one appointed by the orphans' father is exempt even for negligence. Nevertheless, according to them, since they rule in accordance with Rav Yosef, the guardian is certainly a paid custodian and is liable for theft and loss. The Rashba writes this explicitly in his novellae to Perek HaNizakin: A guardian is like a paid custodian because he performs a mitzvah and benefits from Rav Yosef's perutah, for we rule in accordance with Rav Yosef. If one asks that, if he is a paid custodian because of Rav Yosef's perutah, why Rabbi Abbahu said to Rav Tachlifa, 'Bring a kav and measure it for him,' the answer is that if the liability arose only from Rav Yosef's perutah, even one appointed by the orphans' father would be liable. Rav Hai Gaon likewise writes in Sefer HaMekach that a guardian who acted negligently is liable for that negligence, and he proves it from Perek HaMafkid. There, the guardian was exempt only because the owner of the ox was present and the duty of guarding rested upon him. Otherwise, the guardian himself would pay, for he is like a paid custodian. The Ramban writes that this ruling does not remain in the Gemara's conclusion, but that is incorrect, for the ruling was rejected for another reason, as explained there. The Ran likewise writes in Perek HaNizakin that the Ramban holds that one appointed by the orphans' father is exempt even for negligence, lest people refrain from accepting the position, whereas Rabbeinu Tam holds that every guardian who acts negligently is liable, even one appointed by the father. This is proved in Perek HaMafkid, and the reason is that he is a paid custodian because of Rav Yosef's perutah. The Maggid Mishna and Tur likewise write in the Ramban's name that one appointed by beit din is liable for theft and loss, necessarily because of Rav Yosef's perutah, as the Rashba explicitly states in his novellae there. Thus, one appointed by beit din is certainly a paid custodian according to Rav Yosef, unlike the Mechaber. Indeed, even one appointed by the orphans' father is a paid custodian according to most authorities, who equate him with one appointed by beit din regarding payment. This is explicit in the Rashba and Ran cited above. The Mechaber's words, both regarding one appointed by beit din and one appointed by the father, therefore require study. It appears to me that this is an unresolved question of law, and if the orphans have seized payment, it is not removed from them.
And he is liable for negligence, etc. Responsa Mahari of the House of Levi, at the end of siman 3, concludes in the name of Rashdam, siman 314, that money cannot be extracted against the views of the Ramban, Rabbi Chayim, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. See there. Responsa Mahari ibn Lev, part IV, folio 18, writes that one in possession cannot say, 'I rely upon the Ramban and Rabbi Chayim,' because they are isolated opinions. In my view, however, they did not examine Rabbeinu Yerucham carefully. In Netiv 26, part I, he writes that a guardian who acted negligently is liable, whether appointed by beit din or by the orphans' father, and that people do not refrain from accepting guardianship because of this, but only because of the oath, as the Yerushalmi states: a person avoids an oath, not payment. He writes that Tosafot derived this in Perek HaMafkid from the guardian who purchased an ox for orphans. They explained that there one might refrain from becoming a guardian, whereas here he would not. Rav Hai likewise writes this. The Ramban, however, writes that one appointed by beit din is liable for theft and loss, certainly for negligence, while one appointed by the orphans' father is exempt even for negligence. Rabbeinu Yerucham then states that the first view appears primary, that he is liable only for negligence and exempt from theft and loss, while one appointed by the father is exempt even for negligence. These closing words are plainly a scribal error and superfluous. Even apart from that, the middle of his statement appears corrupted. He first wrote explicitly that both one appointed by beit din and one appointed by the father are liable for negligence. Moreover, according to the apparent closing text, Rabbeinu Yerucham would disagree not only with his teacher the Rosh, but with every authority, for no decisor holds that one appointed by beit din is liable for negligence while one appointed by the father is entirely exempt in precisely that formulation. The correct reading is therefore as I have explained, and Rabbeinu Yerucham's view is actually identical to that of the Rosh and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. Nevertheless, the practical law still requires study. The Rashba rejected the Ramban's view in his novellae because he writes that the guardian case in Perek HaMafkid is a strong proof, and Rabbi Yitzchak, one of the Tosafists, is among those who impose liability, following Rav Hai. The Ran likewise implies this. In my view, however, there is no conclusive proof from Perek HaMafkid, for perhaps that guardian had been appointed by beit din. Indeed, even according to the Ramban, one may ask why he needed to say that the ruling did not remain in the conclusion. He could have answered that the guardian there was appointed by beit din. Perhaps the authorities understood that because the Gemara speaks without qualification, it implies that there is no distinction. Even so, this is not a sufficiently clear proof to reject the Ramban and Rabbi Chayim, who exempt one appointed by the orphans' father even for negligence. The Beit Yosef also writes that he found responsa of the Rashba to the contrary, stating that we have only the words of the later authorities who exempt him even from negligence. The practical law therefore requires study. See Responsa Maharan Sasson, end of siman 52; Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, siman 108; and volume II, siman 12.
