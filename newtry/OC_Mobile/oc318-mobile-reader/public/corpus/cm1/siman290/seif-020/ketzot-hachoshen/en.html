Who says that he would have sworn, and this is not called negligence. Maharam was asked about a guardian who advanced in beit din a plea different from the one the owner had instructed, allegedly causing loss. He ruled that a guardian is liable for genuine negligence, but not every lost litigation opportunity counts as monetary damage. If, under the owner's proposed plea, the owner would merely have been entitled to collect upon taking an oath, and under the guardian's plea the other party prevailed without an oath, the guardian can say, "Who says you would actually have sworn?" This resembles the law of testimony where a person who prevents another from reaching a possible oath dependent recovery has not necessarily denied actual money. Maharam extends the same reasoning where the owner says that with the correct plea he himself would have sworn and been exempt, whereas under the guardian's plea the opponent collected. The guardian can say, "Who says you would have sworn?" I find the second extension difficult. Where a single witness would have imposed an oath upon the defendant, denial of that witness's testimony can count as denial of money, even though the defendant might have sworn and escaped payment. The Gemara does not generally say, "Who says he would have sworn?" in that direction. The phrase appears chiefly where the plaintiff's own future oath is uncertain. The likely logic of Maharam is nevertheless symmetrical: before the owner actually took the oath, the guardian can argue that the owner has not shown a definite monetary loss. But where the guardian's wrong plea converted a situation in which the owner would have collected without any oath into one where the opponent can avoid payment by swearing, that is much closer to direct monetary damage. The guardian has reduced an unconditional monetary right to a merely contingent one. In such a case liability for the guardian is substantially stronger. The matter still requires study.
