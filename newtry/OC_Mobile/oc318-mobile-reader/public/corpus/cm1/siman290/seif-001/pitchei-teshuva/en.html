A guardian who lends orphans' money. See Sema, seif katan 13, in the name of Maharik, that if the money cannot be recovered the guardian was negligent even if the borrower was a distinguished and respectable man. Baer HaGolah also cites this. Netivot HaMishpat writes that Sema abbreviated Maharik excessively. The original case states that it was known that the borrower owed others more than the value of his assets. That is certainly negligence, because orphan funds may be lent only against the security of assets, as explained in seif 8. But if other debts were not known at the time of the loan, the guardian appears exempt. One cannot say that because every written debt becomes publicly known, the guardian is treated as though he knew, for Maharik himself emphasizes that the indebtedness was actually known. This implies that without such knowledge there is no negligence. It appears that this qualification applies only where the borrower was distinguished and upright, and therefore presumably obeyed Torah law and was not under communal sanction, since money of orphans may be lent to such a person even without collateral as explained in seif 8. If he was not distinguished and upright, lending to him without secure collateral is negligence even if no other debts were known. Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 34, says likewise. There he was asked about a guardian appointed by beit din and instructed to use the funds as he saw fit for the benefit of the orphans. He lent some of the money on a promissory note without collateral because he considered the borrower reliable. The borrower paid profits for several terms, the guardian extended the principal again, and eventually the borrower went far away and did not return. Noda BiYehuda rules that if beit din explicitly told the guardian to act as he saw fit, then whatever he did was by authority of beit din. Although strict law permits orphan money to be lent only against highly secure collateral, the guardian is not personally liable because beit din authorized him, and perhaps beit din judged that under contemporary conditions such strictness would prevent any investment and the orphans' funds would be consumed by expenses. But if beit din did not expressly authorize such discretion, he should not have lent without secure collateral and must reimburse the loss from his own funds. Nevertheless, if his liability is only for improper investment, one should charge him only as though the money had remained idle. Perhaps, had he not lent it to this borrower, no other person suitable for orphan funds would have been available. Therefore profits already paid by the borrower are credited, and the guardian pays only the remaining deficiency in principal. Noda BiYehuda further indicates that if the guardian was appointed by the orphans' father, there is room to exempt him from monetary liability, because Ramban holds such a guardian exempt even for negligence and Shach leaves the practical ruling unresolved. One might argue that this case is worse than ordinary negligence and constitutes direct damage, because he handed the money to a person to whom the law did not permit him to give it. But that issue itself depends on the dispute at the beginning of siman 301 concerning a custodian who transfers an item to another custodian, whether that is worse than ordinary negligence. He also rules that the guardian has no claim for compensation for his labor when no fee was agreed in advance. In particular, appointment by beit din is an honor and also fulfills a mitzvah, so he is presumed to have accepted the role without compensation. See there.
