Beit din assesses charity upon him if he is one who ought to give. Ketubot distinguishes one who became mentally incompetent from one who deliberately traveled overseas. For the mentally incompetent person, beit din descends to his property for support and for the additional item discussed in the Gemara, which one opinion identifies as charity. Rambam rules that charity is assessed from his property. Ran questioned the source. Kesef Mishneh explains that a person's property is obligated for charity, and one who traveled overseas may be presumed to be giving charity where he presently lives. This implies a true property lien for charity. Rashba and Ran, however, appear to deny an ordinary lien. They say that beit din can compel a person who is present to fulfill the mitzvah by descending to his assets, just as beit din can compel performance of other monetary mitzvot. If he is absent, coercion of the person cannot operate, and without a property lien the assets are not seized. This distinction accords with Rambam's wording that beit din descends to the assets "in his presence." Shach and Bach understood that as including even his presence, but the Talmudic passage shows the opposite: when he is deliberately overseas, beit din does not ordinarily distribute charity from his property. Therefore the phrase means specifically in his presence, not merely including his presence.
Hagahot Maimoniyot appears to hold more broadly that beit din may descend to assets even in a person's absence to enforce a positive commandment. Maharam writes that a father can be compelled to hire teachers for his children, and if he is absent the court can use his property after appropriate notification, just as creditors can proceed in his absence. Maharam's approach can be reconciled with the Gemara by following Ran, who limits the no-charity rule for one overseas to a person who was not clearly wealthy enough to have a fixed obligation. Rama, who in Even HaEzer follows Ran, can therefore also rule in the laws of Torah study like Maharam that beit din uses an absent father's assets to hire a teacher for his children. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch, however, rule that even a wealthy person who deliberately traveled overseas is not assessed for charity from his property. Under their approach, descending to assets for charity is an act of coercion upon the person and therefore operates only when he is present. Kesef Mishneh's own rationale, that an absent person may be giving charity wherever he is, can allow a distinction for Torah education. The father's children are here, and the obligation to teach these children cannot be fulfilled by giving elsewhere. Thus even one who follows Beit Yosef might agree that beit din can use the absent father's assets for their education after proper notice. There is nevertheless strong reason to accept Kesef Mishneh's deeper statement that a person's property is actually encumbered for the mitzvah of charity. We rule that liens are biblical for ordinary debts, and monetary mitzvot written by the Torah can likewise create property liability. Redemption of the firstborn and a woman's sacrificial obligation furnish parallels. This would also explain Rambam's ruling that beit din gives charity from the property of a person who became mentally incompetent. If charity were only a personal mitzvah enforced by coercion, why should an incompetent person, who is exempt from ordinary commandments, be subject to such coercion? A property lien explains why his assets remain liable. It also supports Beit Yosef's responsum that heirs may have to fulfill their father's charitable vow. A mere oath differs: an oath creates only the personal prohibition of violating one's word and does not encumber assets. A vow of charity is a monetary mitzvah expressed by the Torah's phrase concerning that which leaves one's mouth, and can therefore create a lien upon the estate. Rama argued from responsa concerning oaths and from a case of a deathly ill woman who promised charity only after death. Those cases do not refute this. An oath creates no monetary lien, and a vow explicitly postponed until after death never became a living obligation during the donor's lifetime, so no lien attached before death. Arakhin also says that heirs need not fulfill certain valuations made by the father, but those passages can involve oral obligations not collectible from heirs under the older law, or can concern the point at which the obligation actually attached. They do not conclusively prove that charity lacks all asset lien. Even if one rejects a lien for a newly uttered charity vow, the Torah's standing mitzvah of charity itself can still be viewed like other monetary commandments and create an obligation upon property. This explains why the Sages could compel payment even though charity is a positive command whose reward is stated alongside it. The court is not merely forcing performance of a rewarded positive command. It is collecting money already owed, in a legal sense, to the poor. Thus there is substantial basis for Kesef Mishneh's position that charity involves property encumbrance, though the extent to which that permits collection in the owner's absence remains disputed.
Even if one follows Rama that a private vow to the poor does not itself create a property lien and therefore dies with the promisor, the standing Torah mitzvah of charity may still carry an asset obligation. The two questions are distinct. A personal vow is created by the individual's words. If it creates no kinyan and no asset lien, only the prohibition of violating one's word remains, and the heirs are not bound by that personal prohibition. The Torah's general command of charity, by contrast, is itself a monetary command imposed upon the person's wealth. Kesef Mishneh therefore can say that his money is encumbered to the poor even though a particular vow may not be. This distinction also answers Tosafot's famous question how Rabbi Ami could compel charity when the reward for the command is stated beside it. If the obligation is merely personal, perhaps the court does not coerce such a positive command. But if the person's assets are already obligated to the poor, the court is simply enforcing a monetary duty. The presence of a stated reward does not allow one to retain money legally owed to another. This explanation also fits Rambam's wording that beit din estimates what the person ought to give and descends to his property. The assessment converts the general Torah duty into a concrete amount collectible from his estate. I discussed related principles in siman 26.
