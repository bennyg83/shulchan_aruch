A guardian who lends orphans' money. Sema cites Maharik that if the guardian cannot recover the money, he was negligent even though the borrower was honorable and reputable. Netivot HaMishpat notes that this abbreviates Maharik, whose case involved a borrower known to owe others more than his assets were worth. Where no such indebtedness was known at the time of the loan, Netivot considers the guardian exempt. The fact that recorded debts generate publicity does not itself make the guardian negligent, since Maharik emphasized actual knowledge. This qualification applies, however, where the borrower was otherwise honorable and reputable, because such a person may receive orphans' funds without collateral under the conditions stated below. If the borrower was not a fit person, lending without secure collateral is negligent even absent known debts. Noda BiYehuda II, 34, considers a guardian appointed by beit din and expressly authorized to act as he saw fit for the orphans' benefit. The guardian lent without collateral to a borrower he considered reliable; after several interest periods had been paid, the borrower disappeared. Where beit din expressly authorized the guardian to exercise his judgment, the guardian is not liable even if strict law would ordinarily require especially secure investment of orphans' funds, because the court may reasonably recognize that in current conditions such strict investments are unavailable and the funds would otherwise be consumed by maintenance. If the court gave no such express discretion, lending without secure collateral is negligent and the guardian must reimburse the principal. Even then, prior earnings paid by the borrower should be credited, because absent this loan there is no certainty that another lawful profitable investment would have been found. Noda BiYehuda adds that a guardian appointed by the father may have a stronger defense, since Ramban exempts such a guardian even for negligence and the Shach leaves the point unresolved. One might call an unlawful unsecured loan active damage rather than mere negligence, but that question may depend on the dispute whether transferring property to an unsuitable substitute custodian is worse than ordinary negligence. As to compensation, a guardian who did not stipulate for wages at the outset is generally not entitled to them, especially where appointment by beit din is an honor and a mitzvah.
