Up to seventy-one, etc. Because we do not add more judges beyond that number, which is the size of the Great Sanhedrin. Be'er Sheva asks: if so, how can Horayot 3b say, "If one hundred sat to issue a ruling, they are not liable unless all of them ruled"? How can there ever be more than seventy-one members, when the Great Sanhedrin itself does not exceed seventy-one? He leaves the question unresolved, and Mishneh LaMelech likewise remains uncertain. The answer can be based on Sanhedrin 40. There the Gemara says that if one of the students sitting before the Sanhedrin says, "I have an argument in favor of the defendant," they bring him up and seat him among the judges. He does not descend from there for the rest of that day. During that day he therefore has a voice in the deliberation like a member of the Sanhedrin. All the more so, if his argument is found substantive, he does not descend from that place afterward. Surely he does not then sit there silently, forbidden to express his view. He continues to participate and state the halachic conclusion that appears correct to him. Nor do we remove one of the original seventy-one merely because this student was elevated. The effective body of those issuing the ruling can therefore exceed seventy-one. If thirty students successively present valid arguments and are elevated into the deliberation, the body can contain one hundred people who are all actively issuing the halachic ruling. That is the case contemplated by Horayot when it says, "one hundred who sat to issue a ruling." The same mechanism can produce even more. There is therefore no difficulty.
