He may teach him. Since the student can reason independently, it makes no difference that he learned the relevant laws from this teacher. Otherwise every Jew would be disqualified from sitting with Moshe, since all learned Torah law from him. Only where the student cannot reason independently might we say that had he learned from another teacher he would perhaps have received a different explanation. Rashi, cited by Sema. In truth Sema should not have applied Rashi's proof to Mordechai's position. Mordechai holds that unless the teacher taught him the law specifically during the adjudication, teacher and student are always counted as two. If so, there is no proof from Moshe, because where do we find that Moshe taught the Sanhedrin the particular law while the case itself was pending? Rashi's additional reasoning, that an ordinary student is counted only as one with his teacher because had he learned elsewhere he might have offered another rationale, also applies even if the teacher did not teach him during the case. Mordechai therefore needs a different rationale from Rashi's. This requires study. It appears that the Mechaber understood Rashi not to disagree with Mordechai, for otherwise he would not have ruled simply like Mordechai against Rashi, the great luminary of the exile. Knesset HaGedolah also implies that Rashi does not disagree. Reconciling their words, however, is difficult.
Regarding prohibitions and permissions, etc. The wording implies that even if the teacher instructed him during the actual case, they are counted as two. Otherwise the same would already be true in monetary law. The reason is that questions of prohibition and permission do not formally require a counted panel, so they may be treated as two separate opinions. See Tumim, where I write that this rule requires study because Rambam appears to disagree. Rama should not have ruled without qualification against Rambam.
