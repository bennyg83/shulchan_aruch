And regarding matters of prohibition and permission, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah is uncertain how to understand Rambam. Rambam rules in Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 11 that in civil cases a teacher and his student can be counted as two, while in capital cases they are counted only as one, according to the text before Kenesset HaGedolah. He wonders whether Rambam refers to a student who does not need his teacher's reasoning, like R. Ami and R. Asi, and nevertheless distinguishes between civil and capital law, or whether Rambam is speaking even of a student who still needs his teacher's reasoning, yet in civil cases they are counted separately. I do not understand the basis for the uncertainty. Later in that same chapter Rambam explicitly rules that a student who does not need his teacher's reasoning may sit with his teacher even in a capital case. Kesef Mishneh explains that the two are then counted as separate opinions, based on Rav's statement that a person may teach a student and then judge a capital case together with him. Therefore the earlier distinction between civil and capital cases must concern a student who still relies upon his teacher's reasoning. Even such a student can count separately in civil law, but not in capital law. The real difficulty is with Mechaber, who appears to adopt Mordechai's view while failing to mention Rambam's position. We must say that Mechaber understood the two authorities not as disputing the practical law but only as having different textual versions of the Gemara. Rambam can be read as speaking where the student learned the relevant teaching from the teacher previously, not during the current deliberation. In that setting, civil cases count them as two but capital cases count them as one. Mordechai similarly says that where the learning occurred previously, civil law counts them as two. Thus there may be no practical disagreement. However, according to Rambam's text as cited by Kesef Mishneh, the baraita groups civil law together with tumah and taharah and says they are counted as two. If that is only where the student learned the matter previously, then the same limitation should apply to tumah and taharah as well. The baraita places them in one category and gives no indication of different rules. Rama, however, follows Mordechai and rules that in matters of prohibition and permission the teacher and student can be counted as two even if the student learned the matter from the teacher at that very time. That is not Rambam's view on the reading above. We should not rule leniently like Mordechai where Rambam disagrees. Therefore, in matters of prohibition and permission as well, one should be stringent and not count teacher and dependent student as two where the student's present opinion is based directly on the teacher's current reasoning.
