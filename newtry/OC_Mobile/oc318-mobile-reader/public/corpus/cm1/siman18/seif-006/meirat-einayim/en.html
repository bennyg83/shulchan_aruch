Not in the presence of the litigants. The source concludes with the reason: so long as a litigant later brings proof, he can overturn the judgment. It appears simple that this applies only after both litigants have already presented their claims before the judges. Then the judges may state the ruling to one party alone or write the judgment and send it to each party at home. This is specifically monetary law, because any later proof can correct the judgment. Capital law differs, since once the sentence is executed it is an irreparable wrong. Darkei Moshe at the end of siman 17 cites Rashba similarly: a defendant was given a deadline to bring proofs and failed to do so. When beit din wished to rule, he said he had transferred his rights to Levi and the court could not rule in Levi's absence. If Levi is nearby, they notify him. If he comes and immediately presents argument or proof, they hear him. If he does not come, or comes merely to request additional time, they do not listen to him, because in monetary matters we are not concerned to rule in his absence; whenever he later brings proof, it can overturn the judgment.
