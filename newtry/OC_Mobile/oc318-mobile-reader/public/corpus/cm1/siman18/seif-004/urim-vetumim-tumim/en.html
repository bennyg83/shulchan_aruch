Three who sat in judgment, etc. The remaining two cannot complete the judgment. Hagahot HaRosh in the first chapter of Sanhedrin cites Or Zarua: if three sat in judgment and one withdrew before it became clear which way the law inclined, the remaining two cannot complete the case. This is no better than a case where two judges favor one side and the third says, "I do not know." Bach infers from the phrase "before it became clear which way the law inclined" that if the law's direction was already clear to all three, this is considered equivalent to final judgment and the two remaining judges may complete the case. There is no proof. Or Zarua may have specified that the judge withdrew before the law became clear simply because otherwise we would be speaking of a wicked judge. A judge is not permitted to withdraw from a case once he knows which way the law inclines. Before that point, however, withdrawal can be permitted, as explained at the beginning of siman 12. Thus the wording may describe the ordinary lawful circumstance rather than create a substantive distinction in the validity of the remaining two judges. If, for some external reason, a judge left after the direction of the law became clear, or if he improperly withdrew, the remaining two still should not be able to complete the judgment, because Or Zarua derives the rule from the case of a judge who says "I do not know." Had Or Zarua intended to distinguish between before and after the law became clear, he should have said so explicitly rather than merely by implication. This was evidently Beit Yosef's understanding. When he quotes Hagahot HaRosh, he omits the phrase "before it became clear which way the law inclined" and simply writes that if one judge withdrew, the remaining two cannot complete the case. If Beit Yosef considered that phrase essential to the law, he could not have omitted it. Therefore the distinction proposed by Bach is not established. In any event, even according to Bach, he himself explains that Or Zarua's reason is that once the judges know which way the law inclines, that already counts as final judgment. But Hagahot HaRosh earlier cites a dispute concerning the definition of final judgment. Rashi says that final judgment means the court has already declared, "So-and-so, you are entitled; so-and-so, you are liable." Or Zarua explains that final judgment means the judges have fully concluded the matter among themselves even before formally announcing the result. Above in siman 12, seif 2, we rule according to Rashi that final judgment is the formal declaration. Accordingly, even if Bach correctly reads Or Zarua, Or Zarua's underlying definition of final judgment is not the halachah. Thus even according to Bach's interpretation, the Or Zarua ruling would not govern our practical law once we adopt Rashi's definition.
