Seif 1. The Sema, seif katan 3, explains that in monetary law we follow the majority, unlike capital law, because what is a benefit to one litigant is a detriment to the other. According to this reasoning, one might infer that in matters of issur and heter, or in a proceeding whose sole object is to determine that a person is disqualified to marry into the congregation, is a robber, or is otherwise disqualified from testimony or an oath, where the ruling is purely detrimental to that person and confers no corresponding legal benefit on another party, a bare majority of one would not suffice to impose the detriment. One would require two more judges on the side of liability than on the side of exoneration, as in capital law. With ordinary halachic rulings of prohibition and permission, however, one can distinguish. Such rulings do not require a panel of three at all. If there are judges on both sides and those who prohibit exceed those who permit by one, we may set the equal numbers against one another, leaving a single authority who prohibits. Since one qualified authority suffices to rule on issur and heter, that remaining opinion is enough. In monetary and capital cases this reasoning cannot work, because one judge alone cannot complete the judgment. We must join the entire panel and follow the majority as a collective. In capital cases the Torah revealed that a recognizable majority of two is required for conviction. Accordingly, in determining whether a person is disqualified from testimony or from taking an oath, a matter that itself requires three judges, perhaps a majority of only one would not suffice, because at the stage when we are deciding his personal status there is only a detriment to him and no corresponding benefit to another. We follow the present issue. Even though the determination may later affect litigation between two parties, that future claim has not yet arisen. Nevertheless, the Sema’s underlying explanation is not compelled. It may be that the special requirement of two for conviction was revealed only in capital law because of its exceptional severity. Monetary law and all other matters could then follow an ordinary majority of one, regardless of whether the immediate ruling is characterized as both benefit and detriment. If so, no special rule would apply to the disqualification cases described above. This is straightforward.
