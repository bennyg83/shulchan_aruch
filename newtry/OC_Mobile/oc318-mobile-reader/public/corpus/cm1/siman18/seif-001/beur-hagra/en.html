So that if two of the original judges acquit and one of the added judges concurs, judgment is concluded by three." Rabbi Ila said: "Once the case has become fit to be concluded by four, it is not concluded by three." The Rambam explains "one of the added judges" to mean that he too acquits, while the second added judge finds liable and the first judge who said "I do not know" remains in doubt. This is the second clause, "or if they said, etc." Rabbi Ila maintains that all four must acquit, as in Avodah Zarah 72a, as written in siman 13. That is the first clause, "but four, etc." According to Rabbi Ila, even if four acquit, if one of the added judges says "I do not know," it is ineffective, as he says, "once it is concluded, etc." According to the first Tanna, however, only a majority for acquittal is required. (Collected note) Unlike what he wrote, and the second, etc. This requires study. The Rashba's view is that even if two acquit, one finds liable, and two say "I do not know," judgment is concluded, as stated on 17a that one who says "I do not know" is treated as absent, and the judgment is concluded by three. The Yerushalmi's phrase "and one of the later judges" means that he joins them in acquittal, or even in liability, while the remaining two say "I do not know"; judgment is concluded by those who stated an opinion, because one who says "I do not know" is treated as absent, as stated in the Gemara cited above. Rabbi Ila says that once, etc., judgment is not concluded if one of those four says "I do not know," just as initially with three. The halacha follows the first Tanna; see Sema. Rather, the Rav's distinction is correct, as stated on 5b: "there it is different because they accepted, etc." Although according to him two suffice, the same applies for us with four or more.
