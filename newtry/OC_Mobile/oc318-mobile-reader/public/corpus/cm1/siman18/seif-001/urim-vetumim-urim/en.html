It is preferable to begin with the junior judge. This is certainly not obligatory in monetary cases. In capital cases the Torah says, "Do not answer upon the great," not because it is forbidden to disagree with a superior when one sees that the law is otherwise, especially when presenting the disagreement as a question. Even a student before his teacher may not suppress a genuine legal insight. Rather, in a capital case we fear that a junior judge may withhold his view out of respect for a senior, and an unjust judgment could thereby lead, Heaven forbid, to innocent bloodshed. Therefore the Torah says, "the congregation shall rescue." In monetary cases this concern is more remote, so one may begin with the senior judge. Still, it is preferable to begin with the junior so that even this concern is removed. Sema. See Tumim, where I write that the order should in fact always be observed, and see there concerning Sema's reasoning.
But if four say, "He is exempt," etc. Rashba in a responsum rules that if two say exempt, one says liable, and two say "I do not know," the decision still follows the two. Rambam disagrees and requires all judges whose views are counted to reach an operative position. Where three agree on one outcome and two say "I do not know," the Yerushalmi records a dispute. It teaches that additional judges are appointed so that if two of the original judges favor one side and one of the added judges joins them, the decision can be completed by three. Rashba explains that the added judge need only state a view rather than say "I do not know"; he need not agree with the first two. Rambam explains that he must actually agree with them, as the Yerushalmi's wording implies. Rabbi Ila says that once the panel was expanded so that the case came to require completion by four, it may not be concluded by three. Thus the first Tanna and Rabbi Ila dispute whether three agreeing judges suffice or four are required. Maharshal, Sema, and Shach understand Rambam to rule like the first Tanna, as Rashba is cited in Kesef Mishneh, but they understand Rambam's interpretation of the Yerushalmi to require the three deciding judges to agree on the same result. Accordingly, Rambam's example of four saying exempt is not exact. Three who agree would suffice even if two others say "I do not know." Rashba likewise accepts Rambam's interpretation. Bach, however, understands Rambam to rule like Rabbi Ila. Accordingly, once two judges say "I do not know," three matching votes do not suffice because after expansion the operative count is four. Rambam therefore deliberately requires four judges to state an actual view. His wording is very precise, and Lechem Mishneh also appears to understand him this way. I agreed with this in Tumim.
However, it does not appear correct to me, etc. Sema writes that the Rama's distinction between a panel originally constituted as three and one originally accepted as a larger number is unavoidable. Otherwise Rambam and Rashba would contradict themselves. Rashba is even more lenient than Rambam in the expanded panel case, allowing a majority despite some judges saying "I do not know." It must therefore be that the rules differ depending on how the panel was constituted. See Tumim, where I explain Rama's words and what I believe he intended.
A community that selected, etc. Sema asks: Rama just ruled like Rambam that we follow the majority, so why does he later say that where a community selected a panel we do not follow the majority? He answers that when a community selects more than three, all agree that unanimity is required, whereas the earlier dispute concerns litigants who selected the judges. I do not understand this. If a panel of more than three was expressly constituted as a fixed count, whether selected by the community or by the litigants, we plainly do not follow a mere majority when their acceptance implied participation of all. This is derived from the Gemara in Avodah Zarah. The earlier case differs because the panel was not initially constituted as that larger number. It began with three, or, as above in siman 13, the parties expressly stipulated that a larger group would decide by majority. In that setting Rama holds that since halachah ordinarily follows the majority, a judge saying "I do not know" does not prevent decision when a majority exists without him. But when the litigants or the community accepted a panel of more than three without stipulating majority rule, all agree that they accepted them on the understanding that all would participate, so a simple majority does not suffice, and certainly not where some say "I do not know." Sema's explanation is therefore difficult.
