Some say that ideally it is proper, etc. Sema writes, as explained in Urim, that in civil cases Chazal were not as concerned that the lesser judges might suppress their opinions before a greater judge. The reason appears to be that d'oraisa even one judge can be valid in civil law. Therefore, even if the lesser judges refrain from voicing their view in deference to the greater judge, the greater judge alone is still capable of rendering a valid Torah judgment. Capital cases are different. There a full numerical panel is required. If the lesser judges suppress their opinions, the necessary count of active judicial opinions is lacking. This is supported by Tosafot in Sanhedrin 19, who write that the rule forbidding a king to sit on the Sanhedrin applies specifically to capital cases, because there the concern of "do not answer against the chief" arises. It does not apply in the same manner to civil cases. At first glance, however, this creates a difficulty. In Sanhedrin 18b the Gemara asks: "But we do not seat a king on the Sanhedrin." Yet that passage concerns a civil matter, as Tosafot themselves explain in the discussion of testimony. The Gemara does not ask from the separate rule that a king does not judge, because, as Tosafot explain, a king may participate with other judges even if he cannot judge alone. Why then does the Gemara invoke the prohibition against seating a king on the Sanhedrin if that concern applies only to capital cases? According to the explanation above, the answer is clear. The reason a king is not seated with the other judges is that they will be reluctant to disagree with him. Their opinions then become effectively nonexistent. If so, the king is in substance judging alone. But the reason a non-Davidic king does not judge applies precisely when he is acting alone, because of the principle discussed there. Thus once all the other judges are effectively silenced by his presence, the situation returns to the prohibited case of the king judging by himself. For a Davidic king, who is permitted to judge alone, no such problem exists in civil law. Therefore the concern that others may defer to him does not invalidate the civil panel. This also explains the Tosafot distinction. There is, however, another apparent difficulty. In Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot, Rav says: "I was among Rebbi's counting group, and they began with me." The Gemara asks from the rule that in civil matters one begins with the greatest judge, and answers that Rebbi's counts were different because in all of them they always began from the side. According to Nimukei Yosef and Rama, why was this a question? They themselves say it is proper even in civil cases to begin with the lesser judges. The answer is that doing so occasionally in civil cases can be disrespectful to the greater judges sitting on the panel. But if the fixed practice is always to begin from the side, no disrespect is implied. Everyone knows this is the established procedure rather than a judgment about personal stature. Thus the questioner assumed Rebbi did not have a consistent practice and therefore asked why Rav was called first. The Gemara answered that all of Rebbi's counts followed this procedure, so there was no slight to the senior sages. Nimukei Yosef is precise in writing that in civil cases it is good to begin always with the lesser judge. Rama omits the word "always," which creates some imprecision, but he should be understood in the same way. The proper practice is not to begin from the lesser judge only sporadically, but to establish a consistent procedure of doing so.
And perhaps his intent is to distinguish, etc. See Urim, where I already wrote that Sema proves such a distinction is necessary, because otherwise Rashba's rulings contradict one another. At first glance the matter requires substantial study. First, why did Rama see any contradiction in Shulchan Aruch at all? Above, we say that when the court expands from three, they undertook the matter on the assumption of ten judges. Here we can similarly say that they undertook it on the assumption of four active opinions. One of the original three said, "I do not know." Two original judges remain, and two additional judges were added. That leaves four judges capable of expressing an opinion. Indeed, Shulchan Aruch, quoting Rambam, explicitly requires four judges to state their view. It is not enough for three to state a view while two say "I do not know." The reason is precisely that the expanded court was constituted with four active decisional opinions in mind, following R. Ila's view in the Yerushalmi, as I explained in Urim, seif katan 3. Perhaps Rama follows Maharshal and Sema's view, also cited there, that Rambam's mention of four is not exact and that three agreeing judges are enough, according to the first Tanna in the Yerushalmi. Even then, however, from where does Rama derive a contradiction? Perhaps Rambam and Mechaber specifically require four, like R. Ila. Bach indeed takes that view. A second, even greater difficulty is Rama's statement that Rambam and Rashba disagree. What dispute can there be about the rule that where one of three judges says "I do not know," two additional judges are added? This is an explicit Mishnah. Rambam and Rashba cannot disagree about whether the additional judges are added. To understand the issue, we must first clarify the words of R. Moshe HaKohen, cited by Kesef Mishneh in Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 8. R. Moshe HaKohen writes that his teachers did not interpret the Mishnah's addition of two judges as Rambam did. He argues from the Gemara's challenge against Shmuel, who says that if two judges adjudicated, their judgment is valid. The Gemara asks: if one of three judges says "I do not know," why do we add judges? Why not let the remaining two decide? The Gemara answers: "Here it is different, because they sat on the understanding that there would be three." R. Moshe HaKohen argues that the same reasoning should apply after judges are added. Once they sat on the understanding that there would be five, three should not be able to complete the judgment. This seems difficult because Rambam himself requires four, not merely three, according to the straightforward reading and Bach's understanding. What then were R. Moshe HaKohen's teachers saying differently? The answer is that Rambam holds that the judge who originally said "I do not know" may later reconsider and express either a position for liability or for exemption in a civil case. That is why Rambam writes that all five deliberate together. Kesef Mishneh explains similarly. Rambam therefore understands the purpose of adding two judges as follows. When one judge says "I do not know," we do not treat him as permanently absent. The additional discussion may clarify the matter for him and cause him to reach a definite opinion. Why add two rather than one? Suppose only one additional judge were added, and the formerly uncertain judge then reached a conclusion opposite the two original judges. The panel could divide evenly. Therefore two are added. In that sense, once two are added, the court operates with five potential opinions, including the formerly uncertain judge. Rambam in his commentary to the Mishnah goes even further and says that even if four judges say the defendant is exempt, so long as one remains saying "I do not know," more judges are added until that judge reaches a definite conclusion. Rambam later changed that detail in Mishneh Torah, but R. Moshe HaKohen's teachers retained the earlier interpretation. This explains his language precisely. He writes that because they entered on the understanding of five, three cannot complete the judgment. He emphasizes five rather than four because, according to his teachers, the first judge remains part of the required decisional body and must eventually leave his state of uncertainty. The court is truly a five-opinion tribunal. Kesef Mishneh answers him by distinguishing this from the original three-judge panel. Initially, the judges entered on the understanding that three definite judges would decide. Once one said "I do not know," that condition failed and additional judges had to be brought. Afterward, however, the law may be different. Still, such an obvious distinction surely did not escape R. Moshe HaKohen. His deeper question is a dilemma. If after adding judges we continue to say that the court was really constituted only on the original understanding of three, then when two judges acquit and one convicts, the case should already be decided by the majority of the original three. There should be no need for the added judges to create a larger majority. Yet Rambam requires three judges to agree on one conclusion among the expanded panel. This shows that once judges were added, the case is now being adjudicated by a larger court and not merely by the original three. If so, the panel has in fact been reconstituted on the basis of five judges. But if they now sit as a court of five, how can one or two judges say "I do not know" and the judgment still stand? When three judges originally sat, one saying "I do not know" destroyed the panel's ability to decide, even according to Shmuel, because they sat on the understanding of three active judges. The same should apply to a five-member panel. This is a strong question against Rambam. The answer must be that Rambam distinguishes between the reason additional judges are needed in the first place and the numerical majority required afterward. The Gemara's phrase "they sat on the understanding of three" is necessary only according to Shmuel, who holds two judges can produce a valid judgment. We do not rule like Shmuel. According to our halachah, two judges do not constitute a valid civil tribunal. Therefore, when one of three judges says "I do not know," the problem is simpler: that judge is treated as absent, and only two functioning judges remain. Torah law requires at least three judges to adjudicate. The court must therefore be enlarged until at least three functioning judges are present. Once there are three judges who know what to say, it does not matter if the remaining members of a larger panel say "I do not know." The fundamental requirement of three valid judicial opinions is satisfied. This is what R. Moshe HaKohen means when he praises Rambam's explanation: because there are three judges who know how to decide a civil case, the judgment can stand. Thus the phrase "they sat on the understanding of three" is not an independent universal rule for us. It was merely the Gemara's answer within Shmuel's position. Rama's understanding follows this line. Now Rashba's position becomes important. Rashba can consistently hold that when a court is constituted as five or more, all members whose opinions were part of the accepted panel must actually state an opinion, and if one says "I do not know" the entire panel may be impaired. Why then does Rashba accept adding judges to a three-judge court when one says "I do not know"? Because the original panel was accepted only as a three-judge court. The two additions repair the deficiency without necessarily creating a new obligation that all five produce opinions. Under the first Tanna in the Yerushalmi, three decisive opinions are enough. And if one asks: if the original framework of three remains controlling, why should we need a majority of the expanded group rather than merely the majority of the first three? Rashba appears in Kesef Mishneh to hold precisely that in certain circumstances two of the original three can determine the result. As I explained in Urim, his position differs from Rambam's requirement that three judges in the enlarged panel agree. Therefore Rashba's rulings do not contradict one another. But Mechaber follows Rambam, not Rashba, regarding the expanded panel. He requires three judges to agree on one conclusion. That shows the expanded body matters as a new decisional unit. If so, Rama asks correctly: once the enlarged court is treated as a new panel, why can some members say "I do not know" without invalidating the body, while above a court accepted in a larger number is invalidated when one of its necessary members says "I do not know"? Rama therefore suggests a distinction. Perhaps Rambam's rule works because only the minimum Torah requirement matters once at least three definite judges remain, whereas in a voluntarily accepted larger panel all participants were specifically accepted as necessary decision-makers. This is what Rama means by "perhaps his intent is to distinguish." Still, I believe Rambam should be understood more precisely. Although the judge who originally said "I do not know" remains present and may continue the discussion, when the two additional judges are appointed the parties do not necessarily accept a tribunal of five active votes. At that moment, the judge who said "I do not know" is functionally treated as absent until he changes his mind. The enlargement therefore occurs on the assumption of four functioning judges, not five. That is why Rambam requires the requisite majority out of four. This accords with R. Ila in the Yerushalmi and with Bach's reading that Rambam means specifically four. If one of those four functioning judges then also says "I do not know," the court is impaired and further judges must be added. This also explains why Rambam requires three judges to agree on one result. They are the majority of the four active decisional judges. There is no need to invoke Rashba's separate reasoning in responsum. The structure is simple: the original uncertain judge is treated as though he were not present for purposes of constituting the enlarged decisional panel. The two additions create four functioning judges, and a majority of those four must agree. If the originally uncertain judge later resolves his doubt, he may participate, but his potential future opinion did not define the number on which the new panel was constituted. R. Moshe HaKohen disagreed because he viewed the original uncertain judge as remaining fully within the new panel, so that the court was constituted on the understanding of five. Rambam does not. On this reading Rambam's words are coherent and the apparent contradictions disappear.
