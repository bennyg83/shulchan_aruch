This appears to be the primary rule. Ran in his novellae to the first chapter of Sanhedrin discusses Shmuel's rule that if two judges adjudicated, their judgment is valid after the fact. The Mishnah says that if two judges say exempt and one says, "I do not know," more judges are added. The Gemara asks that if two judges can adjudicate validly after the fact, why should the two remaining judges not conclude the case? It answers that they originally sat with the intention of being a panel of three. Ran explains the question: although two judges are an improperly bold court initially, after one of the three says he does not know it would seem that the remaining two should be able to conclude the matter because a two judge ruling is valid after the fact. The answer is that they originally accepted the role as a three judge court and do not wish to transform themselves into a bold two judge court. It follows that where this rationale does not apply, such as where ten judges were selected and one says, "I do not know," one cannot say that they all sat specifically on condition that the panel remain ten. If that were the rule, Ran would not have needed to explain the three judge case in terms of the special impropriety of a bold two judge court. Kesef Mishneh questions the dissenters because the Gemara explicitly says that they sat with the intention of three. Ran's explanation shows that the statement is tied specifically to the concern of becoming a bold two judge court. Since that is Ran's view, the Mechaber's practical ruling is correct.
