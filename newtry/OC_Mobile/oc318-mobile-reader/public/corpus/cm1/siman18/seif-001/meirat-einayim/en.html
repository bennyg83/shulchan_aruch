They remove everyone from the room. This prevents the litigants from knowing which judge favored or opposed them, and also prevents them from overhearing the judges' deliberations and learning arguments with which to plead falsely.
It is preferable to begin with the junior judge. This is obligatory only in capital cases. The Mishnah says that monetary, purity, and impurity cases begin with the senior judge, while capital cases begin from the side, meaning the junior judges, lest they be reluctant to disagree after hearing the senior judge. The verse 'do not answer a rav' is read as caution against answering a greater authority. Nimukei Yosef explains that it is not literally forbidden to disagree. A student must not remain silent where he can teach merit. Rather, the procedure is designed so that he will not feel embarrassed to disagree. This concern was imposed strictly in capital cases, not monetary cases, though even in monetary cases it is good practice to begin with the junior judge. Tosafot suggests a somewhat stricter understanding, that direct contradiction of the senior judge may itself be improper except by respectful questioning, and capital law begins with juniors so that even that inhibition does not arise. Tosafot also offers the view that the verse applies only in capital cases.
They follow the majority: two say, etc. This teaches that unlike a capital conviction, which requires a majority of two for liability, in monetary law one-vote majority suffices, because what is liability to one party is benefit to the other.
And one says, 'I do not know.' They add judges. Majority rule applies only where all three sat and deliberated, each gave a reasoned view, and two agreed. One who says, 'I do not know,' is treated as though he did not sit with them, because had a different third judge participated he might have persuaded the two. After additional judges are added, however, if enough judges remain who state opinions and constitute the panel originally required, the majority of those opinions may decide the case.
They add more judges. Meaning they add another two, as they did the first time. Rambam states this explicitly.
But four say, etc., and one, etc. Maharshal writes that similarly, if three acquit and two say, 'I do not know,' we follow the three. But if two acquit, one finds liable, and two say, 'I do not know,' more judges are needed because there is not yet a majority against the abstentions.
Whether it is the one who first said, etc. The novelty is that even if a different judge now says, 'I do not know,' we do not regard both the original and current abstainer as jointly standing against the others so as to eliminate the majority. See the Perishah for another explanation.
Unlike what was written above in siman 13, etc. See what I write below in seif 4, Sema seif katan 11.
Perhaps his intent is to distinguish, etc. Rama's attempt to explain a distinction in Rashba is difficult, because Rashba expressly writes that where a panel of three had one judge say 'I do not know,' two more are added, and then one of the four remaining judges says 'I do not know,' judgment may be completed by the remaining three as though they had originally sat as a panel of three. If two of those three agree and the third disagrees, the majority controls. The end of siman 13 is also from Rashba. The correct distinction therefore is between a panel originally convened as three, where added judges can leave a valid three-person body, and a panel originally accepted as ten, where their agreement may require participation of all ten. Kesef Mishneh makes this distinction. Rama, who describes the matter as disputed but then rules by majority, later writes that where a community selected four or five arbitrators they do not follow the majority. He must distinguish between arbitrators selected by the community, where all were appointed as a collective, and judges selected directly by the litigants.
For majority rule applies only in beit din. An ordinary beit din is three. But where parties specifically descended to a larger number of appraisers or arbitrators, it resembles the Talmudic case 'as assessed by four,' which requires all four to assess. Rashba concludes that if all do not agree, the defendant remains exempt because money is not extracted until liability is established. Any doubt concerning an enactment is likewise judged leniently for the defendant.
