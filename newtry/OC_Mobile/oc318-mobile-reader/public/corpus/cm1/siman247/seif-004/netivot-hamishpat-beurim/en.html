To her and not to her heirs. One should not ask: the daughters acquired their amount through a shechiv mera gift, so why should their heirs not inherit that right? If a shechiv mera says, "Give one maneh to so-and-so," the recipient's heirs can acquire even if the recipient dies during the donor's lifetime, as appears in Gittin 14 and siman 125. That case is different. There the donor removed the money from his own possession and said "bring it," as Beit Yosef and Shach explain. Here the same mechanism is absent. Accordingly, if the donor has additional sons and the daughter dies, her share returns to all the sons equally, because her portion had been designated only to her and not to a particular son. But one who examines Mordechai will see that the father had transferred all the property to his son, while the sums designated to the daughters were merely conditions imposed on that gift. A condition is a separate matter. Therefore language "to me and not to my heirs" in a condition has the effect discussed in Gittin, chapter Mi SheAchazo, concerning "on condition that you give me two hundred zuz." The entire gift to the son does not fail merely because the condition can no longer be fulfilled. In siman 241, seif 7, Tur and Beit Yosef discuss cases where failure of the condition voids the act. Here, however, the condition was for the daughter's benefit, and where the obstacle to fulfillment does not arise from the recipient, the underlying gift remains effective. Furthermore, here the son could not have fulfilled the condition earlier, because a shechiv mera gift does not take effect until after death, while the daughter died first. This is therefore treated as a failure not caused by the recipient. Once the daughter dies, the condition falls away and the entire property remains with the son, because all the estate had been given to him and the daughter's payment was only a condition attached to his gift.
