Baer HaGolah writes, citing Ritva, that when one says “to my male relatives” or “to my female relatives,” the term includes all relatives disqualified from testifying for him. Whether the nearer relative takes precedence is discussed by Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 127, discusses a wealthy man who left five hundred gold coins to be divided among his poor relatives living in a certain city. Only two or three relatives there were close enough to be disqualified as witnesses, and it was unreasonable to suppose that the testator meant so large a sum for only those few, especially since he could easily have specified them. Chatam Sofer agrees that more distant relatives in that city, even fourth-degree relatives who are called relatives in ordinary speech, should also share. He nevertheless raises a doubt if closer relatives of the third degree live elsewhere. Perhaps the testator intended only second- and third-degree relatives in the designated city, preferring the combination of family closeness and membership in his community, rather than abandoning closer relatives elsewhere in favor of very remote ones in the city. He further rules that relatives who live from hand to mouth, even though they have no fixed capital, are certainly “poor” and may take, all the more so from a private family bequest. Since the testator said “my poor relatives,” however, both dimensions matter: closer kinship gives priority among relatives, and greater poverty gives priority among the poor. The distributors should therefore weigh both closeness and degree of need. In siman 126 Chatam Sofer discusses a will written in German as “these belong to my poor relatives,” and whether the wife's relatives are included. Ordinarily they are not. If the deceased himself used that language, his wife's relatives are not his relatives. In an earlier case, however, the deceased had merely instructed administrators to act for the benefit of himself and his children, and the administrators, for the children's honor, set aside part of their inheritance in a permanent fund. There Chatam Sofer held that the children's maternal relatives could share, because they are relatives of the children just as their paternal relatives are. In the ordinary testamentary case before him, though, the wife's family had no share. In siman 112 he explains further that even if in the husband's lifetime his wife's relatives could be called his relatives because they were disqualified from testifying for him, that relationship terminates at death. He compares the law of a vow not to enter “your house”: after the owner dies, the house is no longer called “your house.” Here the bequest begins only after the family relationship has already terminated. Chatam Sofer later learned that the widow and administrator had already litigated and compromised, agreeing to give a substantial annual sum to the widow's family. He ruled that the administrator had acted properly and should not retract. The widow had a plausible linguistic argument that the German term used can include affinity relatives even after death, and she could also argue that a donor who left so large a fund probably intended some benefit for his children's maternal relatives. Against that stood contrary presumptions, for unlimited inclusion of relatives by affinity would yield absurd consequences. With competing presumptions and a danger of prolonged conflict, compromise was appropriate. He suggested that it be near one-sixth of the annual income, because had the widow's family fully prevailed they might have received roughly one-third, while paternal relatives would take precedence; splitting the disputed third approximately in half avoids an arbitrary equal split of the entire fund. In siman 135 Chatam Sofer considers a testator who ordered debt instruments returned gratis to his “relatives and friends,” thereby forgiving their debts. A man who was the brother-in-law of the deceased's grandnephew claimed to be both a relative and a friend. Chatam Sofer ruled for the administrators who rejected the claim. “Relative” in this context means one within the legal category of kinship that disqualifies testimony, and the claimant did not qualify. “Friend” means an actual intimate or beloved companion; Targum and Talmudic usage support that meaning. Someone who was not in fact among the deceased's close associates cannot claim merely from the broad social sense of the word.
