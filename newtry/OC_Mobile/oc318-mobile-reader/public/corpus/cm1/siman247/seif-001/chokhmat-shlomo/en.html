Seif 1. Note: If a dying person directs that property be given to "his sons-in-law," does that include men who become his sons-in-law only after his death, or only those already married to his daughters? See Mishneh LaMelech, Hilkhot Zekhiyah U'Mattanah and Hilkhot Parah, citing Maharit, who holds that "his sons-in-law" means only those already married. Mishneh LaMelech rejects the proof. I am surprised that he did not cite the explicit verse concerning Lot, "Lot went out to his sons-in-law, those who were taking his daughters." Rashi explains that "his sons-in-law" refers to the husbands of his two married daughters, whereas "those who were taking his daughters" refers to the men betrothed to the daughters still in his home. Had the term "sons-in-law" included future sons-in-law, the additional phrase would have been unnecessary. The verse therefore indicates that the term itself refers only to those already married. It also shows that even men already betrothed to the daughters are not called sons-in-law until marriage. One cannot object that the language of the Torah does not establish the customary language of documents, for Maharit himself adduces proof from Scriptural usage elsewhere. In practice, I therefore incline to the above conclusion. See also my Choshen Mishpat, fifth edition, siman 251. I have also discussed the related verse, "Abraham gave all that he had to Isaac," in my Torah writings to Chayei Sarah. There I initially sought a proof that a gift refers only to persons already in existence, but then rejected it, since Abraham may have repeated the gift after additional children were born so that there should be no doubt that Isaac retained the original grant.
