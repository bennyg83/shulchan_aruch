My property to my sons, in the plural, and so forth. See Responsa Panim Meirot, part 2, end of siman 133, concerning a person on his deathbed who gave the upper level rooms facing the street to one son, the rooms facing the courtyard to his grandson, and the lower level to his second son. He then wrote, "My children may build above," provided they cause no damage to his grandson. Shimon, the son who received the lower level, argued that because the testator used the plural "my children may build above," he too had the right to build upon the upper level, and that the phrase "up to the height of the sky" referred only to the sukkah and associated structure. Reuven argued that the phrase naturally referred to all the gifts described earlier, and that a person can refer to a single son by the plural "my sons," as explained in siman 247. Panim Meirot answered that, taken alone, this proof would not be compelling. Although verses show that a person sometimes calls one son "my sons," that construction is used as against daughters, where we explain that daughters are not included in "sons." If he actually has several sons, however, the plural would ordinarily refer to all of them. Yet this analysis would matter only if it were clear that the concluding phrase "up to the height of the sky" referred solely to the immediately preceding words. In truth, a person's concluding qualification ordinarily relates back to all the matters stated earlier, as may be proved from the Mishnah in the fifth chapter of Shevuot concerning five claimants. The phrase therefore also applied to the rooms first mentioned. Consequently, the plural "my sons" cannot displace an already clear and completed gift. Just as, where a man has daughters and one son and says "my property to my sons," the daughters do not take against the son's Torah inheritance and the plural is explained as ordinary speech, so too Shimon cannot acquire against the definite gift already vested in Reuven merely because of the plural wording. Reuven therefore acquired the entire upper story and all the airspace above it up to the sky. Panim Meirot ruled accordingly in practice.
And likewise a son's son is not included among them. See the Shach below, siman 257, seif 2, cited in Baer Heitev there, seif katan 6, who notes that Responsa Mabit was uncertain whether a fourth generation is included in the expression "sons of his sons." See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 173, who explains that Mabit's doubt arose from the verse describing Yaakov's descent to Egypt, "his sons and his sons' sons with him," where a fourth generation was included. Shevut Yaakov writes that there is no real doubt. That verse proves nothing, while several verses indicate the opposite, that "sons' sons" extends only through three generations and no further. Beyond this, even if Torah usage included a great grandson in the term "sons' sons," a will and similar documents follow ordinary human speech, in which the term includes only a grandson and no more. Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 53, likewise discusses a deathly ill person who directed that a certain sum be given to each of his grandchildren, where one grandchild already had children, raising the question whether those descendants were included. He answers that only a son's son is called a grandchild. Scripture itself uses this meaning in Avimelech's oath, translated as referring to a son's son, and the Gemara likewise uses "descendant and grandchild" with "grandchild" referring to Vashti, the daughter of Nebuchadnezzar's son. There is no basis to create a doubt that ordinary speech differs. On the contrary, in common Ashkenazic usage one adds a separate term when referring to a great grandchild. Nor should we invent doubts about ordinary speech to depart from Torah usage absent any indication in the Gemara. Thus, both Torah usage and ordinary speech exclude a great grandchild from "grandchild." Even if some doubt remained, a deathbed gift is rabbinic and cannot displace a Torah inheritance while the property remains in the heirs' possession. In his view, however, there is no doubt. He further discusses a grandson who had married but whose wife died during the testator's lifetime, where the will made a gift to grandchildren "who have not married." The widowed grandson argued that he now fell within that description. His claim is unfounded. "Who have not married" means those who have never married. This is supported by the Gemara's language in the chapter Mi Shemet concerning one who sent pieces of silk to his household, where the expression "not married" means never married. The same applies here, and the donor's intent strongly supports it because he had already expended funds for this grandson's marriage once. A third question concerned a grandchild born after the will was written. Such a child is certainly not included, because a deathbed donor cannot transfer property to a person not yet in existence. The exceptional rule permitting a gift to a fetus applies specifically to one's own child, because a person's mind is especially close to his child. Siman 210 establishes that a grandson is treated as an outsider for this purpose, so even if the mother was already pregnant, the grandchild did not acquire. See there. See also Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 38, concerning a will establishing a permanent fund whose income was to support Torah scholars until the fourth generation of the testator's descendants, after which the arrangement would cease and the principal would be divided among the testator's grandchildren of the fourth generation then alive. When descendants described as the fourth generation appeared, the heirs and the Torah scholars receiving stipends disputed how to count. The heirs argued that they themselves were the fourth generation mentioned by the testator, citing "the fourth generation shall return here." The scholars argued that the count begins with the testator's offspring, making those heirs only the third generation, and cited Zechariah king of Israel, who is described as part of the fourth generation of Yehu's descendants even though Zechariah was fifth when Yehu himself is counted. Noda BiYehudah ruled for the scholars. The heirs' proof relies on Ibn Ezra's explanation that "the fourth generation" counts from those who were themselves sojourners: Kehat was a sojourner in Egypt, Amram was his son, Moshe and Aharon were Amram's sons, and their children were the fourth generation to return to the Land. But there is no reason to prefer Ibn Ezra over Rashi, who explains that Yaakov descended to Egypt, Yehudah was his son, Peretz his son, Chetzron his son, and Kalev son of Chetzron entered the Land. On that count the fourth generation begins after Yaakov, not with Yaakov himself. Ramban similarly explains the fourth generation of the Amorites by reference to Hashem visiting ancestral sin upon the third and fourth generations, where the fourth generation upon which punishment is visited is actually fifth from the sinner when the sinner himself is counted, as the verse states, "upon children, upon children's children, upon the third and upon the fourth." There is therefore no basis to abandon Rashi and Ramban, leading commentators and halachic authorities, in favor of Ibn Ezra. He notes that it is somewhat surprising that this discussion did not mention that Rambam also explains the matter like Ibn Ezra, as stated in his commentary to the Mishnah, Eduyot chapter 2, and cited by Tosafot Yom Tov there. Moreover, even according to Ibn Ezra there is no proof. Hashem was speaking to Avraham about the fourth generation of the sojourners, so the first sojourner could be included in the count because all were generations descending from Avraham. Where a person speaks about himself, however, or another person speaks directly to him and refers to generations, the speaker or addressee is not ordinarily counted as one of those generations. Noda BiYehudah adds that had the will contained only the first expression, "until the children of the fourth," without later stating that the fund should then be divided among "his grandchildren, the fourth generation," there might have been room to construe the phrase as extending one generation further, effectively to the sixth generation when counting the testator himself. Ibn Ezra explains in Vayechi regarding "children of the third generation" that because the word "children" is construct, those children are one generation beyond the third. Accordingly, "children of the fourth" could mean children of the fifth, and after excluding the testator himself from the count, as shown from "the third and the fourth" in Ki Tisa, this could amount to the sixth generation including the testator. But the later clause clarifies the earlier one, because it directs that the principal be divided among "his grandchildren, the fourth generation." Thus those who themselves constitute the fourth generation are the recipients. See there.
Even if he has daughters. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 169, concerning a deathly ill man who directed that all his books be given to his son. He had no biological son, only a learned stepson and a son's son, and the question was which should receive them. Shevut Yaakov begins by considering the possible merit of the Torah scholar. The Gemara in the chapter HaKotev discusses one who said, "My property to Toviyah," where two people named Toviyah appeared, and states that a Torah scholar takes precedence. Tur, siman 253, cites an opinion that where one claimant is a relative this refers only to a relative who is not an heir, because an actual heir would take precedence. Tur disagrees, reasoning that when the relative is the natural heir there is even greater indication that the donor did not mean him, since he would receive the estate anyway. Thus, if the question here were evenly balanced as to whom the testator intended, one might likewise say that the Torah scholar takes precedence even though the grandson is an heir. Although Tur's objection to the first opinion is not entirely decisive, as discussed below in siman 253, seif 29, the premise of the present case is flawed. There is no real basis to suppose that "my son" meant the stepson. Beit Yosef, siman 247, writes that where a man has no sons but has grandsons and daughters and says, "my property to my sons," Nimukei Yosef cites his teachers that he means the grandsons because they are fit to inherit him. All the more so against a stepson, who is a complete outsider and has no kinship at all. Tosafos in Pesachim 24a, the Tosafos rulings there, and Maharsha in Bava Batra 115b provide clear support. It is therefore certain that he intended his son's son, not his stepson. Shevut Yaakov adds that he trusts that if the learned stepson is indeed a worthy Torah scholar and sees this responsum, he himself will not wish to benefit from property that is not his. If, however, the stepson was raised in the testator's home, the law requires further study, because the Rama at the end of siman 42 writes that one who raises an orphan in his home may call him "my son" in a document, since one who raised him may appropriately write that way.
