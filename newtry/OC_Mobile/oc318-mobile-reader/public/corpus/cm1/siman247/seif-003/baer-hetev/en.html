Alone. Only where a healthy person says, 'Give to my children,' are daughters included, in order to endear married daughters to their husbands or make unmarried daughters more desirable for marriage. A person dangerously ill, however, does not ordinarily divert inheritance merely to endear his daughters to their husbands. Even if they are unmarried, since after his death they are entitled to one tenth of the estate, he does not ordinarily divert additional inheritance for that purpose. Responsa Rosh states this clearly, and the Tur cites it. Tosafot ask: If so, why did he need to say that the property should be given to his sons, when even without his statement his son inherits and his daughters do not? They answer that we presume he needed to say it because he knew he had previously given his property to others through a deathbed gift, and this later command was intended to retract that gift. Sema.
The son. Meaning, if he had two sons and one died leaving a son, we do not say that because he said 'my sons' in the plural he intended also the grandson. Although grandchildren are sometimes called children, ordinary speech is not so. We therefore interpret his words as referring to the one surviving son alone. Ibid.
A son's son. In the Perishah there I explained the Beit Yosef's language, from which it follows that if he made this statement while healthy and at that time had daughters and a grandson, the daughters receive the share appropriate for daughters. If he made it while dangerously ill, everything goes to the grandson, just as where he had a son and daughter. Ibid. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 169.
