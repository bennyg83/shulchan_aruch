Seif 1. It is forbidden by Torah law to steal even the smallest amount. Note: If a theft occurred in a person's home and he has a strong circumstantial basis to conclude that a particular individual committed the theft, may he strike that person in order to make him confess, or is it forbidden to strike him on the strength of circumstantial inference? It appears that he may strike him. This is explicit in the Rosh, Perek 2 of Bava Metzia, regarding Mar Zutra the Pious, whose silver cup was stolen at an inn. He saw a certain rabbinic student wash his hands and wipe them on another person's garment. He said, "This is the one, for he does not care about another person's property." He compelled him and the man confessed. See the Rosh there, who explains that Mar Zutra relied upon his circumstantial assessment and compelled him with lashes. It follows that in such a case one may strike a person on the basis of a strong circumstantial assessment, even when the suspect is a rabbinic student and Torah scholar. This is clear as a matter of law.
Ibid. See the Shach, who discusses at length whether the rule that there is no agency for a transgression applies when the agent acted inadvertently. See what I wrote in a responsum to the community of Schatz, in my work on Even HaEzer, seventh edition, siman 13. I distinguished as follows: where nullifying the agency would also nullify the transgression so that no transgression would have occurred at all, everyone agrees that there is no agency for a transgression in an inadvertent case, because the appointment was made in error. Had the agent known the true circumstances, he would not have agreed to act as agent. But where the transgression would have been committed regardless of whether the agency is effective, and nullifying the agency would instead cause the entire transgression to be attributed to the agent, the agent prefers the agency to remain effective so that the transgression is attributed to the principal. In such a case, these authorities hold that agency for a transgression can operate when the agent acted inadvertently. This distinction resolves several of the Shach's difficulties, but this is not the place for a lengthy treatment. I subsequently saw that the Noda BiYehuda, both in the first and second editions, wrote the same distinction, and my reasoning accords with his.
