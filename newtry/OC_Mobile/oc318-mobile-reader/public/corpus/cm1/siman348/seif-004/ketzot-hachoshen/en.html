And if he brought it into his domain. At the beginning of Bava Metzia, 11a, the baraita derives that a thief's roof, courtyard, or enclosure can acquire stolen property for him from the verse 'if the theft shall surely be found.' Rashi explains the case of his courtyard as where the animal entered his property and he locked the gate before it. This implies that if the animal entered his domain by itself and he merely intended to acquire it as stolen property, he does not become liable through thought alone, even if the courtyard is guarded. For theft, intention alone does not make him a thief until he performs an act, such as locking the gate, even though with a found object a guarded courtyard can acquire for him through intention alone. Thus Shulchan Aruch says, 'if he brought it into his domain,' excluding one that entered by itself. The same law applies if it entered by itself and he then locked the gate, because locking is an act. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 88, similarly writes that even if a person is occupied with the intention of stealing, so long as he has done no act he is exempt. The Mishnah at the end of chapter HaMafkid likewise teaches that one who merely intends to send his hand against a deposit is not liable until he performs an act, such as tilting or lifting the barrel and taking from it. This proves that even though the deposit is already in his house and domain, his courtyard does not acquire it for purposes of liability for unavoidable accidents until he performs an act.
