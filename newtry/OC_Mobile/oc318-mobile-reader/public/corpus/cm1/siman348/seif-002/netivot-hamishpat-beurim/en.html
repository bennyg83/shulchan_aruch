One who steals money from a non Jew. See Shach, seif katan 2, who leaves the matter requiring study. In my view it can be resolved as follows. Even if theft from a non Jew is a d'oraisa prohibition, derived from "he shall calculate" or another verse as discussed in Bava Kamma 113, that does not automatically establish a d'oraisa mitzvah of restitution. Even regarding a Jew, had Torah prohibited theft but not written the positive command "he shall return the stolen item," we would have said that the prohibition was violated but no separate restitution command was created merely by the negative prohibition. For a Jew, Torah explicitly attaches robbery to the positive command of return. For a non Jew, even if a separate verse teaches that taking is prohibited, where do we find that the Torah's positive restitution command was extended to him? Restitution to a non Jew can therefore be required because of kiddush Hashem and rabbinic law, even though the initial taking is d'oraisa prohibited. This is why Rama in Even HaEzer carefully writes that the item need be returned because of kiddush Hashem. One cannot prove otherwise from Bekhorot 13, where the Gemara says that one returns overcharge to "your fellow" but not to a non Jew. If Torah had not excluded him from that verse, then the entire statutory framework, including restitution, could have applied to both Jew and non Jew. But once the verse itself is limited to Jews and the prohibition toward a non Jew is derived separately, the positive return obligation is not automatically imported. The separate verse proving prohibition contains no explicit mitzvah of return. One might still ask: even if he has no Torah duty to return the stolen object, it is not his property. How can a woman be betrothed with property that is not his? Magen Avraham, Orach Chaim siman 637, cites Sefer Yereim that even if theft from a non Jew were permitted, the object would not necessarily count as the thief's for a sukkah. Kiddushin is different. If use of the object is permitted to the woman on the Torah level, and the man is the one who provides her that benefit, she can be betrothed by the value of the benefit he supplied even if the physical object is not fully his property. Ran in the second chapter of Kiddushin writes similarly that if he betroths her with an exchange received for an item forbidden in benefit, the kiddushin can be valid where the exchange is permitted to him. The point is that he supplied her a lawful monetary benefit. This resembles one who says, "I will speak to the ruler on your behalf," where the value of the service can support kiddushin.
