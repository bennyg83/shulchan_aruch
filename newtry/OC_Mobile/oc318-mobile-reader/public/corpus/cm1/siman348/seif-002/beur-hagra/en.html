And he must pay. This is Rambam's wording, based on the verse, 'and he shall return, etc.' See the Bava Kamma passage cited above.
Whether, etc. See Bava Kamma 113b. Rambam, Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch indicate that robbing a non-Jew is prohibited by Torah law. This is unlike the gloss in Even HaEzer 28 attributed to Mahari Weil. Shach and Chelkat Mechokek correctly note that Mahari Weil did not say so. Although Rashi in Sanhedrin 57a mentions a view that the prohibition is rabbinic, the plain Gemara derives it from verses. Bava Metzia 87b states this explicitly. Even the view permitting retention of a non-Jew's property forbids it by Torah law where desecration of God's name results.
(Supplement) And he must pay, whether, etc. The Tosefta states: one who robs a non-Jew must return it to the non-Jew; robbery of a non-Jew is treated more severely than robbery of a Jew in certain respects. (End of supplement.)

And some say. Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot prohibit misleading him, since even deception of another's mind is forbidden in Chullin 94a. Rambam rules similarly, but in a comparable case requires that the party say, 'see, I rely upon you,' as in the case of Rav Kahana. Maggid Mishneh understands the conduct of the Sages there as exceptional piety, which explains Rama's omission. Tosafot and Rashba, citing Arukh, explain the Gemara's phrase as misleading the non-Jew. Tosafot adds that although one may benefit from a non-Jew's own mistake, one may not actively mislead him where he realizes the Jew is taking advantage and pretends not to know. Tur is more permissive. The variant reading attributed to Arukh explains that he gave the non-Jew an extra coin to please him and hasten his departure. See siman 410.
