The Maggid Mishneh writes that the law concerning misappropriation by a Jew is explained in many places, including Sanhedrin 57a, and the law concerning a gentile appears in Bava Kamma 113b. Robbing a gentile is prohibited, and the same applies to stealing from him. Rashi explains the Gemara there as a case where one tells an heir, 'I gave it to your deceased father,' where the gentile does not know whether he is lying. See simanim 359 and 231 and what is written there; those authorities ruled likewise.
dalet) The Rambam likewise writes in chapter 7 of Hilkhot Geneivah, law 8: it is prohibited to deceive a gentile in an accounting; rather, one must calculate precisely with him, as it says, 'he shall reckon with his purchaser.' If this applies even when the gentile is under his power, all the more so where he is not. This falls within the prohibition of acting unjustly. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this is explicit in a baraita in Bava Kamma in the name of Rabbi Akiva.
heh) The Rambam likewise writes in chapter 11 of Hilkhot Gezeilah, law 4, that a gentile's lost property is like his other loss and need not ordinarily be returned. He explains that if the gentile made the calculation and erred, the Jew must say, 'See that I am relying upon your calculation.' The Maggid Mishneh writes that the ruling follows Rav Kahana, who acted this way. See the beginning of siman 266. If one returns a gentile's lost property in order to sanctify Hashem's Name, so that they praise the Jewish people and know them to be trustworthy, this is praiseworthy. The same applies to returning an error the gentile made himself. I record this for future generations because I have seen many who grew wealthy from errors by which they deceived gentiles, but they did not prosper; their property was lost and they left no blessing after them. As Sefer Chasidim, siman 1074, writes, many who sanctified Hashem's Name and returned a substantial error made by a gentile became great, wealthy, and successful, and left their surplus to their children.
