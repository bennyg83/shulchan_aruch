Shimon must return it to him, for why should he enter, etc. Meaning, even if Shimon has some claim against the relative explaining why she took the property, since he himself does not know whether her claim is true, he had no right to enter another person's house and assist her in removing it. This applies even though there were no witnesses to the removal. This is implied by Shimon's answer cited by the Tur and Mechaber, "It is true that I removed, etc." The phrase "it is true" implies that without his own admission Reuven could not have established the fact through witnesses. The preceding law is different. There Shimon did not enter and assist in the theft; rather, Reuven sued him because the stolen item later came into his possession and he returned it to the thief. Although the Tur there implies that the factual incident also involved Shimon entering and assisting with removal, it gives other reasons to exempt him for that act: the thief had already lifted the item and could have removed it alone, and Shimon further claimed that he knew the thief's position was correct. See there and examine carefully.
Some say that the sender is liable, because here one cannot say that the sender assumed the agent would refuse to do what he requested on the principle, "the words of the master and the words of the student, whose words should one obey?" since the agent himself is not legally liable. Darkei Moshe cites Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Metzia in Maharam's name: if one tells another, "Acquire for me the ox in so-and-so's house, for it is mine," and it later turns out not to have been his and that he intended theft, the sender becomes responsible for the ox upon the agent's meshichah, because the agent did not know it was stolen property. Nimukei Yosef, in chapter Merubah, however, writes that the agent is liable, because there is no agency for a transgression even where the agent did not know that the act was prohibited.
