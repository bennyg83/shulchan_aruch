Some say the sender is liable. See Shach, seif katan 6, who challenges Darkei Moshe for citing Nimukei Yosef as though the messenger himself is liable, since Nimukei Yosef does not say so and, if the messenger acted unknowingly, there is no obvious basis for personal liability. In my view Darkei Moshe means that the messenger can become liable to the owner under the ordinary laws of custodians. If, for example, he received the animal with borrower liability and it died, he can be liable to the true owner, just as where a renter lends the rented cow onward and the second borrower can become liable to the owner. Nimukei Yosef indeed writes in that direction. Still, the matter requires study. In an ordinary rental the renter has acquired a valid use-right and can transfer that use-right to a borrower, so the second person's meshichah creates borrower liability. Here the thief never acquired the object. How can he create a valid borrower relationship in favor of another person, especially according to the view that custodial liability requires meshichah and that an unauthorized borrower does not become liable merely from taking until actual use? Shach also seeks to reject Tosafot's principle that where the agent acts inadvertently there can be agency for a transgression. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise adduces the case of a beit din agent who added one lash beyond the authorized count and was liable to exile despite acting inadvertently. In my view that is not a proof. Even where agency for a transgression exists, Torah's novelty is ordinarily to impose liability on the sender, not to exempt the agent from liability that would have existed had he acted on his own. Where the agent's own act independently creates liability, he can remain liable even though the sender is also charged. Shelichut yad differs because the act requires an acquisition. Once Torah makes the acquisition operate for the sender, there may be no separate acquisition left for the agent. Me'ilah is similar: once the sender commits me'ilah through the agent's act and the item thereby leaves hekdesh, the agent may no longer be consuming hekdesh property. Likewise, in slaughter or sale after theft, the special fourfold or fivefold liability belongs to the original thief rather than to a later agent. This explains why the beit din messenger can incur exile. If he had delivered the extra blow on his own, he would have been liable, and the rule of agency does not erase that independent liability. It would have been stronger to ask why the judges themselves are not liable to exile as senders. That too is not decisive, because judges are not liable to exile merely for an erroneous judicial ruling. For capital liability, however, it is clear that a sender is not executed merely because an unwitting agent performed a capital transgression. We do not find a rule that one who tells another to violate Shabbat unknowingly becomes liable to death through the other's act. The derivation that sometimes imposes liability on a sender through an agent who is not personally subject to the prohibition comes from theft-related cases and does not automatically extend from lashes to death. Tosafot in Shevuot imply that where the agent himself is not subject to the prohibition, even a non Jew can sometimes create liability for the sender. This does not mean that telling a non Jew to cook on Shabbat becomes a d'oraisa violation. That principle applies when the prohibited result occurs in the Jewish sender himself, such as a kohen arranging a prohibited marriage or causing a Jew's prohibited haircut. When the non Jew merely cooks on Shabbat, no prohibited act has occurred in the Jewish sender's body or property at that time. As for me'ilah and the case of Yoav, an unwitting agent is still considered a person subject to the law because inadvertence itself can require atonement. One who acts based on another's statement must still investigate where Torah expects him to do so. A woman who remarries based on one witness can be liable for a korban, and Yoav could not simply rely on David where Shaul's servants themselves refused a comparable order. By contrast, where a thief tells another person to take an item from the owner's domain, the recipient can accept it into custody in order to save it from theft. At the moment of taking he need not yet investigate in the same manner, because if he refuses the thief will simply take it himself. Receiving it to preserve it can itself be a mitzvah of returning lost property. Therefore Tosafot can say that the ordinary rule of "the Master's words precede the student's" does not defeat agency at the initial taking in that case.
