There is no agency for a transgression. Rashi holds that where one participant lifts stolen property both for himself and for his partner, the partner can acquire through the principle that since the lifter acquires for himself, he can also acquire for his partner. This applies even in the setting of wrongdoing. Tosafot appears to require that both partners physically lift. I find that difficult. Even when both lift, the reason the act works for each is still that each effective acquisition can extend to the other. If that principle can operate despite the transgression, there is no reason it should fail where one alone lifts for both. Rashi's treatment of slaughter by one partner for both differs because slaughter is not an acquisition act. The special liability for slaughter or sale requires the Torah's independent extension to an agent. The principle that "since he acquires for himself, he acquires for his fellow" applies to acquisitive acts such as lifting, purchase, theft, or robbery, not to a later slaughter that merely generates statutory liability. Therefore, where one partner performs the acquisition act of theft for both, Rashi's rule can make both liable through the shared acquisition. For the later slaughter, the separate special verse is needed.
The agent is not himself subject to liability. The Gemara discusses a thief who tells a custodian to remove an ox from the owner's domain. Rashi explains that the thief becomes liable through the custodian's meshichah even though ordinarily there is no agency for transgression. Tosafot answers that the custodian does not know the ox is stolen, so he resembles a courtyard that acquires involuntarily. Nimukei Yosef disagrees and holds that even an unaware agent does not create ordinary agency for transgression. The Shach supports Nimukei Yosef and argues that if Rashi meant actual agency, the same problem would have arisen in the Gemara's own analysis of the Mishnah. But the sugya can be explained differently. At an earlier stage, the Mishnah can be established where the thief himself stole from a custodian. Only later, under Rav Ashi's reading, does Rashi's explanation become necessary. Still, Rashi himself elsewhere clearly holds that there is no agency for transgression even when the agent did not know. In Kiddushin, if the principal remembers that the money is consecrated but the agent remains unaware, the agent commits misuse. Rashi explains that throughout the Torah there is no agency for transgression and the actor bears liability, even though the agent was ignorant. This directly supports Nimukei Yosef. How, then, can Rashi here make the thief liable through the custodian's meshichah? The answer follows Rashi's rule regarding partners who steal. Custodianship itself requires meshichah. When the unaware custodian pulls the ox, he acquires for himself the custodial status. Since that same act is a valid acquisition for himself, it can also acquire for the thief under the principle that one who acquires for himself can acquire for another. This is not ordinary agency for transgression. This also explains the Gemara's inference that custodianship requires meshichah. If custodians did not need meshichah, the custodian's act would not be an acquisition for himself, and the principle extending it to the thief would disappear. A further proof that ignorance does not create agency for transgression comes from the case of the beit din agent who mistakenly administers one extra lash and thereby causes death. The court's agent is himself exiled for the unintended killing. If ignorance made him merely an extension of the court, his own liability would not arise. Thus even an unaware agent ordinarily remains the actor for transgression.
