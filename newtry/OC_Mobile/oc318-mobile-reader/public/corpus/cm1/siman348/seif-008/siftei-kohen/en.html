One who shows another person where to steal, or sends him, etc. The authorities discuss one who sends another to steal, but not one who merely points out the place of the theft. The Rama combined the two because Darkei Moshe cites Terumat HaDeshen regarding Reuven, who showed a notorious thief where Levi's tunnel was so that he could steal from it. Reuven is exempt because this is indirect causation. This ruling is necessary even according to the view that agency applies where the agent is not legally liable, for the person who merely points out the property remains exempt as a remote cause. It is also necessary according to the view that the sender is always exempt because there is no agency for transgression. One might have thought that, as in the law of one who reveals another's property to a gentile, he is liable under the law of direct causation even without agency. Terumat HaDeshen therefore teaches that this case is only indirect causation and not legally direct damage.
Some say that the sender, etc. I do not know why the Rama presents this as merely 'some say,' since Darkei Moshe cites no dissent. Nevertheless, as I explained in siman 182, the derivation from Hagahot Maimoniyot and the Mordechai is not compelling. In my view there is no distinction between an agent who is legally liable and one who is not. There is never agency for transgression, except in the special case of a courtyard. Darkei Moshe and the Sema cite the Mordechai regarding one who said, 'Take for me the ox in that house, for it is mine,' when in truth he intended the agent to steal it. The sender is said to become liable when the innocent agent draws the ox into his possession. The Nimukei Yosef rules that the sender remains exempt even where the agent did not know he was committing a prohibition. The agent himself is plainly exempt where he was unaware, as Yam Shel Shlomo states. Maharshal attacks the Nimukei Yosef and compares the innocent agent to a courtyard. This comparison is not correct. The phrase 'not legally liable' refers to a person who is intrinsically outside the prohibition, whereas here the agent is a person subject to the prohibition and merely lacks knowledge. Likewise, the principle that the agent could choose whether to act refers to his objective ability to act or refrain, which exists here. Had the Gemara intended to distinguish inadvertence from intent, it would have said so explicitly. The Mishna concerning slaughter by another person does not establish liability of a sender through an innocent human agent. Most commentators explain that Mishna differently, and the Raavad does not adopt Rashi's interpretation. The case of David and Joab also supports the Nimukei Yosef. Joab acted under a mistake, believing Uriah liable as one who rebelled against the king, yet the Gemara still treats the question under the rule that there is no agency for transgression. Shammai the Elder holds David liable only because he maintains agency for transgression generally, or because Scripture specifically states, 'You killed him with the sword of the children of Ammon.' According to the Sages, David was exempt from earthly liability; the Gemara's need to describe Uriah as a rebel concerns punishment in Heaven, not legal agency. The same principle is evident from misuse of consecrated property, which is fundamentally inadvertent, yet the Gemara still requires a special verse to impose liability on the sender. The better conclusion is therefore that even where the agent is unaware, the sender is exempt because there is no agency for transgression, while the innocent agent is also exempt.
