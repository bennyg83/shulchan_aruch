Because he should have returned the lost object. It appears that Reuven can be liable even if the stolen object came into his hands only after the original owner's despair. Siman 341, seif 6, says that if stolen property is consumed by a second person after despair, that second person can be exempt. Why should returning it to the thief be worse than consuming it himself? The answer is that even the consumer's exemption is not absolute where an independent debt runs through the thief. If the second person still owes money to the thief, R. Natan's rule can make that obligation directly subject to the original owner's claim. Ran at the beginning of HaGozel U'Ma'akhil explains this. Therefore, when Reuven knew the object was stolen and a R. Natan lien attached, paying or returning the value to the thief does not necessarily discharge him vis-a-vis the original owner, just as Shach in siman 86, seif katan 14, explains that payment to the intermediate debtor may not help after the R. Natan lien has attached. A difficult case remains where despair occurred, the thief has other assets, but is a violent person from whom collection is practically impossible. Shach writes that the R. Natan lien applies principally through the rules of encumbered property. Yet with encumbered property, if the robber is presently violent, collection may be postponed on the principle that violent men are expected eventually to fall. Perhaps the reason Reuven is still liable is that while the stolen object was in his own hands there was as yet no unavoidable loss to him. He had a duty to return it to the true owner and nevertheless handed it back to the thief. That later voluntary act can make him liable even if collection from the thief was difficult.
