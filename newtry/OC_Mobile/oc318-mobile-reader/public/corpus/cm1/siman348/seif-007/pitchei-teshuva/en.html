The agent is liable to pay. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 212, discusses two thieves. One climbed a ladder, entered through a window, and stole from the house; the second stood in the street, received what the first threw into his lap, and fled. Chavot Yair writes that the second is the principal thief, and the victim may collect the entire theft from him even if he later returned the property to the first thief. If collection from the second is impossible, the victim may still sue the first, who physically committed the theft. He notes, however, the dispute in siman 410 seif 37 regarding two joint tortfeasors when one cannot pay, according to which the other may be liable for only half, and asks whether the same applies to two thieves. Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 178, disagrees with Chavot Yair's comparison. Two tortfeasors who did not act for their own benefit may not become guarantors for each other, but two people who steal jointly for their own benefit and act together to steal become responsible and guarantors for one another, like two borrowers who borrow jointly under siman 77. He therefore rules that if two stole together and one fled, the other must pay the whole amount. Chavot Yair further writes that if one thief paid the victim the full amount and then seeks half from his fellow thief, he cannot extract it from him, even if the entire stolen property came into the fellow's hands. Likewise, if neither was caught and the thief who received the stolen property refuses to share it with the other, the latter cannot recover a share in beit din because the money was never legally his. Even in the heavenly court he owes nothing to the fellow thief; his obligation is to the victim from whom it was stolen, unless they had created an enforceable partnership arrangement in writing. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 131, discusses a penitent who admitted that he and another person, driven by poverty, stole a sack of wool. After becoming wealthy he wished to repay half. His companion admitted the theft but had no assets, and the victim therefore demanded the entire value from the wealthy one. Chatam Sofer distinguishes the act by which the theft was legally acquired. If only one thief removed the sack from the owner's domain or lifted it in a manner by which he alone could carry it, then even if they had agreed beforehand to steal together and divided it afterward, there is no agency for a transgression and only the one who performed the legal act of theft is the thief. If both lifted or removed it together, both acquired it as partners, like two people holding a garment. This follows Tosafot and the other early authorities in Bava Metzia 8a, and Maggid Mishneh to Hilchot Geneivah 2:14, rather than Rashi's interpretation that one removed it for himself and his partner. If both acquired it jointly and immediately divided it at that moment, Chatam Sofer holds that they are not guarantors for one another and each pays only his share. If, however, one thief alone removed the property and then gave half to the other, the law is that of stolen property before despair where another person takes it from the thief: the victim may collect from whichever one he chooses. Thus if the insolvent thief removed the whole sack and gave half to the wealthy thief, the wealthy thief pays only the half he received. If the wealthy thief removed it and gave half to his companion, the victim may collect the whole amount from the wealthy thief. Chatam Sofer adds that a thief's duty to repay applies where the stolen object remained in his possession and he benefited from it, or where he retained its substitute value. If the wool was later lost, even through negligence, or perhaps even if the thief destroyed it directly, one who does not seek to fulfill his obligation in the heavenly court but relies strictly on the civil law may claim to follow Rambam's ruling concerning a burglar who entered premises in circumstances where his life was legally forfeit, in which case the rule of the greater criminal liability exempts him from monetary liability for subsequent destruction. This applies only where the facts fit Rambam's definition of such an entry. Chatam Sofer further rules that if both thieves jointly acquired the theft but before division the entire item remained for some time with one of them, that person became obligated to return the other's share to its owner as a finder of lost property. His own share remains subject only to the liability of a thief, with its special exemptions, while his fellow's share is held as a custodian of lost property and may therefore carry greater liability. If the thieves dispute the relevant facts, the wealthy one must at least pay his own half. The victim may impose a general ban concerning the second half if the wealthy thief is also liable for it. The insolvent thief is not treated as a single witness against his companion, because even if a person is not disqualified by his own confession and even if he must in any event pay either the victim or his fellow thief, he remains interested in the testimony. He prefers to owe his fellow thief, who is less likely to publicize his disgrace, rather than the victim, who may publicly brand him a thief. Chatam Sofer's responsum is extremely concise, and Pitchei Teshuva explains that his reference to Rambam concerns the laws of a burglar entering a house or courtyard in circumstances of possible mortal resistance, as discussed in Sanhedrin 72b and siman 425. Where the greater liability applies, strict civil law may exempt later monetary payment, though the thief remains obligated in the heavenly court. Pitchei Teshuva also explains that the victim's general ban on the second half addresses factual possibilities that would make the wealthy thief liable for the whole, such as where he alone removed the property or held the other thief's share long enough to become a custodian obligated to return it.
