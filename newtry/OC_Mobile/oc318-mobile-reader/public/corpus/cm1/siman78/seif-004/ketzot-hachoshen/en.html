Tosafot, Rosh, and Raavad hold that one who claims payment before the end of the due date is not believed through a migo that he could have said, "I paid today," because saying he paid today would be more audacious. I find this difficult because in monetary law we generally use even a migo from a less audacious plea to a more audacious one. A possessor of collateral seen only an hour ago can still claim he returned it in the intervening hour, even though that is a direct and immediate factual contradiction. Raavad gives a different reason, and his reason can be sustained. Even on the day the debt becomes due, most people do not pay until the end of the day. Reish Lakish says, "If only a person would pay at his proper time," meaning on the actual due date. Thus the presumption against prior payment still operates during the day of maturity until the debtor actually pleads, "I paid today." The important distinction is between an ordinary migo and "the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted." We do not use a migo against a known presumption that already exists independently before us. After the due date has passed, if the defendant himself says, "I paid during the term," then the only reason the court knows to apply the earlier presumption comes from his own statement. In that setting his own mouth created the adverse fact, and his alternative explanation can be heard. But on the actual due date the presumption exists independently before the court. We do not need his statement to know that most people do not pay yet. Therefore an ordinary migo cannot defeat it. This is the proper explanation of Raavad and the reason Rama follows his view.
