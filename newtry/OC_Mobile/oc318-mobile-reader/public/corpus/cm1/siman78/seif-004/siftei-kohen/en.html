This applies when he demanded it at the end of the day, etc. The Mechaber’s wording requires study. The Sema and Bach understand the Tur’s reference to the end of the day as relating only to the migo: only at the end of the due day can the borrower plausibly say that he paid that day and the lender forgot, whereas earlier such a claim is too brazen. I do not accept this. If it were considered a migo of brazenness, the same problem would remain even at day’s end. Tosafos and Hagahot Ashiri indeed imply that throughout the day one cannot support a claim of payment before maturity with a migo of payment that day. Rather, the Tur and Baal HaTerumot mean that even the claim of payment on the due day itself becomes accepted only at the end of that day. This agrees with Ramban’s understanding against the Raavad. Still, the Mechaber’s conclusion is not compelled, because it is more reasonable to read the references to the beginning and end of the day as excluding only the immediate opening moment, and to hold that throughout the due day a person may say that he paid that day. No other authority clearly distinguishes the middle from the end of the day, and this appears the principal ruling. As to the migo of having paid that day when he instead claims that he paid before maturity, from the beginning of the due day until its end the matter remains an unresolved question of law. Tosafos, Hagahot Ashiri, and Raavad reject the migo, while Ramban, Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Baal HaIttur accept it. Therefore the burden of proof rests on the party seeking to extract money.
But for his heirs we do not plead it. Meaning that we do not ordinarily plead for heirs that the deceased paid on the due day, even with an oral loan or rental obligation. Nevertheless, according to those who rule in siman 108 that we plead even an uncommon defense for heirs, the same should apply here. That is why the Nimukei Yosef cites this law specifically in the name of the Raavad. In my view the principal rule is that we plead every available defense for heirs, as I explain at the end of siman 361.
