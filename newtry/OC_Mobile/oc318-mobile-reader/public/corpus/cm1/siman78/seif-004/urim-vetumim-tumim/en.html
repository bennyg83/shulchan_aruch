And when he says at the end of the day, and so forth. As explained in Urim, Tosafot hold that if a debtor claims on the due date itself, "I paid earlier within the term," he is not believed through a migo that he could say, "I paid today." Although monetary law sometimes recognizes a migo involving a more brazen claim, this case already contains a migo against the chazakah that people do not pay within the term. That chazakah is not overwhelmingly strong, so an ordinary good migo may sometimes overcome it. But when the migo itself is weak or brazen, the combination is insufficient. I discuss this in the rules of migo and above in siman 108. Taz writes that Tosafot say this only at the initial stage of the sugya, before the Gemara concludes that a migo can overcome the chazakah. I cannot understand that. Tosafot's text explicitly speaks of one who comes on the due date, and their entire discussion depends on that circumstance. Once the conclusion allows a migo against the chazakah, the question becomes stronger: why is the debtor not believed to say he paid earlier through the migo of payment today? Tosafot's answer must be that on the due date itself the supposed migo is not a valid one of sufficient strength. Thus Tosafot's rule is necessary precisely according to the conclusion. There is still a difficulty from the Gemara's proof involving one who says one day, "Yes, I owe you," and on the next day says, "I paid." The Gemara distinguishes "I paid" from "I owe you nothing" and considers whether the latter means payment within the term. Why not establish the baraita on the due date? Then "I paid today" would be a strong claim, while "I paid earlier within the term" would fail because the alternative is a weak migo. The answer is that the baraita says, "The next day he said, give it to me," implying the creditor can already demand immediate payment. If the entire due date belongs to the debtor, the creditor may not yet be entitled to demand until the day ends. Thus the case naturally concerns after maturity. Baal HaTerumot appears to disagree and allows demand during the due date itself. According to that view, "after the time" can mean after dawn on the final date, and the migo analysis differs. Ramban and Or Zarua record a real dispute whether the creditor may demand during the final due date. Accordingly, Tosafot's conclusion is sound within their timing rule: on the due date itself, a claim of earlier within-term payment lacks a sufficiently strong migo and is not believed.
He is believed by virtue of a migo, etc. This question, whether we apply a migo against a chazakah, was not resolved there in Bava Batra. Accordingly, according to the view that possession is effective in an unresolved question, if the lender seized property, even in the presence of witnesses, and says that the borrower owes him on the basis that the borrower could have claimed, "I repaid you within the term," the property should not be removed from the lender's possession. For he can say, "I maintain that we do not apply a migo against a chazakah," in which case the property is taken from the borrower and given to the lender. Certainly, after seizure he can assert kim li. If so, the Mechaber, who maintains throughout that seizure is effective even after the doubt has arisen, should not have stated here without qualification that the borrower is believed. Rather, he should have ruled that this is a doubt in the law, with the practical consequence, as stated, that if the lender seized property, even in the presence of witnesses, it is not removed from him. The Rambam too requires explanation, for he entirely omitted from his work the unresolved question whether one who claims after the term, "I repaid you within the term," is believed. The Rif cites it and writes that it is an unresolved question. If the Rambam's omission is itself an indication that he regards the claimant as believed because he has a migo, the same difficulty arises, since according to his view seizure is effective in cases of doubt. He therefore should have cited the matter as a doubt, such that if the lender seized in the presence of witnesses it would not be removed from him. It is difficult to say that the Rambam understood the difficulty raised by Tosafot in Ketubot 19a concerning the dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages when their signatures are not otherwise authenticated. According to the Sages the witnesses are believed to say, "We were minors," whereas according to Rabbi Meir they are not believed, because there is a chazakah that a person does not sign unless he has become an adult. Tosafot ask that this implies that Rabbi Meir and the Sages dispute whether a migo is applied against a chazakah, while in Bava Batra the Gemara remains uncertain on this very point. One might suggest that the Rambam held that the entire discussion in Bava Batra follows Rabbi Meir. According to Rabbi Meir the question indeed remains unresolved, even though Rabbi Meir holds in the case of authentication that the witnesses are not believed. Authentication provides no proof, as Tosafot explain there in Ketubot, s.v. modeh bi'shtar, because one fears alleging forgery lest the document later be authenticated, or because by Torah law authentication is unnecessary. Thus a migo based on alleging forgery is a weak migo and is not applied against a chazakah. But according to the Sages, who hold that in the authentication case we do apply a migo against a chazakah, certainly an ordinary migo is effective. See what I wrote above in the rules of migo, that the chazakah that one does not sign unless he has reached adulthood is stronger than the chazakah that a person does not repay within the term. See there. Since we rule like the Sages, the unresolved question is thereby resolved, and therefore the Rambam states without qualification that the migo is effective, and even if the lender seized property it is removed from him. It is likewise difficult to say that the Rambam held that the entire discussion in Bava Batra follows the initial answer of Rava in Kiddushin, but according to Abaye's answer, Rabbi holds that "what concern has he with a falsehood" is equivalent to witnesses and uproots the chazakah, whereas Rabbi Natan holds that it is merely equivalent to a chazakah. Since we rule like Rabbi, the question is resolved because we rule in accordance with Abaye's answer. See the Rosh and the Ran, who sought to distinguish between this migo and the migo there, because there the matter is within his power to accomplish. Consequently, there is no proof from that case.
It appears more correct that the Rambam held that although the question was not resolved in Bava Batra, it was resolved in Bava Metzia 102b. There they asked Rabbi Yochanan about a renter who says, "I paid," while the landlord says, "I did not receive payment." Who is believed? The Gemara asks: When is this? If it is within the term, etc., for we learned: If the father died within thirty days, there is a chazakah that the firstborn son was not redeemed; after thirty days, there is a chazakah that he was redeemed. The Gemara answers that the question concerns the very day on which the term expires: does a person ordinarily pay on that day, etc.? The Gemara resolves it from the rule that a hired worker, when claiming his wages at the proper time, swears and collects. It is specifically a hired worker upon whom the Sages placed the oath because the employer is preoccupied with his workers, but ordinarily a person would not be believed in that manner. The Rambam found this difficult. Since we rule that the thirtieth day is like the days before it, the beginning of the thirty first day is the first time for redemption, and that very day is the day on which the obligation becomes due. This is especially so according to Rabbi Eliezer of Metz, who holds that the thirty days for redemption of the firstborn consist of twenty nine days, twelve hours, and seven hundred ninety three parts. It can therefore occur that the thirty days end in the middle of the day, and the hours thereafter are "after thirty days." This is literally a case of the day on which the obligation becomes due. How then can the Mishnah teach that if the father died after thirty days the son has the chazakah of having been redeemed? If the father died on the thirty first day, that is the very day payment becomes due, and if people do not ordinarily pay on that day, the father himself would not have been believed. Certainly we would not advance such a claim for the heirs, as I wrote above in the name of the Raavad. We must therefore conclude that a person does ordinarily pay on the due date, and that we advance that claim for the heirs. If one answers that redemption of the firstborn is different because a mitzvah is incumbent upon him and the zealous perform mitzvot promptly, creating a chazakah that he fulfilled the mitzvah, then the Gemara could not have asked, "After the term we already learned this," from the rule that after thirty days there is a chazakah that the son was redeemed, since that case would be different because it involves a mitzvah. A still greater difficulty arises from the Gemara's resolution from the law of a hired worker. The commentators have already protested that a day worker's time for payment is at night, and the rule that "at his proper time he swears and collects" refers to that night. How can the Gemara resolve our question from this? One cannot answer that the night is itself considered the day on which the term expires, because there it is obvious that an employer ordinarily pays during the night. Otherwise he would violate the prohibition against delaying wages, God forbid. Would faithful Jews ordinarily transgress a prohibition? But here, is it not normal for people to delay payment of rent for a day or two? What is stated in the Shitah Mekubetzet, that the prohibition against delaying wages applies here as well, is entirely unclear. Does one who does not pay rent immediately on the day it becomes due, but delays for a day or two, violate the prohibition against delaying wages? Such a proposition is neither heard nor reasonable. The Raavad answered, as cited in his name by the Ritva, that he was shown from Heaven that when the Mishnah states without qualification that a worker who claims at the proper time swears and collects, it means throughout the entire period of his proper time, even if he claims immediately before sunset, when there is no time remaining to pay unless the employer had already paid him earlier in the day. Nevertheless, when the employer makes that claim the worker must swear. This proves that people ordinarily pay on the day the obligation becomes due. Yet it is difficult. Why do we need the case of a hired worker? We can resolve the matter from the firstborn redemption case itself. If the father died after thirty days, there is a chazakah that he paid, even if he died immediately after sunset at the end of the thirtieth day, for the Mishnah states the rule without qualification. Necessarily, he could have paid before sunset on the thirtieth day. This proves that people pay at the end of the day on which the obligation becomes due. What difference is there between the case of "after thirty days" and the case of a hired worker claiming "at his proper time"? Therefore, the Rambam understood that the doubt was never whether one who claims, "I paid on the day the obligation became due," is believed, for that is already demonstrated by the Mishnah. Rather, the doubt concerns one who claims, "I paid you before the term." The question is thus whether it is common for a person to pay on the day the obligation becomes due, in which case the claimant has a migo. According to Tosafot, one cannot say that this is a brazen claim, because in comparison it is more comfortable for him to make a brazen claim that is common and is not regarded by ordinary people as a lie than to make an uncommon claim that he paid before the term, when people ordinarily do not do so. Or perhaps people do not ordinarily pay on the day the obligation becomes due, in which case one can indeed say, as Tosafot do, that the proposed migo is a migo involving brazenness, since the alternative claim is itself uncommon, just as the claim that he paid before the term is uncommon. This also accords with what I wrote above in seif katan 11. Otherwise, what novelty does the latter clause teach, as the Gemara asks here in Bava Batra, "After the term, is it not obvious?" Would we ever say that a debtor cannot claim that he paid? Similarly, one must understand why the Gemara says, "If it is after the term, we already learned this." Why does it need a Mishnah? It is obvious that with an oral loan the debtor is believed to say, "I paid." According to my explanation of the Rambam, however, it is clear. The Gemara resolves the question from the rule that a hired worker at the proper time swears and collects. This rule applies even if he brings his claim immediately before sunset, when the employer has no opportunity to say that he paid him a considerable time earlier, but can only say, "I paid you now, at this very moment, in the course of this claim," as the Raavad explains. Why then should the worker swear and collect? Can we say that the employer is preoccupied with his workers with respect to money that he asserts he handed over at this very moment, while the claim is being litigated? Nor can one say that the Rabbis established a uniform rule, just as they did throughout the night, such that even if the employer says, "I paid you at this very moment," the worker nevertheless swears because the Rabbis did not distinguish. That explanation works for the night, because it is possible for the employer to say, "I paid you earlier." The Rabbis therefore established a uniform rule that the worker always swears. Moreover, otherwise the rabbinic enactment would be nullified, because if the employer said, "I paid you earlier," he would have a migo that he could have claimed, "I paid you now, at this very moment," and the Sages did not institute the worker's oath against a migo. Therefore the worker always swears. But in the brief period when it is impossible that the employer paid him earlier, how could the Mishnah state without qualification that throughout the entire period of payment the worker swears and collects? There is then a situation in which he should not swear. This is exactly the Raavad's difficulty. We must therefore conclude that even then the employer can say, "I paid you earlier, while it was still broad daylight." Although that would have been before the term, he has a migo that he could have said, "I paid you now," notwithstanding that this is a migo involving brazenness. The Rabbis therefore still did not distinguish, and for that reason stated without qualification that the worker always swears and collects. Yet the instant immediately adjacent to sunset is virtually the day on which the obligation becomes due, and nevertheless the employer is believed by virtue of a migo. It follows that the Gemara has indeed resolved our question. The Gemara's discussion is therefore sound, the unresolved question is resolved, and this requires further study.
That I repaid you today. It appears that where a person borrowed in the presence of witnesses and the lender told him, "Do not repay me except in the presence of witnesses," and on the day the debt becomes due the borrower says, "I repaid you before the term in the presence of witnesses, but they went overseas or died," he is not believed. What migo does he have? He might claim, "I repaid you today in the presence of so and so and so and so, and they went overseas," but on that very day the witnesses cannot have grown wings like eagles and traveled so far that it is impossible to pursue them and clarify the matter. All the more so if he says that they died, for then their deaths would have had to occur suddenly. Certainly he would not have repaid in the presence of people who were already near death and whose lives in this world were plainly ending. All of this is practically impossible. He therefore cannot sufficiently distance his account by saying that he repaid earlier and that in the meantime the witnesses departed or died. He consequently has no migo. This is clear and correct. On this basis the words of Tosafot in the above sugya, s.v. i lema bizmano peshita, can be understood. Tosafot ask why the ruling is obvious. Since a person does not ordinarily pay before the term, and we are witnesses, so to speak, that this person built the wall, the Mishnah needed to teach that one who lends to another in the presence of witnesses need not repay him in the presence of witnesses. Tosafot answer by distinguishing that when the lender specifically designated witnesses for repayment, we see that he did not trust the borrower. Maharshal had difficulty with this. From where is this distinction derived? Even if we assume that a person does repay before the term, it would still follow that one who borrows in the presence of witnesses need not repay in the presence of witnesses. Maharshal answers that if the borrower could claim that he paid before the term, we would say that he never built the wall from his own funds. See there. This is strained. What connection is there between that point and repayment before the term? Clearly one may say that both of them built the wall, with the wall presumed to belong to both of them. Rather, the entire discussion here must follow the Ramban's explanation, as I wrote above at length, that it is known that this person built the wall from his own funds and thereby enclosed his fellow's property. If so, Tosafot maintain the rule that one who lends to another in the presence of witnesses, etc. It is difficult to say that this is Tosafot's intent, namely that because the Gemara compares the case of the Mishnah to repayment before the term, it necessarily concerns a situation in which we are effectively witnesses that one party built the wall from his own funds, as the Ramban writes, for otherwise there would be no question of repayment at all, since we would say that both parties built it. If so, this is a case of one who lends to another, etc. That is not the implication of Tosafot, and certainly not of Maharshal. See there. According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. In Shevuot 41a, according to the opinion that a borrower must repay in the presence of witnesses, the Gemara challenges from the Mishnah that explicitly teaches: If one says, "Give it to me," and the other says, "I gave it to you," he is exempt. Tosafot explain that according to the opinion that the borrower can say, "I repaid you in the presence of witnesses, and they went overseas," one may say that the Mishnah concerns precisely such a case. But according to the opinion that even then he is not believed, the Mishnah is a refutation. The Gemara answers by distinguishing between an ordinary loan and an admission made in the presence of witnesses, and between a situation in which the lender trusted him and one in which he did not trust him. According to this, Tosafot's initial assumption was that they had not yet considered the distinction between a case in which he trusted him and one in which he did not. This is because the opinion that one need not repay in the presence of witnesses rejects that distinction entirely, and therefore holds that repayment in the presence of witnesses is unnecessary, in accordance with the plain implication of the Mishnah in Shevuot and the Gemara's challenge to Abaye and Rava. They hold that repayment in the presence of witnesses is unnecessary, as stated in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in. This is Tosafot's question. Otherwise, why would the Gemara have raised the question at all, when it explicitly distinguishes there between a case in which the lender trusted him and one in which he did not? Tosafot already wrote this in the preceding passage. Rather, in their question they assumed that according to the opinion that repayment in the presence of witnesses is unnecessary, there is no such distinction. Tosafot then answer that the Gemara regards this distinction as logically compelling and applies it according to all opinions. Yet if so, what was Tosafot's question that perhaps the Mishnah teaches that one need not repay in the presence of witnesses? We already learn from the Mishnah in Shevuot that one who says, "I gave it to you," is exempt. We must say that the Mishnah there would not suffice, because perhaps the claimant said, "I repaid him in the presence of witnesses, and they have gone away." But if so, our Mishnah likewise proves nothing, because perhaps here too he claims that he repaid in the presence of witnesses who then went away or died. For this reason Tosafot wrote in their question that since a person does not ordinarily pay before the term, let the Mishnah be established as a case where the borrower claims that he paid at the proper time, meaning exactly on the day the obligation became due. It would then teach a substantial novelty, that one need not repay in the presence of witnesses. We could not explain that he claims to have repaid in the presence of witnesses who then went away or died, because on that same day such a thing is virtually impossible, as I wrote. This is especially true according to the Tur's view, adopted by the Shulchan Aruch, that the relevant time is the end of the day, and not the middle of the day. How could the witnesses have gone away or died within a single hour? That is impossible. Even if he claims that he repaid in the middle of the period, he likewise has no migo for this reason. It would therefore be proven that repayment need not be made in the presence of witnesses. Tosafot's question is thus entirely sound. In this way I also explained in my novellae the sequence of Tosafot, s.v. u'va, etc. They first write that one cannot prove that Reish Lakish holds that repayment in the presence of witnesses is unnecessary, because there is a distinction between a loan and an admission. They then ask: Nevertheless, let us resolve from here that we do not say, etc. What connection does that question have to whether the document is earlier? According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. Tosafot did not need to distinguish between a loan and an admission, since one could simply say that the borrower claimed, "I repaid you in the presence of witnesses, and they have gone away." But according to Tosafot's textual version, which reads, "and he came at the proper time," meaning on the very day the obligation became due, this explanation is unavailable, as I wrote. According to the Ramban's version, however, which does not read, "and he came at the proper time," when he comes at the proper time he truly does have a migo. It can then readily be said that he claims, "I repaid you yesterday or the day before, and the witnesses have gone away." This is Tosafot's intent when they ask: Nevertheless, let us resolve from here, etc. We must therefore say that their text did not read, "and he came at the proper time," so the case can concern one who claims that he repaid in the presence of witnesses who then went away. Tosafot then write, "One may answer that a migo does not apply," etc., and restore the reading that he came on the day the obligation became due. If so, it cannot be said that he repaid in the presence of witnesses who then went away, and we must instead distinguish between a loan and an admission. This is precise.
We do not advance this claim for the heirs. See the Shach, in accordance with what I wrote in Urim. There is a difficulty according to the Raavad's view. What does the Gemara mean when it says, "If it is before the term, we already learned this, for if the father died within thirty days the son has the chazakah of not having been redeemed"? What is the difficulty? Perhaps it is certainly uncommon for people to pay before the term, yet if the person himself makes that claim he may nevertheless be believed, whereas for heirs we certainly do not advance such a claim. Indeed, even concerning the day on which the term expires we do not advance it for them. The case in which the father died concerns a claim advanced for heirs, and therefore we say that within thirty days the son has the chazakah of not having been redeemed. By contrast, where the renter himself expressly says, "I paid the rent," perhaps he is believed, just as on the day the term expires. We must answer as I wrote above in seif katan 1. When the Raavad holds that we do not advance such a claim for heirs, that applies where the claimant on the other side asserts with certainty that he was not paid. In general, according to the opinion that a certain claim prevails over an uncertain claim, the certain claim would prevail in any event, except that we rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. But with respect to an uncommon matter, we do not advance the claim for heirs. If, however, the claimant too asserts only uncertainty, then we advance the claim even for heirs, for why should one uncertain claim be stronger than the other? The burden of proof rests on the claimant. Therefore, in the redemption case, where even the kohen asserts only uncertainty, as I wrote above in seif katan 1, since who knows to which kohen the money may have been given, the matter is treated as though the father were alive and making a certain claim. The Gemara therefore properly compares the two cases, and all is well.
However, according to this, there is no proof for what the Shach writes on the basis that later we say that we advance for heirs even an uncommon claim. There too the claimant himself asserts only uncertainty. We advance for the heirs the claim that the property was lost through an unavoidable accident, since how can the claimant know that this is false? Here, however, the claimant says with certainty, "I was not paid," and perhaps in that situation we do not advance the claim for the heirs. It is possible to say that the Raavad derived his rule from this very Mishnah, since we have not found another proof for the Raavad concerning this law. As for the expression, "If only a person would pay at the proper time," the commentators already wrote that this is merely an allusion and supporting indication, not something upon which to establish a legal rule. Rather, it appears that the Raavad held as the Yam Shel Shlomo writes in the chapter HaZeroa, that money having no specific claimant, if a kohen claims it, the possessor must give it to that kohen or deposit it with beit din, and God forbid that he should brazenly retain it. See there. If so, why does the Mishnah teach specifically, "If the father died within thirty days, there is a chazakah that the son was not redeemed"? It could have taught a greater novelty: even if the father is alive and says that he gave the money to a particular kohen, and that kohen denies it, the father is not believed and beit din compels him to redeem his son, as the Yam Shel Shlomo writes, since he is claiming payment before the term. If one answers that the father would be believed even before the term by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "I gave it to a kohen at the ends of the earth," or the like, just as one who claims repayment without witnesses is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "I repaid in the presence of so and so, and they went overseas or died," then because he asserts with certainty and no one contradicts him, perhaps he would be believed. But if so, this would resolve the question whether we apply a migo against a chazakah. Moreover, the point itself requires study. Why should the absence of a person contradicting him matter? Since there is a chazakah that a person does not pay before the term, we ourselves say that he speaks falsely. See what I wrote above in seif katan 1, that where an uncertain claim has not been weakened by the fact that the claimant should have known, we apply a chazakah, as explained there. Nevertheless, he still has a migo. In truth, beit din and the kohen would not engage him at all within the first thirty days and tell him to redeem his son, because the time has not yet arrived. The case is therefore properly one in which the father died within thirty days, and after thirty days the kohen claims from the heirs. They say, "Perhaps our father paid." We then say that since he died within thirty days he certainly did not pay. By contrast, while the father is alive, the demand is made only after thirty days, since before thirty days there is no claim. If he then says that he paid within the first thirty days, he has a migo that he could have said, "I paid just now." At any rate, the difficulty remains that this should resolve the question in favor of applying a migo against a chazakah. Therefore it appears to me that the latter clause, "after thirty days there is a chazakah that he paid," concerns the very day on which the term expires, as the Shitah Mekubetzet explicitly writes in the name of Ramach. See there, and see what I wrote above in seif katan 9. If so, the Mishnah teaches a substantial novelty. It is obvious that while the father is alive he is believed to say, "I paid on the day the obligation became due." The Mishnah teaches that even heirs, who ordinarily would not be believed according to the Raavad, are believed in this case, as I wrote, because the claimant too does not assert certainty. This is indeed a substantial novelty. The Mishnah therefore states, "the father died," for the sake of the latter clause. The Raavad's position is thus compelling, namely that one must distinguish between the father and the heirs. Otherwise, the above difficulty remains. I later saw that the Raavad apparently had a proof from the Rif's textual version. According to what the Maggid Mishneh writes in Hilchot Sechirut 7:3, the Rif's version of the Gemara contained a separate baraita concerning this very law, rather than the Mishnah in Bekhorot. Its wording was: "Throughout the thirty days, there is a chazakah that he did not pay, unless he brings proof that he paid. After thirty days, there is a chazakah that he paid, unless proof is brought that he did not pay." This baraita concerns rent due after thirty days. The Gidulei Terumah asks, and leaves the matter requiring study: the thirtieth day is the day on which the obligation becomes due, and one is believed to say that he paid on that day, as the Gemara resolves from the rule that a worker at the proper time swears and collects. How then can the baraita say that throughout all thirty days there is a chazakah that he did not pay, and only after thirty days a chazakah that he paid? The thirtieth day itself should also carry a chazakah that he paid. This is indeed a great difficulty. The Raavad therefore understood from the use of the expression "there is a chazakah," rather than "he is believed" or "he is not believed," that the baraita concerns heirs. Accordingly, even on the thirtieth day there is a chazakah that payment was not made, because we do not advance this claim for heirs. If one asks that the earlier difficulty then returns, namely, what does the Gemara mean by saying, "If it is within thirty days, we already learned this," since for heirs we certainly do not advance an uncommon claim, whereas the question concerns the person himself who makes a certain claim that he paid, one may answer that the Raavad understood the Gemara as I will write in the next seif katan. The Gemara certainly held that this question follows Reish Lakish, in accordance with the accepted law that a person does not pay before the term even in a case involving heirs. The only doubt was whether rental should be treated like a loan with an expressly fixed term, or like an ordinary loan, whose usual term is thirty days. The Gemara therefore properly resolves: We already learned that throughout thirty days there is a chazakah that he did not pay. If rent were comparable to an ordinary loan, we would advance the claim of payment even for heirs, as is well known. It follows that rent is comparable to a loan with a fixed term, and if so it is obvious that the renter is not believed. The Gemara therefore explains that its uncertainty concerns only the day on which the obligation becomes due. All is then correct. According to this textual version, the Raavad's words are compelling. From this it also appears possible to explain, as I wrote above in seif katan 9, that according to the Rambam the question whether a migo is applied against a chazakah was resolved in a different manner. The Rambam was troubled by the Gidulei Terumah's difficulty, but did not accept the Raavad's explanation. He therefore held that there is an additional difficulty. The statement, "after thirty days there is a chazakah that he paid," appears obvious, just as the Gemara asks in Bava Batra. We must therefore explain it as the Gemara does there, that the claimant says, "I paid you before the term." Thus, "throughout the thirty days there is a chazakah that he did not pay" means that he has no migo, even on the day the obligation becomes due, in accordance with Tosafot's view that the migo is nevertheless unavailable. But after thirty days there is a chazakah that he paid during the thirty days, because he has a migo that he could have said, "I paid you now." This is correct, and the question is thereby resolved. In Bava Batra, before the Gemara resolved from the case of the hired worker that a person ordinarily pays on the day the obligation becomes due, it was proper to remain uncertain whether a migo is applied against a chazakah. The baraita could have been understood literally, teaching specifically that after thirty days there is a chazakah of payment, but not that one is believed to say, "I paid you on the thirtieth day." Once Bava Metzia resolves from the hired worker that a person does pay on the day the obligation becomes due, the Gidulei Terumah's difficulty arises. We must then explain the baraita as I have written, and it follows that a migo is indeed applied. This is simple and correct. It is almost more likely to be the true explanation than what I wrote above in seif katan 9, except that there is a slight difficulty: if so, the Rambam should still have written that on the day the obligation becomes due there is no migo and the claimant is not believed to say that he paid before the term, in accordance with Tosafot's sugya. Consider this carefully.
