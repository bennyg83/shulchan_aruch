He is not believed, because there is a presumption. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 136, who is uncertain whether the presumption that a person does not repay before the due date operates in a case of two sets of witnesses contradicting one another regarding payment. Perhaps one cannot say, “Place two witnesses against two witnesses and then rely upon the presumption,” because this particular presumption is itself of the nature of an evidentiary assessment, almost as though “we are witnesses,” and is therefore no stronger than an additional set of witnesses supporting those who say there was no payment. In such a conflict, two are treated like one hundred. This differs from an ordinary status presumption, which is not itself a factual proof. Rabbi Akiva Eiger leaves the matter requiring study. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 67, states this reasoning more decisively. He was asked about one who granted his creditor ordinary contractual credibility and then two witnesses testified that he paid before the due date. The questioner argued that ordinary credibility is treated by the Sema as equivalent to two witnesses. Therefore, with two opposing witnesses, the testimonies cancel, and ordinarily the property would remain with its possessor. But perhaps when the alleged payment was before maturity, the presumption that a person does not repay before maturity should permit collection despite the evidentiary balance. Chatam Sofer rejects this. The presumption is a customary evidentiary presumption, effectively an assessment that people do not generally repay before maturity. It may outweigh the mere presumption of possession, but contractual credibility simply adds evidentiary force on the creditor’s side. Whether there are two or a hundred, once two witnesses directly testify that payment occurred before maturity, the evidentiary forces are balanced, and the ordinary presumption of possession remains. He adds that the broader assumption that a prior presumption always survives two conflicting sets of witnesses is itself not universally correct, because testimony before us can at least weaken the prior presumption.
The presumption. See Responsa Shemen Rokeach, part I, siman 77. Reuven claimed a debt from Shimon before its due date. Shimon answered, “I paid you little by little so that you would not later press me to pay the entire debt at once.” One authority ruled that the presumption that a person does not pay before maturity does not apply where gradual advance payments are advantageous to the debtor, and some scholars challenged him. Shemen Rokeach cites Be’er Yaakov in the name of Brit Shalom, who explicitly rules that if a debtor claims, “I paid you before maturity one coin at a time,” the ordinary presumption does not apply. Be’er Yaakov disputes that ruling and seeks proof from Bava Batra 5b that no such distinction exists. Shemen Rokeach responds that the Gemara does not conclusively refute the distinction. He suggests instead that the issue may depend upon the dispute whether beit din advances on behalf of heirs even an uncommon claim. If an uncommon claim is nevertheless advanced for heirs, then this suggested exception to the presumption is difficult, because one would have to advance for the heirs the possibility of incremental advance payments, which would undermine collection. The Yerushalmi, however, indicates that an extraordinarily uncommon claim is not advanced for heirs. One must therefore distinguish between ordinary uncommon possibilities and a possibility so remote that all agree it is not asserted for heirs. On that basis, the ruling of Brit Shalom can be maintained: repayment little by little before maturity is sufficiently uncommon, especially since the creditor would not normally agree to receive it, that beit din does not raise it for heirs.
Money held as a deposit. See Baer Heitev and Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 144. One rabbi wished to argue that where a loan was structured under a heter iska, the debtor is believed to say that he repaid before maturity because he may have wished to avoid further profit payments. Chatam Sofer rejects this as a general rule and writes that even according to Abaye and Rava the debtor would not be believed merely for that reason. He concludes, however, that this is the strict law. If, in the judge’s assessment, the investor fixed the term primarily for the recipient’s benefit, and would in fact have been pleased to receive early repayment, the judge may perhaps rule on the basis of a compelling factual assessment and believe the debtor. Such a ruling requires a competent, God-fearing judge and a clear, demonstrable basis. His words plainly follow the view of Shevut Yaakov that an early payer under a heter iska does not necessarily have a unilateral right to terminate the arrangement. According to Tumim and Shaarei De’ah, however, who hold that the recipient may repay principal plus accrued profits before maturity, the first rabbi’s reasoning is sound. According to Netivot HaMishpat, who requires an accounting and oath, the debtor may still not be believed. The matter requires study. See also Responsa Maharit, Choshen Mishpat siman 124, Knesset HaGedolah, and Atarat Tzvi on this matter.
