Seif 1: One who fixes a time for his fellow, etc. See Shach, seif katan 1, who challenges the Sema. In my humble opinion his words are difficult. The Sema is certainly correct that where the lender himself is uncertain, the borrower is believed even if he claims to have paid within the fixed time. Since the presumption of possession supports the borrower, the combination of a definite claim and an uncertain claim favors the definite claim. We find similarly in the first chapter of Ketubot concerning the claim “I was violated after betrothal”: where a bodily presumption supports the definite claimant against the other party’s uncertain claim, the definite claim prevails. Although a presumption of monetary possession is ordinarily stronger than a bodily presumption, when the latter is joined to a definite claim it can prevail. The same applies here. Although the presumption that a person does not pay within the time is stronger than ordinary monetary possession, when monetary possession is joined to a definite claim against an uncertain claim, it prevails. The Shach’s proof from heirs is also difficult. With respect, he overlooked the Gemara, the Rosh, and the early authorities at the beginning of the second chapter of Ketubot. The Gemara asks whether the Mishna is not in accordance with Rabban Gamliel and answers that even Rabban Gamliel may agree, because he rules only where one side is definite and the other uncertain, whereas here it is definite against definite. The commentators ask that this explains a divorced woman, but if she was widowed the heirs are uncertain. They answer that since we plead on behalf of heirs whatever their father could have pleaded, their plea is regarded as a definite plea. The Rosh says this expressly, and the Shita Mekubetzet cites all the early authorities accordingly. Thus, even where a definite claim would normally prevail against uncertainty, the heirs’ pleading is considered definite. Consequently, with heirs the presumption that a person does not pay within the time certainly applies, whereas it need not apply where the debtor himself admits uncertainty. The Sema is therefore correct. However, in my Chiddushei Mishpat Tzedek on Choshen Mishpat, siman 59, I concluded that where the debt is evidenced by a document and the claim is payment within the time, two presumptions support the lender: the presumption expressed by “why is your document in my hand?” and the presumption that a person does not ordinarily pay before the due date. The debtor’s monetary possession is then only one presumption against two and has no force. In such a case we no longer say that the definite claim prevails, and the lender collects even where he is uncertain. This is clear and correct as practical halacha. There, seif 1: and he left heirs, even minors. See Shach, seif katan 2, who cites Maharshal’s doubt where the original due date arrived and the lender graciously extended the term, whether during the second term we still apply the presumption that a person does not pay before the due date. Ketzot HaChoshen resolves the issue from the Nimukei Yosef, who speaks of a time fixed at the time of the loan. In my opinion that proof is not decisive. The Nimukei Yosef can mean a case where originally no time was fixed at all. The lender made an ordinary loan without discussing a term, and only later when they met they fixed a due date. Since there had been no earlier term, and loans ordinarily contemplate a time for payment, the later fixing of the time reveals the original arrangement and is considered as though the term had been fixed at the loan’s inception. All the more so if at the time of the loan they expressly agreed that the loan would run until a time they would later fix when convenient. That too is effectively a term fixed at the loan’s inception. But where they originally fixed one due date and later agreed on a second due date, it seems to me that Maharshal’s doubt remains. Nevertheless, a proof may be brought from the Gemara. It asks, “If the case is at the due date, that is obvious; rather, is it not within the due date?” Why not answer that the case concerns a second fixed period, teaching that even within the second period the borrower is believed to say he already paid? It follows that there is no distinction between the first and second fixed terms. Examine this carefully.
