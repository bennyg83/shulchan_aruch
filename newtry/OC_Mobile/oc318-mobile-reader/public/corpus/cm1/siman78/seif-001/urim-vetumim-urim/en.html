And he demanded payment within the due date, etc. This applies specifically where the claimant asserts with certainty that payment was not made. If the claimant himself is uncertain, the presumption that a person does not ordinarily pay before a debt is due does not help him. Sema. See Tumim. This limitation applies where the claimant personally lacks knowledge. If heirs bring the claim, since they naturally cannot know whether payment occurred, then where the obligation is one that might never have matured at all, there is room to say that according to all opinions we rely upon the presumption that a person does not pay before the due date. Where the obligation was certainly due to arise, the Mordechai is uncertain.
The same applies that he cannot say, "You waived it." Waiver is an intrinsically weak defense and is believed only through a migo that the debtor could have claimed payment. Since he is not believed to claim payment within the due date, he is likewise not believed to claim waiver.
From orphans. Meaning minor orphans. This is the agreement of most authorities, and a defendant cannot claim conclusively that the law follows those who hold that money may not be collected from minor orphans in this case. If the due date arrived during the debtor's lifetime but the lender graciously extended the deadline, collection is likewise made from minor orphans, because the debt remains within the extended term. Shach, against Rashdam, and Knesset HaGedolah rules likewise.
And testimony was received, etc. Ordinarily testimony may not be received against minors because that is equivalent to receiving testimony outside the presence of a litigant. A document, however, may be authenticated even outside the litigant's presence. See what I wrote below in siman 110 at length.
Provided that there are witnesses, etc. Even if the original loan was not made before witnesses, it is enough that the debtor later admitted before witnesses or that his liability was otherwise established.
For otherwise he is believed by virtue of a migo. See Tumim, seif katan 2. I concluded that we do not accept a migo that he could have claimed, "I had a condition with you that I should be believed to assert payment even within the due date," although the Mordechai may imply otherwise. See there.
He is established as one who falsely denied. Why would he pay a second time? One might say that he knows the law that his first claim will not be believed and therefore paid again, or perhaps he did not dare deny the debt in beit din because he fears the authority of beit din. The Shach writes that this requires study, and I agreed in Tumim, seif katan 3, at length that the matter is a doubt in law. See there.
And he deals with the money for the benefit of others. Certainly if the money is a pure deposit from which he derives no use, he may plausibly claim that he returned it before the agreed date. Even if he invests it, so long as he receives no personal benefit and all profits belong to the owner, he may likewise have returned it early. But if he personally receives part of the profits of an iska, even though he gives half the profit to the investor, he ordinarily is not believed to say that he returned the capital before the term. That is true of an ordinary iska where no fixed profit payment is guaranteed, so if there is profit he shares it and if there is none he pays no profit. What reason would he have to return the capital early, when future profit might still arise and if not he owes the investor nothing beyond the capital? In the iska documents customary in our time, however, although they formally state that the recipient may establish the amount of profit through an oath, they contain so many conditions, monthly accounting requirements, and similar provisions that in practice he is virtually compelled to pay the stipulated amount of profit regardless of actual gain. In such a structure one can say that he returned the capital early in order to free himself from the continuing burden of stipulated profit, because even without actual profit he would otherwise have to pay. See Responsa Maharit, Choshen Mishpat 124, who ruled that where a person is definitely required to provide a return, such as payments to hekdesh and similar obligations, even if the amount is small he is believed to say that he paid before the due date. It therefore requires study whether our customary iska documents resemble Maharit's case.
It is like any other deposit. Rabbi Aharon Sasson, siman 74, writes that if a repayment date was fixed but the debtor was to repay specifically in merchandise such as lead, one can plausibly say that the merchandise became available before the date in separate quantities and he repaid it piece by piece rather than waiting for the fixed date, lest the merchandise not be available when the deadline arrived. His reasoning is sound. The Shach, seif katan 10, questions it, but his objections are not compelling.
And wages. The same applies to redemption of the firstborn.
Each booklet. The Sema seeks to distinguish between a booklet and an individual page. There is no basis for such strained distinctions. The term "booklet" is simply a French term for a written unit; any written portion may be called a booklet.
Its time is immediately. See Tumim. It appears that this itself is disputed among the authorities, and the party in possession may claim that even here a person does not normally pay before the entire work is due.
The same applies to any divisible work, etc. If a scribe writes a Sefer Torah and completes a single sheet, it does not resemble a completed booklet. A booklet is independently fit for Torah study, so payment for that unit may be considered due. A single sheet of a Sefer Torah is not independently usable, because we rule that one may not write isolated passages for a child to study from, so there is no reason for the client to take or pay for it separately before completion. Once an entire chumash is completed, however, that is a distinct usable unit because separate chumashim are permitted, and it resembles divisible work.
Divisible work. The straightforward meaning is that for every completed booklet the worker may claim payment was already made, but not for a booklet that has not yet been completed. The Shach explains more broadly that he may even claim payment for an unfinished portion, because the employer may have paid in advance to avoid being bothered for payment each time another booklet was completed. See Tumim. I agreed that Rabbeinu Tam's view supports the Shach, although Tosafot's wording raised a difficulty for me.
