One who fixes a time for his fellow, etc. This applies specifically where an explicit time was fixed, revealing that the borrower needed the money until that time.
It does not apply to an ordinary loan with no stated term.And he demanded payment within the term, etc. This is where the claimant states with certainty that it remains unpaid, not where he is uncertain.Even from orphans, etc. We do not suspect that their father deposited funds with the creditor in advance, because while the term has not arrived and the creditor cannot press him, people do not ordinarily set aside such funds.If it was a documented loan.
Since the Tur and Mechaber continue, 'or even an oral loan where the testimony was received during their father's lifetime,' it follows that with a document the witnesses' testimony need not have been received while the father was alive. The reason is that we rule that a document may be authenticated outside the litigant's presence, and the same applies to minors, who are treated as absent.
Once the signatures are authenticated, it is as though they were confirmed in beit din from the time they were signed while the father was alive. Rambam and the Mechaber rule this explicitly. Although the Tur cites the Rosh elsewhere as holding that certain matters cannot be validated during the heirs' minority, that applies particularly to testamentary matters or when the collection time has arrived.
Existing signed documents were treated more leniently because document authentication is rabbinic. Oral loans are different, because testimony cannot generally be received outside the litigant's presence, and a minor is considered absent.And he said, 'I repaid you.' Meaning that a ruling was rendered that he is not believed even after the term to claim repayment, as explained in siman 79.Deposited with him and he uses them for the benefit of, etc.
Hagahot Ashri distinguishes between a loan, which is given for expenditure, and a deposit, which the custodian may not use. Maharik distinguishes between money given for the recipient's own expenditure and money given to him to spend for the giver's benefit. Rama's point here is that the presumption that a person does not repay within the term applies only to a loan.
With a deposit it does not apply. Even where money is given to him for expenditure, if the expenditure is for the giver's benefit, it resembles a deposit and he is believed to say he returned it.
Rama calls it a deposit because the recipient does not bear ordinary loan liability. The more accurate text may be, 'deposited with him and he is prohibited from using it, or he deals with it,' as Darkei Moshe indicates.He is not believed to say that he repaid, etc. The source concludes that the same law applies to redemption of the firstborn.Each booklet immediately, etc.
