And he demanded payment within the term. The Sema, citing the Mordechai and Darkei Moshe, says this means where the claimant makes a definite claim, not an uncertain one. In truth the Mordechai remains uncertain on that point and exempts only by combining other clear considerations. In my view the principal rule is that even when heirs can plead only uncertainty, the presumption that a person does not pay before maturity still applies. This is demonstrated by the Rosh’s responsum concerning heirs who sued on a document where the creditor died within the term; there the Rosh says that although the borrower normally is not believed to claim early payment, actual witnesses to payment cannot be rejected merely because of this presumption.
Even minors. This is the consensus and the correct ruling. Some responsa argue that because the authorities dispute the point, property should not be taken from minor heirs, but the later authorities cited here rule that minors cannot rely on kim li against the rule permitting collection within the term. Maharashdam distinguishes a case in which the original term expired during the borrower’s life and the lender later granted an extension and waived part of the debt. I do not accept that distinction. So long as the debt remained within the currently binding term, collection from the heirs is permitted.
If there were, etc., and he established, etc. These requirements are needed because the heirs are minors. Testimony is not accepted outside the presence of a litigant, and minors are treated as absent. With adult heirs, oral testimony may be accepted in their presence and collection may proceed, as explained at the beginning of siman 108.
A loan by document, etc. The Sema infers that a written document need not have been authenticated during the debtor’s lifetime, because a document may be authenticated even outside the litigant’s presence. That is not correct according to the Rosh’s view cited below. For this rule, the document must have been authenticated before the debtor died, as I explain in siman 110.
Provided there are witnesses, etc. Tosafos, the Rosh, and the other authorities derive this requirement from seif 4 below. See what I write there and apply it here.
For if there are no witnesses, etc. If there is one witness to the fixed maturity and the borrower says he repaid before maturity, he is obligated to take a Torah oath but cannot take it against the witness, and therefore he pays. The Sema states this as well.
He is established as a denier. This requires study. Why should he be established as a liar? He can say that he indeed repaid within the term, but because he knew that claim would not have been believed, he was forced to repay again after maturity. Rabbeinu Yakar therefore remained uncertain on this case.
But if the money was deposited, etc. At first glance, the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber elsewhere appear to disagree. One may distinguish that there half the funds were a loan and the other half a deposit, and since repayment cannot be claimed regarding the loan half, the deposit half is not separated from it. Alternatively, the distinction is whether the holder derives benefit from the money. Here he has no benefit, whereas there the deposit portion supports the loan portion against loss.
And he uses them for the needs of others. Meaning that he receives no personal benefit from the funds. This is explicit in Mahariv. See also Mahari ben Lev.
It is like an ordinary deposit, etc. Maharash Sasson writes that with payment in merchandise we do not apply the presumption that a person does not pay before maturity. The matter requires study. Hagahot Ashiri and Mahariv indicate that early repayment is expected specifically with a deposit that one may not use, or where one handles deposit money solely for the depositor and gains no benefit. Where the holder receives benefit, the usual presumption against early payment remains, and payment by merchandise should seemingly be the same.
And rental payment, etc. The Sema adds that the same applies to redemption of the firstborn. This is explicit in the Mishnah and is explained in Yoreh Deah siman 305.
Each booklet has its own immediate due time. At first glance this means merely that after each booklet is completed the writer may claim payment for that booklet. The Ir Shushan so understands it. In my view, however, once each stage has its own payment time, the worker may even claim at the final maturity that he was paid in advance for all the booklets not yet written. This follows from the Gemara’s answer that each course of a wall has its own time. Reish Lakish agrees that where repeated stages each create their own collection pressure, a person may pay the entire projected amount at the outset to avoid being repeatedly pressed for payment. The same applies to booklets: because each booklet creates a new due point, he may pay for all in advance to avoid repeated demands. The Tur’s formulation concerning wall courses can be read differently, but the legal conclusion appears clear as I have stated. This matters even for us, who ordinarily leave unresolved monetary questions with the possessor, in cases where the defendant has no alternative migo, such as at the very beginning of the final due day or where witnesses establish that no later payment occurred.
