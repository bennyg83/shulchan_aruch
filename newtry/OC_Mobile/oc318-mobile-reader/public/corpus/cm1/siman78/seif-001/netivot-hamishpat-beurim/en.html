And he demanded payment within the term. See Shach, seif katan 1, through his statement that the witnesses require examination and inquiry. Tumim writes that with heirs, who were not expected to know, we apply the chazakah even when both sides make uncertain claims. If the creditor himself says that he is uncertain, however, the chazakah is weakened, unless the defendant likewise says that he is uncertain, in which case the chazakah remains intact. In my view, where both sides are uncertain we do not apply this chazakah. The chazakah of bodily status is very strong, as Ramban writes, yet even it is not applied where the claimant's assertion is uncertain, as is clear from Ketubot 76 concerning the claim, "You have one hundred belonging to my father in your possession," and in many other places. All the more so the chazakah that a person does not repay before the due date, which is only an intermediate-strength chazakah, should not operate where the claim is uncertain. Likewise, the chazakah created by three years of possession is a very strong chazakah, to the point that we do not impose an oath against it, as explained in siman 140, yet it is ineffective where the possessor himself has no claim, because that is a chazakah without an accompanying claim. The same should apply here. Where both sides say "I do not know," the chazakah does not establish liability. The fact that a promissory note remains effective in a case of uncertain versus uncertain claims, as explained in siman 59, is different. A creditor holding a note has not materially weakened his position by saying he does not know, because he relies upon his document. Therefore he does not lose his right.
He has been established as a denier. See Shach, seif katan 7, who expresses surprise at this ruling. In my view the law is straightforward. Siman 72, seif 18, in Rama, explains that even if at the time he claimed that the items were recognizable as ones that need not be returned according to that claim, he cannot later say that he returned them. It follows that even where he later claims repayment, he is not believed. The reason must be that he cannot say, "I knew I would not prevail in beit din under my earlier claim because these were objects ordinarily lent out, and therefore I later returned them." We respond: who told you that the Sages would rule against you? This is the reasoning explained in Bava Batra 5. I also explained above in siman 72, seif 18, that if in Nisan he says, "It never happened," and witnesses contradict him in Tammuz, he cannot then say, "I repaid in Iyar." The same applies here. The case is where the creditor demanded payment before witnesses during the term, and the debtor said, "I already repaid you." Later the creditor sues in beit din and the debtor says, "I repaid you now." We tell him: if it was true that you had already repaid before, why would you pay again now? Who told you that the Sages would obligate you? If his earlier statement before the witnesses was false, he has been established as a denier. If, however, the creditor first sued him in beit din and beit din ruled that he must pay, he is believed to say that he paid afterward, just as one is believed to claim payment against any judgment. In every judgment, beit din necessarily ruled against his prior claim, yet he remains believed to say that he later paid. The same applies here. Tumim also brings proof that if he was demanded before witnesses during the term and said, "I paid you," he cannot later say that he subsequently paid, from the law of a hired worker who claimed during the proper time that he had been paid and later claimed a subsequent payment. He is not believed. One cannot object that an employer is preoccupied with his workers and therefore should not be treated as an established denier, because Tosafot explicitly write there that the concept of an established denier does apply. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if the debtor claimed in beit din that he paid within the term and beit din ordered him to pay, he is not believed to say that he paid afterward. This is incorrect. A person becomes an established denier only when witnesses contradict him, as explained in siman 79. The fact that beit din did not believe him because the debt was still within its term does not make him an established denier, just as one who loses a monetary ruling because his claim was not believed may nevertheless later say that he paid the judgment. Accordingly, if he was sued in beit din within the term, claimed payment, and was ordered to pay, but the creditor delayed collection until after the term and then sued again, the debtor may even repeat that he had paid within the term, as he originally claimed. He is now believed because he has a migo that he could say he paid after the term. Certainly, if after a judgment the defendant seizes property without witnesses and thereby obtains a migo, he is believed. So too here, the present migo functions like possession acquired after the earlier judgment. Ketzot argues that he should not be believed through such a migo, because if he could now be believed by saying he paid after the term, then even that latter claim should fail. We could say to him: why did you pay after the term? You should instead have returned to beit din and repeated your original claim that you paid within the term, since now they would believe you through a migo. This is incorrect. Even according to Ketzot, he still has a migo that he paid on the very day the term ended. On that day he would not have been believed through such a migo, as the Sema writes in seif katan 15, and he therefore had to pay because of the prior ruling. Moreover, even according to those who hold that he would be believed through a migo on the day the term ended, he may say, "I paid because beit din had already ordered me to pay, and I did not know that if I returned later they would now believe me. I relied upon the earlier judgment, since not everyone is learned in the law." Although the Shach disputes this type of reasoning against the Rosh in siman 66, seif katan 26, that is only regarding whether we recognize a migo in such circumstances. It certainly cannot be used as affirmative proof that he did not pay. Otherwise, whenever one is believed to say that he paid a judgment, if it were known that he possessed property of the creditor and therefore had a migo, he would cease to be believed on the theory that since he had a migo he should not have paid. That is plainly untenable. Thus this reasoning cannot establish nonpayment.
Money held on deposit. Although regarding a deposit he is ordinarily believed through a migo that he could claim unavoidable accident, as the Shach writes in siman 108, seif 37, there is still a practical difference regarding the oath. Where he is believed only because of a migo of unavoidable accident, he must take an oath analogous to a Torah oath. Where the chazakah against payment within the term does not apply at all, however, he need take only a hesset oath, just like one who claims repayment of an oral loan. See Shach, seif katan 10, who writes concerning payment by merchandise. His intent is where one obligated himself to make payment at a set time with a particular commodity. In such a case the chazakah that a person does not pay before the due date does not apply, because he may fear that the required merchandise will not be available when the term arrives. The Shach challenges this, because the authorities imply that wherever the debtor benefits from the extension of time, we apply the chazakah that a person does not pay before the term.
Each pamphlet. See Shach, seif katan 12. At first glance one might infer from his words that if one lent another one hundred on condition that he repay ten gold coins each week, the borrower would be believed to say that he paid before the due date, just as in the case of separate pamphlets. This is incorrect. The Shach's rule applies specifically where there is no fixed time for each unit of work, so that perhaps all the work will be completed in one day or two. In that situation, the worker may fear being burdened with repeated collection demands, because each day carries the possibility that the work may be finished. But where each installment has a fixed due date, the case is equivalent to multiple loans, each with its own fixed term, and the borrower is not believed to say that he paid before the relevant due date. The Shach's basic ruling itself can also be questioned. In Kiddushin, chapter HaOmer, Ran writes regarding a condition, "on condition that I give you two hundred zuz," that even if a time for fulfillment was written, once he married her he is believed to say that he gave the money. The chazakah that a person does not pay before the due date does not apply because, once he wanted to marry her, that was effectively the due time, analogous to the rule in the beginning of Bava Batra that each course of stones has its own time. At first glance his words require explanation. It appears that Ran means that at the time of marriage, had he not given her the money she would not have married him. He therefore gained a concrete benefit from making the payment, namely the ability to marry her. This differs from ordinary early payment, where the debtor gains nothing by paying before the due date. For this reason he is also believed to say that he paid after the marriage through a migo. Alternatively, because payment became due in practical terms at the time of marriage, it is possible that they informally established another near-term date. Ran therefore compares it to the rule that each course of stones has its own time. That Gemara itself requires explanation. Why, regarding a courtyard wall, is the period during construction called before the due date? The obligation to build rests equally on both partners, and neither is inherently required to wait for the other. We must say that the case is where one partner first urged the other to participate and the other refused, as Tosafot explain, so the first began building alone and the other eventually agreed to pay as one pays a worker. If they had hired an ordinary worker to build the wall, the employer would not be believed to say he paid during the work, because wages are ordinarily due only at completion and payment beforehand would be before the term. The Gemara compares one partner who builds to an ordinary worker. Whether one improves another's property with authorization or without authorization, the other is not believed to say he paid in the middle of the work, since the valuation depends on what will ultimately be completed, as explained in siman 375, seif 9. The Gemara answers that each course of stones has its own time. Since the partner who built did not obligate himself to complete the entire wall but undertook the work voluntarily, after each course he could have said, "I built one course. You build the next. I do not wish to build more." Because the other partner also needed the wall to prevent visual intrusion, he may well have paid for each completed course in order to induce the builder to construct the next. Thus he had a benefit from making that payment, just as in Ran's case of marriage. Once this is understood, there is no basis for the Shach's broad rule. The Mordechai's proof extends only so far as saying that just as the Gemara presumes from the circumstances that each course of stones constitutes its own payment stage, so too where one hires work divided into separate pamphlets, we presume that each pamphlet was intended as its own payment stage because the work is inherently divisible.
