He is not believed. The Sema writes that the presumption against repayment before the due date applies only where the claimant asserts a certain claim, while the Shach limits the Sema's source and says heirs' uncertainty is treated like certainty because beit din advances the father's claims. That is correct regarding heirs. The more difficult case is where the creditor himself is uncertain whether payment occurred during the term. A creditor who produces a document but says, "I do not know whether I was paid," ordinarily does not collect, despite the document. One possible reason is that a required oath is impossible. If so, where the debt remains within the due date and no oath is required, the creditor should collect even though uncertain. Yet as I explained in siman 59, our practical reason is not simply inability to swear, since we sometimes allow a suspected holder of a document to collect without oath. Rather, where the borrower makes a certain payment claim and the creditor responds only with uncertainty, certainty may prevail when supported by possession. It remains possible, however, that the unusually strong presumption against payment before the due date overcomes the creditor's uncertainty. This requires study. Where both lender and borrower are uncertain, the lender's position is stronger. The within term presumption is at least no weaker than an ordinary document, and where both sides are uncertain a holder of a document can collect because the rule of certain versus uncertain is not implicated. See siman 84, seif katan 2.

He is established as a false denier. The Shach asks why. The borrower can say, "I certainly paid during the term, but because I was not believed I was forced to pay you again after the term." Rabbeinu Yakar indeed expresses uncertainty in such a case. The correct distinction is between one who actually makes that later claim and one who continues to stand on the original story. If he expressly says, "I paid again after the due date because my first payment could not be proven," there is a plausible reason to believe him regarding the later payment. But if he admits that he never paid a second time and continues to say only, "I paid during the term," he cannot be believed through a migo that he could have said he paid afterward. If that migo made his original claim credible, then there would have been no need for a second payment. The alternative claim presupposes that his original claim was not accepted. Therefore it cannot serve as a migo to validate the original claim. This also explains the discussion concerning an employee who says he was paid during the proper time and later, after the time has passed, could claim a later payment. There the employer can have a natural explanation: after the deadline he reviewed his affairs in order not to violate delayed wages, remembered that he had not paid, and then paid. That is a normal and coherent account. It can therefore support a migo even though his first statement was mistaken. This does not prove that every debtor who denied during a fixed term can use the same argument. Where the debtor was challenged only before witnesses and not before beit din, later saying he paid again can be accepted because he was forced to protect himself after learning his first claim would fail. But if he denied before a proper beit din and became formally established as a false denier regarding that money, he cannot later claim payment without witnesses. See siman 79, seif 9.
Each quire. Even though each quire of a commissioned manuscript can be treated as having its own due time for the presumption against early payment, the scribe can still collect with an oath under the enactment that an employee swears and collects. The law applies to an artisan as well, as explained in siman 89. Therefore the fact that each quire has already reached its own due time removes the stronger within term presumption, but does not automatically permit the employer simply to say, "I paid." Ir Shushan says that each quire is immediately due and therefore the employer can claim payment for each separately. That is incorrect where the scribe himself is alive and sues for his wages. Mordechai's case exempting the defendant concerned a deceased scribe whose wife and heirs sued. The special enactment was made for the employee, not his heirs. Once the employee is dead, the heirs cannot invoke it. Even if the scribe sues after completing the entire manuscript, his claim can still be treated as within the relevant wage period because the prohibition of delayed wages does not begin until the whole work for which he was hired is completed. The fact that each quire may be treated separately for the presumption of early payment does not mean the employer already violates delayed wages after every quire. Since the hiring was for the whole manuscript, the employer is not in violation until the work is completed. The rationale for denying the employee's special oath after the due time is that employers are presumed not to violate delayed wages. Where that prohibition has not yet arisen, the employee retains the enactment. Therefore the scribe can swear and collect.
