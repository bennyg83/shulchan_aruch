He is not believed, because there is a presumption, etc. The Sema writes that this presumption applies only where the claimant says definitely that he was not paid, not where he himself is uncertain. The Shach notes that Mordechai was uncertain about the matter and relied on this reasoning only together with other considerations. Responsa Rosh also suggests that the presumption that a person does not pay before maturity can operate for heirs. The distinction is that heirs are uncertain through no fault of their own. They could not know whether their father was paid, and we make for them whatever claim their father could have made. Their uncertainty therefore does not weaken the presumption that people do not pay before maturity. But if the creditor himself says, "I do not know whether I was paid," when he should have kept track of payment, that uncertainty itself weakens the chazakah. A similar weakening occurs where a document was lost and therefore is not returned even before maturity. The Sema speaks of this latter case, and there is no contradiction. There is some proof concerning heirs from the law of redemption of a firstborn, where before thirty days the child is presumed not redeemed. The kohen can hardly know with certainty that payment was not made to some other kohen through an agent. Yet his unavoidable uncertainty does not weaken the presumption. This resembles heirs. One might answer that redemption has the added feature that perhaps the obligation never became collectible at all, strengthening the presumption of nonpayment. Still, in rental cases where similar uncertainty exists, there is strong reason to preserve the presumption for heirs. One may also say that it is not weakened because the defendant too is uncertain.
Provided there are witnesses, etc. If the parties expressly stipulated that the debtor would be believed to say that he paid before maturity, the stipulation is unquestionably valid. Mordechai, however, writes concerning a scribe who prepared booklets that even before the completion of the work the employer may say he paid, by a migo that he could have said, "I stipulated with you that I would be believed even before the due time." Mordechai treats this as connected to an unresolved question in the Gemara. At first sight this would destroy the entire rule that a person is not believed to have paid before maturity. One can answer that where the loan was made before witnesses and a fixed due date was stated, the debtor cannot later invent an undisclosed credibility condition. Mordechai speaks where the employment itself was not created before witnesses; only the completed work is known. There, the employer has a plausible migo concerning the original terms. Knesset HaGedolah writes similarly. It also appears possible that Mordechai intended this only for a worker. A lender has no obvious reason to surrender the ordinary protection of a due date and believe the borrower against himself. "The borrower is servant to the lender." A claim that such a special condition was made is therefore unusual. A worker, however, works for the employer, depends upon his wage, and may accept conditions favorable to the employer. The special migo can therefore be plausible in employment even if not in an ordinary loan.
However. Tosafos appears not to accept Mordechai's broader migo. Tosafos discusses the wall above four cubits, where the builder is presumed unpaid. If the rule were only after the due date, the other party could claim, "I paid you before maturity," by migo. Tosafos says the Gemara could instead establish the claim during the term itself or on the actual due day. Yet the wall was not built through a witnessed loan. Its construction is simply known. The other party could therefore have said, "When you began building, I stipulated that I would be believed to say I paid before completion, and only on that condition did I agree to participate in raising the wall." If Mordechai's migo were generally accepted, the Gemara would still have difficulty. This case resembles employment, because the other party could say he would not have agreed to the extra building without such a condition. Tosafos therefore appears to reject that migo, as do the unqualified formulations of the authorities.
He became established as a denier. Rabbeinu Yakar was uncertain whether a person who falsely denied payment before maturity becomes permanently disbelieved concerning later payment. Perhaps, after realizing that his first denial would not be accepted, he genuinely paid later and saw no reason to trouble himself to go to beit din. Maharam regarded him as definitively established as a denier. The Shach thinks Rabbeinu Yakar's doubt is plausible. Baal HaMaor, in the discussion of a worker, appears to support Maharam. Where the worker proves that he demanded payment in time and the employer claimed then that he had paid, the worker continues to swear and collect afterward, because the employer cannot claim a later payment after having asserted payment at the due time. This sounds as though we do not allow him to say, "Once I learned that my first claim would fail, I paid later." It is difficult to answer that such a later claim would itself be credible but is too weak to create a migo, because in worker cases even a weak migo is often used. Still, the Gemara in Bava Metzia creates difficulty for Maharam. It says that if witnesses establish that the worker demanded throughout the due period, after the period the worker can still swear and collect, and then asks, "forever?" and limits the extension. Why not explain simply that during the due period the employer said, "I paid," and therefore is barred forever from claiming a later payment? The Yerushalmi also gives the employer an additional period when he says, in effect, "Did I not tell you that I paid you from that day?" Asifat Zekenim cites Raavad as using a migo to believe the employer and therefore limiting the worker's continuing right. This supports Rabbeinu Yakar. Maharam himself noticed the comparison and says his case is not like the worker case, but does not fully explain why. Perhaps he follows Rashi, who interprets the Gemara's question "forever?" as asking whether a worker would really delay claiming wages indefinitely. If the worker truly remained unpaid, his failure to proceed promptly against the employer would itself weaken his case. The employer can therefore be believed later despite his earlier statement. According to Tosafos, however, who reads the question as a policy concern about making the worker credible forever, I do not see a principled distinction. The matter therefore appears to be a dispute among major authorities. Since there is explicit support on both sides, the Shach correctly treats it as an unresolved question of law rather than extracting money from a possessor on reasoning alone.
Every booklet, etc. The Rama states this as though undisputed, but in my view it depends on the dispute. Mordechai derives it from the wall case in Bava Batra. The Mishnah says that up to four cubits the builder is presumed paid. The Gemara asks how this can be before the normal due date and answers that "each row and row has its own time." Mordechai extends this to divisible work, such as a book written booklet by booklet. Rashi and the Tur, however, do not require that explanation in the same way. Rashi says the reason the other party is presumed to have contributed is that everyone knows he is obligated to participate, so the builder would not ordinarily finance the whole structure from his own money without immediately taking him to beit din. Ramban says similarly. At first glance this appears different from the Gemara's answer that each row has its own time. The reconciliation is that the Mishnah concerns a case where one party is known to have begun building with his own funds. One could reason either that by starting alone he implied willingness to advance the whole cost until completion, making the period before completion "before maturity," or, conversely, that he merely advanced enough to begin. Once one or two rows were completed, everyone knew the other party was equally obligated and the builder would immediately demand contribution for what was already spent and for the next row. The Gemara rejects the first assumption and adopts the second. Thus "each row is a time" means not merely that payment comes mechanically at the end of each technical unit, but that after each portion the builder has an immediately enforceable public claim against the other party. This also explains why the Tur says that even before completing the next row the time has arrived. Once the first row is completed, the other party can be summoned to contribute toward what was done and toward the next stage. Rashi's explanation and the Gemara's language are therefore one concept. If so, the wall above four cubits is different. There the obligation to pay is not publicly obvious from the outset, and before the additional row is built the other person may have no enforceable duty to contribute. The same "each row" presumption therefore does not operate. This resolves the Gemara's contrast. A hired scribe is not comparable. The employer hired him for the full book, and wages are normally due only at completion. Half a book may be useless to the employer, and the scribe cannot necessarily sue for each booklet as an independently matured debt merely because the labor is technically divisible. If another scribe can finish the work, that can be true even in work not divided into booklets. Therefore Mordechai's extension is not compelled by Rashi and the Tur. A possessor can at least say that the halachah follows their interpretation, and the Rama's rule should not be treated as free of dispute.
The same applies to every divisible labor. Even within Mordechai's view there are two ways to understand the rule. The simple reading is that each booklet has its own due date. The employer is therefore believed to say that he paid when a booklet was completed, but not that he prepaid the next booklet before it was written. The Shach offers a second explanation: because each unit becomes due separately, the worker would repeatedly bother the employer for payment. Even Reish Lakish, who generally says a person does not pay before maturity, may agree that the employer prepays in order to avoid repeated demands. On that view the employer might even be believed to have paid before the particular booklet was written. The Shach notes that the Tur's language concerning the wall suggests only that the beginning of each row is its time, not that payment is made before any work on the row. According to what I wrote, however, the Tur's entire explanation of the wall differs from Mordechai's extension to hired divisible labor, so the Tur cannot be used to refute the Shach's reading of Mordechai.
But. Tosafos requires further examination. Tosafos says that even Abaye and Rava, who generally allow the possibility of payment before maturity because a person wants to avoid later harassment, agree regarding a courtyard wall that he does not pay early, since perhaps the wall will never be built and the obligation will never arise. If so, how can the Gemara answer that every row has its own time because of concern about harassment? Even Abaye and Rava concede that where the underlying obligation may never arise, the debtor does not prepay merely to avoid being bothered. It is forced to distinguish between greater and lesser harassment without a source. Yet I found support for the Shach from Rabbeinu Tam cited in Mordechai. Rabbeinu Tam rules that where a definite repayment date was fixed, such as by Iyar or Sivan, the debtor does not receive an additional thirty days from beit din after that date. He must pay immediately or be compelled. His proof is the rule that every row of the wall has its own time. At first glance this proof is puzzling. Perhaps each row is simply a due date even though, if sued, the debtor could still ask for the ordinary beit din period. People commonly pay when a due date arrives without requesting court extensions. Indeed, the Gemara says directly that people pay on the due day. Why then cite the wall? Rabbeinu Tam's proof makes sense specifically if "each row has its own time" means that repeated demands can come immediately and no additional court period intervenes. Otherwise the practical harassment would not exist row by row. This also clarifies Reish Lakish. Abaye and Rava say that if a debtor happens to have cash during the term, he may pay early so that the creditor will not trouble him later when money may not be available. One might ask why he worries, since when the due date arrives he can ask beit din for thirty more days. The answer is that he fears he may still lack money even after that later period. But Reish Lakish rejects early payment merely to avoid future harassment. If each completed row entitled the builder only to a later thirty day court period, there would be no repeated immediate pressure. By the time one such period elapsed, the entire four cubit wall would likely be finished, creating only one final demand. The Gemara's answer to Reish Lakish therefore implies that no additional beit din period is given after each fixed unit becomes due. This supports the Shach and Rabbeinu Tam. The question from Tosafos remains, but perhaps Rabbeinu Tam and Mordechai answer Tosafos differently, as Ritva records other approaches depending on the dispute whether redemption within thirty days takes effect. At least the Shach's interpretation has substantial support.
