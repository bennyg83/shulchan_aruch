He sued him after the due date. This is an unresolved question, and we rule leniently for the defendant. If the claimant seized, however, we do not remove it, as the Shach writes. At first glance, since seizure is effective, the borrower should not be required to swear until the lender waives his claim. Otherwise he might swear now, and the lender could seize tomorrow, making the oath effectively pointless. The Ran writes similarly regarding a creditor who claims that a payment should be applied to another debt: the borrower need not take a hesset oath until the creditor nullifies the document, because it is improper for the creditor to impose an oath while still holding a document that may later be used. The Shach cites this in siman 38. Perhaps, however, that case is not based only on possible seizure but on concern that the creditor may present the document to another beit din that does not know of the witnesses to payment. Moreover, even if in a case where seizure is effective we ordinarily do not impose an oath until the claimant waives the claim, here the borrower certainly must swear. His statement that he paid before the due date is not believed on its own. It is believed only through the migo that he could have claimed payment after the due date. Just as he would have had to swear on the latter claim, so too he must swear now on the former claim. This explains what Shulchan Aruch teaches by requiring the oath, and answers the Sema's question as to what novelty the ruling contains.
