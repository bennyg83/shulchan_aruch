That he repaid him at the proper time. This includes a claim that he repaid before maturity.
The borrower takes a heset oath and is exempt through a migo. He could have said that he paid after maturity. The Gemara leaves unresolved whether a migo is accepted against the presumption that a person does not pay before maturity, and the authorities rule leniently for the defendant. Accordingly, if the lender seized the money, even in the presence of witnesses, it is not removed from him in those forms of seizure that are effective in an unresolved talmudic question. If he seized without witnesses, he need not take the usual oath based on the presumption against early payment. Even where I wrote above that a possessor generally must swear before collecting despite a migo, here, when he claims early payment, it appears that only a heset oath after collection is required. Ramban, discussing whether a kinyan has the status of a court act, writes that although a court act is treated as though written and can collect from encumbered property, the debtor may still say he paid even where beit din fixed a date, because people sometimes pay within a term fixed by beit din. This suggests that the presumption against early payment applies specifically to a date fixed by the parties, not one fixed by beit din. I do not know the basis for this distinction. Ramban’s proof from Bava Metzia concerns a case where beit din did not grant a future term at all, but told the debtor that he was presently obligated to pay, so it does not establish his proposition. The matter requires further study.
