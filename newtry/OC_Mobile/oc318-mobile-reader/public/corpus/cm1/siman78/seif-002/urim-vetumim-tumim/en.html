And some say that he must. Maadanei Melech infers from Rosh that even if the debtor himself admits he did not pay within the term, a creditor collecting from purchasers must still take the purchaser-facing oath because of concern for collusion. He notes that Rosh mentions death only incidentally. I do not see the proof. Where the debtor is absent or dead, purchasers need protection against a doubtful repayment claim and the creditor takes the formal Mishnah oath. Where the debtor himself stands before us and admits nonpayment, the evidentiary situation is different. The concern for collusion may remain conceptually possible, but one should not multiply oath requirements without a clear source. Nor can one compare this to Rosh's separate rule requiring hesset when a debtor pleads payment against a claimant. Hesset and the Mishnah oath for collection from encumbered property are distinct institutions. There is an apparent tension in Rosh regarding sudden death. In one place Rosh says heirs ordinarily need no special within-term oath because if the father had paid unusually early he would have instructed them. If he died suddenly, that reasoning seems absent and perhaps the creditor should swear. Elsewhere Rosh says sudden death removes the concern of advance ketubah arrangements and allows collection. The difference is ketubah. A husband ordinarily has no reason to prepay a ketubah during his life because the wife may die first and never collect. The realistic concern is that near death he might hand her assets or security. Sudden death eliminates that concern. An ordinary loan is different because a debtor may repay early for many practical reasons. Sudden death does not prove he had not done so. Still, where heirs find no receipt or other sign of an unusual early payment, Rosh may rely on the general presumption against within-term payment. In any event, there is no clear basis to say that even an express debtor admission of nonpayment never helps against purchasers. Since Rambam is also less demanding in related purchaser-oath cases, we should not create an additional dispute unnecessarily. The law therefore remains doubtful rather than as broad as Maadanei Melech suggests.
And this is the primary view. Shach and Taz ask an apparent contradiction. The dispute about hesset must concern the period after payment, because before payment no hesset is relevant. Rama here favors the view that after the creditor collects a within-term debt, he need not automatically take hesset. Yet below in siman 317, where a tenant claims he paid rent within the term, Shulchan Aruch says he may later impose hesset. The difficulty is even sharper for Beit Yosef, who attributes the no-oath view to Rambam, while the siman 317 wording itself comes from Rambam. The resolution is procedural. Everyone agrees that after payment the debtor can bring a fresh claim: "You collected from me twice. Swear hesset that you did not." The dispute is whether beit din automatically appends that oath to the original judgment. One view says: beit din orders, "Pay the creditor, and afterward the creditor must take hesset," because the debtor already pleaded payment. The other says: beit din simply orders payment. If the debtor later wants hesset, he must initiate a new monetary claim after payment. Rama favors the latter. This is exactly how siman 317 is phrased: after paying, the tenant may raise the money as a separate claim and impose hesset. The text itself says it is a new claim. There is therefore no contradiction. This also parallels a note containing a credibility clause. The clause can defeat the debtor's present migo or defense, yet after actual collection the debtor may still bring a new independent restitution claim where the wording permits. Sema's separate contradiction between Tur here and Tur in the rental case can be answered because the rental case involves an unusually remote concern and therefore everyone may require a fresh claim before imposing hesset.
However, Lechem Mishneh raises another difficulty. Rambam rules in the courtyard-wall case above four cubits that the builder, who is presumed unpaid, must nevertheless swear formally and collect. Why, if within-term nonpayment is a chazakah, should the claimant need an oath at all? The answer is that the wall case is not governed by the ordinary presumption against within-term payment alone. Rambam in his Commentary to the Mishnah explains the presumption there by saying, "Who says the Sages will obligate me?" The neighbor may delay payment because he is uncertain whether he will ever become legally obligated for the extra height. That is a weaker and different presumption. Even a formal note can sometimes require an oath when the debtor has a specific substantial defense. All the more so this nonpayment inference based on uncertainty of future liability. Accordingly, Rambam can require the builder to swear in the wall case while allowing an ordinary creditor within the fixed term to collect without an automatic oath. This also answers the objections of Ramban and Ramah to Rambam's wall ruling. The two chazakot are not identical.
