Some say that he must, etc. This differs from collection from heirs, where we collect without an oath. Had the debtor paid, he would ordinarily have instructed his heirs to plead payment, whereas purchasers may be unaware of his instructions. The Ir Shushan gives a better reason: a person is not concerned to protect purchasers and therefore may fail to leave them proof. This appears correct, because according to the opinion requiring an oath, the oath is required even when the debtor is present and admits that he did not pay, and certainly when it is known that he left no instructions. Moreover, as I explain below, if the debtor is alive and claims payment, the creditor need not swear before collection. The distinction must therefore be that a father ordinarily clarifies matters for the benefit of heirs, but not for purchasers.
Some say that he need not, etc. The Rama appears to mean that no oath is required at all, even after collection. This is also the implication of the Tur and is explicit in Baal HaTerumot. The Bach’s objection to the Tur is therefore unfounded. Otherwise the first view would necessarily require an oath before payment, a position not found in the authorities. The Rosh says that a heset oath is required, and a heset oath can only be after payment. The Bach likewise understands the Rosh so. It is therefore difficult that the Rama here rules that no oath is required at all, while at the beginning of siman 317 he follows the Mechaber that an oath is required after payment. It is strained to distinguish rent from a loan, especially since Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef indicate that the claimant’s position is even stronger in rent. The principal ruling is like the Beit Yosef: before payment no oath is required, even for an oral loan, though the defendant may impose a general ban; after payment the creditor must take a heset oath, even on a written loan. Tosafot, the Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, Ramban, and the Rosh can all be reconciled this way. Baal HaTerumot cites an opinion, also attributed to Rabbeinu Chananel, that he swears heset and is exempt, which plainly refers to an oath after payment. The Rama apparently followed the Beit Yosef’s statement that Rambam agrees that no oath is needed. In truth, Rambam rules in Hilchot Sechirut that an oath is required after payment, and all the more so for a loan. The proof from Rambam’s rule that where one witness says the debt was paid within the term the creditor collects without an oath concerns only the statutory oath required before collection when one witness testifies to payment. It does not exclude a heset oath afterward. Since Rambam and the Rosh agree, and Rabbeinu Chananel and other authorities likewise agree, this should be the ruling. The presumption that a person does not repay within the term gives the creditor a position comparable to one holding the money, but like a three-year presumption of ownership it is not strong enough to eliminate a heset oath.
