When a term was fixed. The Sema explains that the debtor's request for a fixed term shows that he needed the money until that date. That is not the essential basis. The presumption arises from ordinary repayment practice. Rent, redemption of a firstborn son, and construction obligations can all have the same presumption even where the debtor never expressly revealed that he needed a term. The controlling fact is that people ordinarily do not pay before the established due time. This is also why an ordinary unspecified loan, though not collectible for thirty days, does not receive the same presumption. People do sometimes repay such a loan within thirty days because no explicit repayment date was fixed. Rav Hai Gaon and Rif say that the thirty day rule merely means the creditor cannot demand sooner; it does not establish a custom that people refrain from paying. Nimukei Yosef adds that an expressly fixed term can be set either at the time of the loan or afterward. Therefore even if the creditor later extends the due date as an act of kindness, the presumption against payment within the newly fixed term can apply.
But in an ordinary loan. Ramban writes that if beit din granted a debtor time to obtain money and the debtor later says he paid within that judicial period, he is believed. The reason is that a term granted by the creditor gives the debtor a right not to pay until the date even if he already has money. People therefore ordinarily do not pay early. A term granted by beit din is different. Beit din gives time only to allow him to obtain funds. If beit din knew he already had the money, they would not postpone collection. Therefore whenever money becomes available during the judicial term, the debtor is already obligated to pay. Although he could evade by saying he has no money, a person who knows he owes an oral debt also could deny the debt and yet normally pays when he knows the obligation is real. Likewise, once this debtor has funds during the judicial extension, that very day is effectively his due date. There is therefore no presumption against payment. If arbitrators or compromisers fixed a term specifically for the debtor's benefit and intended that he need not pay even if money becomes available earlier, that term resembles a creditor's contractual extension rather than a judicial grace period. The ordinary presumption against payment within the term then applies.
