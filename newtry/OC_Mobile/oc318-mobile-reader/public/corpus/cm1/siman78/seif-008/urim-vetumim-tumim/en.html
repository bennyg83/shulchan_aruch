That which we say, etc. The Ramban writes in his novellae to Bava Batra 40 that even when beit din grants a person time to pay, he may nevertheless say, "I paid within the period granted by beit din." The Shach rejects the Ramban's proof and writes that this law requires study. To clarify the Ramban's ruling, we must investigate why, when one lends money to another without specifying a term, although the borrower has thirty days, he may nevertheless claim that he repaid before the thirty days elapsed. The Sema at the beginning of the siman explains that when the lender expressly fixes a term, the borrower thereby reveals from the outset that he needs the money until that date. We therefore apply the chazakah that he did not repay before the term. By contrast, in an ordinary loan whose term is thirty days, there was no initial disclosure that he needed the money until the thirtieth day. These words appear in Responsa Rosh, rule 76, responsum 2. See there. According to this, if a person asks beit din for thirty days and beit din grants him that period, this too reveals that he needs the money until that date. There should therefore be a chazakah that he did not pay during the period, just as when a term was expressly fixed. There are, however, grounds to question this explanation. First, consider what I wrote above in siman 39 regarding Tosafot's and the Rosh's question why a widow must swear before collecting her ketubah, since the ketubah is a debt before its term, as a ketubah cannot be collected during the husband's lifetime. The difficulty is apparent. Why did they ask this? The husband did not reveal that he needed the money or any set aside funds until that time. Rather, the ketubah simply cannot be collected during his lifetime, just as an ordinary loan is by law due after thirty days. I already resolved this above, because with a written instrument a person does not pay before the term. Even with an ordinary loan, without a receipt we do not say that he paid. This also appears from the Bach at the end of this siman. See there. He cites no proof for his statement, and it is clear that he intended what I wrote above. A more serious difficulty arises from the Mechaber's ruling below in siman 317, which is the Rambam's ruling, that when one rents without specifying a term, so that the ordinary rental term is thirty days, he is not believed to say that he paid before the term. According to the Maggid Mishneh, this is an explicit baraita in the Gemara. Why should this differ from an ordinary loan for thirty days? The Shach himself raises this below in that siman, seif katan 2, and seeks to say that the Rambam follows those authorities who do not distinguish between a loan with an expressly fixed term and an ordinary loan. He therefore leaves a difficulty on the Mechaber, who rules in this siman that one does distinguish between an ordinary loan and one with a fixed term. In truth, even if we say that the Gemara's case, where the renter says, "I paid," and the landlord says otherwise, and the landlord is believed before the term, concerns a rental for an expressly fixed term, as the Shach explicitly writes, the difficulty remains. When a loan has a fixed term, the borrower reveals that he has no money until that date. But when one rents a house for thirty days, has he thereby revealed that he has no money until the thirtieth day? The fixed period relates to the use of the house, because he needs the house until the completion of thirty days or whatever other period was fixed. What connection does this have to his money? Payment is merely not due until the end because rent is payable only at the end. This resembles an ordinary thirty day loan. The Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Batra struggles with the case of a scribe who writes a book, asking whether wages become payable with each quire, so that each completed portion marks its own due time, as discussed above at length. There no term was fixed at all. Rather, the rule that wages are payable only at the end applies automatically. The same is true of the Gemara's question from a wall that is being built, where the obligation is before its term until the wall reaches four cubits. Was a term fixed at the outset, thereby revealing that the debtor needed the money? No. The payment simply does not become due until the wall is completed, just as with an ordinary loan.
Therefore, the Rosh's view must be understood as follows. We require disclosure that the debtor needs the money when his obligation to pay is already certain. In that situation, absent such disclosure, we say that a person may pay before the term. But where it is possible that the obligation will never arise at all, as with a ketubah, since perhaps the wife will die during her husband's lifetime, or with rent, since perhaps the house will collapse, and similar cases, as Tosafot write in the above sugya, then even without any disclosure a person does not pay before the term. The same applies to the wall, since perhaps it will not be built. According to this, there is no basis for the Shach's question below in siman 317 concerning rent. Even with an ordinary rental whose term is thirty days, one does not pay before the term, and all is well. This also resolves what I wrote above in seif katan 7 concerning the Rambam's reason in the case of a wall above four cubits. The Rambam explains the ruling by saying, "Who says that he will build it," rather than simply because payment is not yet due. The above difficulty would otherwise arise there as well, since this resembles an ordinary loan in which the debtor did not disclose that he needed the money. He simply is not obligated to pay until the wall is completed. In this case, however, the concern that perhaps he will not build would seemingly not apply, because Maharshal writes that when the matter depends entirely upon his own will, one does not say, "Perhaps he will build." See there. The Rambam therefore understood the Gemara's question as seeking to prove that not only with a fixed term, but even with an ordinary loan, a person does not pay before the term. In truth, however, since the correct law distinguishes between a fixed term and an ordinary loan, as is inferred from Reish Lakish's language, the wall case cannot rest on the rule that a person does not pay before the term. Rather, it rests on the consideration, "Who says that he will build," etc. The Rambam therefore wrote correctly. This explanation, however, does not fit Tosafot's words, for Tosafot explicitly write that the rule applies specifically where a term was fixed, etc., yet they also write that the Gemara could have answered regarding the wall above four cubits that payment was still before the term. The earlier difficulty therefore remains, since according to Maharshal the consideration that perhaps he will not build does not apply there. My principal difficulty with this explanation, however, is the Gemara's uncertainty in Bava Metzia regarding the day on which the rental term expires, whether a person ordinarily pays the rent on that day. Why is there any uncertainty? We have already established that even a rental for an expressly fixed period resembles an ordinary loan with respect to the money. The only reason the renter does not pay before the term is the possibility that the house may collapse. That is understandable during the rental period. But at the end of the term, toward the close of the day, why should it matter whether the house may collapse? The rental period has ended, and he has only a fleeting instant left to remain in the house. Even if this is technically still within the term, he should be able to claim that he paid, just as with an ordinary loan. What then is the Gemara's uncertainty? The Raavad writes that we do not advance such a claim for heirs. But why should their position be worse than that of heirs in an ordinary loan within thirty days? Even if one were to force the issue and say that at the beginning of the day on which the term expires he may still occupy the house for the entire day, what will one say at the end of the day as the sun is setting? If you answer that the Gemara's doubt does not concern that moment, that cannot be correct. Apart from the Shulchan Aruch's view that the entire doubt concerns only the end of the day, even otherwise, how does the Gemara resolve the question from the case of a hired worker at his proper time? There the day has already ended. According to the Raavad's answer, as I wrote above in seif katan 9, the employer is claiming payment made an instant before sunset. That instant should not be doubtful at all. All the more so according to the Ritva, who holds that the landlord can bring the renter to beit din for the rent on the day the term expires. See the Or Zarua, and see what I wrote above in seif katan 8. This demonstrates that at the end of the term we are not concerned that the house may collapse, since the landlord can already sue in beit din. What, then, is the Gemara uncertain about? If one says that this itself is the uncertainty, whether he can sue on that day, then how can the Gemara resolve the matter from a hired worker? A day worker certainly cannot be sued for payment before sunset, as the Mishnah teaches that a day worker may collect throughout the night. I therefore think there is another reason for distinguishing an ordinary loan from a loan with a fixed term. I established above in siman 67 that with an ordinary thirty day loan, if the lender seizes property from the borrower during the thirty days, we do not remove it from him. The thirty day rule means only that beit din does not attend to the lender's collection claim until that date, because perhaps the borrower has no money, for one who borrows does not borrow merely to hide the money in his house. Nevertheless, a personal obligation to pay already rests upon the borrower. Therefore, if the lender seizes property, we do not remove it from him. For this reason Shemittah cancels such a loan, as I wrote there. It is therefore possible that the borrower obtained money and properly discharged his obligation by paying, or perhaps the lender seized property and the borrower had to leave it with him as payment. By contrast, where an express term was fixed, the borrower has no obligation to pay before that date, even if his house is full of gold and silver. If the lender seizes property, we remove it from him, as explained above in siman 67. For this reason Shemittah does not cancel such a debt. The same applies to a ketubah, which cannot be paid at all until divorce or death, and to rent, where there is no obligation whatsoever to pay until the end. Indeed, according to one opinion there is not even the beginning of a loan obligation until the end, as reflected in the dispute whether the rental obligation accrues continuously from beginning to end, etc. If the landlord seizes property before then, it is removed from him according to all opinions. Such a case is therefore like an expressly fixed term, and a person does not ordinarily pay before the term. This applies even to an ordinary rental. Since an unspecified rental is for thirty days, the same rule follows: there is no obligation to pay rent at all until the end of the rental period, and if the landlord seizes property it is removed from him, because the governing principle is that rent is payable only at the end. An ordinary rental is therefore unlike an ordinary loan. With an ordinary loan, the thirty days primarily define the time for collection, while with a rental the thirty days primarily define the period of use. As to payment, we return to the general rule that rent is not payable until the end. Even if the renter has thousands available, there is a chazakah that a person does not pay before the term. All of the questions we raised are thereby resolved, particularly the Shach's question. It now follows that our master the Ramban's ruling is true and firmly established. When beit din grants a debtor thirty days, it does so because he presently lacks money. If he has money, would beit din grant him time? Beit din gives him a period during which he is not pressed for payment, but a personal obligation remains upon him to pay the lender at every moment once money becomes available. The lender did not voluntarily grant him any term at all. Consequently, if money becomes available to the debtor, he certainly may pay within the period. This is simple and correct. On this basis I thought to resolve the words of Ri in Tosafot. Ri writes that the Mishnah, "Throughout the thirty days he has the chazakah of not having paid," does not challenge Abaye and Rava, who hold that a person does pay before the term, because there the rationale that he pays early to avoid being troubled by the creditor does not apply, since this is money that has no specific claimant. This also appears to disagree with the Yam Shel Shlomo's view that I cited above in seif katan 11. The commentators ask Ri: If so, what does the Gemara mean when it says, "If it is before the term, we already learned this: within thirty days he has the chazakah of not having paid"? That case is different, because the rationale that he pays early to avoid being troubled does not apply. At first glance this is a powerful question, to the point that one might wonder how Ri, while engaged with the very sugya, could have overlooked an explicit Gemara. According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. There is indeed a distinction for Abaye and Rava between a case where the debtor may pay early to avoid being troubled and one where that concern does not exist, because they hold that a person pays before the term for precisely that reason. But according to Reish Lakish, who holds that a person does not pay before the term, he is not concerned with avoiding trouble, and despite such trouble does not pay before the term. The Gemara's question concerning a renter who says, "I paid," certainly presupposes Reish Lakish's rule and the accepted law that a person does not pay before the term. Otherwise, why would the Gemara ask specifically about a renter rather than an ordinary borrower? The Gemara's uncertainty was only because Reish Lakish spoke of a loan with a fixed term, whereas an ordinary loan is different. What is the reason for Reish Lakish's distinction? If it is the reason stated by the Rosh and Sema, then it does not apply to a renter, because the fixed rental period defines the time of use and does not reveal that the renter lacks money, as explained above. In that event, even Reish Lakish would agree that the renter is believed. But if the reason is as I have written, namely that with a fixed term there is no obligation upon the debtor to pay even if he has money, then the same is true of a renter, as explained. The Gemara therefore properly resolves the matter from redemption of the firstborn. There too the date is not fixed because the father lacks money. Rather, the Torah says that the child is redeemable from one month of age. Necessarily, the reason is as I wrote, that there is no obligation of payment before that time. One cannot distinguish that redemption is different because there is no concern of being troubled, since according to Reish Lakish we do not invoke that rationale at all. In this way we can also understand, according to the opinion that we do not advance an uncommon claim for heirs, what proof the Gemara brings from redemption of the firstborn. Perhaps paying before the term is merely uncommon, such that the father himself would be believed if he claimed it, as with other uncommon claims, whereas in the firstborn case the father died and we do not advance such a claim for the heirs. At first glance this requires study. According to what I wrote, however, it is clear. The entire uncertainty is whether rent resembles an ordinary loan or a loan with a fixed term. The Gemara already knew that with an ordinary loan we advance the claim of payment even for heirs. The Mishnah therefore proves that redemption has the law of a fixed term, and that is all the Gemara seeks to establish. It does not descend into further distinctions. It is also possible that in the redemption case there is a concern of being troubled, as the Shitah Mekubetzet writes, namely the pressure of fulfilling a mitzvah. According to Reish Lakish, however, we never find a distinction between cases involving such pressure and cases without it. Rather, with an ordinary loan we always say that the borrower may have paid, and all is well. Consider this carefully. Tosafot question Ri's explanation because they hold that even according to Reish Lakish one may invoke the rationale of paying early to avoid being troubled. This is the point of dispute among the Tosafists. God forbid that we should say Ri overlooked an explicit Gemara.
