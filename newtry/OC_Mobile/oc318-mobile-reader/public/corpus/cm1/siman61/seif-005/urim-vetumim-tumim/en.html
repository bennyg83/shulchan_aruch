But conditions such as credibility in a loan, and so forth. Ritva infers from this that even where the custom is to include a credibility clause in loan documents, if the particular note does not expressly contain that clause, the creditor does not receive the special credibility. Beit Yosef cites Ritva below in siman 71. This appears to contradict Responsa Rosh, cited by Mechaber, which says that where the custom of the place is to include such a clause, the scribe may write it without receiving a separate explicit instruction. Gidulei Terumah raises a further difficulty: Rav Hai Gaon requires the credibility clause to be written expressly, yet the principle "we interpret the language of ordinary people" means that established documentary custom is treated as though written even where omitted. Why should a credibility clause be worse than other customary provisions? Shach answers by distinguishing whether the borrower knew the custom. If he knew it, we presume he obligated himself according to the local usage. If he did not know it, how can we impose an obligation he never contemplated? Shach supports this principle from Rambam in Hilchot Ishut. The conceptual distinction is sound, but it does not fit Rav Hai Gaon's wording. Rav Hai says unqualifiedly that credibility does not operate unless expressly written. He does not distinguish between a borrower who knew the custom and one who did not, even though that distinction is the entire basis of Shach's answer. Likewise Rama places Rav Hai's ruling immediately after Mechaber's quotation of Rosh, which indicates they address the same setting rather than two different levels of knowledge. The true answer emerges from R. Meir's doctrine of interpreting common documentary language. Gidulei Terumah's question is really not against Rav Hai Gaon but against R. Meir himself. R. Meir says that customary contractual formulations can govern even when omitted because one interprets according to ordinary usage. Yet R. Meir also holds that warranty is not a scribal error: if a deed omits warranty, one does not collect from encumbered property. Shmuel even entertained that such a document might be invalid altogether. But virtually every creditor ordinarily insists on warranty. Why is the omitted customary clause not supplied automatically under the rule of ordinary language? The difficulty is stronger because R. Meir himself explains the doctrine as equivalent to a condition of beit din. Yet with a ketubah, Chazal expressly established that all the husband's property should secure it. Nevertheless, R. Meir holds that if warranty was omitted from the ketubah, the woman does not collect from encumbered property. See Ketubot 51a. The answer must be that one follows customary language exactly as practiced, without adding or subtracting. In the classic case, "if I leave the field fallow I will pay the best estimated yield," local people treated that as an understood term of a lease even when no document was written. Their commercial practice itself supplied the term orally. But where common practice is specifically to write a term into every formal document and not to rely on an oral understanding, then the custom points in the opposite direction. If the clause is absent from the writing, that deviation from custom is itself meaningful. We must follow the usage of ordinary people as it actually exists. Thus, with warranty, R. Meir holds that people ordinarily wrote the warranty clause expressly because it is a central aspect of the lien. They did not rely on an unwritten assumption. When a deed appears without warranty, it departs from the normal form, and we cannot supply the missing clause through customary interpretation. The same applies to the ketubah. Even though the substantive obligation derives from a condition of beit din, the enactment may have been that the lien be written in the ketubah so as to generate proper publicity. If it was not written, the document lacks that purchaser-facing feature. This principle resolves Rav Hai Gaon as well. Credibility is indeed customary, but precisely because it is a powerful and unusual provision, local practice was to write it expressly in the document. People did not rely upon an unwritten oral assumption. Therefore, when a note comes before us without the credibility clause, one cannot invoke "ordinary language" to insert it. The custom itself was to write it. There is no contradiction with Rosh. Rosh says that when the borrower instructs the scribe to prepare a standard document, the scribe may include the customary credibility clause without asking separately. That follows from ordinary contractual language: the instruction to write a standard note includes all the terms people ordinarily write. Rav Hai addresses a completed document that actually lacks the clause. There the very omission demonstrates deviation from customary form. A scribe may include the clause because it belongs to the standard language, but if the final document omits it, we do not pretend it was written. Thus the two rulings fit perfectly. The distinction is not primarily whether the borrower knew the custom, but whether the custom operates as an unstated term or as a standard provision that people insist on writing expressly. Gidulei Terumah himself considered something like this distinction, depending on whether ordinary people customarily wrote the clause specifically. He remained uncertain. In my opinion the distinction is compelled by the laws of warranty and ketubah and is the correct resolution. See also what I write below in siman 71 and siman 242, seif 5.
