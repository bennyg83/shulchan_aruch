Sema explains this as referring to matters that reasonably appear to have been intended, because without them the substance of the document would not be complete. Shach explains it as referring only to a matter already mentioned earlier in the document and not to an entirely new matter. As practical law, both principles are true.
But conditions such as credibility, etc. Although the Mechaber writes that even with a trust clause we follow local custom and need not state it expressly, and above at the end of siman 42 we say that whatever is customarily written is treated as written even when omitted, Shach distinguishes between a person who knew of the custom and one who did not. If the obligor knew the custom, we follow it even without express wording. If he did not know it, the custom does not automatically bind him and the clause must be stated. See Tumim. I wrote that this distinction is persuasive as practical law, but it is difficult to read it into the gloss, which quotes Rav Hai Gaon. The primary distinction is rather this: where the custom is not to write the clause but nevertheless always to act in accordance with it, we apply it even if omitted. But where the custom is specifically to write a trust clause, then although witnesses instructed to write a document may include such a clause based on custom even without an express direction, if a document comes before us and omits the trust clause, that deviation from custom itself shows that no such credibility was granted. See Tumim, where I proved this decisively from the Gemara. This is also reasonable.
Double the amount. This is an exaggeration and therefore an asmachta. If he obligates himself only for actual expenses incurred, there is no asmachta, like the rule, "If I leave the field fallow and do not cultivate it, I will pay according to its best expected yield," which is not an asmachta, Sema. It appears that once his undertaking is framed as an asmachta, he need not pay even the actual expenses. This differs from the case where he says, "If I leave it fallow I will pay one thousand zuz," where below in siman 328 the Mechaber rules that he nevertheless must pay the ordinary expected yield. There, even had he written nothing, he would still owe the ordinary yield by virtue of accepted commercial language. Here, expenses are owed only from the time of refusal onward if he expressly undertook them. Since the written undertaking itself is an asmachta and has no effect, it is as though he wrote nothing at all. Bach writes that if he undertakes to pay consequential loss, such as where the other party was forced to sell merchandise immediately and lost anticipated profit, that too is an asmachta. It is merely lost profit, and who can say with certainty that there would have been profit? He is therefore exempt. Rashba writes likewise in a responsum.
