Where the custom of the country is to write, etc. See Responsa Maharash Kohen, book 3, siman 106.
With every formulation that protects his rights, etc. See Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 101.
It applies to all the general clauses at the beginning of the matter, etc. The Sema writes that this means that what he accepted upon himself to write 'with every formulation that protects his rights' applies only to general rules and matters that it is reasonable to say were within his original intent. It appears to me that he did not interpret correctly. Rather, the meaning is that it applies to all the general clauses stated at the beginning of that subject, namely all the general clauses of responsibility mentioned at the beginning of the matter. Likewise, if he wrote, 'The credibility clause of this document I accept upon myself like all documents, etc.,' it applies to all general clauses of credibility, not to the clauses of responsibility. This is explicit in Baal HaItur at the end of maamar 5, folio 12d: Rabbeinu Hai states that one who undertakes to write a document in every formulation that protects the other party's rights, even if the scribe does not know how to strengthen it properly, once the document states that it bears responsibility, everything written above is included. Examine this carefully.
But conditions, such as a credibility clause in a loan. Meaning, even where the practice is to write a credibility clause in all documents, as stated in the Ritva, we do not deem it included unless it was specified, for perhaps his intent was not to write a credibility clause. This applies where it is not known that the borrower knew of the custom to write such a clause in all documents. But if he knew the custom, then even if it was not written it is treated as though written. The Rosh and Tur write that regarding anything customarily written in the country, whether a credibility clause or otherwise, one who makes a sudar kinyan for a document intends that it be written according to local custom. It is strained to say that the Rosh and Tur refer only where the credibility clause was explicitly written in the document, because their reason is that anyone who makes a sudar kinyan for writing intends that the document be drawn according to the custom of the country. This implies that even before the document is written, the scribe may include the customary credibility clause, because presumably that too was intended. How, then, can the Rama here write that a credibility clause is not deemed included unless specified? Necessarily the Rama speaks where it is possible that the borrower did not know the custom. This is also clear from what the authorities and the Tur write above at the end of siman 42, that wording which people commonly write in documents is followed even when it is not a rabbinic enactment, and even if omitted we judge as though it were written. This also resolves the difficulty raised by Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 26, folio 120, who notes an apparent contradiction in the Ritva. The Ritva writes in the name of Rav Hai that even where a credibility clause is customary in all documents it is not included unless stated explicitly, whereas Rav Hai also holds that we interpret documents according to common wording. The Gidulei Terumah leaves this unresolved. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. The rule that we interpret common wording applies specifically where it is known that the party knew this was the customary wording. This appears correct to me. I have also demonstrated this above at the end of siman 42, see there.
For double, etc. The Sema, seif katan 12, writes that specifically where he accepted upon himself to pay so excessive an amount is it an asmachta. But if he accepted upon himself merely to reimburse the expense, it is not an asmachta, because he obligated himself to pay the loss that he caused. In this way the Sema resolves the difficulty raised in Darkei Moshe from the rule of 'if I leave the field fallow I will pay from the best,' in siman 207, which the Bach also cites. If, however, the claimant had merchandise ready to purchase and claims that he would have earned a profit from it, even if the document states that the obligor undertook to pay all losses caused to him, he is exempt, as the Tur writes and as the Beit Yosef cites from a responsum of the Rashba. The reason is that preventing profit is not called loss, as stated in the Yerushalmi cited by the authorities: one who idles his fellow's money has against him only a grievance. It is even less than indirect damage, for which one is exempt. Moreover, who can guarantee that there would certainly have been a profit? Perhaps there would have been a loss. So writes the Bach. See below, siman 292, seif 6, in the gloss, and what I write there. However, what appears from the Bach, that the Rama's difficulty in Darkei Moshe is thereby resolved, is incorrect. Clearly the Rama's principal difficulty there concerns the responsum of the Rosh where actual expenses had been incurred and there was therefore an actual loss. This is why the Rama writes in Darkei Moshe, 'But here, where he caused him a loss, it is not an asmachta.' We therefore require the Sema's explanation that the Rosh speaks where the claimant seeks expenses beyond what was proper. Examine this carefully.
