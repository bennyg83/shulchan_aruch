Beit Yosef copied it from Responsa Rashba. It appears to depend on the dispute below in siman 210 concerning one who confers a right upon a fetus "when it is born," whether the acquisition is valid. Tosafot in Gittin 13b explain the distinction. Rav Huna holds that even if one says, "Acquire for the fetus when it is born," the acquisition does not work. But Rav Nachman, who rejects R. Meir's general rule of conveying future property yet nevertheless holds that one can confer upon a fetus when it is born, demonstrates that acquiring for a presently nonexistent recipient can be easier than transferring a presently nonexistent object. Therefore, according to the Sages, and all the more according to R. Meir, one may convey to a person not yet in existence under the proper formulation. Tosafot in the first chapter of Ketubot say similarly. From R. Meir we can therefore infer for our own law of liens. We rule that a person can obligate or encumber himself regarding property not yet in existence. All the more so, he should be able to create a lien in favor of a person not yet born. In this particular form even the Sages may agree. Rosh below in siman 210 holds that one who confers upon a fetus acquires if he says, "when it is born." He therefore omitted the qualification that the holder must already have been born. The lien can become effective in favor of a later-born person. Rashba, however, holds like Rav Huna that even saying, "when it is born," does not work. For him a recipient not yet in existence is worse. Therefore he requires that the bearer already have been born when the obligation was created. This distinction is clear. Rama rules below in siman 210 not like Rosh, and therefore he did not add a gloss here against Shulchan Aruch. But as I explain there, the matter is a major dispute among the authorities. The present law accordingly depends on that same dispute. Bnei Yaakov asks: if Rosh holds that acquisition to a fetus works when one says, "when it is born," why does the Gemara say the rule of ma'amad sheloshtan is a halachah without reason? Could we not simply say that the debtor undertakes in favor of whoever later comes into existence? There is no difficulty. Since Rav Huna considered ma'amad sheloshtan an inexplicable halachah, why should we invent an entirely different basis for Rav Nachman and thereby create a new dispute concerning the nature of ma'amad sheloshtan? The accepted rule is that these three cases are exceptional enactments without ordinary reason. Moreover, according to Rosh, ma'amad sheloshtan can work even against the debtor's will. Suppose the debtor expressly protests, "I do not want to become obligated to anyone except my original creditor." The transfer still works. That cannot be explained by saying the debtor himself created a future lien. In an ordinary future-beneficiary obligation, the obligor can retract before the beneficiary comes into existence, as Rosh writes in the fetus case. Here, however, the debtor cannot retract. Therefore ma'amad sheloshtan remains a special rule. There is another issue. If the holder's right takes effect only when he is born, a note that purports to create a lien from the earlier date can become antedated. Who can determine whether the bearer had already been born when the note was executed? He might collect from property sold during the interval before his birth, even though his lien arose only later. This itself can explain why the Gemara does not simply extend the general rule to later-born bearers. The danger of an antedated lien is real. I used this point to answer another strong question, cited in Batei Kehunah in the name of a great authority. Ran in Kiddushin and Tosafot at the beginning of Perek Ben Sorer hold that by Torah law there is no way to acquire for a minor, because acquisition on another's behalf is based on agency and a minor has no agency. If so, why does the Gemara ask why Scripture needs the rule "he shall recognize" the firstborn, since the father could simply give him a gift? Perhaps the case concerns a minor son, to whom he cannot transfer anything. The answer is that the Gemara's question must already follow the view that acquisition to a fetus works "when it is born." Otherwise, even if we assume there is some Torah mode of acquiring for a minor, the verse could still be needed where the father's deceased son left a pregnant widow and the father now says that deceased son was the firstborn so that the unborn grandchild will inherit the double share. A gift to the fetus would not work according to the opposite view. Therefore the Gemara's question necessarily proceeds according to Rav Nachman, who allows acquisition to a fetus when born. This fits especially well according to those who identify the unnamed Rav Nachman there as Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak, who himself asks the question. One might object that even according to Rav Nachman, if the grandfather dies before the fetus is born, Rosh says the gift lapses. But that is only according to our halachah, which does not follow R. Meir. In the Gemara's discussion according to R. Meir, acquisition of future matters attaches more strongly and may survive the donor's death. Once the question is placed within R. Meir's framework, the difficulty from a minor also disappears. According to Rav Nachman, acquisition conditioned on the minor later becoming an adult should be no worse than acquisition conditioned on a fetus later being born. R. Meir allows transfer of future matters. Therefore the father could say that the gift should take effect when the child matures and becomes legally capable of acquisition. Ran and Tosafot deny acquisition only while the child remains a minor and only according to the Sages who reject future-property acquisition. Thus the Gemara still needs the special recognition verse only for property that arrives later in circumstances where ordinary gift mechanisms cannot solve the problem. Even though I have established that according to Rosh a lien can arise in favor of someone born only later, a further issue remains where the note includes recourse against purchasers. If the note is held by someone born after the loan date, perhaps it is antedated. His lien began only when he was born and became capable of acquiring the obligation. Yet the document bears the earlier date. This is especially problematic in a bearer note written simply "to whoever produces it." One might later transfer it to someone who did not yet exist at the time of the loan. The document can then facilitate collection from purchasers for a period during which no lien in favor of that person existed. It therefore requires study whether such a note is valid against purchasers in the hands of a later-born bearer. Perhaps it should be treated like a postdated or otherwise defective lien document to the extent it can create collection not justified by the true date of the beneficiary's rights. On the other hand, the debtor does remain substantively obligated throughout, so perhaps the danger is not classified as unlawful collection in the ordinary sense. The matter requires study.
