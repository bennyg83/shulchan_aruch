A document in which it is written. One must say that the first creditor acquires on behalf of every later holder. Otherwise, even though the obligor says he is indebted to whoever produces the instrument, he could retract before it ever reaches a later holder, just as an obligor can retract from an undelivered document. Therefore the first creditor acquires for all who may later produce it, and after that the obligor cannot retract. According to this, if a gentile later produces the instrument, the original acquisition cannot have been made for him through ordinary agency because a gentile has no agency in Jewish law. This explains the Mechaber's phrase that the undertaking can be written to whoever produces it, whether Jew or gentile. The novelty is that it is at least effective for a Jewish holder, and it can also become effective for a gentile once the instrument is actually delivered to him by authorization. Before such delivery, however, the obligor can retract as to the gentile portion.
So that any holder may collect. Because this is the foundation of our bearer notes, I will explain it at length, together with the laws of bererah. Rosh rules that a document that does not name a creditor but obligates the debtor to whoever produces it is effective. The witnesses need not have known the future creditor. He proves this from a document saying, "I borrowed from you a maneh," where the identity of "you" is not written, and from the rule that one may obligate himself "to you and to anyone who comes through you." He further proves it from Saul's promise that whoever struck the Philistine would be enriched, which created a monetary obligation to David even though David had not been identified in advance. Maharit questions the proof from David, arguing that the recipient must eventually become identified and make the relevant acquisition, but that question does not undermine the central point. Once David killed Goliath, Saul's promise became a debt to him, and David later could use that debt for kiddushin. The obligation therefore arose from the general undertaking even though the recipient had not been named at the outset. The Tur likewise allows collection by one who comes through the original lender. His phrase that the holder says, "You borrowed from me and I did not need to write my name," does not mean that only the original lender can collect. The Tur adds that where a personal handwritten note is produced, the debtor may claim through a migo of payment that he never borrowed from this holder but from another person who lost the note and that the present holder found it. That wording itself shows that a person who actually comes through the original lender can collect even though he did not lend the money himself. Tosafot in Bava Batra also explains that "from you, even from the Exilarch," means even one whom we know did not personally lend can collect when he comes through the proper chain. The obvious question is bererah. At the time of the obligation, it was not yet determined which person would later hold the note. Ramban writes regarding "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you" that on the Torah level the transfer may depend upon bererah, and therefore the original creditor retains the power of waiver. Ran offers a broader answer: the problem of bererah arises where choosing one necessarily excludes the others. Here the debtor can be obligated successively to every person in the world who later becomes a proper holder. It is therefore not a case of "if this one, not that one." Nimukei Yosef says similarly that because the obligation is capable of applying to every person, there is no true bererah. Maharit proposes another framework. The cases of Saul and "whoever informs me what my wife gave birth to shall receive a maneh" operate through agency or employment. One who says, "Do such and such for me and I will pay you," becomes obligated when the act is performed, without any separate kinyan. At the time of performance the recipient is already identified. He therefore distinguishes between an obligation intended to arise immediately, where lack of present identification can create bererah, and an obligation designed to arise only when the future person performs the service, when identification and acquisition occur together. This is elegant and finds support in the teaching concerning redemption of fourth year produce: an act that takes effect only after the produce is picked may avoid bererah because the relevant object has become identified by then. Rashba, however, indicates that a mere future clarification does not always cure bererah. If a woman appoints any person whom her husband later chooses to receive her get, Rashba says bererah may prevent the appointment, even though by the time the get is actually received the person will be known. The decisive distinction is therefore agency itself. Where one says to ten people, "Write a get," all ten are presently agents. It is irrelevant which two eventually sign. Likewise, "any two of you shall write" creates agency in the entire group, and whichever two act do so under a present authorization. There is no later selection that retroactively turns one into an agent. The same explains Saul's promise and "whoever informs me": everyone capable of performing is presently authorized to perform the service, and the one who actually performs earns the compensation. It also explains formulas such as, "Whoever hears my voice shall write," or, "Whoever hears shall provide food." The problem of bererah begins where present status itself depends on a later selection, as where a woman says, "Whoever my husband later wants shall be my agent," or where a man says he divorces whichever wife exits the door first. There one future event must determine which person had the legal status now. This also resolves the question from one who says at Sukkot, "Whoever receives my lulav shall have it as a gift." He gives the right to everyone subject only to actual acquisition. Whoever receives it acquires through the ordinary act of taking. No person must retroactively be selected as the sole intended recipient. Maharit also suggested that lien differs from acquisition, because one can encumber future property even where one cannot sell it. He therefore proposed that a person can obligate himself toward an unidentified person. His reasoning was challenged because one cannot obligate himself to a person not in existence at all. Yet an unidentified existing person is not identical to a nonexistent person. Tosafot distinguishes a gentile who has not yet converted, who exists but lacks the final legal status, from one not yet born. Since lien is stronger than ordinary transfer and all potential holders already exist as persons, there is substantial room for the obligation. Rabbi Meir's rule concerning future property also clarifies much of the law. If one can transfer something not yet in existence, then an object presently in existence but not yet identified should certainly not be worse. Thus Rabbi Meir can say, "The two log that I will later separate are terumah," despite lack of present identification. The Gemara's assumption that this requires bererah reflects the law according to the Sages, who deny transfer of future property. For Rabbi Meir, lack of present identification is no greater obstacle than lack of present existence. This explains a difficulty in Eruvin. Rabbi Shimon prohibits the two future log, and the Gemara initially attributes this to lack of bererah, yet elsewhere he seems to accept bererah in eruv. One answer is that for the two log there is an additional requirement that the remaining produce be identifiable, or a danger that the barrel may break. Another is that bererah is especially problematic where the contemplated future selection may never occur. If one says, "the two log that I will later separate," perhaps he will never separate them. By contrast, a condition that must eventually be resolved, such as whether a person dies or whether a father consents, has stronger force. Several authorities formulate a general rule that where the matter will certainly become clarified one way or another, bererah can be effective; where the contemplated selection may never happen, it cannot. The sugyot concerning an eruv dependent on a sage arriving from east or west show that Rabbi Yochanan may take a stricter view and reject bererah even where eventual clarification is likely, unless the sage had already arrived. Our practical law need not follow that extreme position in every context. This helps explain Rambam's ruling concerning the pious people who designated money and said that whatever fourth year produce would be picked should be redeemed upon it. Although Rambam generally rules that there is no bererah, this case can differ because all the produce can potentially be redeemed, and the condition is simply whether a given portion will be picked. There is no need to choose one object to the exclusion of another. The matter will either be picked or not picked. This is much closer to a condition than to classical bererah. Maharshal distinguishes the get that takes effect one hour before death. If the condition is simply, "if I die, this is a get," the matter will certainly clarify as life or death. If the get is to take effect during one unidentified hour among many before death from this illness, there is a stronger bererah problem because the relevant hour is not presently known and perhaps death will not occur from that illness at all. Maharsha refines this by saying that when the legal effect itself occurs only in the final hour, that hour is identified at the very time the get becomes effective. This resembles the rule that an acquisition made effective only immediately before theft can work when that moment itself later arrives. The conceptual question is always whether the law must retroactively choose among presently competing objects or persons, or whether the operative legal event occurs only once the relevant subject has become defined. This also answers the cases in Bava Kamma concerning one who declares whatever the poor will pick to be ownerless, and the pious people who redeem whatever thieves are about to pick. If the entire vineyard could potentially become ownerless or redeemed, there is no necessary exclusion of one portion by another. The condition simply says that every portion that reaches the triggering event acquires the stated status. Ran's formulation, that bererah applies only where "if this, not that," is therefore exact. Where every member of the class can receive the same legal status in succession or together, the issue is not ordinary bererah. The practical rule for bearer instruments follows directly. The obligor can undertake liability to the entire class of legitimate future holders, one after another. The holder's later possession does not retroactively create a previously nonexistent creditor. Rather, it identifies the person who presently falls within a class already embraced by the undertaking. Therefore the bearer obligation is effective and can be treated as a Torah level obligation under Ran and Nimukei Yosef, not merely a rabbinic device. This also explains why ordinary agency concerns do not undermine Jewish holders. For a gentile holder, ordinary agency cannot perform the initial acquisition on his behalf, but actual authorized delivery can still create his right. The entire subject thus rests upon the difference between selection among mutually exclusive alternatives and a present obligation capable of extending to every proper holder.
