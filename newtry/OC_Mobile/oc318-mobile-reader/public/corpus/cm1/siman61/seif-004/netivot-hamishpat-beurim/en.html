So that whoever produces it may collect. This ruling is from Responsa Rosh, kelal 28, siman 8. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 23, objects to it. The substance of his argument is that where the obligation takes effect immediately, while the eventual holder is not yet identified, the arrangement should fail because there is no bereirah, just as with one who slaughters the Pesach for whoever first reaches Jerusalem, or one who redeems produce upon whichever sela will come into his hand. See there at length. In my humble opinion the ruling can be sustained. Where a person transfers a presently defined object and merely makes the transaction conditional upon an event that is not yet defined, it is a complete acquisition. It is no worse than making a transaction conditional upon a matter that has not yet come into existence, which is effective, as Mordechai explains from the case, "when you steal it and repay me," at the beginning of Perek HaMafkid. Anyone who makes a condition dependent upon a future event is in some sense making it depend upon something not presently determined. Otherwise, even one who makes a condition dependent upon rainfall would be making it depend upon something not presently determined, because rain depends upon the One who holds the key of rain. Rashi in Gittin 25 similarly explains that a condition dependent upon death is dependent upon the One in whose hand are life and death. If that alone created a problem of bereirah, most conditions would be uprooted. We also do not find a Tanna who says that one who gives a bill of divorce "from today if I die" fails because of bereirah. Rabbi Yehudah holds that the date written in a document proves that it takes effect from now, and yet he does not accept bereirah, and the Gemara in Gittin 25 and elsewhere does not challenge him from that Mishnah. Rather, where the body of the acquisition is presently defined, a condition dependent upon an unidentified future event is effective because the condition is a separate legal matter. This resembles conditioning a transaction upon something not yet in existence while the subject of the transaction itself already exists. The Gemara's challenge to those who accept bereirah from the question, "What is her status during those days?" is not a case of an ordinary condition taking effect from today. It concerns one who says, "from the time when I am still in the world," meaning that he divorces her during the final hour before his death. There the very effective moment of the divorce is unidentified, as Tosafot themselves explain, and Ramban's novellae to Gittin likewise state. Tosafot's principal proof that bereirah can arise even in the setting of a condition is from the Gemara's challenge concerning one who says, "I hereby have relations with you for kiddushin on condition that my father desires." In my view that too can be explained. The sugya there follows Rav Yehudah, who answered Rav Hoshaya that there is no bereirah even where the matter depends on another person's will. Rav Yehudah also holds in Gittin 74 and in Kiddushin concerning "on condition that he gives two hundred zuz" that fulfillment is required later and that "on condition" is not equivalent to "from now." At first glance this creates a major difficulty from the Mishnah that if one says, "I hereby have relations with you on condition that my father desires," and the father later desires, she is betrothed. If "on condition" does not mean that the kiddushin take effect immediately, then the kiddushin are not completed until the father later consents, by which time the act of relations has already ended. It should resemble one who betroths a woman by document to take effect after thirty days and the document is torn before then, where Kiddushin and Gittin state that she is not betrothed. Necessarily, according to Rav Yehudah, that Mishnah works through bereirah. Because the transaction was made dependent upon the father's will, once the father later consents we say that it became clarified that his will attached already at the time of the kiddushin, and the kiddushin were completed at the original act. Ramban likewise writes that where the matter depends upon another's will there is true bereirah, unlike the condition involving two hundred zuz. Accordingly, the Gemara properly proves from that Mishnah, according to Rav Yehudah, that bereirah must apply. But according to our ruling that "on condition" is equivalent to "from now," the kiddushin are completed immediately and the father's later desire is merely a condition. In an ordinary condition there is no need for bereirah, as explained. Therefore we rule according to that Mishnah even though we generally rule that there is no bereirah. According to one who holds that "on condition" is not equivalent to "from now," however, if there is no bereirah the kiddushin would indeed fail because when the matter becomes clarified the act of relations is already over. From this it appears that the principle of no bereirah applies specifically where, at the later time of clarification, the acquisition could no longer take effect, so that one must say retroactively that the matter had already been clarified at the original time. In the case dependent upon the father's desire, if the kiddushin cannot take effect at the later time because the act is over, one needs bereirah to treat the matter as clarified at the original act. Likewise, regarding the status of the woman during the final days, the clarification comes only after the husband's death, when he is no longer capable of divorce, so retroactive clarification is required. So too, one who slaughters the Pesach for whoever first arrives has already slaughtered it when the identity becomes known and no new person can then join it. One who says, "upon whichever sela comes into my hand," refers to a case where he wishes to eat the produce immediately, so by the time the coin is identified the produce has already been eaten and can no longer be redeemed. If the produce still exists when the sela is selected, it appears that the redemption is effective. Similarly, if one says, "I will divorce whichever wife I later choose," or "whichever one exits the doorway," the bill has already been written by the time the woman becomes identified, and it was not written specifically for her. One therefore needs bereirah to treat the writing as having been for her from the outset. The same applies where one says that whatever the poor later gather should be ownerless, because after they gather it he no longer has power to declare it ownerless. All the Mishnayot and baraitot cited in the sugyot of bereirah concern this type of case. But where, at the time the matter becomes identified, the acquisition could still take effect, then even if one said "from now," the transaction is effective. Since he could have arranged for the acquisition to take effect when the matter becomes identified, as Maharit himself concedes, he can also arrange that it take effect from now subject to that future identification. This resembles the opinion of Rabbi Meir that a person can transfer something not yet in existence, where Tosafot in Yevamot 93 write that he can make it effective from now as well. Thus Rosh properly rules that a person may obligate himself even from now to whoever ultimately produces the document, because he can obligate himself when that person becomes identified, and therefore can also obligate himself from now in that form. Ran, at the beginning of Gittin concerning the language, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you," distinguishes in another way. He writes that no bereirah applies where both parties can successively hold the right, and the issue is not necessarily one or the other. Maharit uses this to challenge Rosh, arguing that Ran's reason applies only because all later holders derive from the first creditor. Where the debtor initially obligates himself to an unidentified person and the right is necessarily for one rather than another, there should be a bereirah problem. At first glance I do not understand this objection, because even there Ran's reasoning can apply. The debtor can obligate himself successively to all persons in the world, even if they do not derive their rights from the first creditor. The creditor's intent is precisely that the debtor be liable to whoever presents the document, even one who did not receive it through him. Maharit further writes that perhaps only a monetary lien can be created, but not a personal obligation. This too is difficult, for if bereirah prevents the arrangement, why should even a property lien be valid? It appears that Maharit's intent is as follows. Ran's case, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you," necessarily takes effect from now, because the later holder collects from the date of the original document. If so, one might still say that the rights are mutually exclusive. But where the later person derives from the first creditor, the original creditor can transfer the lien on the property without needing the debtor's new undertaking. The debtor's original commitment to anyone coming through the first creditor is needed mainly for the personal obligation, so that the debtor cannot tell the assignee, "You are not my litigant," because the original creditor cannot transfer that personal aspect without the debtor's undertaking. Since the debtor can subject his person successively to everyone who derives through the first creditor, the later holder can sue him. The property lien follows from the original creditor's transferable right. By contrast, to subject both his person and his property from now to whichever unidentified person later produces the document, without any transfer from the first creditor, appears to be a mutually exclusive choice. This seems to be Maharit's intent. Nevertheless, Rosh's reasoning is as explained above, and that is the principal view. Rosh brings proof from Shaul, who said that the man who would strike Goliath would be enriched by the king with great wealth. David then struck him, and Shaul became liable to David for that wealth. Sanhedrin, chapter two, states that David betrothed Shaul's daughter with that monetary claim. Maharit objects that kiddushin requires the man to say, "You are betrothed to me." If the beneficiary had not yet been identified at the original promise, who said the formula? He therefore concludes that when David came before Shaul and Shaul dressed him in his armor, the condition was repeated and David then said that Shaul's daughter should be betrothed to him with that benefit, at which point David was already identified. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that if this happened before David killed Goliath, when Shaul dressed him in armor, the kiddushin would not be kiddushin with a loan, whereas Sanhedrin implies that the issue was indeed kiddushin with a loan. Ketzot therefore says that after David killed Goliath and Shaul became indebted to him for the promised wealth, David said that Shaul's daughter should be betrothed to him with that debt. But that cannot be correct, because after Goliath was killed, David's unilateral declaration would accomplish nothing if Shaul immediately retracted, as implied by the Gemara there. The Gemara says that Shaul reasoned that David had betrothed her with a loan, implying that there was no intervening new act of consensual kiddushin. Otherwise, why would Shaul need the rule that one who betroths with a loan does not effect kiddushin? Rather, before David killed Goliath, with Shaul's consent, David made the kiddushin declaration. The objection that this resembles saying, "in payment for work I will do for you," is no difficulty. David did not go to kill Goliath as hired labor for purposes of kiddushin. He went for the sake of Heaven to save Israel. Shaul independently obligated himself to enrich whoever would strike Goliath, and David thereafter betrothed Shaul's daughter with that obligation, which Shaul had undertaken from then. Rashi in Sanhedrin likewise explains the "loan" as the promised great wealth. Ketzot also answers Maharit's challenge from the case, "Whoever receives my lulav, it is given to him as a gift," where there should seemingly be no bereirah. He relies on Rashba's distinction concerning agents: where a woman appoints several people as agents, there is no bereirah because all could serve as agents, but if she says, "those whom my husband later chooses shall be the agents," bereirah applies. Ketzot says that in the lulav case the owner gives it to everyone, while actual acquisition remains dependent only on each recipient's taking possession, whereas in the case of whichever woman exits the door the very identity of the divorcee was missing from the original act. In my view this distinction is as thin as a spider's web. There is much more to say concerning bereirah, but this is not the place. For present purposes, Rosh's ruling concerning whoever produces the document should be maintained.
Without authorization. Even according to Shach in siman 66, seif katan 10, who holds that the holder is not believed to say that he purchased the document without a written transfer and delivery, when the holder comes to collect from the debtor, beit din does not suspect that the document was lost by the debtor or deposited by him with the holder. The debtor himself agreed to this arrangement and wrote in the document that payment should be made to whoever produces it without requiring authorization.
And he collects with it. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that whoever produces the document acquires it because the original creditor becomes the agent of the eventual holder and acquires it on his behalf. Otherwise, since the document never reached the later holder, the debtor could retract. According to this, if the later holder is a non Jew, for whom Jewish agency does not operate, the debtor could retract before the document reaches him. This is not correct. At the time of the loan, the debtor can certainly obligate himself even orally. Rashba derives this very rule from the formulation in the first chapter of Gittin, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you," which can apply even orally. All the more so, when he says, "Write a document," he can certainly create a personal obligation before witnesses. Even where he can retract from having a document written, he cannot retract from the underlying obligation. One may retract concerning the document but not concerning the field itself, as explained in siman 243. Here he cannot even retract concerning the document, because there is no concern of multiplying publicity and documents, since the original document has already reached the first creditor. For the same reason the witnesses may write a replacement when a document is erased even without renewed consent from the debtor, as Tosafot write. Moreover, the original creditor can himself transfer the document and its property lien to another even without the debtor's consent. The debtor's special condition is needed only to create the personal obligation to the eventual holder, and from that undertaking the debtor cannot retract because he assumed it at the time of the loan. Responsa Rosh, kelal 68, siman 7, writes that it is obvious that a non Jew cannot collect through such a document if the witnesses could not acquire the right on his behalf, since a non Jew has no Jewish law of acquisition through another. Even so, the fact that the document eventually reached him does not help according to that reasoning, contrary to Ketzot. There is certainly no basis to say that the original creditor automatically acquires for whoever will ultimately produce the document. Who instructed him to acquire on behalf of every eventual holder? Even if the debtor expressly said, "Acquire for whoever later produces it," that itself would involve bereirah, like one who says, "I slaughter the Pesach for whoever arrives first." Rosh therefore invokes the principle that the witnesses' signatures effect acquisition for the beneficiary, following Abaye. Yet Rosh's explanation itself is difficult. According to Rif and other authorities who do not follow Abaye's principle in that form, writing for whoever produces the document should not work. Furthermore, the document merely states that the holder may collect without authorization, which implies that the holder still comes by virtue of the original creditor, only without a separate authorization. This is fundamentally a condition between debtor and creditor, and such a condition does not require the principle of acquisition through signatures. Indeed, according to many authorities even an actual transfer of a debt document can be accomplished by delivery without a written assignment, because at the time the creditor gives the debtor the money it is as though he says, "Take this maneh and transfer or subordinate your field to so and so," which is effective. See what I wrote above in simanim 184 and 103. It therefore appears that Rosh only intended to offer some rationale for the prevailing practice. In truth we rule that the arrangement is effective, as Tur and the Mechaber state here and as Beit Yosef explains. Rosh writes that a non Jew should not collect because a document may be written for a borrower even in the lender's absence. We must therefore consider the possibility that the document reached the non Jew before it ever reached the original creditor. In that event the non Jew certainly could not collect through the doctrine that witnesses' signatures acquire for the beneficiary, because that doctrine does not apply to a non Jew. For this reason one should not initially write such a document for a non Jew. But this is only some explanation for the custom. After the fact, even a non Jew who possesses the document collects with it, because we need not suspect that exceptional sequence. The Mechaber therefore states without qualification that the holder collects, which implies even a non Jew.
Whether a non Jew. See Sema, seif katan 9, through his statement that there is a prohibition involved. Tumim wonders what prohibition exists. In my humble opinion there is a clear prohibition. It is no less problematic than selling land adjacent to a Jew to a non Jew, for which the seller is placed under sanction because the Jewish neighbor can say, "You have placed a lion beside me." All the more so, one who sells a Jew's debt document to a non Jew exposes the debtor to potential abusive claims through the non Jew. Possibly it is forbidden even with the debtor's consent because purchasers of the debtor's property may suffer wrongful loss at the hands of the non Jew.
Who was born afterward. Tumim asks that Tosafot in Gittin 13 write that according to Rav Nachman, transferring to a person not yet in existence is more effective than transferring an object not yet in existence. Since we rule that although a person cannot transfer an object not yet in existence, he can create a lien upon future acquired property, all the more so he should be able to obligate himself to a beneficiary not yet born. This is no difficulty. Nimukei Yosef writes that a person can subordinate future acquired property because of the policy concern of not closing the door before borrowers. That concern does not apply here. Nor can one prove it from the fact that a person may obligate himself in an amount concerning something not yet in existence, because that rests on a different principle: he subjects his own person, and his person already exists. Tumim further cites a difficulty in the name of a Gaon. According to Tosafot at the beginning of Perek Ben Sorer in Sanhedrin, a minor has no capacity of acquisition at all. Why then does the Gemara in Bava Batra ask why Scripture needs the rule of "he shall recognize" concerning the firstborn, since the father could simply give that son the extra portion as a gift? Scripture would still be needed where the father identifies a minor son as firstborn. In my humble opinion the answer is that the father can transfer the property to another on condition that the other transfer it to the minor when he reaches majority. An acquisition on condition to convey onward is effective even for one who does not yet exist, as explained in Nedarim 48, see Rashi and Ran there. Tumim also asks that according to the view that "acquire, you and a donkey" is ineffective, a document payable to whoever produces it should fail when it includes persons not yet born, for it is equivalent to "acquire, you and a donkey." In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. The defective case is "you and a donkey," where one present and one incapable recipient are both made immediate coacquirers. But where the formulation is effectively "you or a donkey," meaning whichever person later performs the specified act, the acquisition is not completed until the identity is clarified, and therefore it is effective. Moreover, I proved in siman 203 that the rule of "you and a donkey" applies specifically to a kinyan sudar. See there.
