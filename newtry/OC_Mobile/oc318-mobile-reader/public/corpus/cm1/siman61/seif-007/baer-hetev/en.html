Money. The Sema explains that the case appears to have been as follows. Reuven and Shimon were partners in several matters. Shimon executed a document obligating himself to Reuven up to one thousand gold coins. The document stated that whatever amount an accounting would show remained in Shimon's hands belonging to Reuven, and that Reuven would provide in his own signed handwriting, Shimon would owe under this witnessed document, enabling Reuven to collect that amount from purchasers. They wrote one thousand gold coins because they knew that Shimon certainly did not owe more than that amount, though the actual balance might be less. The questioner was uncertain from which date Reuven could collect: from the first of Nisan, the date of the witnessed document, or only from the first of Iyar, when Reuven's handwritten accounting was authenticated and the exact debt was thereby established. The answer was that he collects from the date of the original document, because publicity of the lien arose then and purchasers should have refrained from buying. Certainly the document is considered prior to a creditor who lent after that date. Purchasers cannot argue that until the handwritten accounting established the exact sum there was no definite debt. They should still have refrained from purchasing because perhaps Reuven gave Shimon his written accounting immediately. A private handwritten document has no public notice, and even beit din authentication of such handwriting has no public notice upon which purchasers can rely. For this reason one does not collect from purchasers on the basis of a handwritten document even when it has been authenticated by beit din, as stated in siman 69. Thus concludes the Sema.
