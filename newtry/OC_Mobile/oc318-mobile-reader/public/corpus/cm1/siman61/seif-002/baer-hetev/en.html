A draft memorandum. The Tur states: A document that says, 'So-and-so became obligated to so-and-so for such-and-such an amount of gold coins, to pay them at such-and-such a time, with a trust clause, liability, and every strengthening formula the scribe may add,' and the witnesses sign beneath it, is called a memorandum form. They recorded it as a reminder of the matters done before them at the time of the kinyan, so that on its basis they could later write a complete document when they had time. Thus explains the Sema.

They disagree. The Shach writes that in his view there is actually no dispute. The Mechaber, following the Rivash, speaks where the borrower became obligated before two witnesses in a document for one hundred, but did not obligate himself that the scribe would write the document. The witnesses themselves were merely to produce a complete instrument. A draft entered in the scribe's book therefore has no legal status as a document, because they left it with the scribe and never wrote it for delivery to the creditor. The dissenting view cited by Rama follows the Rosh and concerns a different case: the borrower undertook that the witnesses would write that he was obligated to make a document from that day onward with every strengthening formula the scribe would add, and he accepted this through kinyan before the witnesses. If he then fails to do so, the witnesses' own writing stands in place of the scribe's document because they delivered their written testimony to the creditor. Review the Rivash and the Rosh's responsum and their words align precisely with this distinction. Nevertheless, according to all opinions the borrower must authorize the writing. A document written without the borrower's authorization is invalid and no judgment may be based upon it; it does not even have the status of valid witness testimony. Thus concludes the Shach.
