The text of the document. The Shach distinguishes two cases. If the borrower merely obligated himself before witnesses for a maneh, and the witnesses later allowed a scribe to formulate the writing, the scribe's expanded text does not become a document when it was not prepared for delivery to the creditor. If, however, the borrower accepted through kinyan that the witnesses should prepare a document with all the standard enhancements the scribe would normally write, then the witnesses' writing can itself stand in place of the scribe's form and be delivered to the creditor. This reconciles the Rosh and Rivash. The key principle is that writing retained under the witnesses and not intended for delivery has no legal status as a document. Rif in Gittin explains that testimony through writing is invalid where the writing remains under the witnesses' control, because until it leaves their possession their testimony has not yet become fixed. Ramban elaborates that even if the writing once left their hands and was later deposited back with them, if it now serves only as their memorandum rather than as an operative document in the hands of a litigant or third party, its contents cannot be used merely because the witnesses read it. A true document delivered for legal use is treated as testimony already examined in beit din. A private memorandum held by the witnesses is not. Therefore the validity of the scribe's form depends upon whether the borrower authorized the witnesses to create a complete instrument for delivery to the creditor.
