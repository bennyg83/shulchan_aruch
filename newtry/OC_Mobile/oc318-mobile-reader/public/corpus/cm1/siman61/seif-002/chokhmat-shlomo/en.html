Seif 13. One who disputes a provision in his wife’s ketubah, etc. Here it appears that even a document executed by witnesses, without the husband’s personal signature, can bind him to what is written, since the husband does not personally sign the ketubah. Yet Sema in siman 45, seif 3, citing Ramban, implies that the principle applies specifically where the party himself signed. The sources therefore seem inconsistent. It appears that one must distinguish between ancillary provisions and a genuinely new obligation. Where the added language does not create the principal liability but merely concerns subsidiary terms, such as credibility clauses or similar matters, no new monetary obligation is created and the party’s silent consent can suffice. A document signed by witnesses may therefore bind him in such matters even without his personal signature. But where the writing creates a new condition or new monetary undertaking, a person ordinarily cannot bind himself except through an effective document or by instructing witnesses to write such a document. There, actual verbal authorization is required and mere silence is not enough. Thus, if he did not personally sign, his reliance on the witnesses does not create a new obligation unless he expressly authorized it. A personal signature, by contrast, can bind him even if he did not appreciate the particular new term. This distinction appears correct and clear in practical law.
