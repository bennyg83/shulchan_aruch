But if she was a niddah and afterward he brought her under the chuppah. The Rosh writes that if she became a niddah only afterward, he had already acquired marital rights at the beginning of the chuppah, because the end of chuppah is the beginning of anticipated relations; thus chuppah effects acquisition from its outset. Mordechai, however, implies that even if she was pure when she entered the chuppah, if she became a niddah during the chuppah the affection associated with anticipated relations ceased. This is difficult: if he already acquired her at the beginning of chuppah, how could the acquisition later be undone? Perhaps Mordechai holds that the end of chuppah effects the acquisition and the beginning of relations follows only after chuppah, so the acquisition is completed at the end of the seven days of celebration or at the beginning of relations. Mordechai further writes that the case must be one where she became a niddah at her expected period, for if it occurred outside that period she can say, "your fortune caused the loss," as appears from the first chapter of Ketubot. His words are difficult. First, the Gemara itself asks whether, because some women change their regular periods, an unexpected onset should nevertheless be treated like the expected period. Second, that question arose only on the premise that if he or she became ill he must still support her because his field was stricken. Rav Ashi concludes that in every unavoidable circumstance she does not receive support, even where he himself became ill. All the more so if she became a niddah outside her expected period, and all the authorities rule so above concerning support. The same should apply here. Even if she was divorced against her will after erusin, she does not receive the additional ketubah amount, as explained above, siman 54:6, because he added that amount only on condition of bringing her to a chuppah fit for relations. If an unavoidable circumstance makes her unfit for relations, what difference does the nature of the accident make? He never undertook the addition except upon entering a chuppah fit for relations. The Bach calls Mordechai's statement an explicit Talmudic rule, but the difficulty remains. One can answer that Mordechai speaks where she became a niddah only after the chuppah had already begun, as explained in the preceding seif katan. She can then argue that once there had already been the affection of a chuppah fit for relations, she did not lose her entitlement, and may say that his fortune caused the later impediment.
