He may not violate Torah law, etc. This applies specifically where there is no established local custom to descend upon the borrower's assets without beit din permission and without appraisal. Then, even if such language is commonly written in documents, we interpret it as applying only where no judge is available, as the Rosh concludes. But if there is an established local custom to proceed that way, we follow the custom because the borrower accepted it in the document. The Rosh repeatedly couches the matter in terms of custom. The Mechaber likewise rules expressly in siman 103:7 that where the practice is to write that the creditor may descend upon the borrower's property without appraisal, if the practice is not actually to do so he may not, implying that where the established practice is to do so it is effective. This also supports what I wrote in siman 26 in Derishah and Sema: if a person expressly undertakes to be judged according to non-Jewish law because that law gives the other party a substantive advantage unavailable under Jewish law, that undertaking can obligate him. Where it would be forbidden for Jewish judges to implement that substantive non-Jewish-law result, such as collecting a debt canceled by shemittah, the parties may not simply go before non-Jewish judges. But where there is no independent prohibition, Jewish judges can enforce the monetary advantage he accepted. See Rama at the end of siman 369.
