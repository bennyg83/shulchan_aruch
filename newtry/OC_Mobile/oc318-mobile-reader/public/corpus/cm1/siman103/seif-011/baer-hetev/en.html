That it was lost. The Tur does not write "or it was lost," but only that the deposit was in the creditor's possession. This implies that only in such a case is the appraisal considered mistaken. If the deposit was lost, the land does not revert after the creditor sold it. This is also the view of Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam. Nimukei Yosef, however, writes in the name of Maharam and the Ran that the same rule applies where the deposit was lost. I am surprised that the Mechaber ruled this way, for because of the doubt it would have been proper to rule leniently for the defendant in accordance with Rashi, so that the appraisal would not revert once the creditor sold the land. Taz.
The lender. The borrower, however, cannot compel any person to return the land to him once the creditor sold or bequeathed it, because the borrower was at fault for not paying cash. Even if it turns out that he did not then know of the money available to him, nevertheless at times a person values ready cash more highly and gives a creditor land instead. The transaction is therefore properly called a judicial appraisal, and it does not revert once the creditor sold or bequeathed the property. Taz.
