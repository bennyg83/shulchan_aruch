And afterward it was found that the deposit was in his possession, etc. The Mechaber states these cases in ascending order, following Nimukei Yosef. It is obvious that if it was found to have been in the custodian's possession all along, meaning that the custodian himself knew this from the beginning and falsely denied it, there never should have been an appraisal. Even if the deposit was lost through his negligence and he did not know where it was, if he later finds it the appraisal is considered mistaken and both the depositor and custodian may retract. By contrast, where the custodian knew of the deposit all along, only the depositor may retract, not the custodian who knew the truth. For the same reason, the Tur and Mechaber carefully write in the following case, concerning a borrower who is later found to have cash on hand, that "the lender may compel the borrower," implying that specifically the lender may compel the borrower, but not the reverse. The Tur, however, writes only that afterward the deposit was found to have been in his possession, which implies that in its view the rule applies only where the appraisal was mistaken from the outset, not where the item was lost and later found. This is also the view of Rashi and Rabbeinu Chananel, as cited by Nimukei Yosef. See there.
