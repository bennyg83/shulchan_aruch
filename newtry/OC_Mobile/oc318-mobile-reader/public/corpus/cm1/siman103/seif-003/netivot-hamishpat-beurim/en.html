And the appraisers may be relatives of one another. Tumim asks that Tur derives this rule from appraisal of maaser sheni, where relatives can participate. If so, even relatives of the litigants and people disqualified from testimony should be valid, just as with maaser sheni. There is no difficulty. The foundation of judicial appraisal is that when a majority of the three evaluators agree on a value, we accept that the property is not worth more than that amount. Each evaluator must therefore be personally qualified to testify to the litigant regarding the value as it appears to his own judgment. If he is disqualified from testimony with respect to the litigant, we are concerned that he may intentionally state a value higher or lower than his genuine assessment. Maaser sheni resembles an area of ritual law, where even persons generally disqualified from monetary testimony may be believed. Relatives of one another are different. Two relatives are disqualified as a pair only where two witnesses are jointly needed to establish an external fact and their testimony is treated as one unit. In appraisal, each evaluator testifies only to his own independent assessment. No evaluator knows what is in the other's mind. Each therefore gives his own single evaluation, and relatives of one another may serve together.
