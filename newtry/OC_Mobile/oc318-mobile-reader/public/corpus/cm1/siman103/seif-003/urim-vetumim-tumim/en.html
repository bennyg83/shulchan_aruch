They may be related to one another. Rabbi Yehiel, son of the Rosh, derived this rule from the appraisal of maaser sheni, which may be made by relatives. This requires study, because there even non Jews may participate. If one extends the analogy fully, as may be implied by the Magen Avraham in Orach Chaim siman 306, then the owner himself could participate as well. Yet in maaser sheni the owner of the produce can be one of the appraisers, even though he has an obvious financial interest in the valuation. No one has ever suggested that in an appraisal for debt collection the debtor or creditor may count among the three appraisers. Ketubot speaks of an appraisal by beit din and of the rule preserving the authority of beit din. What concept of beit din remains if its members may be relatives, non Jews, or the interested parties themselves? Perhaps Rabbi Yehiel spoke only of the type of valuation described by Rashi and Nimukei Yosef in Bava Batra 107, where three people descend to assess property for the support of a wife and daughters. They explain that case rather than ordinary appraisal between a debtor and purchaser because the baraita says merely "three" rather than "beit din." A widow may sell for support without formal beit din, but three are still needed to establish value, as in Bava Metzia 32. In such a valuation, which requires three assessors but not a judicial panel, relatives may perhaps suffice, just as in maaser sheni. But where the law requires beit din, relatives and the like plainly cannot serve, since an actual beit din is required. Even this limited analogy remains doubtful. Maaser sheni is unusual because by Torah law even property worth a maneh can be redeemed on a perutah, while in ordinary monetary appraisal there is genuine concern for bias and partiality. The rule therefore requires further study.
