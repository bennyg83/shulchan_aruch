And they erred by any amount. One might ask how the error can ever be established, since three appraisers already valued the property, and even if one hundred others later say that it is worth more, ordinarily two witnesses are equivalent to one hundred. This is not difficult. In a matter that is not formal testimony, we follow the majority of opinions, as the Shach writes in siman 46, seif katan 66. Therefore, although the law is that if the creditor merely says, “Let others appraise it,” we do not listen to him, if four appraisers independently come and say that it is worth more, we follow the majority of expert opinions.
Their sale is void. See Sema, seif katan 6. His reasoning may be explained as follows. In siman 207, regarding one who sells to another according to the valuation of a beit din of three, it appears that if it becomes established that the appraisers erred even by a minimal amount, the transaction is reversed. The Beit Yosef there holds that even if three others arrive whom we know to be more expert, the original appraisal is undone. Consequently, when four experts come, it is as though we know that they are more expert, and the appraisal is reversed even for an error of less than one sixth. Although siman 227 rules that where a person agreed to sell according to the appraisal of a specifically named individual, the transaction is reversed only for an error of one sixth, the distinction is simple. There he agreed to rely on the valuation of a particular known person. In siman 207, however, he agreed to sell according to the valuation of three appraisers, meaning three competent appraisers. Once it is established that they erred, it is thereby established that they were not competent appraisers, and he never agreed to sell based on the valuation of people who are not competent. The sale is therefore void. According to this, the Sema's intent here is likewise understandable. With a debtor or heirs who bear personal liability and must themselves exert effort to pay, once three appraisers were selected it is comparable to agreeing to rely on specifically identified persons. The threshold for ona'ah is therefore one sixth, as explained in siman 227 seif 25. A purchaser, by contrast, bears no personal liability at all, and his property is merely subject to collection. His silence when the appraisers were selected is not equivalent to consent to those particular persons. Therefore, once it is established that they erred, it is established that they were not competent appraisers, and the appraisal is void.
