But if the property belonged to a debtor, etc. The Mechaber follows the Maggid Mishneh, who inferred from the Rambam's statement of the rule specifically regarding a purchaser that where an appraisal is mistaken by any amount the sale is void, whereas with the debtor himself the law is like that of heirs. The Maggid Mishneh explains that a purchaser has no personal obligation to pay the creditor. Only his land is encumbered, so the special strength granted to a judicial sale is weaker. Heirs, by contrast, stand in their father's place and bear the obligation of payment, all the more the debtor himself. This reasoning is extremely weak. The Maggid Mishneh himself leaves it in doubt. The Giddulei Terumah already notes that the Rambam follows a responsum of the Rif, and the Rif's text, cited by the Baal HaTerumot in Shaar 3, clearly equates the debtor with a purchaser. The sound reason is that beit din is specially empowered in dealings with orphans because it acts as the father of orphans. Therefore an error of less than one sixth does not undo its sale. A debtor and purchaser, however, could if they wished satisfy the creditor without beit din. When beit din values their property it acts only as an agent, so any error invalidates the transaction. I add a proof. If the Maggid Mishneh's rationale were correct, then when beit din divides an estate among orphans and errs by less than one sixth, why should the division stand? One orphan has no personal debt to another. Yet the Rambam in Hilchot Nachalot chapter 10 rules that a division made by beit din between minor and adult heirs remains valid despite an error up to one sixth. Likewise in Hilchot Mechirah chapter 13 he codifies the Mishnah concerning judicial appraisal by writing specifically that when beit din sells property of orphans and errs up to one sixth, the sale stands. He deliberately specifies orphans. If the same were true of the debtor's own property, why not write the general rule? This is strong proof that the special rule applies only to orphans, while debtor and purchaser are alike. The fact that the Rambam stated the any error rule in the case of a purchaser rather than a debtor is itself a greater novelty. A purchaser cannot simply hand over the field at a privately chosen valuation, because if he later seeks reimbursement from the seller, the seller can say, "Who appraised it for you?" One might therefore have thought that formal judicial appraisal gives the purchaser the same protection as an orphan. The Rambam teaches otherwise. A debtor, who can voluntarily give his own land to the creditor at an agreed valuation, is all the more clearly outside the special protection. This is also the Tur's apparent understanding of the Rambam. There is no difficulty from Ketubot 100, where Rav Nachman distinguishes an agent from beit din because the former is one and the latter several. According to Rabbenu Tam's explanation, the "agent" there means an agent of beit din. The comparison is therefore between the full court administering orphan property and its single agent. One might have thought that the agent shares the court's strong authority because he acts for it, but Rav Nachman says that the protection belongs to the three judges and not to a lone emissary. This is coherent. The Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot Shutafin was uncertain whether the Rambam would invalidate even the mistake of an agent of beit din. According to the foregoing, the answer follows necessarily from their approach.
