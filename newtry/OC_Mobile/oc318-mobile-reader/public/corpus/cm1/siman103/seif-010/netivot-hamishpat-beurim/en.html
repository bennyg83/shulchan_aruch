Many heirs. See Taz, who asks that the Gemara's statement that brothers who divided are like purchasers applies to the division among the brothers themselves, not to their status toward third parties. He proves this from the beginning of HaGozel UMaachil, where if a robber dies and leaves the stolen object to his children, inheritance is not considered a change of domain, because an heir's domain is not like that of a purchaser. This remains true even if there are many heirs who later divide. In my view the cases are not comparable. The rule that despair together with a change of domain effects acquisition is based on the fact that despair alone does not acquire because the object came into the robber's possession unlawfully, while the later transferee receives it permissibly. If two robbers jointly stole an object, a later sale from one robber to the other would certainly not constitute the required change of domain, because the entire object came into both their hands unlawfully. The same is true of heirs. They stand in their father's legal place, so each heir's connection to the stolen property derives from the same unlawful possession, and division among them does not turn the transfer into a permissive acquisition.
A husband is considered a purchaser regarding his wife's property. See Shach, seif katan 19, who explains that this concerns property that was merely prospective. It follows that if the wife dies and leaves heirs, an earlier judicial appraisal can be returned to them, because heirs inherit even property that was only prospective to the deceased.
