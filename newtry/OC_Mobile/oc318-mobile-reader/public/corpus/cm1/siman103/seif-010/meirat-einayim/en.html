He never returns it except for two hundred. If the first appraisal to the first creditor was two hundred, the later creditor can say: since you come against me through your lender, and you would have had to pay him two hundred, pay me the same. If the later appraisal was two hundred, he can say that it would not be good and upright to force him to lose the difference.
Land that was appraised, etc. The Gemara says that if the creditor sold it, bequeathed it, or gave it as a gift, the appraisal does not return. The reason is that only against the creditor himself do we say, "You had only money against the debtor, and here is your money." A purchaser or recipient entered specifically for the land.
Or he appraised it voluntarily to his own creditor. The correct reading is "he appraised it," not "they appraised it." If the creditor voluntarily transferred the land to his own creditor without beit din, it is like a sale and does not return. Only where beit din takes it from him and gives it to his creditor can the original debtor seek its return.
If he died and left it as an inheritance, it does not return. Rambam holds that although the debtor's heir is treated like the debtor and can reclaim an appraisal, the creditor's heir is not treated like the creditor for purposes of being compelled to return it. Rama cites the Rosh and Tur as disagreeing. They understand the Gemara's "bequeathed it" as a lifetime transfer in an inheritance-like form, or as ordinary inheritance where there are multiple heirs and multiple fields, as explained below.
If there is only one heir, etc. This distinction applies to automatic inheritance. A lifetime gift to one child, even framed to take primary effect at death, is not treated as ordinary inheritance if it gives that child this field in addition to his regular share. Because the transfer was arranged during life, the recipient entered specifically for the land. But where there is only one heir and the land devolves automatically, the reason for treating division among heirs like a sale does not apply, and he must return it.
If the heirs divided and this field fell, etc. When several heirs divide the estate and this particular appraised field falls to one while the others receive other fields in exchange, the inheritance is treated like a sale because brothers who divide are regarded as purchasers from one another. The one who received this field effectively sold his shares in the other inherited fields to his brothers, and they sold him their shares in this field. He therefore entered specifically for this land, and it does not return. If there is only one son, this rationale does not exist and the appraisal returns.
A husband regarding his wife's property is treated as a purchaser. He does not return and it is not returned to him. This is the correct text. Rambam understands the Gemara as speaking while the wife is alive. Chazal treated the husband as a purchaser, so if he were treated merely as an heir he would not yet inherit during her lifetime and the law would differ. Rama, following the Rosh and Tur, understands the statement specifically after the wife has died: because the husband is treated as a purchaser, he neither returns an appraisal inherited from her nor can demand return of one taken from her. Had he been treated as an ordinary heir, according to their view he would stand in the same position as an heir of the creditor or debtor. This applies even to nichsei melog. Although elsewhere a husband is sometimes treated as an heir and sometimes as a purchaser, whichever benefits him, that rule does not apply where it would cause loss to another person.
