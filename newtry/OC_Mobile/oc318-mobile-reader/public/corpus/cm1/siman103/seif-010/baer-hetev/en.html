He gave it. The reason is explained in Tosafot. Although ordinarily we say that a seller transfers to the purchaser every right he possessed, this case is different. Strict law does not require even the first creditor to return the land; he returns it only because of the principle of doing what is right and good. Therefore the second holder need not return it. I have a difficulty, however, from siman 312, seif 13, where if a house is sold, the second owner may not remove the tenant until giving prior notice, because the tenant can say, "Your rights are no greater than those of the first owner." Why is that case different? There too the original owner has no Torah obligation to give notice. One may answer that there the seller acted improperly by failing to notify the tenant, whereas here no party committed any wrong. Chazal therefore did not institute the rule of what is right and good in this case. Taz.

When she died. The Bach writes here and at the end of Even HaEzer, siman 91, that even according to the Rosh and Tur the reference to her death is not exclusive. See there. This also appears to me the primary ruling. The Rosh and Tur hold in Even HaEzer, siman 90, seif 9, that the husband may recover even the corpus of the land from purchasers, even where it is nichsei melog and even during his wife's lifetime. Although during her lifetime he has only the right to fruits, and we rule that ownership of fruits is not equivalent to ownership of the corpus, after the enactment of Usha the husband may nevertheless recover the property from purchasers. When she later dies, he is treated retroactively as a purchaser from the time of the marriage, so it is as though he acquired the corpus during her lifetime. This is also implied at the end of Bava Kamma 88. In this way it appears possible to answer Tosafot's question at the end of the chapter Yesh Nochalin against Rashbam, in the passage beginning "and the heir." See there carefully. Thus concludes the Shach. The words of the Mechaber and Rama here and in Even HaEzer, siman 91, are exceedingly difficult. In my Menorat Zechariah there I explained the entire matter correctly. The Shach's further statement that even while the wife is alive the husband is considered a purchaser, for which he cites Even HaEzer, siman 90, where he may recover even the corpus of the land, is not precise. This is explicit in the Gemara in the chapter Yesh Nochalin: because the purchasers caused the loss to themselves by buying from a married woman, and because the husband would otherwise suffer a loss, all agree that he is treated as a purchaser. In the law of judicial appraisal, however, where the husband suffers no loss with respect to the fruits during her lifetime, he is not treated as a purchaser. Consider this carefully. Editor's note: Although I am unworthy to offer an opinion between great authorities, Torah it is and I must learn. I therefore cling to the dust of the feet of the departed Gaon and say, with all due honor, that I do not understand his objection to the Shach from the Gemara's statement that the purchasers caused the loss to themselves. How could one imagine that the Shach overlooked the very Gemara with which he was engaged and which stood before him? The Gemara states this only to answer why Chazal treated the husband as a purchaser despite the purchasers' loss: they caused that loss to themselves. Once we find that the Rosh and Tur hold that even during her lifetime the husband was made like a purchaser, as shown by his recovery of the corpus, the same should apply to the law of judicial appraisal. The Gaon's concluding distinction, that in judicial appraisal the husband suffers no loss, is the reverse of what Tosafot state at the end of Yesh Nochalin in the passage beginning "there": they ask from the beginning of HaMafkid, where property was appraised to a woman who then married, even though the debtor suffers a loss, and answer that since strict law does not require return and it exists only because of "you shall do what is right and good," the debtor's loss is not considered so significant. Tosafot write similarly in HaMafkid, Bava Metzia 35, in the passage beginning "he sold it," and the Taz cites their words in this siman, as noted above in seif katan 22. Anyone who studies Shach, seif katan 19, will judge fairly that most of these points are included in his words. He himself anticipated this objection and maintained his ruling here.
