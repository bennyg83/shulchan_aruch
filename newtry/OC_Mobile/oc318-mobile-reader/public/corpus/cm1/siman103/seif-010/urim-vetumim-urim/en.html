Only for two hundred. This follows from either side. If the second creditor took it at a valuation of two hundred, he can say, "Why should I lose because of you?" If the first creditor had taken it at two hundred, he can say, "Why should you profit? Had it remained with the first creditor, you would have had to pay two hundred." These principles are stated by Rosh. One might ask: if the first valuation was one hundred and the second was two hundred, the land must have increased in value, because why else would beit din change the appraisal? In that event, even had it remained with the first creditor, the borrower would have to pay the full current value. One can answer that when the second appraisal was made, land prices had risen and beit din valued it at two hundred, but by the time the borrower seeks return, prices fell again. Without the second creditor he would therefore need pay only the current lower value. Yet because it passed to the second creditor, the full two hundred remains relevant. An even more satisfactory explanation is that at the first appraisal beit din issued an inspection notice and mistakenly valued land worth two hundred at one hundred. That appraisal stands. At the second appraisal they did not err and valued it at two hundred. Thus, had the land remained with the first creditor, although worth two hundred the borrower would repay only one hundred according to the valid first appraisal. Examine carefully.

And he bequeathed it. Mechaber holds that even where the property passes by automatic inheritance it is not returned. Although an heir did not intentionally enter with the expectation of receiving this land, the Sages did not deprive the heir of his inherited right. See Tumim, seif katan 14, for my resolution of the Shach's question from Even HaEzer.
And some say that if he died, etc. According to this view, the Gemara's phrase that he "bequeathed it" means that he transferred the field through a deathbed gift. Such a transfer has the law of a gift even where the recipient is otherwise a proper heir, because he received it by virtue of the deathbed gift and therefore intentionally entered with expectation of the land.
But if there were many heirs, etc. It is obvious that if the deceased left only this field and money, and the other brothers took the money while this brother took the field, he is treated as a purchaser with respect to the others' shares. Even if the deceased left other fields and the other brothers took those fields, we rule that there is no retroactive clarification. The heirs are therefore treated like purchasers who exchanged their shares with one another. Consequently, the rule that an appraisal is returned does not apply. See Tumim.
Land to a woman. If land was appraised to a woman for collection of her ketubah, meaning the principal one hundred or two hundred, Maharshal and Bach hold that the appraisal is not returned. Rama in responsum and the Shach hold that it is returned. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length and concluded like Rama. I distinguished, however, between adult heirs and minor heirs. If the heirs are adults and the appraisal was made through beit din, their status is like other creditors and the appraisal is returned. If the heirs are minors, beit din acts as the legal hand of the orphans, and the transfer resembles one made voluntarily by the debtor. It is therefore not returned. This distinction resolves many difficulties. See there.
