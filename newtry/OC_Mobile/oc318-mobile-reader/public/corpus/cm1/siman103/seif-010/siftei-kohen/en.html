Or the creditor appraised it for another creditor. This appears to mean that the creditor himself voluntarily transferred the appraised land to his own creditor, as the Sema explains. The Rambam also appears to understand the Gemara's phrase in this way, and it is a sound interpretation. Rashi, the Nimukei Yosef, the Tur, the Mechaber, and Rabbenu Yerucham explain it as the Rama does above. In practical law they do not appear to disagree, for the ruling follows the underlying reason.
"And the Torah does not return to the [Ps 119:105; 119:105; 119:105; 119:105; 119:105; 119:105; 119:130]
They appraised land for a woman, etc. Maharshal rules that this applies only when land was appraised for an ordinary debt, but land appraised to a woman for her ketubah is not restored. His proof is the Gemara's statement that a widow who appraised property for herself accomplished nothing because one asks her, “Who appraised it for you?” Why, he asks, was the reason not simply that an appraisal is reversible? The Rama disputes him and explains the Gemara as referring to land that improved or appreciated. Maharshal objects that it is forced to read an unqualified passage in that manner, and he cites Terumat HaDeshen. Yet the Rosh and Mordechai use that very interpretation. In truth no such answer is needed. The statement that her appraisal was void from the outset has many consequences beyond the rule that an appraisal is reversible. It applies even to movable property, to which ordinary reversal does not apply. It requires her to return past produce, whereas ordinary reversal operates only prospectively. It allows the heirs to satisfy her with different property rather than paying money to redeem that parcel. It also invalidates a sale or inheritance made on the strength of her self-appraisal, whereas an appraisal ordinarily ceases to be reversible after sale or inheritance. Thus the Gemara had ample reason to say that her act was void from the outset. Nevertheless, even land appraised to her for her ketubah remains subject to restoration, and this is the primary ruling.
A husband is treated as a purchaser with respect to his wife's property. The Sema explains that the Gemara needed this classification only while the wife was alive. If she had died, even as an heir the husband would not restore the land according to the Rambam. That point is correct, but the Sema's further description of the husband's rights during her lifetime as a form of living inheritance is unnecessary and incorrect. Rashi states, and the Gemara proves from the enactment of Usha, that the rule applies even where the property was not entered as guaranteed dowry property. According to the opinion cited by the Rama that purchaser status arises only after the wife's death, that limitation concerns usufruct property. With guaranteed dowry property, everyone agrees that the husband is a purchaser even during her lifetime because he bears responsibility for it. Tosafot explain that the Sages sometimes classify the husband as heir or purchaser in whichever manner avoids loss. With respect, that explanation is difficult if it means that the Sages uprooted the Torah law of the husband's inheritance merely to impose upon him the disadvantages of a purchaser. The better explanation is that the property had been appraised away from the woman before marriage and she had not yet reacquired it. Her claim was therefore prospective and comparable to a debt, which the husband does not inherit. The Gemara calls him a purchaser primarily to explain why he himself does not restore property that had been appraised to her. I nevertheless conclude with Tosafot, the Rambam, Semag, and the other authorities that even an inheritance passing automatically is not subject to reversal, contrary to the Nimukei Yosef's attribution to the Rambam. The Gemara may be read precisely: where property was appraised to a woman and she married, the husband is treated as purchaser even while she lives and does not restore it; where property was appraised away from her and she married and died, it is not restored to the husband because her right was prospective and not inherited. Rashi, the Rosh, the Tur, and the Mechaber appear to require her death in the latter case, while the Maggid Mishneh reads both clauses as operating during her life. The principal interpretation is as stated.
Some say only when she died, etc. The Bach writes that even according to the Rosh and Tur, her death is not indispensable, and this appears correct. They rule in Even HaEzer that after the enactment of Usha a husband may recover the corpus of his wife's usufruct property from purchasers even during her lifetime. He is therefore regarded retroactively as the first purchaser from the time of marriage. Where land was appraised to the woman and she then married, he does not restore it even while she lives. Where land was appraised away from her before marriage, however, the Rosh and Tur appear to require her death. During her lifetime she remains entitled to restoration, for the husband is not made a purchaser to his own detriment. After her death, the husband does not inherit the claim because it is prospective property and comparable to a debt.
