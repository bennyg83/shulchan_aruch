When he had the property appraised to his creditor voluntarily. This is Rambam's explanation of the Gemara's case where "he gave it to his creditor in payment": the first creditor voluntarily had the land awarded to his own creditor. This differs from Rashi, who explains the dispute concerning the original borrower who voluntarily gives the land to his creditor. The Shach writes that they do not disagree in practical law. In Asifat Zekenim to Bava Metzia, however, it is stated explicitly that according to Rashi the distinction applies specifically to the first creditor, whereas with the second creditor the fact that he received it willingly changes nothing, because he is no better than the person from whom he derives his title. According to Rambam, who explains the passage concerning the second creditor, the rule concerning the first creditor follows all the more strongly. With the second creditor one could argue, "whatever rights the first had, he sold to the second," and nevertheless the Gemara says that where it was voluntarily awarded, the recipient is like a purchaser and the appraisal is not reversed. Certainly, then, where the original borrower voluntarily awards it to his first creditor, that creditor is treated as a purchaser and the appraisal is not reversed. The Rama quoted Rashi and the Tur in the preceding seif and did not object here to the Mechaber's Rambam based formulation. It is therefore possible that the Rama regards Rambam as primary, so both rules apply. Alternatively, he may regard the matter as doubtful and be lenient in both cases, because one should not remove land from the creditor who possesses it under Torah law.
And if he inherited it, the appraisal is not reversed. The Shach asks that in Even HaEzer, siman 91, the Mechaber says that if property was appraised to a woman and she later married, the husband is treated as a purchaser and does not return it. This implies that if he were only an heir he would have to return it, following the Rosh, who understands the Gemara's term "inheritance" as a deathbed gift rather than ordinary inheritance. Here, however, the Mechaber follows Rambam and says that every heir keeps the appraised property. The Shach later answers that if the husband were merely an heir he would have to return because the ordinary reason for an heir's exemption, that one entered the land with intent to acquire it, does not apply to a husband who consumes the produce. That reasoning is strained. The same principle that a purchaser entered with intent for the land is itself the reason a purchaser ordinarily does not return. If the husband's relation to the land is considered too weak even for that reasoning, then even as a purchaser he should be required to return under the rule of doing what is upright and good. The correct explanation is as follows. The Gemara's language, "a husband with respect to his wife's property is a purchaser and does not return," implies that his status as purchaser is the reason he does not return. Had he been merely an heir, he would return. Tosafos and the Rosh therefore ask: but an ordinary heir also does not return appraised property. The Mechaber can answer that the Sages did not extend the enactment of returning appraised property so far as to weaken the Torah rights of an ordinary heir. It is enough that they weakened the creditor himself; they did not also diminish the inheritance right given by Torah law. But a husband's inheritance is rabbinic. What the Sages created, they can limit. If he were treated merely as a rabbinic heir, the same Sages could require him to return under the rule of upright conduct. Only because the enactment of Usha treats him as a purchaser does he retain the land. Thus the Mechaber's rulings are entirely consistent.
But if there were many heirs, etc. See Urim, where I wrote that brothers who divided an estate are treated as purchasers. Therefore, once they divide, the appraisal is no longer reversed. The Taz objects that the brothers are treated as purchasers only with respect to rights between themselves, such as return of ancestral land at the Jubilee, but not with respect to outsiders. He proves this from the Mishnah concerning one who stole and fed the stolen property to his sons, or left it before them, where the sons are exempt. The Gemara asks whether an heir's domain is like a purchaser's domain. If multiple sons who divide were purchasers even toward outsiders, the Mishnah would already involve a purchaser's domain. An explicit Gemara in Bechorot answers him. If a father became obligated to a kohen for five selaim and died, and the sons divided the estate, they are exempt because brothers who divided are treated as purchasers and an oral debt does not collect from purchasers. See what I wrote at length above in siman 39. This is plainly a rule operating against an outsider and even concerning an obligation written in the Torah. All the more so should it apply to the rabbinic enactment of reversing an appraisal. The Taz's question from the stolen property case is not difficult. First, it would also challenge Rav, who regards heirs as heirs rather than purchasers, and Tosafos there already discusses how the Mishnah fits his view. Second, it is forced to say that "sons" specifically means several sons and that if there were only one son he would be liable. The Mishnah would then have omitted the essential fact that they divided the estate. Until division they are not treated as purchasers, as the Gemara in Bechorot explicitly states. Further, if the father left stolen money and the sons merely divided identical coins, it is difficult to characterize the division itself as a purchaser transaction, because the usual issue of retroactive clarification does not meaningfully apply to interchangeable money. The Taz's objection therefore has no force.
Land was appraised to a woman. The Tur says, "land was appraised to a woman for her debt and she married." Maharshal, responsum 57, cited by the Bach, infers that if the land was appraised to her in payment of her ketubah, the appraisal is not reversed at all. His proof is from Ketubot 98, where Rav Nachman says that a widow who appraised property to herself accomplished nothing, because "who appraised it for you?" Rashi and Ran explain that if the heirs later wish to remove her by paying money, they may retake the property. Maharshal asks: even without the special defect that she appraised it to herself, ordinary shuma hadra should allow the heirs to redeem it. He therefore concludes that property appraised in payment of a ketubah does not ordinarily return. Rama, responsum 6, disagrees, as does the Shach. The Shach says Rav Nachman's case concerns appreciation in the land's value. The Rosh expressly answers Rabbeinu Yonah's question, that the widow had already seized the property, by explaining that the practical difference concerns appreciation after the appraisal. Raavyah says similarly. This is a good answer according to the Rosh, but it does not fully resolve the matter according to Baal HaTerumot and those who follow him, including the Mechaber, who hold that even where the land appreciated the appraisal may still be reversed. For them Rav Nachman's statement remains difficult. The Shach gives another answer: the heirs have no cash and only wish to substitute different land, which would not be ordinary shuma hadra. That does not fit Rashi and Ran, who explicitly say that the heirs remove her by paying money. He suggests further that she may have sold the land onward, in which case shuma hadra no longer applies. But if she sold it, the phrase "who appraised it for you" is difficult, because a widow is authorized to sell without beit din. The best remaining answer in the Shach is that the difference concerns fruits. Under ordinary shuma hadra the creditor keeps the fruits she already lawfully consumed, whereas under "who appraised it for you" her defective self appraisal never gave her that right. Even this raises a question against the Rosh. Why did the Rosh answer Rabbeinu Yonah through appreciation, which Maharshal correctly regards as somewhat strained, rather than simply say that although she seized, she never acquired the body of the land and therefore the fruits remain with the orphans? Perhaps the Rosh holds differently from what I wrote in Urim, seif katan 15. I explained there that although the Gemara says the creditor consumes fruits once the days of public announcement conclude, this is not literal until the final court deed reaches him. The Rosh may hold the words literally for produce: even if appreciation and depreciation of the body depend upon the final award, as stated above in seif 6, the borrower loses the fruits and the creditor acquires them once the announcement period ends. Rav Nachman's case is established where the property was publicly announced. Therefore the widow had a right to the fruits under all views, and the defect of "who appraised it for you" must concern appreciation of the body. This preserves the Rosh's answer and returns Maharshal's question. There is another way to reject Maharshal's proof from Rav Nachman. The Gemara elsewhere relates that Rav Nachman ordered an appraised mansion returned to its owner and asks whether this shows that Rav Nachman holds shuma hadra. Why would that be surprising if shuma hadra is the accepted law? The Gemara must have known that Rav Nachman did not ordinarily accept permanent reversal of appraisal, or that as a Nehardean he followed the view limiting reversal to twelve months and the case occurred later. The enactment of upright conduct is also compared by the authorities to the law of a neighboring owner, and according to Rashi in Bava Metzia 108 Rav Nachman does not apply that law broadly. In any event, the Gemara appears to assume that Rav Nachman did not recognize unlimited shuma hadra. It therefore had to answer that the original appraisal was mistaken. Thus Rav Nachman's separate statement about a widow who appraised to herself follows his own view that there is no ordinary shuma hadra in that setting, and he therefore needed the reason "who appraised it for you." There is no proof from his statement for the general halachah concerning ketubah. Still, there is stronger support for Maharshal from the Mishnah itself. If a court prepared an appraisal and announcement and sold property worth two hundred for one hundred, the sale stands. The discussion there concerns an award to a creditor rather than a sale to an unrelated third party, as appears from the rulings below in siman 109. If it concerns the orphans, how can their sale remain effective? Why can they not later say that the appraisal should be reversed? It is strained to say they have no money to redeem it, especially where property worth two hundred was disposed of for one hundred and the loss is severe. This proof is stronger than the one from Rav Nachman. Netivot HaMishpat also notes that the wording of Rav Nachman and the Tur leans toward Maharshal, but objects from Bava Metzia 16b. The Gemara asks why found court deeds of final award should be returned if perhaps the debtor later paid, since shuma hadra. Why not establish the deed as an award in payment of a woman's ketubah, where Maharshal says shuma hadra does not apply? This is not a strong question. Even according to every view there are other cases where shuma hadra does not apply, such as where beit din sold the property to another person rather than awarding it to the creditor, where a minor has not yet reached majority according to Ritva, or where the debtor made a binding kinyan not to demand return. The Gemara's challenge arises because the Mishnah says "all acts of beit din," implying an unqualified category. If only some awards were nonreturnable, the phrase would be inaccurate. The Gemara therefore answers that, as a general rule, even the apparent loss from repayment can be resolved because the debtor caused his own problem. I initially found another support for Maharshal from Bava Metzia 110b, where three parties appraise improvements and credit them in money, including a creditor and a woman collecting a ketubah from orphans. The ketubah there must refer to the statutory amount rather than dowry, for dowry is ordinary debt. The Gemara asks why the creditor cannot simply be removed with money according to the view that cash can displace land, and answers by establishing an express apotiki. But Ramban writes in Bava Batra that an express apotiki is not normally compatible with the body of a ketubah because of Shimon ben Shetach's enactment that all of the husband's property stands responsible. How, then, can the ketubah case be included? One could answer that with a ketubah everyone agrees the wife has no ordinary lien on movable money during the husband's life but only on land, and therefore the heirs cannot force her to accept cash. If so, there would likewise be no shuma hadra for ketubah at all. This would strongly support Maharshal. Yet that conclusion would require saying that heirs can never remove a woman with money against her will, and we do not find such a broad distinction in the codifiers. Perhaps after the Geonic enactment subjecting movable property like land, cash also became an available form of payment, while shuma hadra remained governed by the earlier law because it is itself only an enactment and we do not make a second enactment upon an enactment. This offers some support to Maharshal but is not decisive. The Rama, on the other hand, notes that when the Gemara lists cases where shuma hadra does not apply, such as sale and inheritance, it does not list a woman's ketubah. Likewise, Perek Af Al Pi lists many respects in which conditions of the ketubah are treated like the ketubah itself, but does not include shuma hadra. If a creditor's appraisal returns while a woman's ketubah appraisal never returns, even within twelve months when all authorities ordinarily allow return, one would expect this significant difference to have been listed. Tosafos raises a parallel question in Bava Batra concerning an authority who distinguishes ordinary creditors from ketubah with respect to property merely expected later. Here the omission is difficult for Maharshal. There is, however, a more fundamental problem. From the earlier Gemara it appears that everyone recognizes shuma hadra for at least twelve months. Yet Bava Metzia 110 says that a creditor, ketubah claimant, and others appraise improvement, and the Gemara initially says that according to the view that a creditor cannot be removed with money there is no need to establish an apotiki. That works for a purchaser because an appraisal made to a purchaser does not return. But how can it work for orphans? Within twelve months, according to all opinions, an appraisal made from orphans should return. Why then can they not offer money from the outset? We do not find an authority who denies shuma hadra entirely even within twelve months. The answer appears from the dispute between Rabbi Elazar and Ravina concerning property voluntarily delivered in payment. Ravina holds that a voluntary delivery is a complete sale and does not return. He must know that even if the debtor came to beit din, the court would ultimately award the land against him. Yet he says that unless it is demonstrated that the transfer was coercive, we do not remove the land from the creditor's possession on a mere possibility that the debtor acted only because he had no choice. If the debtor went to beit din and forced the process, that itself proves coercion, and shuma hadra applies. But wherever the transaction can be understood as voluntary, possession remains with the creditor. Beit Yosef similarly says that if the creditor had to force the debtor to court, this counts as an appraisal subject to return. Now consider minor orphans. Beit din itself is their hand, their guardian, and the agent protecting their estate. When beit din awards land from the minor orphans to a creditor, there is no independent evidence that the orphans themselves resisted. Beit din is the legal actor because minors cannot act for themselves. This resembles a voluntary delivery through an authorized guardian, not a creditor forcing an unwilling adult debtor into court. According to Ravina, in the absence of clear proof of coercion the appraisal should not return. This is especially strong according to the Shach, who requires actual refusal before beit din. This distinction is, in my view, clear. It resolves the entire matter without distinguishing ketubah from ordinary debt. When beit din awards land from minor orphans, whether for a ketubah or another debt collectible from minors, beit din is their hand and their guardian. The award is therefore treated like property the orphans themselves gave voluntarily, and shuma hadra does not apply. The rule in Bava Metzia concerning the three who appraise improvement can therefore concern minor orphans, and there is no difficulty from shuma hadra. The same resolves Rav Nachman's case of the widow appraising to herself, and the Mishnah of court appraisal and announcement, when understood as involving minors. It also explains why the Tur and Ran use the phrase "for her debt" rather than choose the specific case of a ketubah. They wanted an unconditional formulation. In a ketubah collected from minor orphans, return would not apply for the independent reason just stated; with an ordinary debt the same can be true. This, in my view, is the correct practical analysis and resolves all of the difficulties without uncertainty.
He is like a purchaser. See Urim, where I explained that the case can concern the wife's lifetime. If the husband were merely an heir at that point, he would have no inheritance rights in his living wife and would have to return the land. Some asked from Even HaEzer, siman 91, where the Mechaber speaks of the wife having died, implying that during her life the husband is not called a purchaser. The matter is clear from Ketubot 48b. The Mishnah says that once a woman marries, everyone agrees that if she sells or gives away her property the husband can remove it from the purchasers. The Gemara asks whether this is merely the enactment of Usha and answers that the Mishnah concerns the wife's lifetime and the produce, while the enactment of Usha concerns after her death and the body of the land. Thus during her lifetime the husband's purchaser status is an explicit Mishnah. After her death, where he would otherwise be an heir, the enactment of Usha grants him purchaser status concerning the body of the property. Accordingly, the version of our Gemara that says, "property was appraised from a woman and she died; the husband is a purchaser under the enactment of Usha," is entirely correct. During her life he is a purchaser under the earlier Mishnah; after her death the Usha enactment is needed to maintain purchaser status. In practical law he is a purchaser both during her life and after her death, but through two different legal sources. The Mechaber therefore states the present rule generally. In Even HaEzer, where the siman specifically addresses the enactment of Usha, it naturally discusses her death, because the rule during her lifetime belongs to the earlier Mishnah rather than to the Usha enactment. The later authorities labored unnecessarily.
And they do not return it to him. If he were merely an heir, the Gemara implies that the appraisal would be returned to him. Tosafos asks that the Sages generally acted for the husband's benefit: when purchaser status helps him against buyers, they treat him as purchaser, and regarding Jubilee they treat him as heir so he need not return his wife's inheritance. Tosafos answers that where this would cause loss to another person the Sages did not create the favorable status. Here, making the creditor return the land would cause such a loss. The Shach asks that the principle concerning loss to others should prevent the Sages from converting the husband's underlying heir status into purchaser status where that conversion harms another. But if the husband says, "I am an heir," why should that claim fail because another is harmed? Otherwise, why does he not return his wife's land to her family at the Jubilee? That too harms others, yet his heir status is preserved. The Shach therefore gives another answer: the husband's right to redeem land appraised from his wife is merely expected property, and a husband does not inherit property that was merely expected. But this contradicts the Shach's own practical conclusion that even an ordinary heir does not return appraised land. If so, when the Gemara says the husband is a purchaser in order not to return the land to him, it must mean that had he been an heir, return would have been required. If the property were merely expected, even an heir husband would not inherit it, as the Shach himself says, and purchaser status would be irrelevant. Moreover, the right to redeem appraised land should not be classified as expected property. According to authorities who hold that shuma hadra requires no new acquisition because the original award was conditional upon possible redemption, this is obvious. Even according to Ramban, who requires a new kinyan on reversal, the right to redeem is continuously in the debtor's hand. Something presently available to him at every moment is not ordinary expected property. Ramban himself, despite requiring a new kinyan, asks why the land is not returned to the husband on the ground that the Sages act for his benefit. This proves that he did not view the right as mere expected property. The Hagahot Mordechai cited by the Shach, which distinguishes property appraised from the woman before she married, also does not mean that the husband's interest is "expected." Ramban in Bava Batra says instead that because the appraisal occurred before the marriage, refusing to return it is not viewed as causing a loss to the husband. The woman entered the marriage when the land was already gone. One can compare this to property she concealed before marriage or property unknown to the husband, concerning which the husband may lack the ordinary marital expectation. The issue is not the technical category of expected property. Tosafos's answer can be understood in a different way. When we say that the Sages acted for the husband's benefit, it does not mean he may choose at will in every case to be heir or purchaser. His underlying status is that of heir, whether by Torah law or by a rabbinic institution treated like Torah law. The Sages of Usha added purchaser status for his benefit. An ordinary rule is that a person may say, "I do not want the Sages' enactment." Therefore, against purchasers he invokes the beneficial purchaser status; regarding Jubilee he rejects the purchaser enactment and remains an heir. But the right to reject a rabbinic enactment is generally used to retain existing rights, not to extract property from another. Maharam in Mordechai and Yam Shel Shlomo make this distinction: one cannot say "I do not want the Sages' enactment" in order to take property out of another person's possession. This is what Tosafos means by "where there is loss to others." Here, the land is already held by the creditor. The husband cannot say, "I reject the purchaser enactment and revert to heir status," in order to extract the land from the creditor. By contrast, at the Jubilee he merely retains what is already in his possession through his underlying heir status. This is a precise and coherent explanation of Tosafos.
Some say specifically when she died, etc. Regarding the rule that the creditor does not return the property to the husband, this limitation is understandable. While the wife is alive the husband can simply say, "Return it to my wife. Her hand is as my hand. Whether I personally am a purchaser is irrelevant. She herself has the right to redeem, and I act under her authorization." Thus the husband's purchaser status matters only after her death, when no right remains except his own. The question is more difficult regarding the converse rule, that if property was appraised to the husband from another and then came to the wife, whether he must return it. During her lifetime we already know from the Mishnah that he has purchaser status. One must say that where giving the husband purchaser status causes loss to another, the Sages did not apply the purchaser enactment with the same force during the wife's lifetime. Tosafos notes that the case of shuma hadra is only a relatively weak "loss," because the creditor is required to act according to what is upright and good. The Gemara's wording "and she died" indicates that the husband's status is stronger after her death than during her life. Raah similarly writes that while the wife is alive the husband's Usha rights are not as strong, because he may die before her and never inherit the principal. After her death he is certain to succeed to her property, and the purchaser status becomes stronger. Therefore, during her life the Sages may treat him as heir for purposes of avoiding loss to another, even though he has purchaser like rights for other purposes. After her death they do not weaken the purchaser status merely for the limited policy of shuma hadra. This explains why the Gemara mentions her death. The Tur in this siman copies the formula of the deed of appraisal from Baal HaTerumot. It says that after tearing the writ of authorization, beit din appraised and announced the property, found no buyer, and then sent three trustworthy experts who again appraised how much of the land should be awarded to the creditor. The Bach and Gidulei Terumah ask why two separate appraisals are needed. The Bach answers that perhaps the property appreciated in the interval. This is strained, because the interval is ordinarily short, and Responsa Rosh indicates that one does not suspect a change in value without a substantial passage of time. The correct explanation is practical. Suppose the creditor is owed one hundred while the debtor owns a large house or field worth one thousand. Before sale, beit din appraises the entire property, because there may be no buyer interested in purchasing merely one room or a small strip of field. It is better for the debtor that the whole property be offered for sale, with the creditor receiving his one hundred from the proceeds and the remainder returning to the debtor. But if no buyer is found and beit din must award land directly to the creditor, the creditor cannot be forced to take land worth one thousand and pay the debtor the excess in cash. Beit din must then make a second appraisal to identify what portion of the house or field is worth the exact amount of the debt, award that portion to the creditor, and leave the remainder to the debtor. This is why the deed contains two appraisals. Baal HaTerumot's wording supports this explicitly: after announcement they appraise how much of the field corresponds to the debt and award that amount. This is clear and correct.
