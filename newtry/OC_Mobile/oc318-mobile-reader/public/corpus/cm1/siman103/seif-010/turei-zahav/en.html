(Gloss), “and one brother's share fell to him.” I cannot understand the Rosh's reasoning and cannot remain silent. The Gemara states that a purchaser need not return appraised property only where he purchased it from a creditor, because the enactment of what is right and good was not imposed in that relationship. But when brothers divide inherited property, from the moment it descends before them they all stand in their father's place, and the obligation to return appraised property rests upon all of them equally. The principle that brothers who divided are treated as purchasers applies only to legal consequences among themselves, such as returning portions to one another at the Jubilee, and where a creditor takes the share of one of them. It does not apply in relation to a third person. Proof appears from Bava Kamma, at the beginning of HaGozel U'Ma'akhil, where the Gemara says that if a robber died and bequeathed the object to his children there is no change of domain, because an heir's domain is not equivalent to a purchaser's domain. That case plainly involves multiple heirs, since the Mishnah says “his children,” yet they are not treated as purchasers against the victim. Since all of them assumed their father's obligation to the victim, their internal division cannot defeat his claim. The same reasoning applies here to the borrower. Nor can one answer that the passage there concerns heirs who had not divided. Rava could not otherwise explain why they were exempt and had to say that the Mishnah was incomplete, as Rashi explains. Had division altered the result against the victim, the Gemara could simply have answered that the Mishnah concerns heirs who divided. Necessarily, their division is ineffective against the victim, and the same is true here.
