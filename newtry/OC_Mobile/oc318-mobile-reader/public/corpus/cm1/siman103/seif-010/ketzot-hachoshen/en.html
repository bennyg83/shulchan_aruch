If there were several heirs and they divided, and the land fell to one of them, it no longer reverts. The reason is that brothers who divide an estate are treated as purchasers. The Shach objects from the law of stolen property, where death and inheritance by several children is not considered a change of possession. I do not find that decisive. In stolen property, if one heir's share is later taken by the true owner, the division among the brothers can be reopened because they stand with mutual responsibility. Where a seller accepted responsibility, transfer to a purchaser may likewise fail to create the kind of independent change that defeats the original owner. Here, however, even a transfer with responsibility is treated as a new ownership stage for purposes of reversing judicial appraisal. Once the brothers have divided, each is legally like a purchaser of his share. Therefore the appraisal no longer reverts from the heir who received the land.
A husband is treated as a purchaser in his wife's property. Bava Metzia teaches that if land was appraised to a woman and she then married, or appraised from her and she married and later died, the husband is treated as a purchaser rather than an heir for this purpose. The source is the enactment of Usha, which gives the husband purchaser like power over melog property sold by the wife. Tosafot explains that even if the husband were ordinarily an heir, he would not necessarily reverse an appraisal from the lender, because the Sages do not preserve heir status where doing so would cause loss to purchasers or creditors. Conversely, where making him a purchaser would cause him personal loss, such as return at Jubilee, the Sages leave him in the Torah status of heir. The Shach objects that the husband is biblically an heir and the Sages cannot uproot that Torah status merely to create a loss for others. In my view Tosafot's position can be defended. The Sages already showed in the Jubilee context that they actively decide whether to leave the husband under his Torah inheritance status or treat him as purchaser for a particular rabbinic rule. Where maintaining heir status would cause him significant personal loss, they leave Torah law untouched. Where there is no comparable loss to him and treating him as purchaser protects others, they apply purchaser status. The fact that a reversed appraisal is only a rule of "upright and good" is crucial. Failure to return such land to the husband is not regarded as a serious loss to him, because strict law would never have required return at all. By contrast, forcing the creditor to return the land is a real loss to the creditor, since strict law entitled him to keep it. Thus the Sages could apply the Usha purchaser status here without contradicting the Torah inheritance rule in its core operation. This also explains why the Jubilee case differs. There, treating the husband as purchaser would cause him actual statutory loss of his wife's inherited property. The Sages therefore leave him as heir. Here, the only supposed loss to him is loss of a later fairness enactment, while the creditor would suffer real loss. Tosafot's reasoning is therefore sound.
Specifically if the woman died. The Bach and Shach argue that according to Rosh and Tur, the husband can be treated as purchaser even during her lifetime, because after the enactment of Usha he can recover the body of melog property from purchasers even while she lives. Beit Shmuel makes the same point. After reflection, this is not decisive. The enactment of Usha did not give the husband present ownership of the body of his wife's property during her lifetime. She owns the corpus, while he owns fruits. Rashi explains that the Sages merely strengthened his fruit right so that it can block her sale, treating that fruit right as powerful enough to resemble ownership of the body for that specific purpose. The Gemara concerning a slave's tooth and eye makes this explicit: even after Usha the husband does not own the slave's body, but his fruit lien is strengthened so much that neither husband nor wife alone can effect the release. Thus during the wife's lifetime, the body of the land remains hers. If land was appraised from her and she later married, she can still receive the land back while alive because she remains owner of the corpus. The husband has only the strengthened fruit right. Tosafot in Ketubot may sound as though the Usha enactment makes the husband a purchaser retroactively, but the most coherent reading is that his purchaser like status is functional, not actual ownership of the body during her life. This distinction has consequences for prozbul and other rules depending on true land ownership. The same analysis explains "after you" gifts. Ordinarily a second beneficiary can acquire the corpus at the moment the first beneficiary dies, before an heir acquires it. One might therefore ask why a husband's rights under Usha can defeat the later beneficiary, since the husband does not acquire the body until the wife's death. The answer is that the strengthened fruit right already exists during her lifetime and blocks the second beneficiary's acquisition of the corpus at death, just as it blocks her sale. Accordingly, the Tur's phrase "when the woman died" is needed primarily for the case where land had been appraised from her and she married. During her lifetime the appraisal can still revert to her because the body remains hers. After death the husband's purchaser status prevents reversion. The reverse case, where land had been appraised to the woman and she then married, is different. Even during her lifetime the borrower may be unable to recover it, because reversal of appraisal requires a new transfer from the creditor back to the borrower, and her husband can block her from transferring the body due to his strengthened rights. This also explains why the Gemara's first clause does not mention death while the second does. The Shach, following Mordechai, offers another explanation that after the wife's death the husband is excluded because the right is only expected rather than presently owned. That can also explain part of the sugya. Yet the distinction above between reversion to her and reversion from her better accounts for the textual differences. This further resolves the apparent conflict with siman 112, where a creditor cannot collect the body of melog property even during the wife's lifetime. The husband's strengthened fruit right under Usha blocks the creditor's attempt to acquire the corpus, just as it blocks purchasers. Therefore Sema's attempt to distinguish melog from iron flock property is unnecessary.
