If the creditor does not wish, etc. The Mechaber wrote this based on the distinction he proposed in Bedek HaBayit. Baal HaTerumot and the Tur allow the creditor to say to the debtor, 'Sell me the remainder, or pay me.' The Rashba's responsum concerns a creditor who does not wish to purchase the remainder, whereas Baal HaTerumot concerns one who offers to buy it. The choice therefore belongs to the creditor. Rabbenu Yerucham and the Maggid Mishneh likewise support the Rashba's ruling, while Baal HaTerumot cites the Ramban in agreement with the Tur. In truth there is no contradiction. The creditor may invoke the rule of 'buy or sell': either the debtor pays and keeps the entire property, or the creditor takes a sufficient portion and pays for the excess. The same applies if the creditor plans immediately to resell rather than retain the property. Baal HaTerumot expressly speaks where no outside purchaser can be found. In that situation the creditor may require the debtor either to sell him the remainder or to satisfy the debt.

Or mortgage it. The Rashba and Rabbenu Yerucham read, 'or rent it.' The Darkei Moshe explains that it may be rented only where the full debt can be raised at once. The creditor need not wait while it is rented annually for a small amount, because that would close the door to borrowers. It appears more precise that the tenant must pay the rent immediately. Since rent ordinarily becomes payable only at the end of the rental period, it is improper to compel the creditor to wait. The Mechaber therefore omitted rental, because advance rental payment is uncommon, and mentioned sale or mortgage instead.
If the situation was reversed, etc., he cannot, etc. The Sema explains that the debtor cannot compel the creditor only where the creditor insists upon cash. If the debtor offers the creditor a choice, either to purchase the excess for money or to return enough land to leave the debtor a viable parcel, the Tur allows the arrangement. The Mechaber, however, states in Bedek HaBayit that Baal HaTerumot and the Tur disagree with Rabbenu Yerucham and the Rashba: according to the former, the debtor may compel the creditor to take the excess, whereas according to the latter he may not. The Sema's reconciliation is not convincing. If selling a portion would leave the creditor without a viable parcel, the debtor plainly cannot demand it. If the creditor would still retain a viable parcel, the ruling is obvious, because a debtor may always pay cash and may combine cash and land. There would have been no need for Baal HaTerumot to prove the point. Nevertheless, as explained below, the Rashba and Rabbenu Yerucham can be understood consistently with Baal HaTerumot and the Tur on a different basis.
He cannot compel, etc. The Mechaber believed that the Rashba disagrees with Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, but careful reading of the Rashba's responsum shows the opposite. The Rashba's entire discussion establishes that land too small for ordinary use is economically ruined and cannot be forced upon a creditor. He therefore compels the debtor to pay money or rent his share and use the proceeds. This same principle applies in reverse. If taking the creditor's share would leave the debtor with a useless remnant, the loss of the debtor must also be considered. When a parcel is divisible so that each part retains its recognized use, it is divided. Otherwise, one party must buy out the other. The Rashba's concern not to remove a debtor from his home for a minor debt does not mean that the debtor's loss is ignored. It means that an unusably small parcel is treated as no payment at all, so the debtor must sell or rent enough to pay. Where the creditor receives a viable portion but the debtor would be left with a ruined remnant, the creditor must either purchase that remainder or share the property in a manner preserving a viable unit. The Maggid Mishneh's citation of the Rashba concerning a creditor collecting from a purchaser confirms this interpretation. There is no sound distinction between the debtor himself and a purchaser from him. The law always considers whether either party will be left with a parcel below the minimum useful measure. Therefore the principal ruling follows Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, and the Rashba agrees. This applies only when no buyer or mortgagee can be found. If the entire property can be sold, mortgaged, or rented for immediate payment, that course is taken, the creditor is paid, and the balance is returned to the debtor. It also assumes that the creditor refuses joint ownership. If he is willing to leave enough of his share so that the property remains usable and to alternate use with the debtor, he need not buy the debtor out. Since neither party can invoke an ordinary buy-or-sell demand without funds, they remain partners under the laws explained in siman 171.
He can compel him, etc. This is the ruling of Baal HaMaor. Ramban, in Sefer HaMilchamot, disputes it regarding a purchaser, but his objection is not decisive, and Baal HaMaor's ruling is primary. The practical result is as follows. If the debt is too small to give the creditor a viable parcel, the creditor may remove the debtor from the property and take all of it, or sell or mortgage it, because he can say: 'Either pay me and keep the whole property, or I will take a proper parcel and pay you for the excess above my debt.' Conversely, if collection would leave the debtor with less than a viable parcel, the entire property is sold or mortgaged where possible, the creditor is paid, and the surplus is given to the debtor. If no buyer can be found, the debtor may demand either that the creditor leave enough property for joint use or that he pay cash for the excess he takes. These rules apply equally to the debtor and to a purchaser from him.
