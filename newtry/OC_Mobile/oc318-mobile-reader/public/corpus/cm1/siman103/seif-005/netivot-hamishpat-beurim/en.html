And if the creditor does not wish to. See Shach, seif katan 2, who writes that when the creditor does not wish to acquire the property for himself but wishes to sell the whole parcel, this too is included in the rule of gud o agud. What difference is there whether he buys it for himself or sells it to another, since he has the right to acquire it and then resell it? At first glance, the Mechaber's statement that we compel the debtor to sell the land in order to pay this creditor money seems to mean that he may be compelled to sell even below market value. If so, Shach's comparison to gud o agud is difficult, because siman 171 states that one cannot demand gud o agud at less than fair value. It therefore appears that Shach does not understand the Mechaber to require a sale at any price available, even half the proper value. We do not impose an unnecessary monetary loss on the debtor. Rama's statement that we do not consider the debtor's loss means that we may remove him from his house, not that we deliberately destroy value, as Shach explains in seif katan 6. The Mechaber's statement that he is compelled to sell means only when a purchaser can be found at the appraised value. On that understanding Shach correctly says that the case resembles gud o agud. The creditor says, in effect, "I will arrange sale of the whole property." Even according to Rama in siman 171, who does not permit a partner to invoke gud o agud merely because he wishes to sell to a third party, that limitation applies after the parties are already partners. Here the creditor is only now about to become a partner through collection, so there is no reason to distinguish between acquisition by the creditor and acquisition by another.
And if the matter was the reverse. See Shach, seif katan 5, who questions Sema's explanation of Tur and Baal HaTerumot. Sema understands the debtor to give the creditor a choice: either take cash, or give the debtor enough of the land to constitute a field of proper size, leaving the remaining land in the creditor's hands toward the debt; the debtor will then sell the portion returned to him and use the proceeds to satisfy the balance. Shach objects that if, after the debtor sells a proper sized portion, the creditor will no longer retain a proper sized field, it is obvious that the debtor cannot compel this arrangement. If the creditor will still retain a proper sized field, however, what novelty do Tur and Baal HaTerumot teach? Even schoolchildren know that a debtor can pay money whenever he wishes, so why did Baal HaTerumot need proof from Baal HaMaor? In my view the question is unfounded. Suppose the land is worth two hundred, the debt is one hundred fifty, and a minimally usable field is worth one hundred. If the debtor says, "First collect one hundred from half the land, then I will sell the other half and pay the remaining fifty," siman 74 states that once the debt is due, the debtor ordinarily cannot compel the creditor to accept payment piecemeal rather than all at once. If instead the debtor first sells half the land for one hundred, then he possesses cash, and the rule that a creditor is paid in money requires him to give the creditor all one hundred in cash. Only fifty of debt remains, leaving the creditor without a usable parcel and forcing sale of the other half as well. The novelty is therefore that despite the ordinary rule against piecemeal satisfaction, here, to avoid disproportionate loss to the debtor, the debt may be collected in stages.
He cannot compel him. See Shach, seif katan 6, who writes that according to the Mechaber, Rashba's responsa would contradict one another. His point is that the purchaser's position is stronger than the creditor's, as the Mechaber explains, so why should the creditor here be able to compel the debtor? The answer is that where only a very small and economically useless remainder would be left to the debtor, the disproportionate loss allows compulsion even against the creditor.
