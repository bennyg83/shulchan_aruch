He cannot compel, etc. The Shach objects that the Beit Yosef introduced this distinction in Bedek HaBayit only in order to reconcile the Baal HaMaor with a responsum of the Rashba, while in truth the Rashba never says that the creditor cannot be compelled to take the small remaining portion of land. On that textual point the Shach is correct. Still, the Mechaber's practical distinction appears sound. The rule that a purchaser may compel the creditor to take the small remaining parcel for its value is the Baal HaMaor's view. Ramban in Milchamot disagrees. He says that the creditor can answer, "What have I to do with you? I neither sold you land nor bought it from you. I will take only the portion to which my lien entitles me, and what remains is not my concern." Even if we do not rule like Ramban regarding a purchaser, there is reason to distinguish. The purchaser is in possession of his own land and owes the creditor nothing personally. He may say that just as the Sages were concerned not to close the door before borrowers, they should not close the door before purchasers by forcing a buyer to acquire land that he does not want and perhaps lose even his own property to a later seizure. With the debtor himself, however, Ramban's reasoning is persuasive. The creditor can say, "What is it to me if you lose? A borrower is servant to the lender. Sell the remaining land and pay me cash." There is also a genuine lending concern if the creditor who had enough money to buy only a small parcel is forced to take more land than his money warrants merely because the debtor has no convenient way to divide it. He never contemplated such a compelled purchase. The Rif's statements that support the Baal HaMaor concern a purchaser, not the debtor himself. There is therefore no basis to reject Ramban in the debtor's case. Many distinctions properly exist between a debtor and a purchaser.
