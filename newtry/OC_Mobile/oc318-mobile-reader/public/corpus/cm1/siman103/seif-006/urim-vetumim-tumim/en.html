Or it is held as collateral. The Sema infers from the word "or" that if one may sell the land even when it is not physically held as collateral, all the more when it is. He therefore explains that when the property is in the creditor's possession as collateral, there is no need to determine whether the fixed payment date has passed. As explained in Urim, however, this is strained because the essential point is missing from the Shulchan Aruch's wording. The Baal HaTerumot reveals the true meaning. The authorities derive the rule from Pesachim, where Abaye holds that a creditor collects retroactively: when the due date arrives and the debtor does not redeem the pledged property, it becomes clear retroactively that the property was the creditor's. Thus if the creditor consecrated or sold it before maturity, his act is effective. Rava holds that the creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward. These authorities reason that even according to Rava, once the due date has arrived and the debtor has failed to redeem, the property is then the creditor's and he may sell it, just as Abaye would permit even before maturity. This also shows that the Shach's assertion that the first opinion, which denies a right to sell, is proven by the Pesachim passage is backwards. The second opinion derives its principal proof from that very sugya. The first opinion only attempts to deflect that proof through various answers. The Baal HaIttur, citing Rabbenu Chananel, explains that the Pesachim case concerns property actually held by the creditor as collateral. Therefore, even Rava's forward looking acquisition allows sale once the due date passes. Without possession of the collateral, the creditor may not sell. The Mechaber's word "or" therefore introduces an alternative legal formulation: either the rule is that passage of the fixed payment date itself allows sale, or, according to Rabbenu Chananel, that statement is incorrect unless the property is actually in the creditor's possession as collateral. The wording is thus exact. The Giddulei Terumah asks that if Abaye and Rava's entire dispute concerns pledged property, why does the Gemara challenge Rava from the Mishnah concerning a non Jew who lent to a Jew against chametz and answer that the chametz was pledged to him? Before that answer, the Mishnah was not assumed to involve possession of collateral, so it should have been difficult for Abaye as well. The answer is that "pledged to him" there does not merely mean that the object was delivered as ordinary collateral. Had that been intended, the Gemara would have said so directly. Rather, it means that the debtor stipulated, "If I do not redeem it, acquire it from now." Under such a condition everyone agrees that the creditor does not violate the chametz prohibition, as explained in Orach Chaim siman 441. The Gemara therefore did not simply establish an ordinary pledge, which according to Rava would still be acquired only from the due date onward.
He may sell it. The wording appears to mean that he may sell without any appraisal. The Sema asks that above in siman 73 even one who holds movable collateral may not sell it except through beit din. He answers that there a panel of ordinary laymen suffices, and perhaps the same is intended here. This is strained, because neither the Tur nor the Baal HaTerumot, the source of the rule, mentions such a qualification. The Taz distinguishes land from movable property. Movable property can disappear and its former value may later be impossible to establish, so an appraisal is needed. Land remains in place and its value can supposedly be examined afterward. This too is difficult. Land may be improved or damaged, and its market value can change rapidly. Indeed, the difficulty of precise land valuation is one reason land is not governed by ordinary onaah. How then could one reconstruct the earlier value without an appraisal? There is proof from Bava Metzia, where Rav Safra wished to divide property without appraisal and brought proof from a widow who may sell without beit din. If the widow's land required no appraisal because land values remain objectively knowable, there would be no proof for movable property. Likewise Tosafot ask how a widow may appraise property for herself when a bailee who sells entrusted property must sell it to others rather than himself because of suspicion. If land values could always be reconstructed objectively, the concern of suspicion would differ sharply. Tosafot plainly do not assume such a distinction. More fundamentally, if the present rule eliminated appraisal and announcement whenever a fixed due date had passed, much of the Talmudic law of judicial appraisal and public announcement would disappear. It is untenable to say that all those passages concern only loans with no fixed date. The Gemara's ordinary cases include debts with fixed maturity. A widow from betrothal, for example, may collect her ketubah only after the marriage relationship ends, yet the Gemara requires beit din for her sale. The Baal HaTerumot's citation of Rabbi Yehudah Albartzeloni, distinguishing appraisal to oneself from sale to others, likewise cannot mean that all sale to others is exempt from appraisal, because Ketubot 98 clearly indicates that apart from the special widow rule, ordinary creditors require announcement even when selling to third parties. Therefore. The authorities' true point must be narrower. Their entire proof comes from Abaye and Rava in Pesachim. Abaye's statement that retroactive collection makes the creditor's earlier sale or consecration effective does not imply that appraisal and announcement are unnecessary. It only means that the creditor's act is not void as a sale of another person's property. Likewise, according to Rava, after maturity and nonpayment the creditor has sufficient proprietary power to sell or consecrate. Nevertheless, appraisal and announcement are still required to determine how much of the land corresponds to the debt, what the property was worth, and whether a better price can be obtained. Those procedures protect the debtor from losing excessive value. Thus the rule rejects only the opinion that before completed appraisal and announcement the creditor has no transferable interest at all, so any sale is a nullity. Once the due date has passed, his sale can take effect, subject to subsequent appraisal and announcement. If those later procedures establish the value, it becomes clear retroactively how much of the creditor's sale was effective. If the debtor later seeks to redeem the property under the rule that an appraisal may be reversed, he can no longer do so after the creditor sold it, because an appraisal that has been sold onward is not reversed. This understanding is proved by the Rosh in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasu'i. In the case of two mansions, he cites Rabbenu Yonah that the creditor can demand a higher valuation only before beit din has completed appraisal and announcement. Once beit din has appraised and placed the purchaser into the land, the debtor cannot displace him. Ramah adds that the case is where the creditor then sold it to another, after which the appraisal no longer returns. This passage itself contemplates a sale before the entire appraisal process was completed, yet still insists that appraisal and announcement ultimately matter. The only coherent reading is that the earlier sale is valid in principle, while the judicial process fixes its monetary scope. Rabbi Yehudah Albartzeloni can be understood similarly. His statement that where the creditor sells to others the sale can precede completion of the appraisal addresses the time when the proprietary act takes effect, not the elimination of appraisal. His distinction also helps explain the Gemara's rule that the creditor begins eating fruits only after the days of announcement have ended. Where he is taking the property for himself, he has no completed title until then. Where he has sold to another, the sale may already be effective subject to the later valuation. In any event, it is clear that the authorities never intended to dispense with appraisal and announcement. Those procedures remain necessary; only the creditor's earlier sale is capable of taking effect.
