They remove him, etc. The rule is based on "you shall do what is good and upright." "Good" means acting beyond the strict letter of the law by returning a debtor's land to him when he later redeems it. This applies specifically to land, which is an ancestral holding meant to remain for generations, not to movable property.
A purchaser cannot compel return. The Rosh explains that a purchaser who bought the creditor's lien does not himself stand within this equitable rule. He bought the creditor's right for money and can look to the seller for reimbursement, leaving the original creditor's lien intact. Even if he cannot presently recover from the seller, he caused his own loss by purchasing a right known to be subject to another's lien.
He does not receive his expenses. The Tur records a dispute among the Geonim. Beit Yosef says that because of doubt we rule leniently for the defendant, and the Mechaber therefore rules that the creditor receives no reimbursement for expenses. This implies that the original debtor, whose land can be restored, is regarded as the one in possession and the creditor as the claimant. I argued that the opposite is more plausible: once beit din appraised and definitively awarded the land to the creditor, he is in possession and consumes its fruits. Chazal merely enacted that if the debtor or his heirs later obtain funds, the creditor must resell it to them. The debtor is therefore the claimant and, in a case of doubt, should reimburse the creditor's building expenses if he wants the land back.
He swears and takes. Meaning he swears how much he spent and takes that amount, and also receives the proper share of any improvement above his expenses, like a partner who entered property with authorization. Nevertheless, the entire improvement does not belong to him because the original appraisal can be reversed.
He gives him only the amount of his debt. The Tur explains that because appraisal can always be reversed, the money in the debtor's hands is effectively like a loan. If the creditor were also to receive the passive increase in value merely for waiting for his money, that would resemble interest. The Mechaber therefore speaks of market appreciation. There is room to distinguish a physical improvement that arose in the land itself while in the creditor's possession, but perhaps even there the debtor's mazal is considered the cause. In any event, the ruling teaches that even ordinary appreciation in market value need not be paid to the creditor.
Some say that if he spent money and improved it, it never returns. I explained that according to this view even an offer to reimburse the creditor for the expenses and improvement does not restore the land, because the change gives the creditor a stronger acquisition. Passive appreciation, however, remains subject to the rule of "good and upright."
Only at the original high value. The Rosh writes that it is unreasonable to allow a debtor to wait indefinitely for land prices to rise and then redeem, for that would close the door before borrowers.
If he made a kinyan not to remove him, etc. See what I wrote in Perishah.
Voluntarily, without beit din. Beit Yosef infers from Rashi that whenever the creditor had to trouble the debtor by bringing him to beit din, even if the debtor then immediately agreed to give land in payment, it is considered a judicial appraisal and can be reversed.
