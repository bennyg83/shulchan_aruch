When beit din appraised land for a creditor, the borrower may later remove him from that land. Rashba writes that if land was appraised to a minor, the appraisal is not reversed until the minor becomes an adult and is legally capable of transferring ownership. This shows that reversal of the appraisal requires a new conveyance from the creditor back to the borrower. Ramban likewise writes so, as cited by Kesef Mishneh in the laws of lending.
He does not take his expenses. Tur records a dispute among the Geonim whether, when an appraisal is later reversed, the creditor receives reimbursement for improvements or expenses. Beit Yosef rules leniently for the defendant because the matter is doubtful, treating the borrower who can redeem the land with money as the present possessor of the land. Sema takes the opposite view: once beit din appraised and transferred the land to the creditor, the creditor is the possessor, and the borrower's later right to recover it is only a rabbinic enactment of fairness. Taz defends the Mechaber from the law of a tree planted near a city, where if there is doubt which came first the tree is cut but payment is not made unless the owner proves entitlement. Since the tree is certainly destined to be cut and only payment is doubtful, possession follows the party presently holding the money. Taz analogizes this to reversal of appraisal. In my view, however, Sema's approach is supported by Rosh regarding a neighboring owner. Rosh says that once the purchaser lawfully acquired the land, the neighboring owner's later right of preemption exists only by the rabbinic rule of "you shall do what is upright and good." Therefore, in doubt, the purchaser is the possessor even though the land is ultimately subject to transfer if the neighbor proves his right. Rosh expressly distinguishes the case of heirs who say, "We improved," where the creditor's direct lien in an apotiki makes the land effectively collected and places the burden on the heirs. The same logic applies to reversal of judicial appraisal. The creditor already received the land lawfully. The borrower's right to get it back is only a later enactment of fairness. That should normally make the creditor the possessor for doubtful subsidiary payments. The Mechaber's ruling can perhaps be reconciled by distinguishing uncertainty in the factual transaction from a dispute among decisors. When the facts are uncertain, the Sages never extended the fairness enactment against the existing possessor. But where the facts are clear and the only uncertainty is which halachic opinion governs reimbursement, the borrower can say, "I follow the authority who says the appraisal reverts without expenses." Since everyone agrees the land itself is to revert, the dispute concerns only the extra money. This may justify treating the borrower as stronger in that narrow halachic doubt.
