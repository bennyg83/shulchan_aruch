They remove him from that land, etc. This implies that no new act of acquisition is required. Mere removal of the former holder suffices. The Nimukei Yosef writes similarly in the first chapter of Bava Metzia concerning Rabbi Zeira's explanation of the Mishna as referring to final writs of judicial award, and he states that this appears to be the Rambam's view and the interpretation of the Ran.
Some say that a purchaser, etc. The Maggid Mishneh and Baal HaTerumot agree with the Rambam and the Mechaber, as the Beit Yosef records, and Ramban likewise rules so in his novellae. Ritva writes that where land was appraised and transferred to a minor, the appraisal is not reversed until the minor reaches majority and is capable of conveying title. Ritva evidently maintains that reversal requires a new act of acquisition. He therefore explains the Gemara's statement that the creditor should tear the original document or write another document as requiring either a renewed kinyan sudar from the creditor to the debtor or an entirely new deed. Ramban, cited by the Kesef Mishneh, writes similarly. According to the Nimukei Yosef and Ran, however, no new acquisition is needed, and only removal of the creditor is required. On that view, the land may be restored from a minor as soon as the debtor seeks to remove him. Perhaps this is why the Darkei Moshe, after citing Ritva, referred to the Nimukei Yosef's discussion whether reversal of an appraisal requires a new document or whether return of the original document suffices. See Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Cohen, part II, siman 81.
He takes an oath and collects. The Sema explains that he swears how much he spent and receives his expenses together with a share of the increase beyond those expenses. This is incorrect. Why should he receive a partner's share in the increase? He is not a partner. Rather, he receives compensation from the increase under the law governing local planters, like one who entered with permission, as explained below in siman 375.
He does not give him, etc. The Bach rules likewise. The Sema distinguishes land that increased by itself, such as ground upon which alluvium accumulated or a palm tree that grew stronger. In my view, even such natural increase is treated as interest, as is explicit in the Rosh concerning one who sold land that was later found not to belong to him. The Sema's position is also difficult on its own terms. If recognizable increase beyond expenses belongs entirely to the creditor, it should certainly do so where the palm merely grew stronger. The correct rule is therefore as stated here. See Responsa Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 157.
If he improved it, it is not restored at all, etc. This wording appears to mean even if the debtor is prepared to pay the entire increase, including the portion beyond the creditor's expenses, and the Sema explains it so. In my view, the Rosh also agrees that in such a case the land is restored. He means only that it is not restored when the debtor refuses to pay the whole present increase, just as land that rose in value is restored only if the debtor pays its current value. Otherwise there is no reason why improvement alone should permanently defeat the law that an appraisal is reversible. Indeed, every creditor could make a slight improvement and thereby prevent restoration entirely. The Rosh couples improvement and appreciation together, indicating that the same principle governs both. It is unreasonable to require the creditor to wait indefinitely while the debtor watches whether land values rise, and it is likewise unreasonable to require him to wait while the debtor benefits from improvements made by the creditor. Thus restoration is denied only unless the debtor pays the full increase. The Tur's wording also indicates this, for he says that the land is not restored even if the debtor offers merely to reimburse the creditor's expenses. That excludes payment of expenses alone, but not payment of the entire increase. The Tur therefore disputes Baal HaTerumot only as to whether the creditor has the ordinary rights of one who improved property with permission. Where the debtor pays the whole increase, even the Rosh requires restoration. If one nevertheless interprets the Tur and Mechaber as denying restoration even when the debtor pays the full increase because the creditor acquired the land through a change, then they must also disagree with the earlier ruling concerning demolition and rebuilding without added value. The Rama's unqualified acceptance of the earlier ruling would then be difficult. This requires study.
Voluntarily, without beit din. The Sema writes that the Beit Yosef inferred from Rashi that once the creditor troubled the debtor to appear before beit din, the transfer is deemed a judicial appraisal and is reversible, even if the debtor immediately consented to give land for the debt. In my view, Rashi implies the opposite. Unless beit din appraised and transferred the land against the debtor's will, the debtor has not truly burdened beit din, even if he appeared before them. We similarly rule that although a bailee was brought before beit din for an oath, if he afterward paid voluntarily he acquires the right to the double payment. The phrase that he did not burden beit din means that the court did not compel payment. See Tosafot, the Rosh, and Mordechai there, and Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Cohen, part II, siman 24.
