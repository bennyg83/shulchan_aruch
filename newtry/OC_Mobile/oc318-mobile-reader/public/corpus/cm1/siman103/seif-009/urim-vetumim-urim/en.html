Where land was appraised to a creditor, etc. Nimukei Yosef concludes that where an appraisal is subject to return, no new kinyan or deed is required. Once the debtor repays the creditor, the land automatically returns to him. Although the land had previously been definitively transferred to the creditor, and the creditor was not merely a secured lender, for otherwise he would have been prohibited from eating its produce, the Sages had power to annul the original appraisal and restore the land to the debtor. Rashi in Bava Metzia 16b likewise implies that no new deed is needed, and that one who nevertheless writes one has not erred.
To a creditor. If the creditor to whom the land was appraised is a minor, Shach holds that there is no need to wait until he reaches majority because no new kinyan is required. See Tumim, seif katan 8, where I wrote that this requires study in practical law.
But an appraisal of movable property. See Tumim for the reason. Even movable property that is generally unavailable for purchase is not subject to the rule that an appraisal returns. The enactment applies only to land.
They remove him. This is based on the principle of what is right and good. The land was taken from the debtor against his will because he had no money to pay, and the Sages therefore enacted that when he later has money, the creditor must return his land.
And some say that a purchaser, etc. If the purchaser can recover his purchase money from the seller, he obviously suffers no loss. Even if the seller has no money and the purchaser cannot recover, who authorized him to purchase land already encumbered to others? The principle of what is right and good therefore does not require the creditor to return it to him.
He does not receive his expenses. This is disputed by the authorities cited in Tur. Some say the debtor must reimburse the creditor's improvements because the creditor is like one who entered the property with permission. Others say he need not reimburse. The Mechaber rules that the burden of proof is upon the claimant and the debtor is exempt. Sema writes that the opposite appears correct, because the creditor is lawfully in possession of the field and the debtor seeks to remove him only by virtue of a special enactment. The creditor should therefore have the stronger hand and need not leave until all his expenses are reimbursed. See Tumim, seif katan 10, where I discussed this at length and gave reasoning for the Mechaber's position. Nevertheless, the matter requires study, and Knesset HaGedolah agrees with Sema.
And he receives compensation like one who entered with permission. Sema explains that he receives all his expenses plus a share of the improvement, like a partner who entered with permission. Shach writes that there is no partnership here. Rather, he is compensated like an authorized agricultural worker. This is correct.
He does not give, etc. Tur explains that giving the creditor the entire appreciation would appear like interest. Bach writes that this reason is not literal. Rather, because the land is destined to return, any appreciation occurred while it remained under the debtor's underlying right. If a palm tree grew thicker or alluvial soil improved the field, Sema is uncertain. Shach concludes that the same rule applies, and the creditor receives only according to the money owed because the additional gain likewise resembles interest. This is correct. The reason based on appearance of interest does not require the creditor to return produce already consumed, as Bedek HaBayis suggests, because below in siman 207 an unoffset mortgage is only rabbinic interest, which is not recoverable after payment. This case is no worse. It is simple.
If it improved, it does not return at all. Even if the debtor wishes to pay the full present value including the improvement, Sema holds that the property is now considered changed, with a new form, and the creditor need not return it. The deeper reason is that the Sages feared that otherwise a creditor would never invest in improving the property, knowing that the debtor could later come and take the benefit of his labor. There would be no principle of what is right and good toward the debtor in such a case. Shach disagrees and holds that if the debtor pays the full current value, the creditor must return it. See Tumim, where I brought proof for Sema from Rabbeinu Yeruham and explained the source of Rosh's proof from the Gemara.
At the time of the higher price. Otherwise the debtor would always wait until property values rise and then offer only the original amount paid. That would not be conduct that is right and good. This is the view of Rosh and Sema.
And if they made a kinyan from him, etc. Just as below in siman 175 a neighboring landowner may relinquish his right of preemption by kinyan, so here the debtor may relinquish his right to return of the appraisal. See siman 175 seif 29.
Voluntarily, without beit din. Beit Yosef and Sema understand this to mean that the debtor never came to beit din at all but voluntarily gave the land as payment. Shach writes that even if beit din conducted an appraisal, if the debtor came voluntarily without compulsion, the appraisal does not later return. See Tumim. It appears that if beit din appraised the property but never formally transferred possession, and the debtor afterward gave it to the creditor according to the appraised value, it nevertheless does not return because no beit din transfer actually occurred.
