Where land was appraised to a creditor, etc. The Ritva holds that if land was appraised to a minor, the appraisal is not reversed until the minor reaches adulthood and can convey the property back to the debtor. The Shach correctly explains that the Ritva must follow the authorities who regard reversal of an appraisal as requiring a new conveyance from creditor to debtor. According to Nimukei Yosef and those who hold that no new kinyan is required, the minor's incapacity to convey should make no difference and the appraisal could be reversed immediately. The Ritva indeed holds that a new kinyan is required, as appears in Shitah Mekubetzet. I have a different reservation. Many authorities compare the requirement to act uprightly and well that underlies reversal of an appraisal to the same principle underlying the law of bar metzra. Below, siman 175:47, we rule that where land is sold to a minor orphan, the adjoining owner has no bar metzra claim at all. Rivash siman 507 explains explicitly that the reason is not merely that no one is available to trouble on behalf of the minor, but that the command to act uprightly and well is not imposed in the same way upon a minor orphan. If so, the same reasoning should apply here. Since reversal of an appraisal rests entirely on that equitable principle, perhaps a minor should not be compelled to return the property at all. Practical law therefore requires study.
But an appraisal of movable property does not return, etc. Pilpula Charifta labors to explain why movable property differs from land, but the matter is simple. Movable property is commonly available for purchase. A person who receives money can ordinarily acquire equivalent movable goods elsewhere, so there is no special demand of upright and good conduct requiring return of the particular items formerly his. Land is different. Land is not commonly available, and a person naturally values his own land more than another's. The Sages therefore applied the principle of upright and good conduct and required reversal of a land appraisal. For that reason the Ritva considered whether unusually rare movable items might also be subject to reversal. He was pressed by the Gemara in HaMafkid, which asks whether Rav Nachman holds that appraisal returns based on his direction to return certain jewels to their owner. Since jewels are movable property, the Ritva suggested that rare movable items may differ. But most authorities explain that the Gemara's question was not from the jewels themselves. It was from Rav Nachman's accompanying rule concerning return of a mansion. Once that is the source, there is no need to distinguish among types of movable property. The Sages established one uniform rule: appraisal of movable property does not return.
He does not receive his expenses, etc. The Tur records a dispute whether the creditor whose appraised land is being redeemed is reimbursed for his expenditures. The Mechaber rules that because of the doubt money is not extracted from the debtor who is redeeming. The Sema objects that the creditor is lawfully in possession of the land, while the debtor removes him only by a rabbinic enactment. The creditor should therefore be considered the possessor, and the debtor should not recover the land until he satisfies the creditor's demand. At first glance the Mechaber has support from the Rosh in Bava Metzia, chapter HaMekabel. In the law of bar metzra, where the adjoining owner says that the seller reduced the price for everyone while the purchaser says that the reduction was personal to him, the Rosh places the burden of proof on the adjoining owner. The land belongs to the purchaser by strict law, and the adjoining owner's claim rests only on the rabbinic principle of upright and good conduct. The Rosh distinguishes the case of heirs who say that they themselves created the improvement, where the creditor must prove otherwise because the land is already destined for his collection. The Rosh explains that the latter case concerns an apotiki, where the creditor's claim is inherently tied to the land. The Mechaber below, siman 115:5, follows the Rambam that even without a specific apotiki the heirs bear the burden regarding improvement. If so, one might reason that here too the debtor redeeming the land should be treated as the one entitled to it, leaving the creditor to prove his expense claim. That would support the Mechaber. But then in bar metzra the Mechaber should likewise place the burden on the purchaser, whereas in siman 175:7 he follows the Rosh and requires the adjoining owner to prove the price reduction. An apparent contradiction remains. The better reading is that the Rosh mentioned apotiki because that was his own legal view and therefore stated the strongest form of the case, but the conceptual distinction does not depend entirely on it. In an ordinary creditor case the creditor collects by substantive law, so the property can be regarded as standing for collection. In bar metzra, by contrast, the adjoining owner has no legal title at all without the special rabbinic enactment. Any doubt in that enactment reverts to ordinary law, leaving the purchaser in possession. This explains the Mechaber's ruling in bar metzra even while he follows the Rambam concerning heirs. The present case nevertheless seems to resemble bar metzra, which strengthens the Sema's objection: reversal of an appraisal is also only a rabbinic rule founded on upright conduct, while the creditor presently holds the land by law. The Rama creates an additional tension because below in siman 115 he follows the Rosh that without apotiki even land destined for collection remains sufficiently in the heirs' possession to place the burden on the creditor, yet here he accepts the Mechaber's statement that the creditor, who already possesses the land and can be displaced only by enactment, must prove his expense claim. The resolution is that the disputed expenses do not represent improvement embedded in the land itself. The creditor's claim is only that because he entered lawfully, the debtor who now removes him should reimburse what he spent. It is therefore an ordinary monetary claim against the debtor, not a possessory dispute over the land. The debtor can say that in a dispute among authorities the burden of proof rests on the claimant and assert kim li. The creditor's possession of the land does not allow him to hold it merely as security for this separate expense claim where the land itself contains no corresponding improvement. By contrast, when heirs say that they themselves improved the land, the very substance of the disputed value is physically in the land. Likewise in bar metzra, where the purchaser claims that the seller personally discounted the price for him, the disputed benefit concerns the value of the land itself. There the party in possession of the land has the stronger presumptive position. Here the dispute is only a separate claim for reimbursement, so the debtor as holder of the money is considered the possessor. This reconciles the rulings.
It does not return at all, etc. The Rosh writes that this applies specifically where the creditor improved the land or where land values increased. It is unreasonable that the debtor should watch throughout his life until land rises in value and then redeem the field, for that would close the door before lenders. The Shach understands the Rosh as opening with two cases and concluding with one rule: both improvement and market appreciation are treated alike, and if the debtor pays the creditor the added value the appraisal returns. He therefore disagrees with the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, which rule that where the creditor himself improved the land, the appraisal does not return at all. Maharsach had already taken the Shach's position. Yet Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 6, expressly states as the Shulchan Aruch does: improvement prevents reversal entirely, while ordinary market appreciation permits reversal if the debtor pays the current higher value. The Rosh's wording itself supports this distinction. If the two cases had precisely the same law, there was no need to mention improvement separately. It would follow all the more from market appreciation. Rather, improvement changes the land itself, giving it a new character, as the Sema explains. No amount of payment restores the original appraisal. With mere market appreciation the land is still the same land, and the Rosh's concern that the debtor might wait for a rise in value is fully answered by requiring him to pay the current price. The main question is the source for the Rosh's distinction. There is a proof from Bava Kamma and Bava Metzia, where the Gemara says that three categories have an improvement appraised and paid in money: a firstborn against an ordinary heir, a creditor against heirs, and a creditor against a purchaser. Rabbenu Chananel, cited by the Rashba, understands the Gemara to be exhaustive, apart from an arus whom the Rashba explains as included in the category of a wrongful possessor. If land appraised to a creditor could later be redeemed after the creditor improved it merely by paying for the improvement, there would be a fourth case: debtor against creditor in a reversed appraisal. The debtor would recover the land while the creditor receives the value of his improvement. Since the Gemara does not list such a case, the Rosh concludes that once the creditor has improved the land the appraisal does not return at all. The Rosh then adds market appreciation. It is equally unreasonable for the creditor to lose the increase merely because the debtor waits for a favorable market. Therefore the debtor must pay the current value. But that situation still need not be listed among those who receive payment for improvement, because there was no physical improvement to be appraised. Thus the two clauses are deliberately distinct and the Rosh's law is well founded. One might suggest that where the creditor improved the land, he could retain in the land itself a portion corresponding to his improvement, as may appear from the Ritva. The Rosh, however, appears to hold that reversal of an appraisal, founded on the principle of upright conduct, was not enacted partially. Either the entire field returns or none does. This is also indicated by the rule concerning heirs who are treated as purchasers: even where there are only two heirs and one might conceptually be heir to half and purchaser of half, the Rosh does not order a half reversal. Thus improvement prevents reversal of the whole appraisal.
Voluntarily, without beit din, etc. The Gemara says that where the debtor himself transferred land in payment of his debt, Rabbi Acha and Ravina dispute whether the appraisal returns, and we rule that it does not. Rashi explains that beit din did not appraise the land against the debtor's will. Rather, the debtor arose on his own, did not trouble the creditor to litigate, and said, "Take this land for your debt." From this the Beit Yosef infers that the case involves no beit din proceeding at all. If the parties had come to beit din and the debtor ultimately agreed to give the field at the court's valuation, it would still be treated as a judicial appraisal and would return. The Sema accepts this. The Shach disagrees and holds that even where beit din made the valuation, so long as the debtor did not force beit din to remove him against his will but voluntarily said to the creditor, "Take it," the dispute applies and practical law is that it does not return. Although Rashi's wording leans toward the Beit Yosef, practical logic favors a middle understanding. If the debtor transferred the land wholly outside beit din, how did the creditor acquire it? Presumably through an ordinary deed of sale or conveyance. There was no judicial appraisal at all. Every purchaser privately estimates the value of what he buys, yet no one calls that an appraisal subject to later reversal. If the debtor executed an ordinary deed transferring the land in satisfaction of a preexisting debt, that is a genuine sale. It is indistinguishable in form from a cash sale and there is little basis even for an opinion that the rule of reversible appraisal should apply. The Ritva goes farther and holds that even after judicial collection, if the creditor received an ordinary deed for the land, the appraisal no longer returns. All the more should a wholly private sale be final. On the other hand, the Shach's reading that the entire appraisal and placement occurred in beit din, with the debtor merely cooperating, does not fit the Gemara's explanation of the opinion that the appraisal returns because the debtor avoided beit din out of embarrassment. If he was already before beit din and simply obeyed their instruction without resistance, embarrassment is unnecessary. That is simply proper conduct for one who accepts a Jewish court's direction. The best explanation is therefore that the valuation was initially made through beit din, but before beit din issued the final writ or formally placed the creditor into possession, the debtor himself voluntarily placed the creditor into the field. One opinion says that because the process originated in beit din it remains a reversible appraisal. We rule that because the final transfer occurred voluntarily and without troubling beit din, it is treated like a voluntary disposition and does not return. This preserves both Rashi's language and the Shach's essential insight. However. A difficulty remains from the Rosh's discussion in Mi SheHayah Nasu'i concerning the mansion case.
Ramah and Rabbenu Yonah struggle to explain why the creditor can prevent return of the mansion despite the general rule that an appraisal returns, and from there derive that an appraisal from a purchaser does not return. Why not simply answer that in that case the purchaser voluntarily allowed the final placement, making it nonreturnable under our rule? It is strained to say that those earlier amoraim necessarily followed the minority opinion in the later dispute. The distinction appears to be between the debtor and a purchaser. A debtor may voluntarily satisfy his personal obligation by giving his own land. When, after an appraisal, he chooses to hand it over without forcing beit din to complete the process, that voluntary act can become a genuine sale. A purchaser, however, owes the creditor nothing personally. The creditor reaches his land only by force of the preexisting lien and judicial seizure. If the purchaser simply hands over the land, he cannot later recover from his seller without a recognized judicial taking, and in substance the transfer remains dependent upon beit din. As between purchaser and creditor, therefore, there is no meaningful independent voluntary payment that would transform the seizure into an ordinary sale. Thus the Rosh's case remains a judicial appraisal and required the separate rule concerning purchasers. This distinction is persuasive, though because I have not found it stated by the authorities, practical law still requires study.
