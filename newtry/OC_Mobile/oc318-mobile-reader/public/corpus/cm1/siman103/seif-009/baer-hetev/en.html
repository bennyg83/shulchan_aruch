They remove him. This implies that no new act of acquisition is needed. Mere removal of the creditor from the property suffices. Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia writes that this appears from the Rambam's words and that the Ran explains them likewise. Shach.
His expenses. The Tur writes that the Geonim disagree about this. The Beit Yosef writes that because of the uncertainty we rule leniently for the defendant. Here the Mechaber rules that the creditor does not receive reimbursement of his expenses, which implies that the debtor, who has the right to redeem the land from the creditor, is viewed as the party in possession of the land. The Sema writes that the opposite appears correct. The creditor, after receiving the property through a beit din appraisal and having it definitively assigned to him, is the possessor, which is why he eats the fruits of the field. The Sages merely enacted that if the debtor or his heirs later obtain funds, the creditor must sell it back to them. Therefore the debtor is the claimant seeking to extract, and because of the doubt he must reimburse the creditor's expenses if he wishes to recover the land. The Taz writes that the Mechaber's ruling is primary and brings proof. Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses there, answers the Taz and rejects his proof at length.
Swears. The Sema explains that the creditor swears how much he spent and receives his expenses, together with a share of the increase beyond those expenses, like a partner who entered the property with authorization. The Shach writes that this is incorrect. What connection does partnership have here? Rather, he receives the increase according to the law of an authorized cultivator, like one who enters another's field with authorization, as stated in siman 375. See the Sema there. See the Taz.
By itself. The Sema writes that the text deliberately gives the example of the property rising in market price. If the land physically improved by itself, such as where sediment deposited and enlarged it or a palm tree grew stronger, then because the improvement is visibly attributable to the period when it was in the creditor's hands, the debtor must pay him for the improvement. The Shach disagrees. Even where sediment accumulates or the palm grows stronger, giving the creditor the increase would constitute interest. The Rosh says this explicitly in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding one who sells a field that turns out not to be his. Moreover, according to the Sema's reasoning, if there were improvement beyond the creditor's expenses, because that improvement is likewise visible, the debtor should have to give the creditor the entire improvement, all the more so from the case of the strengthened palm. Rather, the Shach's view is correct. See Responsa Mahara Sasson 157 and Rashach II, 81.
At all. The Sema writes that even if the debtor wishes to pay the creditor the value of the improvement and expenses, he cannot compel return where the creditor acquired the land through a material change. If the land improved by itself, however, the principle of doing what is right and good applies, and acquisition through change does not apply because one can say that the debtor's fortune caused the improvement. The Shach challenges this and writes that even in such a case the Rosh agrees the property returns. See his lengthy discussion.
The increase in price. The Rosh writes that it is entirely unreasonable for a debtor to wait and see whether land prices rise and only then come and redeem the property, because this would discourage lending. Sema.
Willingly. The Sema writes that the Beit Yosef proves from Rashi that whenever the creditor had to trouble the debtor by bringing him before beit din, even if the debtor immediately agreed there to give land in satisfaction of the debt, it is considered a beit din appraisal and is subject to return. The Shach writes that Rashi appears to indicate the opposite. Unless beit din actually appraised the land against the debtor's will, merely standing in judgment before them is not considered having troubled beit din, and the property does not return. See there.
