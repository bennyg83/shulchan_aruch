And some say, etc. This follows Tosafot's explanation and rules like Rabbi Yehuda in the Torat Kohanim, as do Rif and Rosh. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch rule like Rava, for Rambam states expressly that a person does not waive damage to his body. Maggid Mishneh explains that Rabbi Yehuda does not disagree with Rava: when one says 'strike me,' a person may waive, as Rashi explains, and the difference is whether the words were said as a question or with assent. Rambam omits this because it is obvious. Rashba cites this entire explanation in the name of Raavad. It requires study.
One who says to another, 'Throw, etc.' Kiddushin 8b, concerning placing money on a rock. Rashba asks why the woman is not betrothed under the law of a guarantor. Some distinguish between a case where she says, 'and I will be betrothed to you,' and one where she does not. Rosh cites this, with further qualification. Ramban distinguishes between throwing to a dog, where the recipient expends money at her direction, and throwing onto a rock or into the sea, where no one capable of acquisition receives it. In monetary law as well, saying 'throw a maneh into the sea and I will become obligated' is ineffective according to Rashba, since no legally competent recipient acquires it. The Yerushalmi supports this: throwing into the sea does not effect betrothal, whereas giving to a poor person can, because the woman wishes to be betrothed while benefiting the poor person, through guarantor law. Rosh says one should be stringent in matters of betrothal. See Even HaEzer 30.
(Supplement) One who says, etc. In summary, Tosafot and Rosh distinguish according to who initiated the proposal; Rashba distinguishes whether she expressly said, 'and I will be betrothed to you.' In a case of throwing into the sea, however, even an express undertaking is disputed: Rosh is uncertain, Rashba exempts, and Ramban distinguishes between a dog and a rock or sea.
(Supplement) And some say, etc. The Yerushalmi supports Rashba. Ritva, citing Ramban, says the Babylonian Talmud disagrees and bases the poor-person case on guarantor law. See 598.
