Seif 3: Our documents are documents of acquisition. Note: I am uncertain whether this refers only to a document executed with witnesses, or whether a handwritten document also suffices. A deed stating in the present tense, “My field is sold to you,” can effect acquisition even without witnesses. But where the writing merely records that the property was already sold, perhaps only a witnessed document works as a document of acquisition. Since the seller can no longer deny a witnessed evidentiary document, his decision to create such a conclusive writing demonstrates that he now fully intends the transfer. A private handwritten statement is different, because he may have relied on the possibility of later denying the handwriting or otherwise avoiding its effect, and perhaps he did not fully resolve to transfer title. There is also room to suspect that a private acknowledgment of the sale was written merely as an insincere declaration, particularly since the alleged buyer himself knows that no prior sale occurred. The practical law therefore requires study. At minimum, in the sale of chametz where land is included, one should be stringent and transfer the land through money as well, expressly explaining to the non-Jew that the money is intended to acquire the land. Even if chametz may be treated like property sold because of its undesirable condition, so that a deed alone might suffice, there are multiple uncertainties: perhaps such a sale does not fall in that category, and perhaps a handwritten evidentiary document does not itself function as an acquisition deed. Money without a deed does not ordinarily acquire such land, but a leasehold interest in land is acquired by money, and the handwritten document can at least serve as evidence. This appears the proper practical course.
