Our documents are intended to function as acquisition documents. The Gemara in Gittin asks about a get written on a writing tablet belonging to the woman. Since a valid get must be the husband's "book," how can it work? The Gemara answers that the woman is treated as transferring the tablet to the husband, who then gives it back with the get. This teaches that an acquisition document likewise must be written on material belonging to the transferor. The document itself is the transferor's instrument of conveyance. Therefore, even when the purchaser pays the scribe, the paper must first be transferred to the seller so that the seller owns the acquisition document and can then transfer it to the purchaser. The seller must give the document completely. If he says, "The paper remains mine," the written words are like letters suspended in the air and there is no valid acquisition document. In a get, the Sages specially enacted that even when the woman pays the scribe, the paper is treated as transferred to the husband because of the concern for preventing women from being trapped in marriage. That enactment is specific to divorce. It should not automatically apply to an ordinary acquisition document. Accordingly, where our customary sale documents are paid for by the purchaser and the paper is never transferred to the seller before the seller gives it to the purchaser, the document may fail as an acquisition instrument. It can still function as evidence, because an evidentiary document does not require the material itself to belong to the transferor. This point is surprisingly absent from many of the practical discussions of sale documents.
The land is acquired through such documents. Beit Yosef cites Rashba concerning a widow who sought payment of her ketubah and received land from the debtor in satisfaction of the amount. A formal sale document was executed. Rashba ruled that she acquired through the document even though it was written in the ordinary narrative form of a sale deed. Such documents can function as acquisition documents just as though they expressly said, "My field is acquired to you." Rashba adds that the absence of cash does not prevent acquisition. Although Shmuel says that a purchaser ordinarily does not acquire by deed until paying, the reason is that a seller does not fully intend to transfer until he receives payment. Here the cancellation of the ketubah obligation stands in place of the purchase price, and the seller is not waiting for any additional money. It resembles a person selling undesirable land, where the seller's intention to transfer is complete. I find this difficult. The Gemara explains the force of an ordinary sale deed in part through the fact that sellers would not knowingly compromise their own credibility by signing a false sale. Here, however, the ketubah debt itself does not operate as money to acquire land. If no actual purchase money is being paid, why does the ordinary narrative deed acquire, especially since a gift document written in that style does not acquire? Perhaps the force of the local document rests on the law of the kingdom, as discussed in siman 68.
