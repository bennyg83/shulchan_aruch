The buyer is believed. See Shach, seif katan 3, citing Maharit siman 152. Maharit there explains that Rashba disagrees and holds that the buyer is always believed. Once the land has been acquired by the buyer, the seller has only a monetary claim for the purchase price, which is like an oral loan, and the buyer is believed to say, "I paid," just as a person cutting a tree can claim that he already paid for it. Maharit concludes that Rashba agrees with Rav Hai where the deed does not acquire until the price is paid. There the seller can say not merely that money remains due, but that the land was never sold at all. The land therefore remains in the prior owner's chazakah absent clear proof of sale. But where the deed alone completes the purchase, and the seller's repeated demands do not allow cancellation, the buyer is believed to say he paid. According to what I wrote in siman 190 from Shitah Mekubetzet, however, even where the seller did not repeatedly demand payment, if the buyer refuses to pay at all the sale is void unless the seller converted the price into a loan. Therefore the seller should ordinarily be believed when he says that the price was never paid, because according to his claim the sale itself failed and the land remains in the prior owner's chazakah. Only where witnesses establish that the price was made into a loan at the time of sale is the buyer later believed to say he paid it like any other oral debt. Rav Hai's ruling is thus understandable. Nevertheless, Rav Hai's words require explanation in light of several sugyot. Ran in Kiddushin asks why a found conveyancing deed should be returned, since perhaps the price was never paid. Likewise, why may a purchaser collect from encumbered property on the strength of a sale deed if we should advance for subsequent purchasers the claim that the price was not paid? The same problem exists in loan documents, where Ramban distinguishes whether the money was given before or after writing. Why is the lender not believed to say that the document was handed over merely in trust before he received any money, especially since loan documents can be written for a borrower even before the loan is given? We see generally that where borrower and lender, or seller and buyer, appear before witnesses and instruct them to write a document, we do not suspect that the contemplated payment will never be made. I therefore understand Rav Hai as speaking specifically of a conveyancing deed, such as one saying, "My field is sold to you." Such a document is valid even on material capable of forgery because its primary purpose is immediate acquisition, not later proof. Since it was not drafted as evidence, the seller can say that the statement "I received the money" referred to the anticipated future payment, and that he handed over the deed only to effect the land transfer, not as proof of receipt. As I explained in simanim 28 and 46, a conveyancing deed is fundamentally the language of the litigant, like a get, not testimony by the witnesses. The witnesses attest to the validity and delivery of the instrument, not independently to every factual recital. Therefore the deed proves the acquisition itself but not payment. But where the land was acquired by another kinyan and the deed was written only as evidence, the buyer is certainly believed. There is no reason for the seller to hand him an evidentiary deed for full ownership without receiving payment. Such a deed is no different from an ordinary debt instrument whose evidentiary recitals cannot be casually contradicted. Even if it does not expressly say that the price was paid, a deed written merely to prove completed ownership supports the buyer's claim that the price was satisfied. Siman 238, seif 3, likewise shows that in an evidentiary deed the buyer is believed. This resolves Ran's question concerning a found conveyancing deed when the deed also contains another kinyan: if the field was acquired by kinyan sudar, the document serves only as evidence and the seller cannot contradict it by saying the price was unpaid. Likewise, the Rashba's question from a document claimed to be merely for appearances concerns an evidentiary deed. The earlier comparison to the case of a fallen courtyard wall must instead be explained as Maharit says: a pure conveyancing deed leaves the sale vulnerable if the price was not paid, because absent an actual loan conversion the sale may be canceled. Thus, if there are witnesses to kinyan sudar but no evidentiary deed, the seller can still be believed to say he did not receive the price, either for the reason I gave or because the sale remains weak until the price is paid. But if the buyer has an evidentiary deed, he is believed. Likewise, if the buyer has already taken possession of the field with the seller's knowledge, he is believed because a seller normally does not let a buyer descend into possession before payment, as Maharit says. If the buyer was already occupying before the sale, such as as a tenant, or entered with the seller's consent on the express understanding that payment would come later without converting the price into a loan, the seller can still be believed. The same applies if the buyer entered without the seller's knowledge. In all ordinary cases, however, where the evidentiary deed is in the buyer's hand or the seller allowed him to take possession as owner, the buyer's claim of payment is accepted.
