Our deeds. See Nachalat Shivah for the standard form of a deed of sale used among us. Responsa Beit Efrayim, Choshen Mishpat 6, writes that he had long wondered about the wording of our sale deeds, which state, "we, the husband and wife, sold." What force does the wife's sale have? She owns no share in the land itself and ordinarily has only a ketubah lien. A creditor cannot sell the borrower's property before collection, because the law is that the creditor acquires it only from the time of collection onward. The expression that a purchaser "bought from the wife" means only that he purchased a release of her lien, as Rambam explains and Beit Yosef cites in Even HaEzer 90. Although Tosafot in Ketubot uses language of sale regarding the wife, that may refer to nichsei tzon barzel, property specifically associated with her. But in our deeds the same wording is used even where the husband sells his own land in which she never had any proprietary interest, so the language that she "sells" appears difficult. Beit Efrayim resolves this on the basis of Rambam, Hilchot Mechirah chapter 10, who rules that a woman's attempted sale or gift of her husband's property is ineffective unless the husband later affirms it, in which case it becomes effective. Thus the language of sale can properly be applied to the wife. When the husband does not void her act but ratifies it, her sale becomes a valid sale. He further discusses the standard wording by which both husband and wife accept responsibility under the deed. Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer 93:83, writes that the modern custom of taking a kinyan from the wife and then from the husband proves that the usual responsibility clause does not ordinarily bind the wife. If responsibility truly rested upon both, the kinyan could be taken from the husband first. The wife's kinyan ordinarily serves only to prevent her from later contesting the transaction and to remove her own lien, unless her assumption of responsibility is explicitly stated. Taz rules similarly. Accordingly, where our deeds explicitly place responsibility on the wife as on the husband, she may indeed be obligated to indemnify the purchaser. The Taz, however, expresses doubt, because such language may have been inserted without her particular knowledge merely as standard enhancement of the deed. Beit Efrayim analyzes the issue at length and concludes that it remains a serious question. One should investigate established custom, for we have not commonly heard of collecting indemnification from the wife in such cases. See there carefully.
