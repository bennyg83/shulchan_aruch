If the money was lost, when he said, Acquire from now. This is difficult because the Gemara indicates that even without the words "from now," if the original money was later consumed the acquisition can still take effect. The reason is that the original money was given as acquisition money and does not become an ordinary loan merely because it is no longer physically present. Sema explains that the ruling concerns money lost in a way that leaves no right of reimbursement. Beit Yosef's reason was that if the sale failed the recipient would have to return the money, so the continuing reimbursement obligation supplies the monetary basis. Sema therefore infers that where no reimbursement is possible the sale fails. Taz objects. If the money was given for kiddushin, the woman consumed her own money at the time she used it. Even if she later cannot pay, the original kiddushin money should not become invalid retroactively. The Shach says Ran, Maggid Mishneh, and others support Sema, but their language only says that if the recipient retracts he will have to return the money. Even if he is presently insolvent, the debt remains and can be paid later. Insolvency does not erase the monetary obligation. In a land sale the issue is even weaker because the purchased land itself remains available. One could establish Sema's case as money lost through an unavoidable accident in circumstances where the recipient would ordinarily be exempt from repayment. Yet even there, if the recipient now retracts from the kiddushin or sale, I would impose liability. Rosh says that in a gift on condition of return, failure to fulfill the return condition can cause the recipient to be treated retroactively as one who held the property without authority and liable even for unavoidable accident. So too, where money was given for kiddushin or purchase, a recipient who voluntarily rejects the transaction can be treated as retroactively responsible for the money. Even under the view that a gift recipient is exempt when the gifted object was lost unavoidably before the return condition could be fulfilled, that is because the object entered his hands as a true gift and the condition became impossible through no act of his. Here the recipient can still uphold the sale or kiddushin. If he chooses instead to retract, he removes the basis on which he lawfully used the money and should become liable retroactively. Accordingly, so long as there is a legally enforceable obligation to repay the purchase money upon retraction, the missing money can still support the acquisition, even if the recipient has no present funds. This applies to sale and kiddushin alike.
