Witnesses who testified, etc. The Rambam's formulation shows that when witnesses are rendered zomemim, their testimony is void and the statutory consequences of hazamah apply according to the precise manner in which the later witnesses establish that 'you were with us.' This is not merely ordinary contradiction. The distinction has practical consequences for payment, disqualification, seizure, and whether money already collected can be recovered. Where the first witnesses were only contradicted, each pair may conceivably be truthful; where they were formally rendered zomemim, the Torah treats the first pair as false for the law of hazamah. The authorities cited by the Tur and Beit Yosef must therefore be read carefully so that the rules of contradiction are not confused with the special decree governing zomemim. The court must also distinguish between testimony delivered orally and testimony preserved in a document, between a document that states it was written at its proper time and one that may have been postdated, and between monetary liability and the witnesses' personal disqualification. These distinctions explain the various rulings of the Rif, Rambam, Rosh, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Ran, and later authorities. 14512204060100270300380520
There is a practical difference, for a zomem witness is disqualified from testifying, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that another difference is that if one party seized property, it is not removed from him. In my view there is also a difference where the litigant already paid on the strength of their testimony and recovery from the recipient is impossible, for example because he is violent, left overseas, or dissipated the money. The witnesses must then pay, as ruled above in siman 1, seif 4. Had they merely been contradicted by another pair, without the second pair saying 'you were with us,' they would not pay, because perhaps they spoke truth and the others falsely. The Ran cites the Baal HaMaor as holding that formal hazamah applies only to oral testimony and not to a document, even if the document says it was written on its stated date, because hazamah is a novelty and is limited to its explicit form. The Rif, however, holds that a dated document may also be subjected to hazamah. The Bach suggests that even the Baal HaMaor limits only the monetary penalty, while agreeing that the witnesses are disqualified. I agree with the conclusion, though not because this is merely contradiction outside their presence. Rather, the Torah's innovation is that zomemim pay despite not having completed the act; punishment cannot be extended beyond what the Torah imposed. The Baal HaMaor's precise words are that there is no hazamah 'to pay money' except through oral testimony. He nevertheless agrees that a document expressly stating that it was written at its time can be invalidated through hazamah. This is clear from Sanhedrin, where a dated document may be challenged by witnesses saying that its signatories were elsewhere, unless we can say it was postdated. The entire discussion assumes that a document capable of precise dating can be rendered invalid through hazamah. The Rif likewise speaks of invalidating the document, not necessarily imposing the monetary penalty, so there may be no dispute. The Ramban did not challenge the Baal HaMaor on this point, which also indicates agreement. Rivash no. 266 writes that hazamah outside the witnesses' presence at least disqualifies them, relying on the Rambam and Rashi. In my view those proofs are not decisive, because their cases differ. Even according to the Rivash, however, one cannot disqualify a person outside his presence by an oath concerning his own wrongdoing, even for robbery, because a personal disability may not be imposed in his absence; ordinary evidentiary consequences are different. 130100260
