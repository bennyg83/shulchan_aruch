They are believed regarding themselves. The early authorities ask from Makkot, where Rav Yehudah in Rav's name says a plotting witness pays according to his share. The Gemara initially explains this as where only one witness is refuted, but objects that witnesses do not become plotting witnesses until all are refuted. Rava then explains that the witness says, "I testified falsely," and the Gemara asks, "Is it within his power?" since once he testified he cannot retract. If his statement is nevertheless believed against himself, why should he not have to pay for the damage his false testimony caused? Rabbeinu Chananel and Rambam indeed hold that he does not pay ordinary damages on his own admission. Ramban explicitly states that they disagree with the authorities who impose liability for direct causation. Knesset HaGedolah suggests that they view liability for direct causation as penal and therefore not collectible on self admission. I have shown elsewhere that direct causation can in principle be paid on one's own admission, but one can resolve the Makkot passage differently. False witnesses are also liable to lashes under "You shall not bear false witness." Where both money and lashes would apply, ordinarily lashes displace payment. Plotting witnesses are an exception because Scripture expressly multiplied monetary payment through "as he plotted." That exception applies to the special hazamah payment, not to ordinary tort damages caused by the testimony. Thus according to Reish Lakish, who holds that even unwarned lash liable conduct displaces money, a witness admitting false testimony would remain exempt from ordinary damages because the underlying conduct carries lashes. Rava follows Reish Lakish there. The Gemara's objection is therefore sound. In ordinary circumstances where no lashes displace payment, one who admits causing actionable direct loss can indeed be liable. A related difficulty arises from an interest bearing document. We rule that the lender recovers principal even though the borrower too violated Torah prohibitions connected with interest. If lash liability always displaced the debt, how could principal be collected? This depends upon whether the borrower's prohibition begins before payment of the interest and upon the rules of inadvertent lash liability. The matter requires further analysis.
There is a practical difference. Beit Yosef writes that the practical difference between the authorities is seizure: if the claimant seized money from witnesses who caused his loss, it is not removed from him. It appears that where two witnesses testified that a person owed a maneh, he paid, and afterward two other witnesses contradicted them and testified that no loan occurred, if the debtor seizes from the first witnesses for the loss they caused, his seizure is effective because the matter is now a two against two uncertainty and direct causation liability can support possession in doubt. Rambam rules concerning witnesses who testified that a master knocked out his slave's tooth and afterward blinded his eye, and were then refuted, that they pay the slave's value and the value of his eye. Raavad objects that according to Rambam's own ruling the master pays the value of the eye only if the slave first seizes. Why then must the plotting witnesses pay something that the master himself would not necessarily have paid? Kesef Mishneh answers that because the slave can easily seize, the witnesses are treated as having plotted that loss. Migdal Oz similarly invokes the language of "as they plotted." Yet Bava Kamma's hazamah sugya is difficult according to this. Where one group testifies tooth then eye and another group eye then tooth, the consequences depend upon which group is later refuted. If mere potential seizure sufficed to create hazamah payment, some of the distinctions there would collapse, because a two against two situation can support seizure. The better rule is that plotting witnesses ordinarily pay only what would have been judicially concluded on their testimony, not every amount that might later be secured through uncertain seizure. Therefore Rambam's ruling remains difficult and requires further study. Another application: two witnesses who testify that a person's wine became prohibited through libation and are later refuted should pay the wine's value under ordinary hazamah principles, even though their testimony concerned the status of the wine rather than a personal debt. This resembles witnesses who testify that a person's ox committed a capital offense and must be killed, who pay the value of the ox when refuted. Rambam's formulation concerning an ox that killed a person, however, requires careful reading because he writes that the witnesses receive lashes and do not pay kofer. That may concern a case where the ox itself is not subject to stoning. Finally, if one witness caused a monetary payment only because the defendant was suspected and therefore unable to swear, or because the defendant answered, "I do not know," it is doubtful whether hazamah payment applies. Tosafot implies that whenever one witness's testimony actually causes monetary liability, the principle of "as he plotted" may apply. Yet the verse is written concerning two witnesses, and in cases where the payment also depends upon the defendant's inability to swear, causation may not be sufficiently direct. Ordinary direct causation liability likewise may fail where the damage was not certain at the time of testimony.
