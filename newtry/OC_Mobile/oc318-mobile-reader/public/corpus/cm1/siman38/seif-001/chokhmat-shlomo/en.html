Seif 1. Witnesses who testified in beit din and afterward said, “We testified falsely.” I was asked by Rabbi Chaim Moshe Schorr of Monasterzyska about the following case. A person produced a deed of purchase bearing the seller's signature for a place in the local study hall, together with the signatures of two valid witnesses. Another person produced a lien document on that same place, bearing two witness signatures, and claimed that the seller had no right to sell it. It later became known that this lien document was false, because one witness admitted that he had signed it only eight days earlier, had not seen the loan, while the document was dated two years earlier. The second witness was a business partner of the lienholder. What is the law? In my view there is no room for doubt. The witnesses are not believed to invalidate their signatures even where their handwriting is not otherwise established, because by their present statement they render themselves wicked. Even a migo does not help in such a case, and certainly if their handwriting is established from elsewhere they are not believed. The fact that the writing appeared fresh is not conclusive. Even if it were conclusive, it remains possible that the obligation was acquired two years earlier and that the witnesses were permitted to write the original date. The lien therefore remains effective, and the witnesses do not disqualify themselves by their own confession, since a person cannot establish himself as wicked. As to their admission for purposes of monetary liability that they signed falsely, this is not identical to the case in Makkot and Choshen Mishpat 29 and 38 of witnesses who say, “We gave false testimony,” because here they did not necessarily cause the claimant to lose the principal debt: they can say that they nevertheless know the debtor owes him the money. They did, however, cause a loss by making an oral loan appear to be a documented loan and thereby diminishing the debtor's property value. Their liability therefore depends on a beit din appraisal of the value of converting an oral debt into a debt evidenced by a document, analogous to the valuation of the benefit of a ketubah discussed in the first chapter of Makkot. This appears correct. Further. In the same seif: Rambam holds that when contradiction and hazamah occur together, the matter is not treated as hazamah and the original testimony is testimony incapable of being subjected to hazamah. This raises a difficulty in capital cases. Warning is required, and without warning there is no execution. If the witnesses testify that they warned the offender, their testimony about the warning would seemingly be incapable of hazamah, because if later witnesses say, “You were with us,” it follows automatically that the first witnesses did not warn him, so contradiction and hazamah occur together. The difficulty is especially strong where the witnesses say that no one else was present and that they themselves administered the warning. Perhaps only testimony concerning the criminal act must itself be capable of hazamah, while testimony concerning the warning need not independently satisfy that requirement. Thus even if “You were with us” contradicts them concerning the warning, the criminal act itself could still be true, while hazamah applies to the witnesses concerning the act. The matter nevertheless requires further study. Lechem Mishneh asks from where Rambam derived that contradiction together with hazamah is not hazamah. I find an even stronger difficulty from Makkot 5. The Mishna says that witnesses become zomemim only when the second witnesses target the witnesses themselves: if they say, “We testify that so-and-so killed a person,” and the second set says, “How can that be, for the alleged victim, or the alleged killer, was with us elsewhere,” this is not hazamah of the witnesses. Why did the Mishna not teach the greater novelty that even if the second set says, “You and the killer were with us,” the first witnesses are not executed? The wording implies that when the witnesses themselves are placed elsewhere, they are zomemim even if the second testimony also concerns the killer. The Gemara later likewise says that if one pair says the murder occurred Sunday and another pair says the same person killed the victim Monday, the first pair can still be zomemim. Since the second pair thereby also contradicts the time of the killing, contradiction and hazamah are combined, yet the witnesses are punished. This requires study. In my novellae to Bava Kamma, chapter Merubah, I brought a proof for Rambam; see there. Urim VeTumim cites the commentators' difficulty with Rambam's ruling that with lashes we do not say “as he conspired, but not as he carried out,” and that even if the accused already received lashes the false witnesses may still receive lashes. In my view the distinction is straightforward. With execution Scripture expressly says that the witnesses' hands shall be first against him to put him to death. Thus, where the false witnesses themselves participated in carrying out the punishment, the verse reveals that the law of hazamah applies only to what they conspired to do and not to what they themselves actually carried out. With lashes, however, the witnesses do not administer the lashes; the agent of beit din does. Likewise, in monetary cases the witnesses do not personally collect the money; beit din does. The witnesses themselves performed only the act of testimony, so the law of hazamah continues to apply to them. This is clear and correct.
