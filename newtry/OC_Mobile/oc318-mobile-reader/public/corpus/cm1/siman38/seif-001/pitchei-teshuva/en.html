To pay him. See Baer Heitev, through the words, “and afterward it became known that he had erred,” etc. See the Shach, who discusses this law at length. See Shevut Yaakov, part I, end of responsum 147, who writes as follows: The Shach discusses this at length, but the plain language of Rabbi Eliezer, who writes simply that they do not pay on the basis of their own admission, and likewise the plain language of the Rambam in chapter 18 of Hilchot Edut, imply that in every case they do not pay on the basis of their own admission, even under the law of garmi. It therefore appears to me that the principal distinction is this: when Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rambam exempted them from payment, they meant where the witnesses admitted that they had testified falsely before money had actually been collected on the strength of their testimony. In that case they cannot be held liable even under the law of garmi, because they performed no act at all, but only spoke. They spoke, and they now retract what they spoke. Although we do not believe their retraction because once a witness has testified he may not retract, nevertheless we do not impose liability upon the witnesses where they themselves returned and admitted that their testimony had been false. Since their retraction is not believed as testimony, they likewise cannot be charged with garmi merely on account of that statement. By contrast, where money had already been collected on the strength of their testimony, or where they had signed a document and their falsehood had thereby produced an actual legal act, then even if they later say, “We testified falsely,” they may be held liable on their own admission under the law of garmi. This distinction is reasonable and reconciles the words of the early authorities without any dispute. See there. See also Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 6, on this matter, and see Nachalat Tzvi for what he writes on it.
