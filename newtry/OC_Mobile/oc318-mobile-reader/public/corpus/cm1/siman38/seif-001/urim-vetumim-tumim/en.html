He was brief where he should have elaborated, etc. We will therefore explain somewhat the words of Tur and the details of partial hazamah, and examine what is needed to understand the matter. Rambam writes in Hilchot Edut 18:2: witnesses came and said, "We saw this person kill a person," or, "He borrowed one hundred from so-and-so," on a certain day and in a certain place. After they were examined, two others came and said, "On that day and in that place we were with you and with these litigants all day, and the event did not occur. This one did not kill and this one did not borrow." This is hakchashah. Likewise, if they say, "How can you testify so? The killer or the victim, or the borrower or lender, was with us on that day in another country," this is contradictory testimony. But if they say, "We do not know whether this one killed that one on this day in Jerusalem as you say, and we do not testify whether the act occurred or not. Rather, we testify that you yourselves were with us on that day in Babylonia," these are zomemim and are executed or pay, because the witnesses who refute them do not address the substance of the event at all, whether true or false. Rambam's view is explicit: if the later witnesses say both, "You were with us," and, "the litigants themselves were also with us, so the event could not have occurred at that time and place," this is not hazamah but ordinary contradiction. Tosafot Yom Tov and Lechem Mishneh write that Tur disagrees and holds that once the later witnesses address the bodies of the first witnesses and say, "You were with us," it remains hazamah even if they also contradict the underlying event by saying the litigants were with them. Lechem Mishneh says he found no Talmudic support for Rambam. It appears, however, that Rambam has a proof from the Gemara's questions, "Let the murdered person save him," and, "Let the sodomized person save him," followed by the answers that the sodomy or murder occurred from behind. The commentators ask what disqualification attaches to the person sodomized or murdered such that his presence among the witnesses would invalidate the entire testimony. Even if, with difficulty, one explains the sodomized person as having participated willingly, what can be said regarding the murdered person? I already cited above, at the end of siman 33, from Sifrei Zuta that the victim himself can testify. There is another difficulty. According to R. Yosi, and R. Yosi's view is accepted by Rav Yehudah in the name of Shmuel, no murderer or sodomite should ever be executed unless he acted from behind. If the act occurred in front of the victim, the victim's presence would invalidate the testimony. That is extremely strained. I heard in the name of my relative, the gaon R. Chaim Yonah Teomim, a difficulty against Rambam. If a man says, "So-and-so sodomized me willingly," and another witness joins him to execute the offender, this appears to be testimony incapable of hazamah. If later witnesses say, "You were with us," they are necessarily also saying that the underlying act as described could not have occurred, because the litigant himself, who is one of the original witnesses, was with them. That would make the later testimony hakchashah rather than hazamah under Rambam, leaving no possibility of hazamah. The difficulty is worthy. One can answer that since we apply palginan dibura, we divide his statement. We do not accept "he sodomized me willingly" as self-incrimination. We accept only that the offender sodomized someone else. Thus the witness's legal testimony in beit din is effectively, "So-and-so sodomized a person." If later witnesses say, "You were with us," they have not contradicted the substantive act, because the offender may indeed have sodomized another person elsewhere or at another time. They address the witness himself and thereby create hazamah. One might ask about "he sodomized me by force," where the witness is valid without self-incrimination. There, perhaps we do not divide his words at all. Yet I found that Radbaz, responsum 231, writes even regarding "so-and-so sodomized me by force" that we divide the statement and treat it as if he sodomized another person. Radbaz therefore says that where one says, "He murdered me," which cannot be divided because the murdered person is actually before us dead or wounded, the testimony is different. Radbaz's reading is difficult because R. Yosef says "he sodomized me by force" is believed, while R. Yosef does not generally accept palginan dibura in the same way as Rava concerning self-incrimination. We must say that even R. Yosef accepts division of the factual statement, but he differs from Rava in another point: R. Yosef holds that a person's admission can make himself wicked, just as a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses in monetary matters. Thus, although the legal testimony is divided and the act can be treated as directed at another person, his own confession that he participated willingly still disqualifies him. Rava holds that a person cannot make himself wicked by his own statement. According to this, Radbaz is coherent. Even so, the ordinary implication of the commentators is that "by force" does not require palginan dibura. There is another answer to the original difficulty. Hazamah does not require the later witnesses to say, "You were with us in a different city." If they say, "On that day we were with you in that same place all day and never left you, and we know you could not have seen the event you described," that is also hazamah regarding the witnesses themselves. The distinction is not geographical location as such, but whether the later testimony attacks the witnesses' presence and capacity to observe rather than the substance of the act. This is indicated by the Gemara concerning one witness saying the event occurred on the east side of a palace and the other saying the west side. If the two places are visible from one another, their testimony can stand. The same logic shows that hazamah can arise even within one location. Thus, if one says, "So-and-so sodomized me on such-and-such day in this place," later witnesses can say, "We were with you face to face throughout that entire day, and you could not have seen him sodomize anyone from the front as you claim." It remains possible, theoretically, that the offender sodomized someone from behind beyond the witness's view, but the witness could not have seen the act in the way he testified. The later testimony attacks his capacity to witness the event and therefore can be hazamah. This explains the Gemara's answer that the act occurred from behind. At the stage of the question, before that possibility was introduced, the Gemara treated the victim or sodomized person as part of the group in a way that could make the testimony incapable of hazamah. If one of the witnesses cannot be subjected to hazamah, R. Akiva's rule concerning one witness who says "I do not know" teaches that the entire group can be invalidated as though one of them were disqualified. Rambam elsewhere limits that rule according to the Tannaitic framework, but according to R. Yosi it certainly operates. Thus the Gemara asks, "Let the victim save him," meaning: the victim's own participation creates testimony incapable of hazamah and thereby invalidates the entire group. The answer is that where the act was done from behind, the victim's testimony can still be subjected to hazamah in the manner explained. This supports Rambam's distinction. Where the later witnesses necessarily contradict the body of the underlying event itself, the case is hakchashah. Where they can address only the first witnesses' capacity to have seen or been present, while leaving open whether the event occurred, it is hazamah. This also answers the challenge of Mahari ibn Lev against Rambam from Bava Kamma 73, where the Gemara speaks of reversing the order of injuries and also hazamah, apparently combining contradiction and hazamah. One can establish that case differently. The first witnesses need not have personally seen the master knock out the slave's tooth and then blind his eye. They may testify that in beit din, on a certain day, other witnesses testified to that sequence and judgment was rendered accordingly. The later witnesses can then say: "We were present at the actual incident, and the sequence was the opposite," thereby contradicting the underlying report, and also: "On the day you claim you heard the beit din testimony, you were with us elsewhere," thereby hazeming the first witnesses. The contradiction and hazamah concern different objects. The contradiction concerns the original injury sequence. The hazamah concerns the testimony of those who falsely claimed to have heard the judicial proceedings. Even if one does not adopt this precise establishment, another distinction is possible. Where the later witnesses add a contradiction unnecessary to their hazamah, such as saying both, "You were with us," and, "the alleged victim is alive," the unnecessary contradiction can reveal that they intend primarily to dispute the event itself. Then one may doubt whether the special law of hazamah applies. But where their statement concerning the reverse sequence is necessary to produce a legal consequence, such as freeing the slave or determining compensation, the contradiction is not a mere attack on the first testimony. It is independent evidence needed for judgment. In that circumstance, the hazamah component can retain its status. This may explain why Abaye speaks of both reversal and hazamah rather than accepting Rava's simpler structure. In my novellae I have elaborated. Tur writes that the testimony of the first witnesses is void and, if they were hazemed in a monetary matter, they must pay. It is obvious that if the litigant himself says the original witnesses spoke truthfully, then even if later witnesses hazem them, the original witnesses are exempt from the penalty of zomemim regarding that litigant, because he admits their testimony was true. This is explicit in Bava Kamma 75b. Where the thief says before the original witnesses ever testify, "I stole and slaughtered or sold in front of these witnesses," their later testimony cannot become ordinary hazamah testimony against him, because he has already admitted they speak truthfully. Chazal dispute whether such testimony is considered incapable of hazamah or whether his support of their statement allows it to function. Yam Shel Shlomo writes that this is specifically where the thief identifies the witnesses beforehand, saying, "I stole and slaughtered before these particular witnesses." But if the witnesses testify first and only afterward he admits their words, the testimony was fully capable of hazamah when given and remains valid. Otherwise every defendant in a fine case could defeat the witnesses after they testified simply by admitting their story. This is correct regarding the validity of the original testimony. At the moment they testified, it was testimony capable of hazamah, and the defendant's later admission cannot retroactively invalidate it. But the separate question remains whether the penalty of hazamah can later be imposed upon them when the litigant himself now says, "You spoke truthfully." In my view, even if the original testimony remains valid, the later admission can block the hazamah penalty. Hazamah is a special fine. If the very person for whose sake the penalty would operate says the first witnesses spoke truthfully, the later refutation should not automatically create a fine against them. This is especially clear in monetary fines, where the payment belongs to the litigant. If he says the first witnesses were true, he has admitted that they did not wrongfully seek to impose liability upon him. Perhaps in capital punishment or lashes we do not rely on his admission in the same way, since punishment belongs to beit din and "by the mouth of two witnesses shall a matter stand." Yet regarding the special fine of zomemim, the novelty of the law should not be extended where the litigant supports them. This distinction helps explain Tosafot in Makkot 5b. Tosafot labor to distinguish why witnesses who say a person slaughtered on Sunday, when in fact the slaughter was Friday, can be hazemed in one setting but not another, and why capital and monetary cases differ. They do not simply answer that the defendant could always preempt hazamah by confessing the act in front of witnesses. The reason is that mere confession without identifying the witnesses does not make the original testimony incapable of hazamah. The later witnesses can still say, "You were not there." Only where the defendant's admission specifically confirms those very witnesses does the hazamah penalty become problematic. This also explains the opening Tosafot in Makkot, who ask how witnesses can obligate exile when the defendant could claim he acted intentionally. Tosafot answer that his silence is treated as admission to their description. Why did they not say the case is one where he expressly admits it was accidental? Because, according to Rashi's understanding that exile has features of a penalty and admission can exempt from penalty, an express admission could itself alter the procedural framework. Silence avoids that complication. There is, however, a broader distinction between monetary fines and other punishments. Rambam rules that if witnesses falsely testify that a person deserves lashes, and the person was actually lashed before the witnesses were hazemed, the zomemim still receive lashes. This differs from the rule that if the falsely convicted person was already executed, the zomemim are not executed. Kesef Mishneh asks why. Tosafot give explanations for "he was executed, they are not executed," such as the Divine providence that would not allow an innocent person to be killed through false testimony. The same reasoning might appear to apply to lashes. A possible explanation is connected to the defendant's ability to support the witnesses. Before execution, as long as the accused remains alive, he can say, "The first witnesses spoke truthfully." If he does so, the foundation for punishing the alleged zomemim is weakened. After he has already been executed, we can no longer know whether, had he seen the later witnesses facing death because of him, he would have confirmed the first testimony to save them. Since "the congregation shall judge and the congregation shall save," we do not execute the later witnesses amid that uncertainty. By contrast, after lashes the punished litigant is still alive. If he continues to deny the first witnesses and supports the later hazamah witnesses, there is no comparable uncertainty. The zomemim can therefore be lashed even though the first punishment was carried out. This is a rational distinction, though one should not rest Rambam solely upon it. There is a stronger Talmudic proof. Sanhedrin 41a discusses R. Yosi b. Yehudah, who says a learned person does not require formal warning. The Gemara asks how a learned married woman can ever be executed for adultery, since the witnesses can say, "We intended only to prohibit her to her husband," making the testimony incapable of hazamah. The Gemara establishes repeated adultery or cases involving relatives of the witnesses. Tosafot ask that the witnesses could still say, "We intended only to disqualify the man as a witness." They answer that if that were their goal, they should have testified in a formulation directly suited to that purpose. This discussion suggests that where the witnesses' stated purpose can be separated from the punishment ultimately imposed, hazamah depends on what legal consequence their testimony was aimed at producing. Capital punishment is unique because before execution the testimony itself has not yet produced the ultimate bodily result. The penalty of zomemim is imposed because of their malicious intent, "as he intended to do," not because they physically caused the death. If they can credibly say they intended only a lesser legal consequence, the special punishment can fail. Lashes are different where the person was actually lashed. Once their testimony caused the physical punishment, the witnesses themselves have brought about the harmful act. Their liability need not depend solely on their stated intent. This helps explain Rambam. It also yields an important rule: with lashes, the zomemim may still be punished after the false sentence was carried out. The Mishnah supports this. It states, "We testify concerning so-and-so that he is liable to forty lashes." R. Meir gives eighty lashes and the Sages forty, discussing only the number of lashes. They do not distinguish whether the defendant had already been lashed. If the Sages held that once he was lashed the zomemim are entirely exempt under "as he intended and not as he did," that would have been a far greater dispute than whether forty or eighty are given. The silence indicates that even after the lashes were carried out, the zomemim are still subject to lashes. Another proof appears at the beginning of Makkot regarding witnesses who say a kohen is the son of a divorced woman or a chalutzah. The Mishnah says they receive lashes rather than suffering the status they sought to impose. Once beit din has already ruled that the kohen is disqualified, the legal declaration itself has taken effect. If "not as he did" applied simply because a judgment was rendered, the witnesses should never receive lashes once the decision was entered. Yet the Mishnah treats them as punishable. This supports Rambam that the exclusion "not as he did" is not a broad rule nullifying all hazamah penalties once some consequence occurred. It is specifically tied to the death penalty and related contexts. The monetary case differs again. Most authorities, including Rambam, Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Kamma, Rashba, and authorities cited in Ramban's name, hold that even if the defendant already paid money under the false testimony, the zomemim still pay when later hazemed. Ritva disagrees. Ritva asks concerning witnesses who testified that someone possessed land for three years and were later hazemed. If judgment was already rendered, the possessor is already established in the field. Why is that not "as he did"? Ritva answers that the case must be one where the possessor has not yet physically entered the land and still requires an act of beit din to place him there. Mishneh LaMelech challenges Ritva from the law of a slave whose master knocked out a tooth. Once judgment declares him free, the slave already holds himself. Why do zomemim still pay? The answer is that the slave still requires a document of emancipation. Even though he is treated as in possession for some purposes, the legal process is not fully complete. This is supported by Tosafot in Ketubot, who say the slave's claim is not treated as "here it is" because he still lacks the emancipation document. Thus there remains an act to complete. Still, Ritva's position faces a severe general difficulty. Torah originally required interrogation and examination even in civil law. Testimony incapable of hazamah should therefore have been invalid. How could witnesses to repayment or a receipt ever help a borrower? Before final judgment, zomemim are not yet liable to pay. After final judgment, the borrower is already exempt and, according to Ritva, "as he did" would prevent the penalty. There would seemingly be no point at which the testimony is capable of hazamah. One could answer by following Shach's defense of Baal HaMaor that even where the full hazamah penalty cannot operate, the witnesses can still be personally disqualified as liars. Perhaps that suffices to call the testimony capable of hazamah in civil law. Rivash writes that for personal disqualification, witnesses can become disqualified through the proof of their falsehood even before final judgment. But I am not satisfied. The expression "testimony that you can subject to hazamah" in civil law should refer to the Torah institution of hazamah, not merely ordinary proof that the witnesses are liars. The Gemara in Bava Kamma concerning a thief who says, "I stole and slaughtered before these witnesses," is particularly difficult. Even if the later witnesses could disqualify the first as liars, the Gemara still calls the testimony incapable of hazamah where the defendant himself supports them. This shows that mere personal disqualification is not enough. Accordingly, Ritva's broader rule remains difficult. Perhaps he would distinguish between a completed transfer of money, which is a new physical act, and a judicial declaration that leaves property where it already stood. If a borrower remains in possession and beit din merely declares him exempt, no new act occurred. A later hazamah can reverse that declaration and obligate him again, so the process remains reversible. But that would answer Ritva's own land case and remove his difficulty. Thus the issue requires study. The more accepted view is therefore preferable: in monetary matters, even where payment has occurred, the zomemim can still be liable because money is reversible and can be restored. "As he did" does not exempt them in the same manner as an irreversible execution. This is the approach of Rambam and the majority of Rishonim. A related issue concerns witnesses who testify to disqualify a person as a witness. According to Rambam's logic, where the Torah can impose reciprocal disqualification, the zomemim should receive that consequence rather than lashes under the separate verse "and they justified the righteous." The special lashes derived from "and they justified the righteous" apply only where the ordinary reciprocal law cannot be fulfilled, such as witnesses who sought to make a kohen a son of a divorced woman or sought to impose exile. Where reciprocal witness disqualification is possible, we apply "as he intended" directly. This helps with Tosafot's question that witnesses in many capital cases could say, "We intended only to disqualify him from testimony." If their testimony actually takes the form of one integrated capital accusation, the court does not automatically assign them the lesser purpose unless the structure of the case supports it. Rambam's ruling concerning kidnapping also bears on this. He writes that if two witnesses testified that a person kidnapped a Jew and another two testified that he sold him, then if either set is hazemed, that set is executed, because the kidnapping is the beginning of the capital liability. This appears to say that the kidnapping witnesses are executed even if the sale witnesses were not hazemed. One might ask that we rule zomemim are not executed until all witnesses necessary for the capital testimony are hazemed. Here the sale is essential to the death penalty. Rambam evidently treats the kidnapping testimony as an independent unit capable of half the operative event. Why does he not say similarly in the case of three sets of witnesses establishing an ox as forewarned, where each pair testified to one goring? If the first pair is hazemed, they are not necessarily punished as though they completed the entire future liability. One possible distinction is that in the ox case each set's testimony has an independent monetary function: it obligates half-damages for that goring. The witnesses can say they came for that immediate legal consequence, not to create future full-damage liability. In kidnapping, the kidnapping testimony has no legal purpose except the capital sequence of kidnapping followed by sale. It is therefore treated as the beginning of the death-producing testimony. This distinction is conceptually attractive. Yet Tosafot in Bava Kamma 24b appear not to accept it. They ask that the first two sets in the forewarned-ox case be established as testimonies to gorings of ownerless or non-Jewish animals, where no half-damages are payable. Then the witnesses could not say they came for an independent monetary purpose. If Rambam's distinction were accepted, in that case the first set should itself become fully punishable when hazemed, because its only purpose would be to establish future forewarning. The baraita does not say so. Yam Shel Shlomo later notes this difficulty. The Gemara in Sanhedrin also speaks broadly of "witnesses of kidnapping and witnesses of sale who were hazemed," with Chizkiyah and R. Yochanan disputing. The wording suggests the dispute is about the kidnapping witnesses, while sale witnesses are treated differently. It does not clearly support Rambam's broader formulation that any hazemed set is executed. Thus Rambam's reasoning here requires further study. One explanation of why Rambam rules like R. Yochanan against Chizkiyah is that Chizkiyah's position depends upon R. Akiva's rule of "a whole matter and not half a matter." If kidnapping testimony alone were legally "half a matter," the witnesses could not create the capital sequence at all. Under the Sages, however, each stage can count as valid testimony toward one completed crime. Rambam follows the Sages and therefore follows R. Yochanan in the hazamah consequence. This also resolves a difficulty in Maggid Mishneh's explanation that the prohibition of kidnapping is a warning for a capital offense and therefore does not receive lashes. Chizkiyah could still give lashes if, according to him, the kidnapping testimony never enters the completed capital category because it is only half a matter. The normal rule of "a prohibition given as warning for a capital offense" may not block lashes in the same manner where the capital structure never legally forms. Rambam, by ruling the testimony is a valid independent component under the Sages, treats it as part of the capital prohibition and therefore no lashes apply separately. This is also supported by the Mishnah in Makkot, which lists novel categories of lashes but does not list kidnapping witnesses as receiving lashes. That fits R. Yochanan rather than Chizkiyah under the accepted halachah. Nevertheless, the exact scope of Rambam's hazamah liability in the kidnapping case remains somewhat difficult. Tur also writes that witnesses are not made zomemim for punishment until all witnesses in the one testimonial unit are hazemed, even though the substantive testimony can be established by two. This applies where all testified within the period of a short utterance. If there was a break longer than that between the first pair and later witnesses, the testimonial units are separate. The first pair can be punished when hazemed without waiting for the later pair. Rambam writes similarly that if the later witnesses came after a break, they are not punished even though the entire testimony can be void. Raavad objects. If the break separates the witnesses into distinct testimonial groups, why should the later testimony also be void? And what could Rambam mean by saying the later witnesses are "not punished" when they were not hazemed? Shach in siman 36 presses these objections strongly and says that had Rambam seen Raavad's criticism, he would have agreed. I do not know what Shach would say about Tur, who saw Raavad and nevertheless rules plainly like Rambam. Lechem Mishneh answers one point: Rambam means the later witnesses were also hazemed, yet remain exempt because they can say, "We thought the defendant was already a condemned person based on the first pair, and therefore our testimony did not create a new punishable danger." This is not merely conjecture. Nimukei Yosef, explaining the Mishnah's phrase "the third witness came only to make his law like theirs," writes that the novelty is that even though two witnesses already sufficed, if the third joined them within the period of a short utterance and was hazemed, he too is punished. The implication is that if he joined only later, after the period ended, even if later refuted he can be exempt. Thus Lechem Mishneh's reading has an explicit Rishon behind it. If the later set cannot be punished even when hazemed, then after the first set is hazemed we cannot preserve the conviction based solely upon the later set in a category requiring testimony capable of hazamah. The later set's testimony is not capable of producing the reciprocal penalty because they can say the defendant was already condemned. This explains Tur's statement that the entire testimony is void. Admittedly, this reasoning applies directly to capital cases, fines, kiddushin, or other matters where full interrogation and hazamah capacity is required. In ordinary civil cases, where interrogation is relaxed and testimony incapable of full hazamah may sometimes be accepted, the result can differ. Still, it is enough to show Shach's complete rejection is too broad.
However, it is almost impossible to fit Rambam's own reason into this explanation. Rambam says the entire testimony is void because "testimony partially void is entirely void." That is not the same as saying the later testimony is independently incapable of hazamah. Bach and Perishah explain the later set as analogous to finding one witness related or disqualified. Shach correctly objects: the later witnesses were not related or disqualified at the time of seeing, and because they were hazemed, we know they were not even present with the first group at the event. The ordinary rule of a related or disqualified participant therefore seems inapplicable. R. Yaakov Berav likewise recognized the difficulty. There is a way to understand Rambam more precisely. First note that the phrase "the later witnesses are not punished" is not pointless even on its face. Mahari Tzayach Gaon, cited by Nimukei Yosef on the Mishnah, interpreted the verse concerning the third witness very strongly: the third participant can receive the same punishment as the first two even if he himself was not formally hazemed, because Scripture equates the one who joins the transgression with the perpetrators. Rambam can therefore be excluding that ancient view. He teaches that later participants who were not hazemed do not receive punishment merely by association. This itself gives meaning to his words. Still, Mahari Tzayach's view appears extremely difficult. How could an honest third witness who happened to testify truthfully be executed or flogged merely because the first two were liars? He did not know they were false. The answer is that the third witness who "joins them" necessarily says he saw the first two with him at the scene of the event. Otherwise he would not be part of one witnessing group under the rule that some of the witnesses must see one another. If the first two are later hazemed with the statement, "You were with us elsewhere," then the third witness's claim that he saw them at the scene is itself false. He may not have been personally subjected to classical hazamah, because the later witnesses did not say, "You too were with us," but his statement concerning seeing the first two has been disproved. He therefore did join false testimony and cannot claim complete innocence. This makes Mahari Tzayach's opinion more understandable. In the academy I suggested that this also explains why R. Akiva derives two rules from the verse "by the mouth of two witnesses or three witnesses": both that a third witness who joins the transgression can be punished, and that if one of a group is related or disqualified, the entire testimony is void. At first glance, how can one verse generate both? The answer is that the punishment of the third witness presupposes that he functioned as part of one testimonial unit by saying he saw the first witnesses. Once the verse makes the group one unit for punishment, it also teaches that a disqualification within the unit can invalidate all. With four witnesses, the issue changes. If the later two say they saw the first two, but the hazamah witnesses say the first two were elsewhere, there can now be two against two concerning whether the first pair was at the scene. The simple mechanism of punishing a single third participant no longer works. The broader "witnesses" formulation is therefore needed to establish the group rule independently.
Therefore, Raavad may have understood Mahari Tzayach in exactly this way. He then argues that Rambam cannot be excluding that opinion in the present case, because Rambam's later witnesses are a separate pair. If they had said they saw the first pair at the event, their testimony would itself contradict the hazamah witnesses about the first pair's location and we would have two against two, not straightforward hazamah. Thus Raavad asks: what punishment could anyone have imagined for the later pair? Rambam's own structure, however, may be more subtle. Rambam rules in Hilchot Edut 4:2 that several witnesses form one unit for hazamah where some see the others, even indirectly. If one group does not see the other, they are separate testimonies, and if one group is hazemed the other can stand. Suppose here Reuven and Shimon are the first pair, Levi and Yehudah the second. Levi says he saw Reuven and Shimon at the event, while Yehudah saw Levi. This is sufficient to connect all four into one witnessing unit under Rambam's rule of partial mutual visibility. The later hazamah witnesses prove that Reuven and Shimon were elsewhere. Levi's assertion that he saw them at the event is therefore false. Yet Yehudah may have truthfully seen Levi. Now the effect is clear. Levi's own testimony must be void because a central assertion of his about the witnessing group has been disproved. Since Levi and Yehudah were one pair who saw one another, testimony partially void within their pair is entirely void. Thus the later testimony fails as well. Why are the later witnesses not punished? Rambam can say that although Levi's testimony is false in part, he was not personally subjected to the special classical hazamah statement, "You were with us elsewhere." The Torah's penalty of zomemim is therefore not imposed upon him. It is enough that his testimony is invalidated. This yields exactly Rambam's formulation: the later set is not punished, but all the testimony is void because testimony void in part is void in full. It also explains why the first pair's own claim that they saw the later witnesses does not help. Once the first pair has been hazemed, their assertions have no evidentiary force. The linkage must come from the later witnesses' own words. Further, Rambam follows Rif that a valid witness must know of the disqualified person's participation for that participant to join the group. The same principle should apply to visual linkage. If the supposedly valid later witness never saw the first pair, there is no unit to invalidate. This reading is therefore highly precise in Rambam. It also resolves the famous dispute of Mahari Berav with Maharlbach, Radbaz, and Maharam Alashkar concerning testimony to kiddushin of a minor girl. One witness was hazemed, and Mahari Berav ruled that because the witnesses did not testify within a short utterance of one another, the remaining testimony was not void. His opponents argued that Egypt followed Rambam and the remaining witnesses should be lost. According to what I have written, everything depends on whether the later witnesses themselves said they saw the hazemed witness as part of the same witnessing group. If they did not, even Rambam agrees their testimony is separate and not void. Thus Mahari Berav's practical ruling can stand even in Rambam's locale, provided the factual linkage was absent. This is, in my view, correct halachah. Tur next writes regarding witnesses who testify that a husband divorced his wife, thereby obligating him to pay her ketubah, and are later hazemed, that they pay according to the value of the financial benefit they sought to cause. Rambam similarly says they pay the value of the benefit. Lechem Mishneh understands Rambam to mean that if a ketubah of one hundred would sell for thirty while the woman remains married, the witnesses pay thirty to the husband. This appears backwards. The witnesses sought to make the husband pay the entire hundred now. Without their testimony he faced only a contingent future liability whose present market burden is thirty. Their actual harmful increase is therefore seventy. They should pay seventy, not thirty. It seems Rambam's phrase "the value of this benefit" refers to the value the buyer would demand as profit for paying the woman immediately for a contingent ketubah right. In other words, the market discount is the benefit; the witnesses pay the amount by which immediate forced payment exceeds that discounted present burden. Although the wording is compressed, this is preferable to attributing to Rambam a result contrary to the economic substance of the damage.
And there is a further difficulty from the Gemara at the end of Perek HaKotev and Even HaEzer siman 100. If a woman has no witnesses to her divorce and the husband says, "I divorced you and already paid the ketubah," he is believed through a migo that he could have said, "I never divorced you." But even if he could have denied divorce, he would then remain exposed to the present market value of her ketubah. She could sell that contingent right. By asserting that he divorced and paid, he extinguishes it entirely. Why is he not required to pay at least the lost market value? I found in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia that even though he is believed now, if he dies she can still collect the ketubah from the heirs. If so, she retains some saleable value: a buyer can purchase her right to collect if she becomes a widow. That does not fully answer the question, because if he says, "I never divorced you," she can potentially collect either upon future divorce or death, so the right is more valuable. If he says, "I divorced and paid," only widowhood remains. Still, perhaps the difference is small because he has no present marital obligations toward her once she herself admits she was divorced. She claims no food, clothing, or conjugal rights, and he has little reason ever to give her another divorce. Thus the additional divorce contingency has limited market value. In any event, it is surprising the poskim do not mention explicitly that upon his death she can collect from the heirs according to this approach. Tosafot ask in the false-divorce-witness case why the zomemim do not compensate the wife for the loss of maintenance, clothing, and conjugal rights. They answer that the woman herself says, "I was divorced," and thereby waives those rights. This answer is somewhat difficult according to Rosh and Rambam's method of valuation, which considers factors such as whether she is quarrelsome and likely to prefer divorce. If she is already wholly separated and has no marital rights, why should her temperament toward her husband affect the valuation at all? Perhaps the valuation still considers the likelihood of formal divorce because the future ketubah right depends on how the legal bond ends, even if ordinary marital life has ceased. Another difficulty in Tosafot is economic. If the value of the lost maintenance is less than the reduction in ketubah value caused by the false testimony, then why would the wife claim maintenance loss from the witnesses? Their testimony actually sought to give her the entire ketubah immediately, a benefit greater than the maintenance loss. If the maintenance is worth more than the ketubah discount, then why do the witnesses pay the husband the full ketubah loss without credit for the maintenance obligation from which their testimony would have freed him? One cannot answer that even without their testimony she would have said, "I was divorced," because according to Rambam and Mechaber in Even HaEzer siman 100, where a woman says she was divorced she can in some circumstances be believed regarding her ketubah. Thus the monetary accounting is subtle and the simple formulations of Tosafot require careful application. Rambam in Hilchot Edut 21:4 writes: two witnesses testified that a master knocked out his slave's tooth and afterward blinded his eye; if they were hazemed, they pay the master the value of the eye and the value of the slave. Raavad objects from Rambam's own ruling in Hilchot Chovel that until the master gives a document of emancipation, the slave's claim to payment for the later injury remains doubtful. The Gemara leaves unresolved whether the slave can collect before receiving the document. Kesef Mishneh answers that because if the slave seized payment he could retain it, the false witnesses created a possible loss to the master and therefore pay him. Lechem Mishneh calls this strained. Indeed, the zomemim are not liable until final judgment. What final judgment can be entered merely that "if the slave seizes, he keeps it"? Beit din ordinarily dismisses the claim while the uncertainty remains. There is a better reading. Rambam means the witnesses testified that the master knocked out the tooth and gave the slave a document of emancipation because of it, and afterward blinded his eye. Once the emancipation document was given, the master certainly owes the eye damages. Rambam did not spell out the document because he relies on his earlier laws, where he explained that the slave has no fully enforceable claim until emancipation is completed. His purpose in Hilchot Edut is only to state the hazamah consequences. The Gemara in Gittin 42b, when it discusses the baraita, says "he goes free with the tooth and receives the value of the eye." Why does it not simply establish the case where a document was given? Because the wording "he goes free with the tooth" itself implies that freedom flows by law from the injury, not from a separate completed emancipation document. If a document had already been given, the phrase would be superfluous. Thus the Gemara's proof concerns the underlying substantive dispute, while Rambam's hazamah illustration can presuppose the legally effective document without difficulty. This also answers Lechem Mishneh's later question. Rambam says: witnesses testified that the master first blinded the eye and then knocked out the tooth. They were hazemed. Later it was discovered that the truth was the reverse, tooth first and eye second. The witnesses pay the eye value to the slave. Lechem Mishneh asks: even if the true injury sequence occurred before their testimony, the master had not yet stood in court and therefore was not yet legally obligated in the same way. Why should the later discovery change the zomemim liability? According to my explanation, the witnesses had testified that the master first blinded the eye and on that basis gave an emancipation document, then knocked out the tooth. The later proof showed that the tooth was actually first and the emancipation document had been given because of the tooth. The slave was already free when the eye was blinded. Thus the witnesses are not liable for the value of the slave, because at the time they testified the slave had already received his emancipation document. They are liable only for the eye damage. This fits Rambam precisely. It also helps with another wording difficulty. Rambam says, "and afterward it was found..." Kesef Mishneh asks why "afterward" matters, since we rule like Rava that hakchashah can be the beginning of hazamah. Lechem Mishneh adds that where one amount is included within the greater, even Abaye may agree. If the case involves the slave already possessing the relevant payment, as Lechem Mishneh suggests, the contradiction may have been judicially resolved in the slave's favor under the rule that in two against two a possessor can retain. Then the first testimony was already effectively displaced before hazamah, and Rava's rule becomes more nuanced. But there is another possibility. Rambam holds that hakchashah is called the beginning of hazamah only where the later hazamah adds a legal consequence not already produced by the contradiction. If one set says, "He killed," and another says, "He did not kill," the contradiction leaves a doubt. Later hazamah adds decisive consequences against the first witnesses. But where the contradiction has already completely destroyed the first testimony and no additional legal effect remains to be produced against the litigant, a later hazamah may not revive the special framework in the same way. In the slave case, if possession already settled the dispute, the contradiction has fully operated. "Afterward" therefore has significance. Mahari ibn Lev's concern about simultaneous reversal and hazamah can also be avoided by saying Rambam prefers a two-pair structure rather than multiplying witnesses unnecessarily. Where a short formulation with two groups gives the result, Rambam does not need to state a three-group case. Rambam also writes in Hilchot Edut 21:9 that where witnesses testified that a person kidnapped a Jew and another pair testified that he sold him, then any set that is hazemed is executed, because kidnapping is the beginning of the capital liability. As noted, this suggests the kidnapping pair can be punished even without hazamah of the sale pair. The conceptual basis may be that the kidnapping testimony has no independent purpose except the capital sequence, unlike the first two goring testimonies of a forewarned ox, each of which can independently obligate half-damages. Still, Tosafot's question from gorings of ownerless animals makes this distinction difficult, and therefore Rambam's exact scope remains open. Another point concerns a husband who brings witnesses that his wife committed adultery, and her father brings witnesses who hazem them. Rambam rules the husband's witnesses are executed. The Gemara adds that they do not pay money. Rashi explains that one might have thought they should pay for the ketubah loss they sought to cause her, but the death penalty absorbs the monetary payment. Then when the husband later brings a new pair who hazem the father's witnesses, those father's witnesses are executed and also pay the husband the one-hundred-sela fine he had become obligated to pay. Why do they not also compensate him for the ketubah value their testimony sought to preserve for the wife? If the wife really committed adultery, she loses the ketubah. Their false hazamah causes him to remain liable. The answer may lie in the special nature of the ketubah's market value. Mordechai asks why a woman cannot simply sell her ketubah back to her husband, despite the rule that a husband may not remain married to a woman without a ketubah. One could structure the sale conditionally: if he dies, she has no ketubah, but if he divorces her he must still pay. If such a conditional sale were economically meaningful, it would show the husband suffers a measurable loss even while unable to divorce. Rashi may hold that no buyer meaningfully purchases such a right where divorce is legally blocked. Tosafot note that, statistically, the contingency of the wife dying first is significant, and much of the market value of a ketubah depends upon the possibility of divorce. If the husband is barred from divorcing her, the ketubah may have little or no present sale value from his perspective. In the case of a husband convicted as a motzi shem ra, he cannot divorce her all his days. According to the false testimony of the father's witnesses, that prohibition applies. The ketubah therefore may not represent a presently saleable monetary loss to him in the way it does in an ordinary marriage where divorce remains possible. This can explain why the father's witnesses pay the fixed fine but not a separate ketubah market value. The reverse case, where the husband's witnesses falsely accused her of adultery, is different. Without them, she could still have a normal marriage in which divorce is possible. Her ketubah therefore has a real market value that their false testimony would destroy. Rashi consequently needs the rule that the death penalty absorbs the money. This distinction is not entirely free of difficulty but fits the structures better than treating both sides identically. Another question arises from Rambam's law of motzi shem ra. He writes: two witnesses testified that a man had relations with a betrothed maiden and were hazemed; they are executed and do not pay. If they testified that he had relations with so-and-so's daughter and were hazemed, they are executed and pay a fine to her father. Lechem Mishneh asks what fine this means. If it refers to the one-hundred-sela motzi shem ra fine, the husband is not liable merely because witnesses testified to adultery. He pays the fine only where his witnesses are themselves hazemed in the required procedural setting. Why should the later false witnesses pay a fine the husband would not otherwise have owed? Also, if the husband himself already must pay the father, why should the false witnesses pay the same hundred again? The answer is that Rambam is stating the sequence compactly. The husband first brought witnesses that she committed adultery. Her father hazemed them. The husband's first pair were executed, and the husband thereby became obligated in the one-hundred-sela motzi shem ra fine. The husband then brought a second pair who again testified that she had committed adultery and whose testimony, if accepted, would undermine the father's entitlement to that already-created fine. The father hazemed this second pair as well. Those second zomemim are executed for the capital accusation and also pay the father the hundred they sought to deprive him of, because the husband's fine had already been established. This is why Rambam's wording can be brief. He is copying the Gemara's legal category, while the necessary sequence follows from the substantive laws of motzi shem ra. With this, the major difficulties in the chapter are resolved. The general principles that emerge are these: Hazamah differs from ordinary contradiction because it addresses the witnesses themselves rather than merely the underlying event. Where the later testimony necessarily contradicts the substance of the event in the same breath as it attacks the witnesses, Rambam can classify it as hakchashah rather than hazamah. Testimony can be divided where self-reference would otherwise prevent hazamah, and the legal content accepted by beit din determines whether the testimony is capable of hazamah. The rule "as he intended and not as he did" applies differently to death, lashes, and money. In money, reversibility generally preserves liability even after payment; in lashes, Rambam holds the zomemim can still be lashed; in death, once the victim was executed, the zomemim are not executed. The later participation of additional witnesses depends on whether they joined one testimonial unit through timing and mutual observation. A later witness whose own claim of seeing the earlier pair is disproved can invalidate the paired testimony without necessarily receiving the special hazamah punishment. And in financial valuation of false testimony, we measure the actual present economic loss created by the witnesses, not merely the nominal face value of the obligation. There is still much to clarify in the intermediary disputes, and they bear directly on Rambam's formulations. Regarding the rule that if the litigant himself supports the first witnesses, the later testimony cannot create ordinary hazamah against them, one must distinguish between the qualification of the testimony and the punishment of the witnesses. If the defendant admits only after the witnesses testified, the testimony was valid when received. It was then fully possible for later witnesses to say, "You were with us," and therefore the testimony is not retroactively defined as testimony incapable of hazamah. This is why the defendant cannot defeat a fine simply by confessing after testimony. But when the later witnesses actually arrive and seek to punish the first witnesses, the defendant's present admission can still matter to the special fine of hazamah, because he says there was no false scheme against him. This is not contradictory. Capacity for hazamah is measured at the time the testimony is given. Actual liability as zomemim is measured when the later witnesses appear and the court decides the hazamah. The same distinction explains the dispute of the Sages and Sumchus in Bava Kamma where the thief had previously said, "I stole and slaughtered before these witnesses." According to the Sages, once he specifically identifies the witnesses and confirms the act before they testify, their testimony lacks the ordinary possibility of hazamah in relation to him. According to Sumchus, because he supports rather than attacks them, perhaps this does not render their statement procedurally defective. Yam Shel Shlomo correctly limits the case to a prior specific identification of the witnesses. If the confession comes only afterward, the testimony was already accepted in proper form. There is also no contradiction from one who admits a fine and afterward witnesses come. The question whether his admission exempts him from the fine is a law of confession to fines. It does not mean the later witnesses were incapable of hazamah when they testified, unless his confession had specifically established those very witnesses as truthful beforehand. This distinction is necessary. Now consider exile. At the beginning of Makkot, witnesses say a person killed inadvertently and must go to a city of refuge. Tosafot ask how the witnesses can obligate exile where the defendant could say, "I killed intentionally," and thereby remove himself from exile. They answer through his silence. One might instead formulate the case where he expressly says, "I killed inadvertently." But according to Rashi's tendency to treat these special punishments as fines in relation to self-admission, an express confession can itself change the procedural basis. Silence is cleaner: it leaves the witnesses' testimony as the source of the obligation while avoiding an affirmative contradictory defense. This is why Tosafot phrase the answer as they do. Regarding Rambam's ruling on lashes after the false sentence was already carried out, there are further proofs. R. Meir and the Sages dispute whether false witnesses to a forty-lash obligation receive eighty or forty. R. Meir gives one set for "you shall not bear false witness" and one for "as he intended." The Sages treat the prohibition as the warning supporting the hazamah punishment and give forty. Had the Sages believed that once the defendant was physically lashed there could no longer be any hazamah punishment, the Mishnah would need to distinguish the completed-punishment case. It does not. Moreover, the witnesses who testify a kohen is a son of a divorced woman cannot themselves be transformed into sons of divorced women. Scripture therefore redirects them to lashes. Once beit din has declared the kohen disqualified, a legal result has already occurred. Yet the witnesses still receive lashes if later hazemed. This demonstrates that "as he intended and not as he did" is not a universal bar once a judicial consequence occurred. Its strongest operation is in capital execution, where the irreversible death has already taken place. For money, the matter is even clearer. Money can be restored. A payment made under false testimony does not create an irreversible state comparable to death. This is why Rambam and most Rishonim obligate zomemim to pay even after the defendant has paid. Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Kamma distinguish monetary damage from capital punishment precisely through reversibility and related reasons. Ritva's contrary position must therefore be read narrowly if possible. His land-possession question may concern a judgment where the claimant has already been judicially installed into a new legal possession and the specific intended transfer is fully complete. But in an ordinary debt case, where money can simply be returned, the majority rule remains more persuasive. There is also a proof from the Torah requirement of interrogation in monetary law before the rabbinic relaxation. If every payment, discharge, or retention immediately triggered "as he did" and prevented hazamah, then no monetary testimony could ever satisfy the Torah's original requirement of testimony capable of hazamah. Before judgment there is no penalty; after judgment the result is complete. The whole system would collapse. This reductio strongly supports the majority view that "as he did" does not block monetary hazamah in the same manner. The distinction concerning a judicial declaration that leaves existing possession unchanged is even stronger. A judgment that a borrower owes nothing does not physically create a new state. It merely confirms the status quo. It can be reversed if the underlying testimony is later exposed as false. Accordingly, even one who wishes to preserve some version of Ritva's view must concede many monetary cases remain capable of hazamah. Now to the question of witnesses who say their intention was merely to disqualify the defendant as a witness. Tosafot ask in the learned-adulteress case why they cannot say, "We came only to disqualify him," thereby avoiding capital hazamah. One answer is that where the accusation itself is formulated in a capital manner, especially after warning or with details suited only to the capital charge, the claim of a lesser intent is not credible. If their aim were only witness disqualification, they could have testified to a simpler offense carrying lashes or another recognized disqualification. Rambam can use this reasoning in many cases. This also addresses the kidnapping witnesses. The first set, who testify to kidnapping, can theoretically say they intended only to disqualify him as a witness. Yet where the sale witnesses arrive immediately and the two groups together present one integrated capital accusation, the natural interpretation is that they came as one sequence to establish the capital offense. Rambam's phrase that kidnapping "is the beginning of his liability to death" reflects this. Chizkiyah's dissent can be tied to R. Akiva's half-a-matter doctrine. If kidnapping alone is legally incomplete testimony toward the final capital offense, the first set may receive only lashes or no capital hazamah because their testimony is not itself the whole capital matter. R. Yochanan, aligned with the Sages, treats each stage as a valid evidentiary component of one offense. Rambam follows this. The rule that Chizkiyah and R. Yochanan ordinarily follow Chizkiyah is therefore not decisive, because here the underlying Tannaitic halachah favors R. Yochanan's structure. This also explains Maggid Mishneh's reason why the kidnapper himself does not receive lashes for the kidnapping prohibition. The prohibition serves as warning for the capital offense. Chizkiyah can still discuss lashes for the witnesses if, under his half-a-matter model, their testimony never legally reaches the capital structure. But once Rambam follows the Sages, the prohibition is fully tied to the capital offense and separate lashes are unavailable. There is support from the Mishnah in Makkot, which enumerates special lash cases but does not list kidnapping witnesses in the way Chizkiyah's view might suggest. Nevertheless, Tosafot's ownerless-ox question remains. If the first goring witnesses testify to an event producing no half-damages, their testimony also has no independent monetary purpose. Why are they not like kidnapping witnesses? One can distinguish the legal structure. The first goring is not itself an element of a single present offense. It changes the ox's future status. The later full-damage obligation arises from a different future act of the ox. Kidnapping and sale, by contrast, are two statutory elements of one capital crime committed by the same person in one criminal sequence. Thus even if the first goring has no immediate damages, it remains only preparatory evidence of future status, not the first half of one present capital offense. This may defend Rambam despite Tosafot. The matter still deserves study, but Rambam's distinction is less arbitrary when framed this way.
Know now regarding hazamah given outside the witnesses' presence. Tur rules that witnesses are hazemed only in their presence, but ordinary contradiction can be received even outside their presence. Rivash, responsum 266, holds that even if formal hazamah cannot be completed outside their presence, the first witnesses can still become personally disqualified because the later witnesses establish that they testified falsely. Shach objects. He says testimony that someone robbed or committed another disqualifying offense attacks the witness's own conduct, whereas hazamah testimony concerns the prior court testimony and therefore differs. I do not understand this distinction. Saying, "You robbed," and saying, "You testified falsely because you were with us elsewhere," both address the witness's own wrongful conduct. Indeed, the essence of hazamah is that it attacks the witnesses personally. Maharlbach, in the controversy over semikhah, had already challenged Rivash differently. Hazamah is a Torah novelty. We should not extend even its disqualification consequences beyond the precise conditions Scripture requires. If it was given outside their presence, there is no formal hazamah and perhaps no personal disqualification either. Maharit defends Rivash. He says the novelty of hazamah concerns imposing the reciprocal punishment upon the first witnesses. Personal disqualification is ordinary law: if two witnesses prove that someone testified falsely, why should he remain valid? This answer has force, especially where one witness is opposed by two. In that situation the usual novelty of believing the later pair over an equal first pair is absent. Two against one is ordinary evidentiary law. Rivash's actual case involved one of two kiddushin witnesses being hazemed. There is therefore strong basis to disqualify that one witness even outside his presence. But where two first witnesses are opposed by two later witnesses, the matter is harder. Tosafot repeatedly characterize even the personal disqualification of equal-pair zomemim as part of the novelty of hazamah. In ordinary two-against-two contradiction, each pair can testify separately according to Rav Huna. Thus Maharlbach's objection remains substantial for equal pairs. This dispute may parallel the disagreement between Raavyah and Riva concerning testimony received outside a litigant's presence. Raavyah proves from the rule that hazamah outside the first witnesses' presence is at least contradiction that testimony received outside the litigant's presence can be valid after the fact. Riva responds that the relevant litigant is present even if the first witnesses are absent. The contradiction concerns the substantive case, so it is being received in the presence of the person whose money or status is at stake. The first witnesses themselves are not necessarily the "litigants" for the contradiction aspect. Raavyah's proof makes sense if he assumes Rivash's view that the out-of-presence hazamah also disqualifies the first witnesses personally. Then testimony has indeed been received to their detriment outside their presence. Riva's rejection can reflect the opposite view: only the underlying testimony is contradicted, while the absent witnesses themselves are not personally disqualified. This is a coherent way to map the Rishonim. One might object that Raavyah himself should then allow formal hazamah punishment outside their presence if testimony received outside a litigant's presence is valid after the fact. The answer is that hazamah punishment is a fine and resembles capital procedure. Even if ordinary monetary testimony can be accepted after the fact outside a litigant's presence, fines and quasi-capital penalties retain the stricter requirement of presence. The Mishnah concerning a murderer mixed with other murderers shows that final judgment in capital law cannot be completed outside the defendant's presence even after the fact. The same special strictness can apply to the fine of zomemim. Personal disqualification, however, is not itself a fine in the same way. It follows from the fact that they are wicked witnesses. Thus Raavyah can accept Rivash's disqualification while denying the special hazamah punishment. This produces an important practical distinction. Where out-of-presence testimony is received solely to invalidate a document and save a defendant from payment, the Sages have reason to allow it in pressing circumstances, much like witnesses about to depart overseas. Where the same testimony is offered to invalidate a receipt, impose liability, or alter kiddushin status, there is less basis for accepting it outside the absent witness's presence. This may explain why Tosafot and Ramban speak simply about receiving testimony that document witnesses are robbers even when those witnesses are absent. The purpose is often to allow the defendant to resist collection. It resembles testimony received for the defendant's protection. Maharlbach himself wondered why testimony of robbery should be accepted outside the alleged robber's presence. The answer is necessity: otherwise a plaintiff could collect on a false document whenever the witnesses had left the country and the defendant would be helpless to prove their disqualification. Chazal permit such defensive testimony in a case comparable to witnesses who are about to depart. Once we understand that exception, Rivash's extension to out-of-presence hazamah can be limited to the same defensive setting. If the testimony is offered to invalidate a receipt and enable extraction, or to establish a severe personal-status consequence, the exception does not automatically apply. Shach proposes a different distinction: perhaps out-of-presence hazamah can disqualify the witnesses from ordinary testimony but not from oaths, where the consequence would be that the opposing party swears and collects. This has some intuitive force because oath disqualification produces immediate monetary advantage to the opponent. But if the entire basis is the emergency rule allowing defensive testimony outside presence, it is difficult to draw Shach's exact line. The Rishonim do not make this distinction in ordinary robbery-disqualification testimony. The matter therefore remains uncertain. There is, however, one point that is clear. Even according to Rivash, hazamah outside the witnesses' presence is not formal hazamah. It can at most establish that the first witnesses are personally false or wicked. Therefore it does not trigger the special rule that if some of one hazamah unit are hazemed, the entire testimony is void by the Torah's hazamah framework. The ordinary rule of "one found related or disqualified invalidates the group" applies only where the disqualified witness actually joined the others in seeing and testifying as one unit. Here the hazemed witness is proven not to have been at the scene at all. He therefore cannot invalidate the genuine witnesses merely by being later found false. This resolves Rivash's wording in the kiddushin responsum. He says that once one witness was disqualified through out-of-presence hazamah, only one witness to kiddushin remains, and one witness in kiddushin is ineffective. Mahari Berav and Maharlbach asked: why say one remains? If the hazamah voids part of the testimony, all should be void. The answer is that formal hazamah did not occur. Only personal disqualification occurred. There is no special partial-hazamah rule and no original joint witnessing by the false witness, because the later testimony proves he was elsewhere. Thus the other witness remains a valid single witness. This is precise and strongly supports Rivash's formulation. Now to warning of zomemim. Tur and Rambam rule that zomemim require no warning, as is explicit in several Talmudic passages. Ritva asks: do we not already frighten and warn witnesses before testimony? What more warning could be needed? He answers that the ordinary judicial warning does not specifically tell them, "If you are later proven zomemim, you will receive the exact punishment you sought to impose." Thus it is not the formal warning used for punishment. For Tur's immediate purpose of witness disqualification, the issue may appear unnecessary because a person can be disqualified for wickedness even without formal warning once the act is known to be deliberate. But it matters for actual fines and punishments, including cases of seizure. There is also a conceptual difficulty. Ordinary capital warning requires the offender to accept the warning and say, in effect, "I know, and nevertheless I act," thereby permitting himself to be subjected to death. How could that ever work for witnesses? If, before testimony, they say, "We knowingly testify falsely and accept death," then they have admitted the testimony is false and no testimony exists. If they say, "We know the truth is with us, but if we are later found zomemim we accept death," they have not admitted any wrongdoing and have not accepted punishment for an act they acknowledge as forbidden. This is worse than merely saying, "I know," without accepting the warning. It is therefore difficult to imagine full capital-style warning for zomemim at all. The Gemara's discussion in Ketubot, asking when they should be warned, may therefore refer only to a lesser warning sufficient to distinguish accidental from deliberate conduct. Rambam in Hilchot Sanhedrin explains that warning serves in part to determine whether the offender acts knowingly. Even that lesser warning is difficult for zomemim because of timing. Before the false testimony, they may forget the warning. At the moment of testimony, any statement explicit enough to function as warning risks revealing the testimony as false. After testimony, the testimony is already fixed and cannot be retracted. Thus Torah dispenses with warning. Another explanation is even stronger. The requirement that the offender expressly accept death is derived in the ordinary capital offenses. For lashes, such acceptance may not be required in the same form. The Gemara in Ketubot is discussing why zomemim pay money rather than receive lashes in a context comparing monetary and lash consequences. Its warning discussion can therefore concern lashes, not death. If so, there is no need to force the full capital "permit himself to die" formula into the sugya. This yields a practical law: for lashes, ordinary warning need not include the same explicit self-subjection formulation required for death. This is a sound reading.
And behold, Rambam writes in Hilchot Edut 20:4: "There is no inadvertence for zomemim, because their offense has no act; therefore they require no warning." Kesef Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh understand "there is no inadvertence" to mean the witnesses cannot excuse themselves by saying they acted unintentionally. They struggle to explain the connection to "there is no act." The plain meaning is different. Rambam means that there is no law of a sin-offering for inadvertent zomemim. If witnesses mistakenly believed their conduct permitted, or erred in their testimony in a manner that would otherwise resemble inadvertent commission of a capital offense, they do not bring a sin-offering. Why? Because a sin-offering for inadvertent capital prohibitions requires an act. The Torat Kohanim and the Gemara in Sanhedrin 65b and Keritot derive explicitly: "one who does," excluding zomemim, whose offense lacks an act. Rambam is paraphrasing that teaching. His words "there is no inadvertence because there is no act" therefore mean: there is no sacrificial category of inadvertent transgression for this offense. Raavad asks: how does that prove no warning is needed? Blasphemy also lacks an ordinary physical act, yet warning is required. There are two possible answers. First, Rambam may understand that the principal purpose of warning is to clarify whether the person is acting deliberately rather than accidentally. If the Torah has no sacrificial distinction between inadvertent and deliberate zomemim because the offense falls outside the action-based sin-offering structure, then there is less purpose in the ordinary warning framework. One can object that moral inadvertence still exists even without a sin-offering. The second answer is better. Why is speech by zomemim not considered an act? Moving the lips can count as an act in other laws. Sanhedrin asks this and answers that zomemim "exist through seeing." Rashi's explanation is compressed. The underlying point is that the death or monetary liability of zomemim does not arise at the moment they utter the false words. At that moment, nothing may yet happen. Beit din can find another basis to acquit, the defendant can flee, or the testimony can fail for another reason. The special liability of zomemim crystallizes only when beit din renders judgment upon the litigant based on their testimony. By then, the witnesses are sitting silently. Their earlier speech did not itself complete the punishable result. Thus there is no single physical act at the moment their own capital or monetary liability becomes complete. This explains both the sin-offering rule and warning. Formal warning for a capital or lash offense ordinarily must precede the act closely, within the relevant time. For zomemim, the critical liability point can occur hours after the speech, when the judges finish deliberating and render judgment. The earlier warning is too remote, and the later point involves no act by the witnesses. This is more severe than ordinary uncertain warning, because the completion of the offense lies in the hands of other people, the judges, and can be prevented by new testimony or judicial acquittal. Nevertheless Torah imposes the hazamah punishment without warning. That is precisely the "stringency" of zomemim referred to by the Gemara. This also answers why the Gemara can compare zomemim to blasphemy and other speech offenses without making Rambam's distinction explicit. For blasphemy, the offense is complete in the utterance itself. For zomemim, the utterance only initiates a chain whose punitive completion depends upon later judicial action. The categories are therefore different. The Gemara's phrase that zomemim "exist through seeing" can also mean that beit din does not rely merely upon their speech. Their authority rests on their claim that they actually saw an external event. The supposed seeing is part of what produces the judicial act. Even if one prefers that explanation, Rambam's initial statement that their inadvertence lacks an action-based sin-offering remains correct. The variant textual readings in the Gemara make it possible Rambam did not even have precisely our wording of the final answer. What matters is that his legal statement is supported by Torat Kohanim and Keritot. Now return to the multiple-witness case. Tur and Rambam say the first witnesses are not punished until every witness who joined the one testimony is hazemed, even though substantive testimony can be established by two. The group remains one testimony only if the additional witnesses testified within the period of a short utterance and were linked as one witnessing unit. If there was a break, the later witnesses form another group. Lechem Mishneh's explanation that later witnesses can say, "We thought he was already condemned," is especially apt in capital law. They did not think their testimony could newly kill a person. They thought they were merely confirming a judgment already inevitable from the first pair. Therefore even if later hazemed, the reciprocal death penalty may not apply. Nimukei Yosef's comment on the third witness confirms that the timing of joining matters precisely for this punishment. The difficult part is why Rambam says the whole testimony can nevertheless be void. The visual-linkage explanation given above solves this. Suppose Levi in the later pair testifies that he saw the first pair during the event. The hazamah of the first pair proves Levi's statement false. Levi is therefore an invalid witness in his own right regarding this event, even without receiving the special hazamah punishment. Yehudah, who saw Levi and joined with him as one pair, loses his testimony through the rule that testimony void in part is void in full. This is the precise meaning of Rambam's reason. It is not the ordinary case where a relative happened to be among the viewers. It is a case where the later witness's own description of the witnessing unit has been disproved by the hazamah of the first pair. If the later witnesses never said they saw the first pair and did not otherwise join them as one viewing unit, the reasoning does not apply and their testimony remains independent. This distinction is critical in practical kiddushin cases and similar matters. It also explains why Rambam elsewhere says that if groups did not see one another, hazamah of one does not destroy the other. There is no contradiction. The current case presupposes a factual linkage sufficient to make the second group dependent upon a witness whose account has now been exposed as false. If the linkage is absent, they stand. This is, in my view, the correct reconciliation of Rambam with Raavad and Shach. One should therefore not simply reject Rambam's law, as Shach does, nor apply it indiscriminately to every later witness. The factual question of who saw whom controls the result. Finally, in the ketubah valuation case, the actual measure of loss deserves precision. Suppose the ketubah face value is one hundred. While the woman remains married, because collection depends upon future widowhood or divorce and because she may die first, a purchaser would pay only thirty for that contingent right. If false witnesses testify she was divorced and the husband must pay the whole one hundred now, the husband is forced to exchange a present contingent burden worth thirty for an immediate certain payment of one hundred. His net loss is seventy. That is the amount the witnesses sought to impose beyond his prior economic burden. Rambam's phrase "the value of the benefit" must be understood in that manner. The thirty is the present value of the woman's right. The remaining seventy is the value of the immediacy and certainty the false witnesses wrongfully create. Lechem Mishneh's reading that the witnesses pay merely the thirty would invert the calculation. The same economic logic governs every case where zomemim transform a contingent obligation into a present certain one. We do not simply ask what the underlying document is worth. We compare the defendant's position before and after the false testimony. This is also why factors such as the woman's health, the husband's health, the likelihood of divorce, and the character of the marriage matter to the appraisal. They change the market value of the preexisting contingent obligation. The false witnesses pay only the difference between that value and the liability they sought to create. This principle should also guide the related motzi shem ra and ketubah questions discussed above. Where divorce is legally impossible because the husband is obligated to retain her, the market value of the ketubah changes sharply. Where the only remaining contingency is death, it can be far lower. Thus one cannot transfer the valuation mechanically from an ordinary marriage to a motzi shem ra case. All of these details confirm that Rambam's chapter is not a series of isolated rulings. It follows a coherent conception of hazamah: personal attack upon the witnesses, completion only when their testimony reaches a judicial result, punishment calibrated to what they legally sought to accomplish, and group consequences determined by the actual structure of the witnessing unit.
