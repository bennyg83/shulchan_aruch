The agent fulfilled his agency. See Sema, seif katan 8, who explains Maggid Mishneh as asking why we do not deduct for the worker's remaining idleness just as with one hired to bring produce who arrives and finds none. Sema says Maggid Mishneh answers that there the walking is secondary to a full day's employment. In my view this cannot be Maggid Mishneh's intent. When we deduct for idleness, we already appraise all the effort actually expended and subtract only the value of the work not performed. If one hired a laborer for a full day and he walked part of the day, the walking is included in the appraisal. There is no separate difficulty merely from one journey. Maggid Mishneh's real question is why we do not distinguish according to which party should have known of the accident, as siman 334, seif 1, and the Gemara do in the case of one hired to bring produce and finds none, depending on whether the land was inspected the night before. He answers that there the person is a day-worker seeking payment for work not yet performed because the employer caused his day to be lost. If the employer was not at fault and knew no more than the worker, there is no basis under dina d'garmi to charge him for the lost labor. Here, however, the worker was hired to go to a particular place and he actually completed the entire journey. The only difference is that he did not return carrying the expected load. Since he performed the agreed walking at the employer's instruction, he must be paid for the actual service rendered. We appraise only the difference between returning loaded and returning empty. Why should an employer not pay for work already performed at his command? There is no difference in principle between spending money at another's instruction and expending physical labor at his instruction. This is unlike the case in Bava Kamma 117 of one who descends to rescue property and fails to save it. There the worker still receives the ordinary value of his labor. What he loses is only the extraordinary premium promised for successful rescue, because there is an evident assessment that such excess was promised only upon success. That is why the Gemara there challenges from one hired to bring expensive medicinal produce for a sick person. Tosafot explain that the challenge concerns an above-market premium. The ordinary labor wage is unquestionably due. Likewise here, the worker receives the fair wage for the actual journey completed. Only any special premium dependent on bringing the load can be reduced when the result did not occur.
