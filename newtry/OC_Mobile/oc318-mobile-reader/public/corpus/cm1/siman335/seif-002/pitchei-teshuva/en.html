To bring him on his mission. Sema and Shach cite Beit Yosef in the name of Rashba: if Reuven hired Shimon to serve him during a journey and Reuven died halfway, Shimon receives his full wage. Mishkenot Yaakov writes that this appears to depend on a major dispute. One could compare it to an employee prevented by an accident, where the worker bears the loss because he should have stipulated otherwise; some Rishonim distinguish a contractor from a day laborer and award the contractor full pay when unavoidable circumstances prevent completion. Netivot HaMishpat concludes that we do not follow Raavad broadly on this point. If the destination was specified at hiring and Reuven died before reaching it, the heirs are exempt from paying for the remaining route. But if Shimon was hired generally to serve Reuven for as long as he remained on the road, so that even a prolonged journey would have required continued service, then the agreed wage can become due even if Reuven died after only one day. Chacham Tzvi likewise questions the broad statement attributed to Rashba and inclines that where no further labor is performed there is no reason to pay for the remainder. See there.
He gives him his full wage. See Lechem Mishneh, chapter 9 of Hilchot Sechirut, halachah 8, who asks from the law of one who descended to rescue another's property but did not succeed, in siman 264 seif 4, where he receives only his customary wage, whereas here the employer must pay everything he stipulated, even though the agent likewise did not accomplish the mission. See there. Divrei Mishpat answers that here, when the employer sent him to bring the item, that item was not available at all in that place, although the employer thought it would be found there. Thus the unforeseen circumstance did not befall the agent's undertaking, but rather the employer's assumption itself. See there.
