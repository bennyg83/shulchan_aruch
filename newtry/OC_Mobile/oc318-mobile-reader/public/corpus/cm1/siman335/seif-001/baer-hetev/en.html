He showed. It seems that this applies specifically where he did not hire him for this particular work for one day, but merely said, "Work with me at this task." If he hired him for a day, even though he showed him that task, the worker may say: since you hired me for a full day, I assumed you certainly had similar work elsewhere for me to do after I completed the task you showed me. Another. Maharam in the Mordechai writes that teaching wisdom is unlike ordinary labor where one may tell the worker, "Go hire yourself for other work," because one student understands readily and learns easily while another requires much effort. It appears that according to him, even if the replacement student is as sharp as the first or sharper, the teacher cannot be compelled, since students' dispositions generally differ, and there are many matters unrelated to the learning itself in which a teacher finds one child more agreeable than another. The dissenting opinion holds that everything depends on the learning, which was the purpose of the original hiring. See Maharik, shoresh 112. Ibid.
That he is liable. In my view no authority holds so. Maharam's statement in the Mordechai and Hagahot Mordechai, that this is no worse than one who descends into another's field, merely means that just as there he pays because he benefited, here too, since he told him, "Teach my son," thereby revealing that he wants the service, he must pay the value of the benefit. Even if Maharam intended the Rama's reading, the Rashba's view appears primary, because his proof from Nedarim 37a is clear. The Rosh, Ran, and Tosafot there also imply this, and all authorities rule without qualification that one who vows not to benefit from another may not study with him where teachers are paid, but may nevertheless teach his son, as stated in Yoreh Deah, siman 221, seif 2. See there carefully. Shach.
