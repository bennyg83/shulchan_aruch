One who hires a worker for unspecified work or for a particular task. It contains three seifim.
If hired for a particular task that is completed at midday, the worker must perform similar or easier work if available. If none is available, he receives wages as an idle worker. Rama: The employer may obtain similar work from another and assign it to him. Some say that if wages are increased, he must even do heavier work. Digging and agricultural workers who would become ill from idleness receive full wages. This applies when the employer did not show him the work beforehand. If he saw that it was less than a day's work and did not stipulate, he receives nothing after completion. If hired generally for a day, the employer may switch him from light to heavy work. Regarding a teacher whose student becomes ill, see the end of siman 334. Where the employer bears the loss, a teacher generally receives full wages because idleness weakens him and speaking to one is like speaking to one hundred, though beit din assesses whether he benefits from idleness. An employer may substitute another equally capable student, but not a more difficult one. One who teaches another's child without the father's knowledge is, according to some, entitled to payment like one who improves another's field without permission; others disagree. If asked to teach without fixing a fee, he receives the customary amount. A rabbi who served a community for years and only later fixed future compensation cannot claim for the past, for he presumably waived it. A worker who accepted all unavoidable events is nevertheless exempt for an entirely unforeseeable event. One who rented a horse for an eight-day distant journey but returned after two days may use it locally for the remaining six days even for heavier customary work, because the owner prefers heavy local work to distant travel.
