And he shall give it to him after he swears, etc. This applies where the loss exceeded the normal allowance, as stated by the Sema, seif katan 28, and by the Bach. He must therefore swear. One should not object that here, and likewise with money in the following case, this should be a case in which one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so must pay, since as a custodian he is obligated to swear that his fellow's property was lost and he cannot know this with certainty. The answer is that he swears how much was lost, and afterward we calculate according to the proportions and presume that the loss was distributed proportionally.
And he placed them together with his own money, etc. This means in one sack. But if they are in separate purses, the law is like that of distinct coins or pieces.
According to the proportion of the money, etc. Even if one party is in possession, he cannot say, "Perhaps it was yours that was lost." Nor do we follow the majority by saying that whatever separated is presumed to have separated from the majority. Rather, we calculate according to the proportions. For example, if ten belonged to him and twenty to his fellow, and three were lost or stolen, he loses one and his fellow loses two. If the proportions were reversed, twenty belonging to him and ten to his fellow, he loses two and his fellow loses one.
Perhaps yours was lost, etc. Although he is a custodian and must swear that his fellow's property was lost, and he cannot know that with certainty, we do not say that this makes him one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and therefore must pay. Once he swears, or there are witnesses, or he admits that certain individual coins were lost, that suffices, and he may say, "Perhaps yours was lost." This is explicitly proven by what the Bach writes in siman 294, end of seif 2. See there and examine carefully.
Yours was lost, etc. The party in possession loses nothing. Even though his property constituted the majority, we do not follow the monetary majority against possession, as stated by Terumat HaDeshen there.
And if two people deposited, etc. In that case no one has a presumption of possession, so we follow the majority. Terumat HaDeshen there adds a further novelty: where two people deposited with one custodian and the custodian himself owned the majority, for example Reuven deposited ten with Shimon, Levi gave him five, and Shimon's own money was twenty, even though the custodian loses nothing because he is in possession, nevertheless with respect to Reuven we follow the majority. See there. The Sema writes that the rule, "if two deposited," applies specifically to the case that follows, where they deposited distinct coins or pieces. If they deposited ordinary money, the loss is allocated to all according to proportion, not according to majority or possession. This is explicit in Terumat HaDeshen. This refers to money that was mixed together. But if each person gave him money in a separate purse, its law is like that of distinct coins or pieces. This is explicit in Responsa Maimoniyot and is also written by Terumat HaDeshen.
We follow the majority. This applies to something mobile, such as animals, as the Ir Shushan writes, and he is correct. Otherwise, in a case of theft it would be considered taken from a fixed location. Examine carefully.
Whatever separates is presumed to have separated from the majority. Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 199, similarly cites a responsum of Maharam of Or Zarua concerning four partners who brought etrogim in one basket. One had twenty, another eight, another three, and another two. Each person's fruit was marked by knots. A violent non-Jew emptied all of them from the basket, untied the knots, took ten, and returned the remainder. The identifying marks were then lost and it was unknown whose fruit had been taken. The ruling was that whatever separates is presumed to have separated from the majority, so the non-Jew is presumed to have taken from the one who had twenty. Mahari Weil there challenges this ruling, and Maharil defends it. As for Maharil's question why this should not be treated as fixed, it appears to me that the case concerns the non-Jew taking the ten outside our presence. See Yoreh Deah, siman 110, and examine carefully.
