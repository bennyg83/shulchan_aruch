And whatever separates is presumed to have separated from the majority. This matter is difficult, for the Yerushalmi in the chapter Shor Shenagach teaches: likewise, if two people placed money into one purse, one contributing one hundred and the other two hundred, and the money was stolen, they divide the loss in proportion to the amounts contributed. The Yerushalmi asks from another teaching concerning stones that were stolen, where half the loss is borne by each, and Rabbi Simlai answers that stones are large objects. It is therefore clear that with large items, which do not intermingle together, the loss is divided half and half even though the majority belonged to one of them. Only with coins, which are small, become intermingled, and are taken in groups, do we attribute the majority of what was taken to the one who owned the greater amount and the minority to the one who owned the lesser amount. But with large objects the loss is divided equally. Why do we not follow the majority? See Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of the chapter HaBayit VehaAliyah. Perhaps the Yerushalmi is speaking in a place where the thieves were Jews. In that case the items have the law of kavu'a, which is treated as half and half, as stated in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 28: You asked concerning four or five purses in your chest, some yours and some belonging to others, and your daughter took two dinars but does not know from which purse. In my judgment, since she took from something fixed in its place, it is treated as half and half and we do not follow the majority. One should not distinguish between a custodian and another person and compare it to property found in the possession of a non-Jew, because a Jew took it from the fixed place. All the more so here, where your daughter took the two dinars, which is like a custodian himself taking them, since anyone who deposits does so with the understanding that the custodian's wife and children will also have access. Since the matter is half and half, the burden of proof rests upon the one who seeks to extract. So wrote Rabbi Yitzchak son of Rabbi Avraham.
Were I not afraid to depart from the accepted view, I would say as follows regarding what the poskim wrote, that if a non-Jew took the item we say, whatever separated is presumed to have separated from the majority, as Mahari Weil writes in the name of a responsum of Maharam in the case of etrogim, that the non-Jew took from the majority, cited by the Shach, seif katan 29. In my opinion, they distinguished between a non-Jew and a Jew only in the case of nine shops selling properly slaughtered meat and one selling neveilah meat. Since the prohibition of neveilah does not apply to a non-Jew, the rule of kavu'a does not apply to him, because the doubt has no legal relevance to him. But regarding theft, a non-Jew is commanded against theft just as a Jew is, and the prohibition is even severe for him, because violation of his prohibition is punishable by death. What difference, then, is there whether a Jew took the object or a non-Jew took it? Accordingly, even if a non-Jew took it when no one was present, the rule of kavu'a applies and it is treated as half and half. The Yerushalmi's ruling concerning the stolen stones is therefore well understood, because in a case of theft the rule of kavu'a always applies, even if they were stolen by a non-Jew. Examine this carefully.
