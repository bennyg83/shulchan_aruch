That he swear. This refers to a case where the loss exceeded the ordinary allowance, as stated by the Sema and Bach; therefore he must swear. One cannot say that here, and similarly below regarding money, he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so and must therefore pay, since he is obligated to swear that the other person's property was lost but does not know precisely how much. Rather, he swears that such and such an amount was lost, and we then calculate and presume the loss occurred proportionately. Shach.
Its responsibility. Belongs to the depositor. This is explicit in Terumat HaDeshen there and is also evident from the matter itself. Sema.
His money. This is where the coins were in one sack. If they were in separate purses, the law is like distinct pieces. Shach.
Proportion. Even though one party is in possession, he cannot say, "Perhaps yours was lost." Nor do we follow the majority and say that whatever separated is presumed to have separated from the majority. Rather, the loss is apportioned according to value. For example, if one person's share was ten and the other's twenty, or vice versa, the owner of twenty bears two-thirds of the loss and the owner of ten bears one-third. Ibid.
Yours. The person in possession loses nothing. Even if the majority belonged to him, monetary law does not follow the majority against possession. So writes Terumat HaDeshen there. Although he is a custodian and must swear that the other person's property was lost, without knowing this with certainty, we do not therefore say that he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear and must pay. Once he swears, or there are witnesses, or the other person admits that the distinct pieces were lost, that suffices, and he may say, "Perhaps yours was lost." This is explicit from the Bach, siman 294, seif 2. Ibid.
Two. Where neither is in possession, we follow the majority. Terumat HaDeshen further writes an even stronger case: where two people deposited with one person and the custodian himself owned the majority, such as Reuven depositing ten with Shimon, Levi depositing five, and Shimon owning twenty, even though the custodian himself loses nothing because he is in possession, with respect to Reuven we follow the majority. See there. The Sema writes that the rule, "if two deposited," applies specifically to distinct pieces. If they deposited money, the loss is shared proportionately. See Terumat HaDeshen. This means mixed money; if each gave him money in a separate purse, the law is like distinct pieces, as is explicit in Responsa Maimoniyot. Ibid.
The majority. A movable object, such as animals. So writes the Ir Shushan, correctly, for otherwise in a theft it would be a case of taking from a fixed place. Ibid.
Separated. So too in Responsa Maharil, siman 169, citing a responsum of Rabbi Chaim Or Zarua concerning esrogim brought in a single basket belonging to four partners: one owned twenty, another eight, another three, and another two. They marked each owner's fruit with knots. A non-Jew came, emptied them from the basket, untied the knots, took ten, and returned the rest. The identifying marks were then lost and they did not know whose were taken. Whatever separated is presumed to have separated from the majority, so the non-Jew is deemed to have taken from the owner's group of twenty, etc. Mahari there questions this, and Maharil resolves it. As to Maharil's question why this is not considered fixed, it seems to me that the case is where the non-Jew took the ten out of their presence. See Yoreh Deah, siman 110. This is the Shach's language. According to this, it requires study why he wrote above that the case must specifically concern moving animals, since in a theft the thief likewise took them outside the owner's presence. See Orach Chaim, simanim 329 and 439.
