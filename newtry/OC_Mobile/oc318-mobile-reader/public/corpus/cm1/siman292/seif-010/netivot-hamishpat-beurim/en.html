He should not mix them. See Sema, seif katan 24, through his statement that this is considered "here it is," and see Shach, seif katan 21. The reference belongs to the Shach's question, "What does the rule of here it is have to do with this case?" The Shach apparently did not have the Perishah before him, for the Perishah explains that if the dispute involves a partial admission concerning the shortage, the custodian is exempt from an oath because the admitted portion is immediately available. The Mechaber's ruling that if he violated the instruction and mixed them refers to mixing produce of equal quality. If he mixed the depositor's produce with his own inferior produce, that is negligence, and he is liable for an accident where there is reason to attribute the accident to that negligence.
We apply the rule that what separates is presumed to have separated from the majority. This is difficult. In Maaser Sheni, chapter 2, regarding second tithe money mixed with ordinary money and then scattered, the Mishnah teaches that where the coins were mixed or scooped, distribution is according to proportion. Tosafos Yom Tov explains that once they are thoroughly mixed, one cannot say that the handful was taken entirely from only one category; it necessarily contains from both in proportion. Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of HaBayis VeHaAliyah explains the Mishnah as referring either to coins that were originally mixed or to coins that were later scooped together, whereas if they were picked up one by one the loss is assigned to the ordinary money. Rambam rules this Mishnah as law. The inference seems even stronger here. Even where the ordinary money is the majority, we do not say that the missing coins came from the majority and that what remains is second tithe money. This is so even though uncertainty regarding second tithe is treated stringently, as shown by the rule regarding individually gathered coins, yet with a mixture we treat it as certain that the loss occurred proportionally rather than following the majority. Although that Mishnah concerns money, the Sema and Shach explain proportional division of money by saying that people do not insist on keeping individual coins separate, while that explanation does not fit second tithe money. Moreover, before they were scattered each category had been in its own purse. The same difficulty arises from the rule that a prohibited mixture which falls into another mixture transmits prohibition only according to its proportion, for we treat the amount that fell as having come proportionally from the mixture. All the more should money belonging to Reuven and Shimon that was mixed from the outset and then partly lost be divided according to proportion. Even according to the explanation requiring both prior mixing and later scooping, the Yerushalmi and Rambam in chapter 6 of Hilchos Maaser Sheni state that taking from the heap is equivalent to mixing and scooping. It follows that an amount falling from such a heap should have the same status. The later ruling that where property was stolen each party bears half the loss is also difficult. Theft is treated as fixed in place, yet the Yerushalmi at the beginning of the chapter Shor Shenagach, cited by Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of HaBayis VeHaAliyah, explicitly teaches that where two people placed money into one purse and some was stolen, the loss is proportional. The Yerushalmi contrasts this with stones, where each bears half the loss, explaining that stones are large whereas coins are small, and it defines the size that permits effective mixing. The ruling here distinguishes between money and rings, implying that even small rings comparable to coins result in equal loss upon theft, whereas the Yerushalmi implies that small items comparable to coins are divided proportionally. Otherwise the Yerushalmi could simply have distinguished between coins, whose owners do not object to their being mixed, and stones, whose owners do object. The Yerushalmi further asks regarding the baraisa of stones that were stolen, "Are we discussing the stolen amount or the amount remaining?" Rabbi Yosi bar Bun answers that the discussion concerns the stolen amount, for otherwise the measure of justice would be distorted. Suppose one party had fifty and the other one hundred fifty, then the owner of fifty might lose nothing. In my view the Yerushalmi means that the statement "half to each" concerns allocation of the loss itself. If one had ten stones and the other five and five were lost, each loses two and a half. It does not mean that the remainder is split until the owner of the smaller amount recovers his original contribution. Since each remaining stone is of uncertain ownership, one might otherwise have treated the remainder as property in doubt and divided it. The Yerushalmi rejects that because it would distort justice. Penei Moshe struggled with this passage and I could not reconcile his explanation. Under the Yerushalmi's rationale there is no basis to distinguish between theft and loss, whereas under the Shulchan Aruch's rationale the Yerushalmi should simply have said that theft is fixed and therefore treated as equally balanced. It therefore appears that the ruling of Terumas HaDeshen and Rama regarding a lost portion, applying the majority principle to what separated, cannot mean that if one party had ten and the other five and ten were lost, the owner of five takes all five remaining. In a mixture of one kosher animal and ten nonkosher animals, if ten separated and only one remained, we would certainly not say that all ten came from the majority and therefore the remaining one is kosher. We see similarly that when all but one of a prohibited group of offerings were sacrificed, the final animal is burned rather than presumed permitted. The same principle appears in Yoreh Deah siman 110: when all items separate, the final two retain the doubtful status, and if two prohibited items had been mixed, the final four retain that status. Accordingly, where one person contributed ten and the other five and five were lost, the entire loss is assigned to the owner of ten under the majority principle, just as the five found items would be attributed to the majority even if we did not know from whom they fell. But if seven or ten were lost, only the portion exceeding five is divided equally, because the five of the larger contribution corresponding to the smaller contribution are like the final two items of a mixture, a balanced doubt that must be shared. This also allows the Maaser Sheni rule to be reconciled. For example, if there were ten ordinary coins and five second tithe coins and ten were lost, the majority rule assigns five of the loss to ordinary money while the remainder is balanced. Since there are in any event ordinary coins among what remains, one can redeem the second tithe status onto those coins and avoid any prohibition. One should not object that the doubt is equal and therefore all should be treated as second tithe, because if ordinary money is the majority, second tithe is nullified in that majority by Torah law. Coins are not nullified only because of their significance, as Tosafos write at the end of Meilah. The case therefore resembles a significant living creature that was mixed and later one fell away, where we attribute the separated item to the majority. If second tithe is the majority, then the majority principle places more second tithe among what was lost and leaves enough ordinary money among the remainder for redemption. The reason for stringency with second tithe appears to be that all the money belongs to one person, who can redeem one portion upon another without financial loss. As to the Yerushalmi, it appears that Rama understands its statement that stones are large and coins are small to mean that people insist on keeping the former identifiable and separate, whereas coins readily mix and cannot be distinguished. Nimukei Yosef in HaBayis VeHaAliyah, however, expressly disagrees with the ruling here. Broken stones are comparable to items that were lost, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 101, seif katan 10, for the majority rule is also applied to something cut away. Yet Nimukei Yosef rules here that we do not apply the majority principle. This is also logical. We find the rule that what separates follows the majority only when adjudicating the status of the separated item itself, because it did not acquire a fixed status in its new place. When adjudicating what remains in the fixed mixture, however, the rule is always that what is fixed is treated as an equal doubt. Otherwise, if one prohibited item were mixed with two permitted items and one item fell away, we would say that the one that separated certainly came from the permitted majority, leaving one prohibited and one permitted item, a Torah prohibition. If another item then fell into the sea it could no longer be permitted. Likewise, where some animals separate from a mixture, many authorities permit the separated animals while prohibiting what remains fixed. Thus we do not infer the status of the fixed remainder from the status of what separated. Nor can we say that when some mixed money is lost, all remaining money is a balanced doubt and therefore divided equally. If Reuven's ten coins were mixed with Shimon's five and five were lost, five of the remaining coins are certainly Reuven's; only the other five are doubtful, and only the doubtful portion is subject to division. Terumas HaDeshen's proof from Teshuvos Maimoniyos, and Rashba's responsum concerning a daughter who took two gold coins and it was unknown from whom, is not conclusive in my view, because there the question concerns the coins that were found or taken away, and regarding those we certainly apply the majority principle, just as with birds that leave a dovecote. The proof from Or Zarua cited by later authorities may likewise concern thieves who took everything, retained ten coins for themselves, and returned the rest. In that situation we are effectively judging the items that separated. It therefore appears that this law is a dispute among the authorities. If part of the lost property is later found and it is unknown to whom it belongs, then with large items that were not mixed or scooped together we apply the majority principle to what is found. The reason we do not follow the majority in the case of ordinary and second tithe money that became mixed is that second tithe is comparable to something that can be permitted through another means. If one party had ten and the other five and seven were lost, according to Rama the first five of the loss fall entirely upon the owner of ten and the remaining two are divided equally. If six were later found, the entire ultimate loss falls upon the majority owner, because whenever we judge the items that were lost we apply the majority principle, while the items found are treated as though they had never been lost. It must also be stated that this case concerns an agent who mixed the funds with the depositors' consent, as explained in Terumas HaDeshen, responsum 314. If the funds were delivered to him in two separate bundles and the agent mixed them himself, he must pay both depositors where each makes a definite claim that the remainder is his. If both are uncertain, he is exempt, as explained in simanim 300 and 77.
And if it is fixed. See Shach, who distinguishes between a Jewish thief and a non Jewish thief, and in seif katan 28 writes that the case in which theft is considered fixed refers to a Jewish thief. Ketzos HaChoshen questions this, arguing that regarding theft there should be no distinction, since the descendants of Noach are also prohibited from robbery. According to what I wrote in the laws of theft, however, a non Jew is not subject to the positive command of returning stolen property, because a descendant of Noach is liable for the offense and the law was not given as a restitution obligation. The non Jew nevertheless does not acquire the stolen item, under the principle that an act the Torah forbids does not take legal effect, and the Jewish owner may therefore recover his own stolen property directly. The distinction is consequently clear. With a Jewish thief, the doubt arises regarding the person who is obligated in the mitzvah of restitution. There is no uncertainty concerning the prohibition of stealing itself, because taking either victim's property is forbidden. The uncertainty concerns only to whom restitution must be made. With a non Jewish thief, there is no mitzvah of restitution incumbent upon him, so no corresponding doubt exists regarding an obligation owed by him. The uncertainty is only between the persons whose stolen property will bear the loss, while as against the original owners the uncertainty arose outside their presence. We therefore apply the rule that what separates follows the majority, as with any doubt that arises away from the fixed location.
