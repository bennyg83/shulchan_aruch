He may use it. See Shach, seif katan 8, who says the custodian can repay with other money. Taz in siman 73, seif katan 19, cites R. Tzvi's objection that Tosafot say this only according to Rabbeinu Chananel, while according to Ri, whose view we follow, he must return those very coins. In my view the ruling can nevertheless be explained. Rav Nachman also agrees that the custodian has permission to use the money. This means the depositor effectively gave him an option: if he wishes to purchase the coins for his own use, he may do so; if he does not wish, no acquisition occurs. This resembles one who gives another an object and says the recipient may choose whether to acquire it. Once the recipient elects to acquire, he can do so without the giver's ability to retract, while if he never elects, there is no acquisition. See siman 200, seif 11. Accordingly, the custodian can say, "I never intended to acquire these coins." This is similar to Shach, seif katan 11, who recognizes that the custodian can say he never intended to make the money available under the particular condition. This also explains why Rav Nachman's position is not contradicted by the law of me'ilah that once the custodian spends, he commits me'ilah. Until expenditure, he can still say, "I do not wish to acquire the coins," because the decision is his. Once he expressly chooses to acquire them, however, the depositor cannot cancel that election. Rav Huna, who says the custodian immediately has borrower-like liability, must hold that by accepting the money under permission to use he immediately accepted it as a loan and acquired it at once. That is why the Gemara asks that me'ilah should occur immediately.
He already used it. It appears that even if he spent only one perutah, he becomes liable for the entire sum. Rashi in Kiddushin 47 explains similarly that once he begins to spend even a perutah, the whole deposit receives loan status. Here too, once he starts using the money he has demonstrated that he holds it for use as his own. This also explains why Rosh needed to give a special reason that returning the remaining money without the owner's knowledge does not restore the deposit status. If the entire sum had already been spent, repayment would plainly require actual acquisition by the lender; until the lender acquires the repayment money, payment has not occurred. The novelty is where most of the original coins remain physically present and only a small amount was used. Even then, commencement of use transformed the legal status of the whole.
He is liable even for accidents. See Shach, seif katan 9, citing Tosafot in Bava Kamma 96. At first glance Tosafot say that with an ordinary loan, if the currency falls in value, the borrower repays according to the lower present value. In my view Shach's point is more limited. Tosafot there speak of a coin that was invalidated in the local place but still circulates elsewhere and the borrower has access to that place, like the rule, "Go and spend it in Meishan." If the currency becomes wholly invalid and no longer has the legal name of coin at all, the borrower cannot discharge the loan by handing back those invalidated coins even if they are the same physical money. Hagahot Maimoniyot says this explicitly, and so does responsa Lechem Rav. Shach's principal proof from Tosafot is their statement that loan money is treated as though no longer physically present even while the identical coins remain. There is also a textual error in Shach, seif katan 11. The text should read, "If he fulfilled the condition, he would be a paid custodian; the novelty is..." The word "not" is erroneous.
If he profited with the money. See Sema, seif katan 20, who explains that the depositary expressly disclosed that he was trading for his own benefit. This would have to mean he made that declaration before witnesses, as Rama concludes. That interpretation is strained. Rama joins together cases where the depositary had permission to use the money and where he did not. If he had permission, the profit can belong to him even without such an express declaration. Rashba's responsum also indicates the opposite of Sema's reading, as Sema himself notes, and Mordechai at the beginning of HaGozel similarly speaks otherwise. It therefore appears that Rama's text contains an error. It should read: "If he disclosed his intent that the money be used for his benefit," meaning that the depositor disclosed that he wished the depositary to trade with the money on the depositor's behalf. Then, in a case where the depositary ordinarily had no right to use the money, we do not assume he traded as a robber for himself. We presume he followed the depositor's disclosed intention. This is logically compelling. If the depositor said nothing, why should the depositary engage in business for the depositor, giving the depositor all profit while personally bearing the risk? We do not assume a person acts irrationally. But if the depositor expressly indicated that he wanted the money invested on his behalf, why should we presume the depositary stole the use for himself? We instead assume he acted for the depositor. Rashba's language supports two independent grounds: disclosure by the depositary, or disclosure by the depositor. Either can establish for whose benefit the trading occurred. Likewise, what Sema cites from Mordechai, that the profit belongs to the depositary only if he already paid the principal, is difficult if read generally, because in an ordinary unspecified case the profit already belongs to the depositary. Mordechai must be speaking where the depositor disclosed that he wanted the money invested for the benefit of both parties, or where the depositary admitted that he traded for both of them. In such a case, even if the depositor later demands his principal, so long as the depositary has not yet paid him out, the depositor's share in the profit has not been surrendered, and they divide according to the original arrangement.
If the owner of the deposit comes. Mishneh LaMelech explains Rama as follows. In the first clause, the owner comes while the deposited money is still intact in the depositary's possession. Even if the depositary says, "Accept the business venture, both profit and loss," and the depositor says, "I do not want you to invest at all; return my money," if the depositary refuses and invests it, he must give the depositor the profit. The usual rule that one who merely prevents another from earning is exempt applies only when he himself did not profit from the withheld capital. Where he actually earned with the other person's money, he must surrender the profit, provided it is established that the owner had a real opportunity to earn, as Mordechai says, "the money was ready for him to lend." This does not contradict siman 183, where an agent given money to buy wheat for profit changes and buys barley for himself, and the profit belongs to the agent. Here the depositary is liable because he prevented the depositor from using his own money and simultaneously earned with it. Shach disagrees even in such a case. Mordechai says that where the profit must be given to the depositor, only half the profit is due. This appears to concern a case where the depositary admits that he invested for the benefit of both parties. Rama's second clause concerns a depositor who demands the money only after the depositary has already invested it. Until the depositor appeared, the profit lawfully belonged to the depositary. Therefore, if the depositary says, "Accept the venture with both profit and loss," and the depositor refuses and demands immediate repayment, the prior profit remains the depositary's.
The law favors the depositary. See Sema, seif katan 23. His wording that the depositary "spent it with permission" is not exact. Even where he had no permission to spend the funds, the same rule follows from the rationale just stated, and Mordechai says so explicitly.
