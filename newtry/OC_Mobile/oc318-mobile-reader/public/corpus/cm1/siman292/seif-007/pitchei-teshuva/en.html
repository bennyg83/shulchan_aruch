He is permitted to use it. Netivot HaMishpat above, siman 121, seif 5, seif katan 10, indicates that this permission applies specifically to a custodian. A messenger of transmission, whether appointed by the lender to carry money to the borrower or by the borrower to deliver money to the lender, has no right to use the money even if he is a moneychanger and the funds are unsealed. Beit Yosef states this in Yoreh Deah, siman 169, because the money was not entrusted to remain with him for any period, but only to be delivered. Certainly, where the borrower gave him the money with the formula "bring and acquire for him," so that the money already belongs to the lender, and the lender never entrusted unsealed funds to him, the messenger may not use them. By contrast, where the lender appointed a receiving agent to accept payment from the borrower, the agent may use unsealed funds because this is equivalent to the lender depositing unsealed money with him. See also siman 125, seif katan 3.
Even beforehand. See Baer Heitev. Shach writes in several places that once use of the deposited money is permitted, the custodian may replace those coins with other money. Chacham Tzvi, in his glosses to Taz, siman 73, seif 19, strongly rejects this and argues that Tosafot proves the opposite: according to our ruling that the custodian is a paid custodian, if the depositor demands the money before it has been spent, he must return those very coins and may not substitute others. Tumim agrees, and Rabbi Ozer likewise challenges Shach. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 49, also disagrees with Shach. Beit Ephraim nevertheless distinguishes that if the owners are present and insist on receiving those very coins, they must be returned, but if they have not come and the custodian needs the money, he may exchange it by crediting the owners with other money, on the principle that he benefits while they suffer no loss, since they have no particular interest in those coins. His language implies that this may apply even where there is otherwise no permission to use the funds, such as one who is neither a moneychanger nor a shopkeeper. This requires study. See also Netivot HaMishpat, who defends Shach, and Nachalat Tzvi.
He is liable even for unavoidable accidents. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Gilya Masechta, end of siman 13, discusses one who gave money to a wagon driver to purchase salt for him in Pinsk and the money was stolen on the way. The wagon driver certainly has the status of a paid custodian, even if he received no separate fee for guarding and even if his transportation fee had already been paid, because of the benefit that the owner chose him over others. In addition, since he had permission to use the money, he is plainly a paid custodian. There is reason to consider him liable even for unavoidable accidents. Sema explains that permission to use deposited money is less valuable than use of the proceeds of a lost object because the custodian fears that the depositor may suddenly demand his money. Here, however, the money was given specifically to procure salt in Pinsk. The owner could not demand the money itself, only the salt, so the driver need not fear using the money in commerce or exchanging currency. This could make him liable even for unavoidable accidents. Maharik, shoresh 131, however, imposed only the liability of a paid custodian, perhaps because his case involved sealed money. See also Responsa Shevut Yaakov III, siman 143, concerning a sealed sack of silver coins containing sealed gold coins sent through a gentile courier. The recipient gave written acknowledgment that he had received the sealed sack correctly, but a week later claimed that the gold was missing. Since the sender had stated the weight and instructed him to inspect the seal, failure to check immediately was gross negligence. Even an unpaid custodian would be liable, and certainly this recipient, who was a paid custodian and, after opening the sack, could use the money until purchasing the merchandise. Shevut Yaakov also discusses the evidentiary weight of suspicious circumstances. See Shach, seif katan 9, concerning currency that becomes invalid, and Netivot HaMishpat and Responsa Beit Ephraim, siman 50, who discuss Shach's citation of Tosafot. See also Responsa Noda BiYehuda, siman 35.
He must give the profit. See Baer Heitev, who cites Maharshal that one who merely keeps another person's money idle causes only indirect loss. Responsa Panim Meirot I, siman 82, discusses a merchant who brought iron to a fair. A townsman with a claim against him pretended to be a buyer, completed the purchase, and took possession of the iron, intending only to seize it against an alleged debt. He postponed payment until the fair ended and then revealed his intention. Since the fair had passed, the seller could no longer find buyers and could not easily transport the heavy merchandise elsewhere. Panim Meirot rules that this is direct actionable damage. He compares it to an employer who causes workers to lose the opportunity to hire themselves elsewhere, who must pay them as idle workers. Here too, once the buyers had departed because of this purchaser's conduct, the loss was sufficiently direct to require payment of the seller's expenses. It is no defense that his intention was to protect his own claim, because liability under dina de-garmi applies even without intent to damage, and he should have disclosed his position immediately rather than delay until after the fair. Shevut Yaakov II, siman 158, questions an implication of that responsum, namely that the sale itself was void because the buyer never intended to buy, and argues that the sale remains valid because undisclosed intent is legally ineffective. Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 76, similarly rules that absent an objectively clear indication or prior declaration that he did not intend to buy, his secret intention is only a matter in the heart and the sale stands. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 178, develops the laws of one who keeps another's capital idle and lists seven resulting distinctions. In siman 157 he writes that where a testamentary gift vested immediately and the beneficiaries ordinarily invest such funds at a fixed lawful return, the loss of that established return may be recoverable under Shulchan Aruch, siman 292, seif 7, although Shach inclines to Maharshal that the case is only indirect loss. Where the party already paid the annual loss, however, it should not be recharacterized as principal, especially where the lost return was fixed and readily obtainable. In siman 48 Chatam Sofer discusses charitable principal entrusted to an agent for investment at interest with gentiles. If the agent instead used the money in his own business, the ordinary rule is that one who idles another's money is exempt in beit din. The charitable administrator may, however, accept a voluntary payment if permitted by the relevant laws of interest, but should not continue appointing such a person as agent because the charitable fund ought not depend on his voluntary generosity.
