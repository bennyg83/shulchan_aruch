If he profited with the money, etc. Whether he had permission to use it or not, the case concerns a custodian who revealed his intention that even without permission he was using the funds for himself and intended to keep the profit. This explains Rama's later statement, "or in a place where use is prohibited, where ordinarily, etc." The word "ordinarily" means that if he did not reveal any contrary intent, the presumed rule applies. This is how I would reconcile Rama here with Darkei Moshe, which attributes the law to Rashba. Yet the matter is difficult. I searched and did not find that ruling in Rashba. On the contrary, responsum 938, cited by the Beit Yosef, says that the custodian's conduct proved that he was investing for the owner, because he originally gave the owner all the profit. Once the owner saw that profit was being made and accepted it, he revealed his consent to that arrangement. Therefore even later profit earned without further express instruction belongs to the owner. That case involved investment in circumstances that were initially improper, yet Rashba still required the revealing conduct just described. Without such evidence, he did not say the profit belongs to the owner. See also Mordechai at the end of HaMafkid. It writes that if a custodian lent the deposited money to a gentile on trust, he was negligent. If, when the depositor later demanded his money, the custodian first paid the depositor, the custodian then acquires the interest. If he has not yet paid, even if he says, "I will pay," he does not acquire the interest, analogous to a borrower who receives all the benefit and says, "I will pay," who does not acquire the double payment until he actually pays. See siman 295. It is surprising that Rama omitted these distinctions here.
