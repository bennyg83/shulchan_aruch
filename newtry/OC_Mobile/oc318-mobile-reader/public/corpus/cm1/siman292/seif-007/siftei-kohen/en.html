He is liable even for unavoidable accidents. The Rif writes that the money is deemed a loan in his possession and stands entirely at his risk, so that he is liable if it is lost through accident. Rav Hai Gaon writes likewise. It appears that the Rif means that he is treated as an actual borrower of money, with the legal status of a debtor. The practical consequence is that if the currency becomes invalid, he must pay in currency currently accepted, even if the original coins remain intact. Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 4 of the Laws of Loans, and Tosafot, Bava Kamma 97a, state this. If he were merely an unauthorized borrower, and therefore a robber, he could say, 'Here is your property before you,' as explained there and below at the beginning of siman 363. The ruling therefore teaches that he is not an unauthorized borrower. Since he is permitted to use the money, he is a debtor upon it. Additional distinctions between a borrower and a robber appear below, siman 365, in the Sema, seif katan 3, and elsewhere. Perhaps this is why the Rif needed to cite Rav Hai Gaon. Although the Rosh gives another reason for citing the Gaon, namely that liability continues even after the money was returned to its place, this additional implication may also be correct. Examine carefully.
Even after he returned it, etc. I have already written above that an unauthorized borrower of deposited property remains liable for unavoidable accidents even after returning it to its place. Nevertheless, the Rosh had to distinguish money here, because in this case the custodian used it with permission, since he was permitted to use it. The Rosh states this explicitly.
And if they were deposited with a householder, etc. The Beit Yosef cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that the recipient does not become a paid custodian merely because of the condition, and questions his words. It appears to me that the case is as follows. One deposited money with another on condition that, if the latter supplied him with certain merchandise, he could take the money as his own. He later refused to supply the merchandise and wished to return the money. From that point onward he was forbidden to use it, even though previously the arrangement could have permitted him to use it. Rabbeinu Yerucham derived this from the Rosh in Perek HaZahav concerning the man who gave money for sesame. The Rosh explains that one cannot say he ceased to be a paid custodian only because he told the buyer, 'Take your money,' implying that before that declaration he was a paid custodian merely because he could spend it, although the sale had not been completed. He had no right to use the purchase money before completion of the sale. Rather, the meaning is simply that he was not a paid custodian because he received no compensation. Even according to the Rosh's conclusion in the name of the Rif, that the first version of the incident is rejected and the recipient is liable even for unavoidable accidents, that applies where the money was initially delivered as purchase money for him to spend. Here, however, it was delivered as a deposit, with only a condition that he could appropriate it if he supplied the goods. Once he stated that he would not supply them, he no longer had permission to use the money, even if he did not expressly say, 'Take your money,' and he is not a paid custodian unless compensation was given. Indeed, even if the money was lost before he announced his refusal, he may say that he never intended to supply the goods, so he never acquired permission to use the money. This is compelled by the Rosh and is correct in law. Rabbeinu Yerucham's wording is precise. He writes, 'even if it had been arranged that he could use the money,' in the past tense, indicating that the condition could once have allowed use, but that he presently lacked such permission. One might have thought that the former possibility itself made him a paid custodian; he therefore teaches otherwise. Although the Rosh writes that according to Rabbeinu Tam, who permits merchants to use ordinary deposited money because their principal business is with interest, this ruling has little practical effect, that refers to the particular case there. It still applies where the money was tied and sealed and the parties made this condition. See Responsa Rama, end of siman 80.
