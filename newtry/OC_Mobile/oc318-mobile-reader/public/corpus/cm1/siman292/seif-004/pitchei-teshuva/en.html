Also for unavoidable accidents. See Baer Heitev, who writes that one should not buy merchandise with deposited money lest the depositor suddenly arrive and demand it. See Responsa Galia Masechta, end of siman 13, concerning one who gave money to a wagon driver to buy salt for him in Pinsk, and the money was stolen on the road. He writes that the driver certainly had the status of a paid custodian even if he received no additional compensation for guarding and even if his transport fee had been paid before departure, because a hired person is treated as a paid custodian by virtue of the benefit that the owner chose him rather than another. See what I wrote below in siman 306, seif 1, seif katan 1. In addition, he certainly has the status of a paid custodian because he had permission to use the money. On that basis there is even room to hold him liable for unavoidable accidents. Sema in siman 292, seif katan 16, distinguishes ordinary deposited money from the proceeds of a lost object, explaining in Raavad's name that with a normal deposit the holder fears using the money for merchandise because the depositor may suddenly demand repayment. Here, however, the money was given to the driver specifically so that he should bring salt from Pinsk. The owner cannot demand the coins themselves, only the salt the driver undertook to bring. The driver therefore has no reason to fear using the coins for another transaction or exchange, and it is arguable that he becomes liable even for unavoidable accidents. Maharik, shoresh 131, cited partly by Sema in siman 291, seif katan 31, however, imposes only paid custodian liability, perhaps because the money there was tied and sealed. See there. Shevut Yaakov, volume 3, siman 143, discusses Reuven who sent Shimon, through a non Jewish courier, a tied and sealed bag containing silver coins, with another tied and sealed bundle of gold coins inside, instructing Shimon to buy merchandise for him for compensation. Reuven weighed the outer bag and wrote its weight and instructions to inspect the seal. Shimon acknowledged receipt of the sealed bag, but about a week later claimed that it contained only silver and no gold. Reuven argued that by confirming receipt in accordance with his letter, Shimon must have weighed it and checked the seal immediately. Shimon responded that he did not inspect carefully because the courier was considered reliable. Shimon himself was not especially trustworthy and there were circumstances suggesting he may have misappropriated the money. Shevut Yaakov rules that no inference from those circumstances is needed. Shimon was a paid custodian because he received compensation, and there could be no greater negligence than failing to check the stated weight and seal after being expressly instructed to do so. Even an unpaid custodian would be liable, because immediate notice might have enabled recovery from the courier. Moreover, even without separate compensation for guarding, he became a paid custodian once he opened the bag and had the right to use the money until the merchandise came into his possession, as stated in siman 292, and Sema explains that nowadays everyone is treated like a money changer for this purpose. See there, and what I wrote above in siman 15, seif 5, seif katan 9. Baer Heitev further cites Shach, seif katan 9, concerning the practical difference where the currency is invalidated. Shach cites Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 4 of Hilchot Malveh, and Tosafos, Bava Kamma 97. Netivot HaMishpat already notes that Tosafos there appears to say the opposite and attempts to reconcile Shach. See also Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 50, and Noda BiYehuda, siman 35.
