He wishes to commit shelichut yad against the deposit. See Shach, seif katan 4, who writes that Scripture created agency for a transgression in shelichut yad only to impose liability on the principal, not to exempt the agent. These are essentially the words of Perishah, but I find them very difficult. Shelichut yad becomes complete when one lifts with intent to take for oneself, as Bava Metzia 41 teaches. Since Scripture makes the principal liable through the agent's shelichut yad, it must be that the principal acquires the stolen status through the agent's lifting. Otherwise why would the principal be liable, since shelichut yad requires an acquisition act? Indeed, if a later physical change occurs in the stolen object, the principal will certainly acquire through that change, because it is his act of robbery. Thus the agent's lifting creates the robbery on behalf of the principal. If so, what acquisition remains for the agent? Why should the agent be personally liable as robber when his lifting legally acquired the item for the principal? This resembles me'ilah. Where an agent acts under the principal's instruction in the special case where agency applies to the transgression, the agent does not commit me'ilah merely because he physically ate and benefited after the item was already transferred through his act to the principal. Once the principal acquired it and it left hekdesh, the agent consumes the principal's property rather than hekdesh. So too here. This is not comparable to one who says, "Steal for me." In ordinary theft there is no agency for a transgression, so the principal does not acquire through the agent. The agent's own physical act therefore creates his own robbery liability even if he intended the benefit for the principal. In theft, unlike an ordinary find, one can become a robber even without intending personal acquisition; taking solely to damage another can create robbery. But in shelichut yad, where Scripture expressly makes the principal acquire through the agent, there should be no independent acquisition left for the agent. Therefore Sema's case should be understood where the principal instructed the agent not merely to lift but to consume the object. The principal acquires it immediately through the agent's lifting, as Tosafot in Kiddushin 43 explain. The agent then eats property that legally belongs to the principal. Nevertheless, the agent can still be liable to the original owner in the same manner as one who consumes stolen property before the owner's despair. The owner may collect from either the robber or the later consumer. Thus, even though there is no ordinary agency for the later act of consumption, the agent is no worse than any third party who eats stolen property before despair. Sema's further statement, however, that if the agent has means the owner collects only from him and not from the principal, remains difficult. Since the principal acquired through the agent's lifting and became the robber, the owner should be able to collect from either one. As for Sema's reference to an agent who is not legally liable, such as a minor, I explained in siman 182 that although a minor lacks agency for creating ordinary legal transactions, the principal can still incur liability through use of a person not subject to personal liability where Torah assigns responsibility to the one who caused the act. That issue is distinct from whether the minor can create a valid acquisition for himself.
