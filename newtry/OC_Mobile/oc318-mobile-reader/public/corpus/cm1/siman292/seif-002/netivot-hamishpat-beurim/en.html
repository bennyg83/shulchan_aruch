Wine can sour if the barrel is no longer full, so the remainder supports the part taken. Sema cites Maggid Mishneh's question. In the Gemara, the unresolved question concerning a purse of dinars appears only according to the opinion that shelichut yad requires actual deficiency. Maggid Mishneh answers that the Gemara's rejection means the issue is not really dependent on that dispute. Rambam can be explained more directly. Even according to the opinion that shelichut yad does not require deficiency, one who commits shelichut yad against only part of a deposit does not automatically become a robber of the whole. If one deposits one hundred sheep and the custodian selects one for unauthorized use, even if he taps all of them with a stick so that they run while he chooses, he certainly becomes liable only for the particular sheep he takes. Separate bodies are like separate deposits. Rambam therefore emphasizes that liability for the whole depends on a single physical body. The Gemara asks about a purse of dinars: is guarding one dinar meaningfully different from guarding the purse as a whole, so that by putting them together in one purse the owner shows insistence on their remaining as one guarded unit and they are treated as one body? Or does the owner ordinarily have no objection to division among different places, so they remain like separate deposits? A true supporting basis exists principally in a barrel of wine, where the amount left in the barrel protects the remainder from souring. A barrel of oil is one physical body because the contents are mixed, but it need not have that special supporting relationship. Maggid Mishneh's own explanation can be developed as follows. Shitah Mekubetzet and Mahara Aharon Sasson explain that ordinary robbery is taking for oneself without payment, while shelichut yad is taking for one's own use with intent to pay. The dispute whether shelichut yad requires deficiency can apply even where he intends to take the whole for himself with payment. If he takes only part, he does not become a robber of the whole unless the remainder serves as a supporting basis for the part taken. Thus the Mishnah's barrel case, where shelichut yad in a portion can impose liability for the whole, is conceptually separate from the dispute whether deficiency is required. For a barrel of wine, taking a portion while leaving the rest can be treated as use of the whole because the remainder functions as the base preserving the taken portion's context. This can make the entire barrel subject to liability even according to the opinion requiring deficiency. For a purse of coins, Rav Ashi asks whether guarding the purse differs from guarding each coin in a sufficiently unitary manner to create the same result. To understand the halachic outcome, distinguish several cases. According to the opinion that deficiency is required, if one lifts an animal or barrel intending to take the whole and pay for it but then returns it without actual use, one can say he retracted and no shelichut yad occurred. The same can be true if he intended to take only one quarter and then returned it before using it. But with wine, where the remainder serves as a basis for the part he intended to take, lifting for partial use can count as using the whole and the return no longer proves complete retraction in the same way. For coins, the matter remains doubtful whether the purse creates that unified relation. According to the opinion that deficiency is not required, where the custodian uses the whole animal he commits shelichut yad even without diminishing it. With a barrel of oil or with coins, if partial use does not legally count as use of the whole, he becomes liable only for the part actually used. Where the object functions as one supporting unit, he can become liable for the whole. This explains the Gemara's inference from the animal that deficiency is not required. The animal case is one of actual use of the whole body, so the dispute applies directly. Rambam's practical rule is therefore: use of an entire animal can constitute shelichut yad without deficiency; a barrel of wine can likewise be treated as one supported body; a barrel of oil or purse of coins can remain doubtful whether partial use counts as use of the whole. There is still a question. If he actually takes one quarter from the barrel, why should he ever become liable for the entire remainder when an armed robber later causes an accident? The answer is based on another principle. The Gemara compares deposited money that the custodian is permitted to use. According to Rav Nachman, he is exempt from accidents until he actually spends some of it. Kiddushin 47 implies that once he spends even a perutah, the entire deposit can be converted into a loan framework. Likewise, once a custodian actually takes part of a deposit for his own use with intent to pay, that act can reveal that he is holding the entire deposit under a general permission-to-use framework, as though the depositor himself had authorized use. The custodian's own appropriation is no weaker as evidence of intended use than express permission from the depositor. That is why actual taking or expenditure matters. Until some amount is actually removed, the custodian may merely have contemplated a temporary one-time use. Once he diminishes the deposit through use, the conduct reveals an intention to hold the whole in a continuing use framework. This does not apply where he takes a part with intent to steal it outright and never pay for it. Such theft of one part gives no reason to infer that he plans to take the rest. Raavad is correct that in such a case he does not become a robber of the whole. This also answers Tosafot's question why, if deficiency is not required, the rule should not simply be learned from a borrower. The larger liability for the remainder after partial taking depends on this special evidentiary mechanism, which arises only after actual use or depletion. Even where the depositor expressly authorizes use, accident liability does not arise under Rav Nachman until the custodian actually spends. It cannot therefore be derived from an ordinary borrower before that event. Tosafot's question from a borrower concerns only the case where the entire object is used as one body. There they ask why a verse is needed to teach that deficiency is unnecessary. Their answer can now be understood. An unauthorized borrower does not necessarily count as having already completed the relevant use merely because he took possession. The case of placing staff and bag upon an animal shows a form of use, yet the view requiring deficiency still exists. One cannot answer that this is not a borrower because the user does not receive every benefit while the animal continues grazing, for then even after deficiency ordinary borrower-law could not supply the derivation. Rather, once actual deficiency occurs there is no need to learn from borrower at all. The act itself shows that the custodian is holding the deposit for his own repeated use, and through that unauthorized appropriation he becomes liable. There remains another question: shelichut yad makes him a robber who pays according to the value at the time of taking, while a borrower pays according to the time of the accident, and the rules of return also differ. This is included in the Gemara's statement that one should not say, "It is enough that shelichut yad be like a borrower and therefore exempt when the owner is with him." The verse teaches that shelichut yad is liable even under shemirah b'baalim, showing that its final legal status is robbery, not ordinary borrowing. The same distinction therefore governs the other differences between robber and borrower. The fact that an ordinary unrelated person does not incur shelichut yad liability is also explained. A depositary who partially takes with intent to pay reveals that he is holding the remaining deposit under a continuing use-right he has arrogated to himself. That evidentiary structure arises from his prior custodial possession. An outsider who simply takes part has no such relationship to the remaining property. The practical result is: If a custodian intends partial shelichut yad, lifts the object, but takes nothing from it, he does not thereby become a robber of the whole except in a barrel of wine where the remainder functions as a supporting basis; with a purse of coins the matter is doubtful. If he actually takes part with intent to pay, he can become liable for the whole because his act reveals an intention to treat the entire deposit as available for his use. If he takes part intending outright theft and no payment, he is a robber only of the part taken, not of the whole.
