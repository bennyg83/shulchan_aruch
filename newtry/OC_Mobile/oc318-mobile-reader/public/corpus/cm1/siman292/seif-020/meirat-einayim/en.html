For he certainly deposited it with him on this understanding. This resembles deposited money left unsealed with a money changer or shopkeeper, as discussed above in seif 7. It follows that if the depositor wrapped and sealed the book with an unusual knot, thereby revealing that he objects to its being read, we know he certainly does not want the custodian to copy from it. Reading from it is then prohibited and counts as unauthorized use, all the more so copying from it. Mordechai at the end of HaMafkid, cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, writes that a Torah scholar may copy and study from such a book, comparing it to unsealed money deposited with a shopkeeper. It then suggests another possible reason from the Midrash on Mishlei, "They will not despise a thief if he steals," teaching that one should not disgrace a person who steals words of Torah and copies them. According to that rationale, it might be permitted even where we know the owner objects. Rama, however, omits that reason and instead writes that the owner certainly deposited it on this understanding, which implies that if we know he objects, it is prohibited. Mordechai itself offered the Midrash only as a possibility. The Ir Shushan mixes these reasons together. He first copies Rama's rationale, then discusses compelling a person to lend books, and finally cites the Midrash and adds that the copier must pay for deterioration caused by his study and copying. Read this way, his own formulations conflict. The matter requires study.
