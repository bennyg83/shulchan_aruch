He rolls it once every twelve months. So rule the Rambam, Semag, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. Although regarding a found scroll they require rolling every thirty days, and the Mechaber so rules above, siman 267, seif 4, a deposit differs because it was entrusted knowingly, and the owner did not require more frequent rolling. The Tur, however, requires rolling every thirty days here as well. The Mechaber follows his view in the Beit Yosef that the Rif and Rosh agree with the Rambam, but their words do not prove this, as the Bach notes. Rashi likewise treats a deposit like a found item and understands the Mishna as Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov's view. This appears primary, since we follow an anonymous Mishna and Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, whose teaching is considered exceptionally reliable. The Bach rules accordingly, and this also appears to be the Maadanei Melech's view. The Sema's wording, 'if he rolls it within thirty days,' follows the Tur mechanically; according to the Mechaber it should say, 'within twelve months.'
For he certainly deposited it with him on that understanding. The Sema criticizes the Ir Shushan and understands the Rama to disagree with the Mordechai. In my opinion, the Ir Shushan correctly understood his teacher, the Rama, and careful reading shows that the Mordechai and Rama agree. The Mordechai writes that one who deposits a Torah scroll with another may have it rolled every twelve months, but the custodian may not initially read from it. Rav Yehuda Gaon explains that just as he may not read from it, he may not copy even one letter without permission, because this wears the deposit. This applies to an ignorant person, but a scholar may read and copy it, even initially and without permission, when he lacks another copy, because the depositor knew he was a scholar and entrusted it on the assumption that he would study from it. The Mordechai then adds that perhaps the reason is the Midrash on 'They do not despise a thief if he steals to satisfy his soul when hungry,' meaning that one should not despise one who steals words of Torah by copying them. The Tosefta likewise praises one who secretly learns from another scholar. These are not competing explanations. The first explains why the scholar may use the scroll despite the slight wear, because the depositor accepted that use. The second explains why he may copy it even though the owner presumably objects to copying. Thus both reasons are necessary. The Rama first permits reading because the deposit was made on that understanding, and then rules that a beit din may compel a person to lend his books, which includes permitting copying despite objection, based on the principle that one should not despise a thief who steals words of Torah. This is also the Ir Shushan's explanation, and it is correct.
