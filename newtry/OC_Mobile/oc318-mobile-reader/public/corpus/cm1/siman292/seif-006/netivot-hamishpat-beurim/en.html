For use of its place. See Shach, seif katan 7. There is a textual error there, and the text should read: "See Sema, seif katan 12. It appears to me that this is a textual error and should read, for its own need, and so Rashi explains." His meaning is that the Sema's reference to seif katan 12 was itself mistakenly attached to the words "for use of its place" and should instead be attached to "for its own need."
The accident occurred because of the change of place. See Sema, seif katan 13, who writes that this applies even where the second place is more secure than the first. In my view this is difficult. Even a custodian who improperly transfers property to another custodian, although he certainly may not do so because the owner does not want his deposit in another person's hands, is nevertheless not regarded as negligent merely for that act, as Tosafos explain in Bava Metzia 37. There is likewise no reason to distinguish, in the case of a custodian who transfers to another person whom the owner regularly trusts, between whether a location had been specifically designated to the first custodian and whether it had not. This resembles one who rented an animal to travel in one place and went to another equivalent place, or one who lent the rented item to another, where he is exempt if witnesses establish that an unavoidable accident occurred. Only where he was hired to take the animal through the mountain and instead took it through the valley is he liable. In locations of equal risk he is certainly exempt. Nor can one impose liability through the principle that the matter began with negligence merely because moving the item made theft easier, for once the negligent condition has ceased he is not liable for a later accident, as explained below. Furthermore, according to the Sema's interpretation the phrase "liable for negligence" is inappropriate, because the very act of moving it would itself be the negligence. The ruling should then have said that he is liable for accidents caused by changing the location because the matter began with negligence. It therefore appears to me that "liable for negligence" means that he was negligent in choosing the new location with respect to some particular danger, such as reducing the level of protection against fire. The new location might still be generally guarded, so that an ordinary custodian would not be liable merely for choosing it, but where the owner specifically designated the first location it is as though he stipulated that the property remain in the place specially guarded against fire. This is comparable to money where the owner expressly stipulated that it be kept in the ground, which creates negligence even under our present standards if the condition is not followed. Once his relocation constitutes negligence with respect to one danger, he can be liable even for another accident if that accident resulted from the negligence. Without such a factor, however, it appears that he is exempt when the second place is more secure, for the custodian himself is certainly no worse than one who transfers the deposit to another person whom the owner regularly trusts.
He is exempt once he put it down. At first glance this is surprising, for he should seemingly be exempt even if the object broke while still in his hand, since when no specific location was designated he has permission to hold it in his hand as well. Perhaps the intent concerns breakage: if it broke while in his hand, one may attribute the breakage to the burden of carrying it and to the movement itself. Once he puts it down, however, he is exempt. A matter that began with negligence creates liability for a later accident only where the negligent condition still exists when the accident occurs, as in the case of flimsy huts exposed to fire. Here, once he put the object down, the negligence associated with possible breakage from carrying it had already ended.
