(Or in a place where it is prohibited.) The Sema was troubled because he did not find a source for this principle. I found it in Maharam, responsum 600, where two authorities dispute the matter. Rabbi Eliezer son of Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Moshe HaKohen hold that a person is not presumed to make another wicked, and therefore the depositor certainly intended only that the custodian should earn a profit for the owner of the money. Raavyah holds that although the custodian sins, nevertheless, regarding a deposit where the owner suffers no loss or deprivation, the owner has only a grievance against him. The Rama ruled in accordance with the first view. It appears to me that his ruling is correct, for we find the same principle in the Ran in the second chapter of Kiddushin, cited by the Rama at the beginning of siman 183. Even where there is merely concern that people may regard him as dishonest, we presume that he will act permissibly unless he revealed his intention before witnesses. All the more so here, where an absolute prohibition is involved. Regarding the statement that the law favors the depositor, the Sema raises a difficulty from siman 81 and the Rosh's responsum there, which rules that if one says, "I will give you," this is merely words. Why then does the present ruling obligate him to give the depositor the profit earned from that point onward? The Sema answers that siman 81 concerns a case in which the time for payment had not arrived, or alternatively the creditor had not demanded his money. I am astonished, for if these distinctions are relevant, why did the Rosh not investigate in that responsum when the stipulation to give the profit was made or whether the creditor had demanded his money? It therefore appears to me that one must distinguish between a debt and a deposit. A debt is given for expenditure. Even after the payment date arrives, the debtor commits no prohibition merely by withholding it. With a deposit, however, even where the custodian is permitted to use the money, as soon as the depositor demands his deposit and states that he wishes to earn with it, while the custodian withholds it, he prevents the owner from using his capital. Although the Mishna teaches that one who prevents another from using his capital is subject only to a grievance, that is where the withholder himself earns no profit. Where he earns a profit, he is liable for causing the loss. Mordechai states this explicitly in the chapter HaMafkid, calling it prevention of the owner's use of his capital. This principle does not apply to a debt. From the day of demand onward, the money reverts to the status of a deposit, and the custodian may not use it. If he does use it, he is a robber, as with any other deposited property. A debt differs, because although the debtor is obligated to pay when the due date arrives, if he delays payment and earns with the money he is not termed wicked, provided he remains prepared to pay later. It therefore appears to me that the principal halachah is that regarding a debt, even when the due date has arrived and the debtor says, "I will give you profits," he may later retract unless he executed a document, made a formal kinyan, or stipulated at the time the money was originally given that if payment were delayed he would give profits. Only then is the undertaking effective. Deposited money is different, even where the custodian is permitted to use it.
