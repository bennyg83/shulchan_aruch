Or through his agent. Scripture derives liability for a custodian's unauthorized use performed through another person from the phrase "for every matter of trespass." Sema suggested that this is needed where the agent did not know the object belonged to someone else, where the agent was not legally liable, or where he had no money to pay. Otherwise, under the ordinary rule that there is no agency for transgression, the agent himself should bear liability. Shach defended Sema, but the issue is deeper. Rambam writes that throughout the Torah there is no agency for transgression except for misuse of consecrated property. Yet the Gemara also lists custodial misappropriation and slaughter or sale of stolen animals as special cases. Maharit explains that in those cases the Torah did not necessarily create ordinary agency. Rather, the principal becomes liable because the relevant statutory wrong occurred through his direction. In slaughter or sale, the thief receives the benefit of the act. In custodial misappropriation, the custodian's duty itself was breached. The Torah's phrase "for every matter of trespass" means that the custodian is liable for his own failure of custody even where the act was carried out through another. It is therefore closer to statutory liability for his breach than to the rule that the agent becomes legally identical with him. This approach also explains why the agent can remain liable if the agent himself is legally capable of the wrongful act. The special verse need not exempt the agent. It adds liability to the directing custodian. Sema's categories can still identify practical cases where the principal's liability matters most, such as where the agent is a minor, a person lacking liability, or insolvent. Misuse of consecrated property can likewise occur through a minor because the offense depends upon the transfer or use, not on the minor having ordinary agency capacity. I nevertheless question Sema's premise that the ordinary agent is necessarily liable under the special law of custodial misappropriation. That law belongs to custodians, and the agent is not himself the custodian. He may be liable as an ordinary thief or robber if his conduct meets those rules, but the custodian's special liability is distinct. This distinction also resolves a question concerning a principal who tells an agent to slaughter a stolen animal on Shabbat. Mishneh LaMelech argued that the additional Shabbat prohibition should break the special agency. According to Maharit, however, liability for slaughter and sale does not depend on ordinary agency at all. It follows from the thief's statutory liability for the benefit produced through the slaughter. Therefore the added Shabbat prohibition does not necessarily eliminate his payment liability, unlike misuse of consecrated property, which Rambam treats as genuine agency.
Sema's formulation still leaves a difficulty. The agent in a case of custodial misappropriation is ordinarily not himself subject to the custodian's special law because he was never a custodian. The very term "misappropriation" in this chapter refers to the special custodial rule, not merely to ordinary theft. Accordingly, the agent is often exempt from that special liability without needing the theories that he was unaware, legally incapable, or insolvent. The principal custodian remains liable because Scripture imposed liability upon him for the custodial breach carried out through another. If the agent's conduct independently constitutes ordinary theft or robbery, he may of course be liable under those laws. In such a case the special rule remains significant where the agent cannot pay. The same distinction supports Maharit's explanation of slaughter and sale. Rambam's statement that true agency for transgression exists only in misuse of consecrated property does not conflict with the thief's liability for slaughter done by another. That liability follows from the statutory fact that the thief caused the prohibited slaughter and received its consequence, not from treating the agent as his legal hand. Therefore, even if the thief told the agent to slaughter on Shabbat, the additional transgression does not necessarily eliminate the thief's payment liability for slaughter and sale. The special rule is not ordinary agency that collapses when another prohibition is introduced. This is different from misuse of consecrated property, where Rambam actually relies on agency.
