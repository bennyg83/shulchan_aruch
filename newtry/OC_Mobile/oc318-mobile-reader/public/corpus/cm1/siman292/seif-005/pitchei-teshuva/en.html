See Responsa Panim Meirot, volume 1, siman 82, concerning a merchant who came from afar to a fair with iron to sell on market day. A townsman who had a claim against him approached deceptively as though he were an ordinary buyer, agreed on a price, and took possession of the iron. The seller relied upon him to pay after the fair. When market day had passed, the buyer admitted that he never intended to purchase and had acted only to seize approximately fifty gold coins against his own claim, offering to return the balance. The seller protested that after the fair he could no longer sell the heavy merchandise, while many buyers had been present during the fair. Panim Meirot rules that this is direct actionable damage. He proves from the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia, chapter HaOmanin, cited in siman 333, seif 2, that even where workers have not yet begun work, if employers have already hired the available workers and the dismissed workers can no longer find employment, they must be paid as idle workers. Here too, because the iron buyers had already left and this person's conduct prevented the seller from finding others, he is treated as a direct damager and must reimburse the seller's expenses. Although his intention was not to injure but to secure his own claim, siman 66, seif 23, with Sema and Shach, establishes that one liable under dina degarmi pays even without intent to damage. Moreover, he should have informed the seller immediately rather than delaying him until after the fair. Panim Meirot therefore writes that this conduct was improper and that the buyer should be penalized and made to pay the seller's full loss. A similar support appears in Radbaz, volume 2, siman 299, cited above in siman 176, seif 14, seif katan 20. Shevut Yaakov, volume 2, siman 158, cites Panim Meirot and questions the implication that, apart from damages, the sale itself is void because the buyer secretly intended only to seize against a debt. That is incorrect. Even in such circumstances the acquisition is valid and the goods are in the buyer's domain, as shown by Ketubot 110, cited above in siman 85, seif 1, and Mordechai at the end of Ketubot, siman 278, cited in siman 190, seif 6, and siman 204, seif 11. It appears, however, that Panim Meirot would agree, and that his case involved a buyer who repeatedly demanded payment of the purchase money, in which case acquisition had not become final under siman 190, seif 17; his point was only that damages were nevertheless owed. Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 76, addresses a similar case and rules for the seller. Secret intention not to buy is merely an unexpressed thought and has no legal effect. There was no compelling external indication otherwise, because the supposed buyer may well have intended a genuine purchase while planning to retain the purchase money against his debt. He discusses Panim Meirot at length and concludes that unless there was some outward disclosure, such as a prior declaration before others that he did not intend to buy, the sale is valid. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 178, discusses extensively the laws of preventing another's money from earning profit and concludes with seven practical distinctions. See also siman 157 there, concerning a deathbed gift where the recipients were to acquire immediately and were expressly to receive any appreciation before payment. Since the charitable society acquired its five hundred gold coins at the instant of death, and its normal practice was to invest funds at permitted interest or in government paper earning a fixed return, the lost profit was ascertainable. Shulchan Aruch, siman 292, seif 7, would therefore require compensation, although Shach inclines to Maharshal that such loss is only indirect. Where the eldest son had already paid the society its lost annual profits, Chatam Sofer refuses to recharacterize those payments as principal merely in order to treat the actual lost profit as nonrecoverable, especially where the loss was objectively fixed by lawful investment opportunities. In siman 48 he discusses a charitable endowment for Torah scholars whose money was entrusted to an agent to lend at interest to non Jews for a fixed commission. The agent negligently failed to lend it and instead traded with it himself. He notes the general Gemara rule that one who prevents another's money from earning profit is exempt, as Shach cites several times, though the parties may stipulate otherwise. He adds that the charity treasurer should not reappoint such an agent, because doing so would make support of the poor depend on the agent's voluntary payment rather than on the donor's intended arrangement.
