Seif 1. Note: See the Ritva to Bava Metzia, chapter 3, on the mishna of one who rented a cow and then lent it to another. He writes that the renter is not liable for the borrower's unauthorized use because there is no agency for wrongdoing. This is difficult, because with misuse of entrusted property there is agency even for wrongdoing. I discussed the resolution, with God's help, in the addenda to Nidrei Zerizin, part 2, and in Shiyurei Taharah, page 105. There: A custodian may not make unauthorized use of a deposit. I was asked by the community of Skalat about a man traveling to Lvov to purchase merchandise for himself. Others gave him money to purchase merchandise for them as well, without paying him any fee. The question was whether, when the money was stolen from him on the road, he had the status of a paid custodian. The questioner suggested that he is a paid custodian under the rule concerning a money changer in siman 292, especially since today people generally use entrusted money. That is a sound point. Although he is an agent to buy merchandise for them, if another business opportunity arises he could use the funds to buy and resell in Lvov and later replace the money. Furthermore, it appears that he receives a benefit from purchasing for others, since sellers may give him gifts for bringing larger purchases, as merchants commonly do. Even if they do not, his own merchandise may be sold to him more cheaply because his combined purchase is larger. For this potential benefit he is a paid custodian, just as a finder of lost property is treated as a paid custodian because he might encounter a poor person and be exempt from giving charity while occupied with the lost property. However, if the entrusted money consisted of banknotes rather than coin, he is exempt. Banknotes have the status of documents and are excluded from the laws of custodial liability. I wrote this in my youth regarding interest, heard the same from several great authorities, and found it in the siddur of the Gaon of Lissa concerning redemption of the firstborn, where he writes that banknotes cannot be used because they are documents. Even if, under the law of the kingdom, banknotes are treated as currency, so that for some monetary purposes we follow that law and he could be liable for negligence, he would nevertheless be exempt for theft or loss. If we follow Torah law, the notes are documents. If we follow the law of the kingdom, he has no right to use them and would not become a paid custodian on that basis. Even given the other incidental benefit, we should not impose liability where the very obligation would arise only through the law of the kingdom. Nor can one argue that under the law of the kingdom every custodian is liable even for theft and loss, because we do not use dina de-malkhuta to impose stricter interpersonal liability except in taxes and similar sovereign matters. With currency itself it may define what counts as money, but not otherwise. Therefore, if these were banknotes, he is certainly exempt.
