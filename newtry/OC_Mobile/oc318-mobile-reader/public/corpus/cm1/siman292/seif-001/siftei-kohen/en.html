And if he returned it, etc. This ruling is astonishing. In Perek HaMafkid, Bava Metzia 41a, Rav Sheshet explicitly explains the Mishna as a case where the custodian moved the barrel to use it as a platform for chicks. Even if he returns it to its place, he remains liable for accidents, because an unauthorized borrower is treated as a robber and, according to Rabbi Akiva, return requires the owner's knowledge. Rabbi Yochanan also establishes the Mishna according to Rabbi Akiva. The Rif and Rosh rule so explicitly, as do the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber below, seif 6. The Rama derived his ruling here from the Mordechai, but that derivation is incorrect. The Mordechai writes that where a custodian borrowed an article from the deposited property and later returned it to its former place, he is no longer treated as a borrower but as an ordinary custodian, because the custodian's knowledge is equivalent to the owner's. Even Rabbi Akiva, who says that the original guarding ended, says so only of a thief, whom the owner no longer trusts, but not of a borrower. The Rama understood this as borrowing without the owner's knowledge. That cannot be correct, for Rabbi Akiva would then certainly treat him as a thief. Moreover, the Mordechai says that he 'borrowed' the article, implying that he borrowed it with the owner's consent. Its point is that after a permitted borrowing, he need not return it to the owner personally, as an ordinary borrower must, because his original custodianship was never terminated. The Sema and Bach explain the Mordechai as referring to articles that people customarily lend and whose owner does not object to their use without permission. Even on that reading, the Rama's ruling is difficult, because he calls the custodian a robber. In truth, the Mordechai plainly concerns borrowing with permission. The ruling suggested by the Bach and Sema regarding articles customarily lent is itself uncertain, because the person remains an unauthorized borrower and may not use the item whenever he pleases. Shiltei Giborim also struggled with the Mordechai, but his explanation contradicts Rav Sheshet and the explicit rulings of the Rif and Rosh. Nor can one say that Rabbi Yochanan disagrees with Rav Sheshet on the law, for the Gemara shows that he accepts the legal principle and differs only in explaining the wording of the Mishna. The Ir Shushan later strained to limit seif 6 to one who intended to diminish the item, in order to avoid contradiction, but the Gemara and authorities explicitly apply it even without such intent. The Sema correctly rejects that limitation there. It is therefore surprising that the Sema does not reject the Rama's ruling here more directly. Until a clear resolution is found, we have no authority to rule like the Rama against the Gemara and all the authorities.
