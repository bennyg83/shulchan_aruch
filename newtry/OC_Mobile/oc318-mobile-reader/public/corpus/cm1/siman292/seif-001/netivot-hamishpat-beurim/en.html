Only from the time of use. Nimukei Yosef explains the Mishnah concerning the barrel differently, holding that in a case of full unauthorized borrowing the person can become like a robber even before actual use. Therefore Mechaber's present rule concerns a type of use that does not diminish the object and to which the owner ordinarily does not object, as Sema explains. In such a case the person does not become a full robber merely by taking possession. To create the accident liability of an unauthorized borrower, actual commencement of use is required.
Only because he is an unauthorized borrower, who is like a robber. This applies specifically where no particular place was designated for the deposit. If the owner designated a place and the custodian uses the object while leaving it within that place, it appears that he does not become a borrower at all. Ramban, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet on Bava Metzia 41, asks why the case of placing one's staff and bag on another's animal should not simply create liability as unauthorized borrowing. He answers that a borrower is one who takes the item fully out of the owner's domain during the borrowing. Here the animal remains grazing with the herd in the very place the owner intended it to be. Ran similarly says part of the animal serves the owner by remaining with the flock and part serves the shepherd. This resembles taking only a partial use-right, and ordinary unauthorized borrowing does not extend automatically to the whole object. Shelichut yad is a special Torah category. Nimukei Yosef discusses a barrel where a place had been designated and the custodian used it there. He writes that according to the rule that the owner's guarding has ended, the owner no longer receives any benefit from the depositary's guarding, so all benefit of the object is now the user's and he can be treated as an unauthorized borrower. But Rama here rules that the guarding has not ended, because the use is of a type to which owners generally do not object. Accordingly, where the owner designated a place and the custodian uses the item there in a non-diminishing manner to which the owner ordinarily does not object, the authorities cited above support exemption even during the use. The person has not taken the item fully out of the owner's domain. Rashi may disagree and impose borrower liability even for use within the designated place. But the primary ruling follows Rashba, Ran, Ramban, and Nimukei Yosef, who are the majority.
And if he returned it. See Shach, seif katan 1, who questions Sema and holds that even with an item whose owner ordinarily does not object to such use, returning it to its place does not restore the original guarding. In my view Sema is correct. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 30 write concerning silver utensils that one may use them with cold substances even for one's own benefit where such use cannot cause damage. Shitah Mekubetzet explains that the same applies to a deposit. The Gemara's question, "What is a deposit doing with him?" means only that where the owner is present the custodian should inform him before using it. This proves that such use does not make the custodian a robber. At most, one should not use without informing the owner when the owner is available. That impropriety is not enough to say the prior guarding relationship has ended completely. While he is actually using the object, however, he can have the liability of a borrower. An unauthorized borrower is no lighter than one who borrowed with permission, and even where the owner generally does not object to the use, one who takes the entire benefit during the use can be liable for accidents. Shach compares this to deposited money that the custodian is permitted to use. In my view the comparison is incorrect. If ordinary utensils permitted for limited use were governed like deposited money, then any such permission to use would itself create accident liability even before use, just as some hold regarding money. Tosafot clearly do not say this. Money is different because spending it creates a complete proprietary conversion. The custodian may replace it with other coins, and money is by nature given for expenditure. Utensils are ordinarily intended to remain the same physical objects. Limited use that does not damage them does not show that the owner transferred any proprietary interest in them. Therefore mere meshichah does not make the custodian a robber or buyer where the taking was without the owner's knowledge and the contemplated use is one owners ordinarily tolerate. But once actual use begins, he can become liable as an unauthorized borrower, because even an authorized borrower can become liable through commencement of use without a separate meshichah, since his full benefit creates the obligation described by Torah. This also explains Mechaber's wording that he is "like a robber," using a comparison rather than saying he is literally a robber. The act is prohibited without the owner's knowledge, but it does not create full robbery where the owner ordinarily does not object to the use. There is some support from the Gemara's question concerning the shepherd who placed his staff and bag on the animal. Rashi explains the question against R. Yishmael. Why not establish it according to R. Akiva? The answer is that even R. Akiva can agree that with an unauthorized use to which the owner ordinarily does not object, return to the original place restores the prior status. A staff and bag are the sort of use the owner does not ordinarily object to, as Shitah Mekubetzet explains.
