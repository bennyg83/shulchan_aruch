He may use them. One person sent her sister two gold coins from a country where they were inexpensive to a country where they were worth more. It appears that this is not included in the rule that deposited money may be used, thereby making the custodian a paid custodian, because there is a particular objection to exchanging such coins in the more expensive country, where their value is not fixed. It would also be difficult for the messenger to spend them and then replace them immediately with identical coins. Even if one sometimes permits substitution where the custodian intends later to replace the money, here the sender and recipient may specifically object. In a case where the loss resulted only from concealment or from travel circumstances, and the messenger could not state with certainty that there had been necessity, I arranged a compromise. Similarly, one who possessed a five-reinish banknote and bought meat for half a reinish, temporarily handing over the large note because he did not want to break it and have the money dispersed, may have manifested an insistence that the note remain intact. It would then be like sealed money, so the custodian could not break it and spend it merely intending to replace it, since the depositor himself could readily have exchanged it for another such note. If the depositor might arrive before replacement is made, there is a clear concern for his objection. Even if the custodian has identical notes at another house, the depositor may object to waiting while he retrieves them. Permission to take an item held for sale exists only where an immediately available equivalent of better quality is present and is allocated to the depositor, and in that case there is no benefit to the custodian that would create paid-custodian status. If one mistakenly pays another more than he owes and the recipient also fails to notice, and the mistake is discovered only after the money is lost, the recipient bears no custodial liability, not even as an unpaid custodian, because he accepted it in error and would never have agreed to guard it had he known. If he knew of the overpayment but retained it because of a possible old debt, it is doubtful whether he becomes a custodian, because the payer did not deliver it for safekeeping. Perhaps, however, since the payer would immediately have demanded its return and at least required him to guard it, he becomes an unpaid custodian, or even a paid custodian because the money is available for use. If he retained it because of a doubtful old debt, the benefit of holding it may also create paid-custodian status. If there was no such claim and he merely hesitated out of temptation whether to return it, then if at the time of taking he definitely resolved not to return it he would become liable even for unavoidable accidents. Where he retains it only because of a genuine doubt, he may not be liable for accidents, though there may still be unpaid or paid custodial liability. If the owner demands it and he evades payment merely because of temptation, a greater custodial obligation may arise.
