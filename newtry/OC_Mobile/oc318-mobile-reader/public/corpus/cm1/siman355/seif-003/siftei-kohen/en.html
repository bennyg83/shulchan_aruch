And there are witnesses that he stole it, etc. The Sema, following the Perisha, explains that the Rambam and Tur specified witnesses because, had the thief returned the item voluntarily, his repentance would show that the owners were willing for him to resume guarding it. The Bach writes similarly. In my opinion, however, witnesses are mentioned either to exclude a case in which he denies the theft, or to teach the greater novelty in the latter clause, that even when witnesses attest that he returned it he remains liable. In the Rambam, the reference to witnesses plainly serves to establish liability for double payment. In truth, one who admits the theft has the same law as one against whom there are witnesses: he remains responsible until he informs the owners. This is also indicated by the Gemara in HaMafkid, which had to assign the first clause to Rabbi Yishmael and the latter to Rabbi Akiva, rather than establishing both according to Rabbi Akiva and distinguishing between the absence or presence of witnesses. Moreover, the other authorities state without qualification that the owners must know, and do not mention witnesses. The Shiltei Giborim's objection is therefore no objection. Once he stole the item, his original custodianship ended; subsequent repentance does not restore that status, and he must return it into the owners' own possession. I later found the Ma'adanei Yom Tov writing similarly.
