Will documents, etc. The Tur, citing a responsum of the Rosh, explains the rule. A deathbed gift of all the property, or even of part where the donor explicitly acts because of impending death, requires no kinyan. The oral instruction itself acquires, and the document serves only as evidence. Such a will document can therefore be valid even if produced through a gentile court. But an ordinary partial deathbed gift not made because of anticipated death requires a kinyan like a healthy person's gift. If the transaction is supposed to be made through delivery of the document itself, a gentile court's deed cannot serve as the Jewish law instrument of acquisition. The Rosh adds that in his time neither Jewish nor gentile practice ordinarily used deeds as the actual method of acquisition in sale or gift. A true deed of acquisition would say, “My field is given to you” or “sold to you,” and the purchaser or recipient acquires when the deed is delivered. Instead, people completed transactions through other recognized acquisitions and used the written deed merely as evidence. Therefore even gentile court documents could function evidentially. In siman 191, however, the Mechaber writes that contemporary deeds can be acquisition deeds, following the Ran, because they contain language of sale. For that reason the Mechaber abbreviates here and retains only the rule that will documents produced in gentile courts can be valid, since deathbed words themselves effect the transfer and the document remains evidentiary. The Ir Shushan incorrectly copies the Rosh's historical statement here while elsewhere ruling that contemporary documents effect acquisition.
