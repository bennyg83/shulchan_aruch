He takes one sixteenth, and some explain, etc. I explained the dispute in the Perishah and will summarize it so that the Mechaber's present and following seifim can be understood. In Bava Batra, the Gemara asks about one who says, “Give so and so a share in my property.” Does “share” mean half, as language of division, or does it mean whatever share beit din deems appropriate, perhaps even a minimal amount? Ravina bar Kisa brings a proof from a baraita: if one says, “Give so and so a share in the cistern,” Sumchus says not less than one quarter; if “for a barrel,” not less than one eighth; “for a pot,” not less than one twelfth; “for a drinking vessel,” not less than one sixteenth. The Rambam interprets the cistern as containing wine. The first opinion of the Rambam and Mechaber holds that Ravina resolved the question from the end of the baraita. Although the particular fractions for barrel, pot, and drinking vessel may depend upon fixed practical needs and cannot automatically be generalized, we see at least that the word “share” was never assigned less than one sixteenth. Therefore an unspecified “share of my property” is at least one sixteenth. The second opinion holds that Ravina resolved the question from the opening clause, where an unspecified share in the cistern is one quarter, and therefore an unspecified share of one's property is one quarter. Rama's later statement that some say all these cases receive only a minimal amount applies also to the present case. That is the Rosh's view. The Rosh follows the explanation that Sumchus gives these fractions because he holds that property subject to monetary doubt is divided. The phrase “a share” is uncertain: perhaps it means half, or perhaps only whatever portion beit din sees fit. Sumchus therefore gives one quarter, half of the possible half. We, however, do not rule like Sumchus in general monetary doubt. We follow the rule that the burden of proof is upon the claimant. Therefore the recipient receives only the minimum amount that certainly satisfies the word “share.” Other authorities understand Sumchus differently. They say he did not derive the fractions from doubt at all, but that the Sages knew as a matter of usage that an unspecified “share of a cistern” is one quarter, a share for a barrel is one eighth, and so forth. They therefore treat those specific measures as substantive rules and not as products of Sumchus's general law of doubtful money. Under Rashbam's explanation of Sumchus, the fractions can be reconstructed. If a barrel ordinarily needs half the cistern, an uncertain “share for a barrel” is either half of that half, namely one quarter, or some minimal amount, so Sumchus divides the doubt and gives one eighth. A pot ordinarily needs one third of the cistern. Half of that is one sixth, and Sumchus gives half of the doubtful sixth, namely one twelfth. A drinking vessel needs one quarter of the cistern. Half is one eighth, and Sumchus gives half of that doubtful amount, one sixteenth. From Sumchus's approach we infer that the Sages, who place the burden on the claimant, would give only a minimal amount in all these cases. This is Rama's final alternative view. The first two opinions agree that the Sages do not disagree with Sumchus here, but disagree over whether Ravina's proof comes from the end of the baraita, yielding one sixteenth, or the opening clause, yielding one quarter. The third opinion, the Rosh and Ri according to Rashbam's explanation, holds that Sumchus's fractions arise from his special rule of dividing monetary doubt, while the Sages would give only a minimal amount. This also explains the later rules concerning “for a barrel,” “for a pot,” and “for a drinking vessel.”
