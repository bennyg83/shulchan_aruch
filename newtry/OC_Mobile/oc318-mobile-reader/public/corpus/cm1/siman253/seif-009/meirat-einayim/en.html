That is, he reconsidered. Siman 250 already discussed reconsideration in determining whether a disposition is a partial gift requiring a kinyan, so that if he later recovers he cannot retract. Here the issue is different. We ask whether all the gifts were regarded by the donor as one continuous distribution, or whether the earlier recipient should take his full stated share before later recipients share what remains. If the donor paused, we say that when he gave to the first recipient he had not yet decided to give to those named later. The first recipient therefore takes his full amount, and the later recipients divide what remains, because at the time of the first gift the estate contained enough to satisfy it. This assumes that the first gift was supported by the kinyan required for a partial gift. The pause does not itself mean that he retracted from the first gift. Retraction applies where he first gave all the property to one person, or directed a particular property to one person because of anticipated death, and afterward gave that same property to another. That is not the case here. Rama's shorthand “that is, he reconsidered” merely refers back to the technical law in siman 250 and teaches that the first gift should not be treated as an ineffective partial gift without its required kinyan.
Therefore, if he left only nine hundred. Some delete the word “only,” which makes the wording clearer. The three gifts of two hundred, three hundred, and four hundred total nine hundred. If the estate contains less than nine hundred, the available assets are divided into nine proportional shares: the recipient of two hundred receives two parts, the recipient of three hundred receives three, and the recipient of four hundred receives four. The Tur states this here and the Mechaber states the same rule in siman 111. The Rambam's text, copied here, also has the word “only,” apparently because the opening clause is stated for the sake of the later case involving a creditor even though it is otherwise obvious. The Mechaber certainly agrees that where less than nine hundred is found, division is proportional to the amounts given. This differs from several creditors of equal priority who are owed two hundred, three hundred, and four hundred. There each creditor has a lien upon all the debtor's assets, so if there is insufficient property they may collect equally according to the relevant creditor rules. In gifts, however, the donor's differing stated amounts reflect differing degrees of intended benefit, so each recipient takes according to the proportion of his gift. The Tur deliberately states gifts of two hundred, three hundred, and four hundred in ascending order because of the later novelty that, when a creditor intervenes, the later recipient can lose first even though his gift was the largest.
The same applies to a healthy person who caused others to acquire, etc. The Tur writes that the same proportional rule applies where a healthy person vested the gifts through a third person. But if he personally made separate acquisitions with each recipient, it is known who acquired first, and the one who acquired first has priority. The Tur's choice of wording is precise. With a third person he uses the language “vested,” not necessarily kinyan sudar, because it can mean that the donor handed one purse of money to the third person and said, “Give two hundred to this one, three hundred to that one, and four hundred to the third.” If instead he handed each recipient's separate gift successively to the third person, priority would again be evident.
