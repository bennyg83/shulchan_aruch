If a debt instrument is produced against him, the creditor collects from the last recipient. Bava Batra teaches that if a deathly ill person successively says, "Give two hundred zuz to A, three hundred to B, and four hundred to C," the earlier named recipient has no priority merely because he was mentioned first. Therefore, if a creditor later appears, collection is apportioned according to the rules governing the gifts. Rashbam says that if the creditor's document appears before the recipients actually receive the money, the creditor can collect. Alternatively, the gifts may have consisted of fields worth the stated amounts, in which case the creditor can seize even after the recipients have received them because recipients of deathbed gifts are treated like heirs and inherited land remains subject to creditors. The movable case is difficult. If the deathbed words are treated as written and delivered, why should a creditor prevail merely because the recipients have not yet physically collected the money? A debt or deposit held by a third person ordinarily goes to heirs rather than a creditor under Rabbi Akiva, because creditors still require collection while heirs do not. The deeper basis of Rabbi Akiva's rule is not the oath itself. Tosafot and Rosh, citing the Jerusalem Talmud, explain that heirs are not considered to require collection. Wherever the decedent's property is, it is legally theirs. Creditors, by contrast, still need an act of collection. A recipient of a deathbed gift is different from a true heir. Even though the Sages gave him a status similar to an heir, before the property reaches him his right can still be considered "awaiting collection." Terumat HaDeshen says similarly that money bequeathed to a granddaughter but not yet received is considered expectant property, so her husband does not inherit it as property already held by her. Thus the deathbed recipient and the creditor are both parties who still require collection. The recipient is not the same kind of automatic possessor as an heir. In such a case even Rabbi Akiva can agree that the creditor can prevail. The point still requires further study, as indicated by the sources.
If the last recipient has insufficient property, he collects from the one before him. Ran asks why deathbed gifts are treated here like encumbered purchaser property, so that collection proceeds from the last recipient first. If so, an oral loan within its term or the widow's and daughters' maintenance should likewise be unable to collect from such gifts, contrary to the rule that a widow is supported even though ordinary inheritance is Torah law. Ran answers that the passage concerns a deathbed disposition reinforced by a kinyan, which therefore has the law of a healthy person's gift. In an ordinary gift of all one's property as a true deathbed disposition, where the first recipient received medium quality land and the last poor quality land, the creditor should be able to collect from the earlier medium quality property rather than being restricted by purchaser ordering. Rif, Ramban, and Baal HaTerumot follow this approach. Tur and Rama, however, state the last recipient rule for an ordinary deathly ill person and only afterward say that the same law also applies to a healthy person who transferred through another. Their wording implies that the first case is a genuine deathbed gift, not merely a healthy person's transfer disguised by illness. The better explanation is Rashba's. Treating deathbed gifts like purchaser encumbrances is relevant only where it does not cause the creditor an actual loss, such as deciding whether he collects medium or inferior land. Where the effect would be to destroy the creditor's claim altogether, such as maintenance, an oral loan entitled to estate collection, or similar obligations, deathbed gifts remain like inheritance and do not defeat the creditor. Accordingly, even in a genuine deathbed disposition, if the first recipient has medium land and the last has inferior land, the creditor can be required to proceed first against the last recipient. He still receives his debt and suffers only a difference in quality. But where last recipient priority would eliminate the debt, the creditor can collect from the earlier gift. This reconciles Tur and the Shulchan Aruch with the maintenance cases. I noted earlier that an analogous ordering can exist even among heirs in a theoretical case of earlier and later inheritance claims, and the law concerning one who successively takes possession of an heirless convert's property provides a useful comparison. Thus the last recipient rule need not transform a true deathbed gift into an ordinary purchaser transfer.
