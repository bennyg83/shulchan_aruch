So much to so-and-so. Knesset Yechezkel, siman 88, discusses a will expressed partly in German. The testatrix said, in effect, “My sister so-and-so shall have the brocade dress.” Although Shach writes that saying “this thing shall be to so-and-so” is a gift, Knesset Yechezkel distinguishes the German verb meaning “shall have,” which does not naturally mean “shall be” and can instead mean merely “shall enjoy” or “shall possess for use.” It therefore resembles Maharik's rule that saying only “so much to so-and-so from my property” may be ineffective. The will opened with a general formula of a deathbed gift, but Knesset Yechezkel says that would clearly govern all beneficiaries only if no later recipient were separately described with explicit gift language. Here, however, the testatrix later said regarding one married sister that five hundred Reichsthaler were “as a gift,” and regarding a brother that two strings of pearls were “as a gift.” That specific repetition can indicate that the opening gift formula was intended for those recipients, whereas the brocade dress was not given as a gift. Talmudic and halachic rules treat superfluous wording as deliberately adding or excluding something. By the same reasoning, where the testatrix later said that two hundred Reichsthaler “shall be given” to her mother and that a certain garment “shall be given” to another person, although the German phrase literally sounds like “give,” the repeated explicit gift language used for other beneficiaries suggests that these clauses were not intended as outright gifts. Knesset Yechezkel therefore raises the possibility that the testatrix merely appointed those persons as administrators or users rather than owners.
