Whether he said merely, "a maneh." See Sema, seif katan 39, who says the novelty is that we do not suspect a buried maneh. Rama, responsum 92, cites Rivash that specifically where the donor said "Give" we do not suspect a buried maneh, but if he did not say "Give," we do. However, Rama himself rules at the end of siman 240 that if a person gives "land" without further specification, we presume he meant the known land and do not suspect that he owned some other parcel. That law is from Rashba's responsum cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 253, and Rashba derives it from the rule that we do not suspect a buried maneh. This shows Rashba does not follow Rif's limitation to a case where the donor said "Give." Rather, whenever he refers simply to land or a maneh, we presume he means known assets, even without the word "Give." But if the donor made no designation at all and there is merely a document or witnesses proving a deposit, then even where he trusted the depositor to say it had not been returned, we may still have to suspect that the deposited property is located elsewhere. Certainly if the deposit consisted of money, which is ordinarily hidden for safekeeping, there is reason to suspect that it was buried elsewhere. Even if it consisted of objects, perhaps they were stored in another location. A proof is siman 250, seif 10, where one who gives all his property raises concern that he has additional property elsewhere. Since Chazal treat that possibility as realistic, why should heirs not be given the claim that perhaps the deposited property remains elsewhere? This seems difficult from siman 108, seif 4, concerning an iska document against heirs. Some authorities say we do not plead for them that an accident occurred, and therefore they must pay. Why not at least plead that the iska property itself may still exist somewhere else and that the assets before us are the father's own property? Why should his own property be bound to repay a deposit that may remain elsewhere? It appears those authorities follow the view in siman 341, seif 4, cited by Sema, seif katan 13, that failure to inform the owner where the deposit was placed can itself be negligence, causing the father's estate to become liable. Shach there proves from Bava Batra 70, where the Gemara says, "Perhaps the Angel of Death prevented him," that the failure to notify can be an accident. That proof can be limited to a case where there is independent indication that the deposit was not returned. Perhaps the custodian had been negligent by not informing immediately but relied on the expectation that the location would become known before his death, and death prevented the final notification. Since it remains possible that he actually returned it, the heirs are exempt despite the earlier delay. Here, however, if because of his failure to notify the deposit can no longer be recovered at all, that failure itself is treated as negligent loss, and the estate can be liable.
But where he did not transfer by kinyan. See Shach, seif katan 24 and 25, who explains that the case is one where the donor expressly said this should be a shechiv mera gift, and on that basis attempts to resolve Sema's unresolved question. His words are very difficult. If the donor expressly says it is a shechiv mera gift, the recipient acquires even without kinyan by virtue of the special law of a shechiv mera, and he can acquire even coin and debts, as explained in siman 250. Yet Shulchan Aruch here says that without kinyan he does not acquire such property and the instruction is treated merely as a command. Sema, seif katan 40, also distinguishes the whole estate from a partial gift in a death-related command. I do not understand this. Where one expressly commands because of impending death, there is no distinction between all and part. Likewise, when one distributes his entire estate, the circumstances themselves support the presumption that the instruction was made because of death.
