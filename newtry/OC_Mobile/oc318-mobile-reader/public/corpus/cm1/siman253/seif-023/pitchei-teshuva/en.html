And one is a relative. Baer Heitev cites the Tur in the name of some authorities that the case concerns a relative who is not actually fit to inherit him. The Tur himself disagrees and writes that, to the contrary, if the relative is fit to inherit there is stronger proof that the testator did not mean him, because there was no need to make a special bequest to one who would inherit in any event. The Shach, however, appears to rule in accordance with those authorities. The Bach explains their view cogently and concludes that it is primary. Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 169, likewise writes that the Tur’s objection is not entirely clear, although he ultimately says that since the other early authorities did not distinguish between one fit to inherit and one not fit to inherit, and the Talmud, Shulchan Aruch, and later authorities likewise make no such distinction, he would not reject the Tur’s ruling in law, certainly not in practice. Perhaps he did not see the Bach. Avodat HaGershuni, siman 33, appears to agree with the Tur and explains that the dissenting opinion may also agree. In his reading, they speak of one who is “fit to inherit,” meaning sufficiently close in kinship that he would be an heir were there not a closer relative, but who is not in fact the legal heir. Such a person may be preferred over a neighbor because the testator naturally feels closer to him. If there is no closer relative and this person is the actual heir, those authorities would also agree that the ambiguous designation “Toviyah” was not intended for him, since he would inherit without any bequest. This requires study.
