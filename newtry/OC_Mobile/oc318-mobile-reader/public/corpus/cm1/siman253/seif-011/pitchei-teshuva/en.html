Whichever of them he wishes, he may take. Shaar Mishpat records a case of a person on his deathbed who distributed his estate among his sons. His wife held a ketubah for one hundred adumim together with all jewelry belonging personally to her. Before his death he directed that she receive two hundred adumim on account of the ketubah, but only half of the personal jewelry, and he also exempted her from the ketubah oath. The widow claimed the two hundred adumim and exemption from the oath, but rejected the limitation regarding the jewelry, arguing that under the ketubah she was entitled to all of it and that half alone was worth more than one hundred adumim. The heirs argued that the additional money and exemption from the oath were granted only on condition that she take only half the jewelry. He ruled for the heirs. This is unlike the case stated here, where the recipient may choose the two hundred zuz and decline to marry the testator’s daughter. There, the testator first said “give him two hundred zuz,” which is the primary gift, and the later statement “and he shall marry my daughter” is a separate direction, so the matter depends on the recipient’s choice. Here, however, the testator plainly intended to reduce the widow’s ketubah rights for the benefit of the heirs, since he expressly directed that she receive only half the jewelry, which was worth more than the extra one hundred adumim. We cannot say that he added one hundred adumim to the ketubah while leaving her free to take all the jewelry, because those two conclusions contradict one another. The same applies to the waiver of the oath. He exempted her only if she accepted half the jewelry. A clear proof is brought from Ketubot 43b: whenever the husband strengthens her position in one respect while diminishing it in another, we presume that he made the two dependent upon one another.
