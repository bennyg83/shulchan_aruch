For he disclosed his intent regarding what he has. This law comes from Maggid Mishneh, chapter 10 of Hilchot Zechiyah, in the name of Ritva. Ritva concludes that if the money was lost, the loss is not charged to the gift recipient unless the donor gave him a specific identified item. At first glance this is difficult. If one simply says, "Give him two hundred zuz," or, "two hundred from my property," and afterward part of a mixed fund is stolen or lost, the loss is ordinarily allocated proportionally where no specific item was designated. Why then does Ritva say the recipient does not bear the loss? The answer appears to depend on when the loss occurred. Ran and Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef, say that where one simply says, "Give him two hundred zuz," and afterward money is stolen or lost, the loss is shared proportionally. Ran explains this by comparison to two persons who contribute to a common purse, one contributing one hundred and one two hundred; loss is borne proportionally. That reasoning applies specifically after the donor's death. A shechiv mera gift takes effect only after death. At that moment, one portion belongs to the recipient and the remainder to the heirs, so they resemble two parties with funds in one purse. But a loss occurring during the donor's lifetime happened before either the recipient or heirs had acquired any share. There was no common ownership yet, so the analogy does not apply. The case of wine in seif 13 is different because the donor identified a specific fund or class of property from which the gift was made. If the designated wine soured, the donor can say, in effect, "Your gift was in that identified wine." According to Rashi he can say entirely, "Your wine was lost," while according to Tosafot, because it was mixed, the loss is proportional. Even if the wine soured before death, the designation itself ties the gift to that specific property. But where one says only, "two hundred zuz," even if he adds, "from my property," this means no more than "from what is mine," as Ran explains. It is as though he said that two hundred should be given from whatever property remains after his death. Losses to the estate during his lifetime therefore do not reduce the gift. Only where he counted and described the total amount of assets he presently had do we treat later deterioration in those identified claims as affecting the gift proportionally, because his calculation revealed that he was measuring the gift against that particular pool. This applies especially to outstanding debts, each of which is inherently uncertain. But ordinary theft or loss during his lifetime is treated like a general gift "from my property" and does not reduce the recipient's stated amount. After the donor's death, however, any loss to the mixed fund is borne proportionally, because the recipient and heirs then stand like partners in a common purse.
