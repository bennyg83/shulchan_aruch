The heirs did not acquire them. See Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat siman 45. A woman married and brought into the marriage her mother’s house as part of her dowry. She had a daughter from a prior husband, and with the present husband she had two more daughters. Years later the husband died. The widow compromised before beit din with his heirs concerning her ketubah of five hundred gold coins, his obligation to marry off her daughter from the first husband, and maintenance for his two daughters. The heirs transferred the house to her absolutely, on condition that by a formal kinyan she undertake to support and maintain the minor daughters until marriage and to marry them off with the same dowry she would give her first daughter. The agreement further stated that if she failed to do so, she would have no right in the house, which would belong to the daughters and their representatives. One daughter died before reaching marriageable age. The heirs claimed either that they inherited her rights or that the widow failed to fulfill the condition. The widow answered that she undertook to give a dowry to the daughter when she would need one, not to her heirs, and that she fulfilled everything capable of fulfillment. Mishkenot Yaakov rules clearly for the widow. The heirs’ inheritance claim is baseless, because the obligation arose only when the daughter reached marriageable age and needed a dowry. Even while alive she could not demand it earlier. After her death her heirs inherit nothing, as explained in siman 253, seif 16. Nor can the heirs rely on an assessment that they transferred the house only because they expected the daughter to marry. The Rama’s rule that money given specifically for marriage returns if the recipient dies rests on an obvious assessment of the donor’s intent, because the very purpose of the original gift was marriage. Here there is no clear assessment that the heirs would not have transferred the house had they known one daughter would die. They still benefited by removing from themselves the burden of raising, feeding, clothing, and supporting the daughters. Moreover, there the money itself was given for marriage, so if there is no marriage the gift fails from inception. Here the house was transferred to the widow, not to the daughters, while the widow separately obligated herself to marry them off. The issue is therefore one of fulfillment of a condition, not an implied condition based on intent. There is a further distinction between a gift and a sale. Here the transfer was in exchange for debts owed to the widow. Even if the house was worth somewhat more, there is no law of onaah for land up to half its value, and land has no fixed price. Nor does the claim that the widow failed the condition have substance, because performance became impossible without fault on her part. From the sugya in Gittin concerning a get conditioned on nursing a child or serving the husband’s father, one learns that where the person whose benefit was intended dies and the impediment does not come from the obligated party, the condition may be regarded as fulfilled because the intended benefit is no longer needed. Even authorities who rule more strictly regarding gittin do so because a husband may have intended to cause distress, but in monetary matters they agree that where the benefit is no longer needed actual performance is unnecessary. He supports this at length from Mordechai and from the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 335, and concludes that the ruling is clear and beyond doubt. See there.
