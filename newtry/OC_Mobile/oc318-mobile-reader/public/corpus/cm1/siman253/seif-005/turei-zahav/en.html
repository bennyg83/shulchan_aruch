(Seif 7: When do we say that he acquires, etc.) The Beit Yosef, citing Maharil, expressed surprise at our master's statement that if the recipient is his son, he is appointed only as guardian. In siman 246 our master ruled like the Rashbam that there is a distinction between explicit language of inheritance, through which the son acquires fully, and explicit language of gift, through which he is only a guardian. Here, however, the donor used ambiguous language, such as "My property to my son" or "Let it belong to my son," which may be interpreted either as inheritance or as gift. The Rashbam expressly writes that it may be understood as inheritance. It appears to me that our master accepted the Rashbam only on this limited point, namely, that there is a distinction between explicit language of gift and explicit language of inheritance. But where one says simply, "My property to so and so" or "Let it belong to so and so," the case addressed by Rav Huna, if the recipient is his son we certainly interpret it as an appointment as guardian. Our assessment is that he did not intend entirely to uproot the inheritance of his other sons, because ordinarily all of a person's children are dear to him. We therefore prefer to construe the words as an appointment as guardian. Only where the circumstances show that he did not intend a guardianship, such as where the recipient is among other heirs, is there a transfer of ownership. The remaining question is whether it operates as inheritance, which cannot be interrupted, or as a gift. On that question we follow whether the recipient is fit to inherit him. This appears to me the clear and reasonable understanding of our master's position.
