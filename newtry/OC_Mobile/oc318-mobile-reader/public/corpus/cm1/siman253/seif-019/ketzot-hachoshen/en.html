Unless he said: from my property, or another similar expression. Rashba explains that saying, "I give so and so a maneh from my property," is not like designating one specific asset. The phrase does not strengthen the gift by identifying a particular fund. Its purpose is to clarify that the donor means value from his estate rather than necessarily a particular coined maneh already present. Mabit nevertheless ruled that even if the donor has a written debt owed to him by another, the recipient does not receive that debt merely from the words "from my property," because the donor means a present tangible maneh. The discussion in Gittin helps clarify this. Rav initially says that the Mishnah's order, "Give a maneh to so and so," works only if the money is piled and present. Rav Pappa later explains that a deathly ill person who says, "Give a maneh to so and so from my property," is obeyed, while a bare "maneh" can raise concern that he meant a particular hidden maneh. According to Tosafot's preferred textual version, "from my property" means he is making a gift from wherever in his estate value can be found, whereas a bare statement can sometimes be an admission concerning a specific deposit. The Mishnah itself can still concern a gift. The difference is that a bare "maneh" is ordinarily a particular coined maneh, so if no such exposed maneh remains, there may be nothing to give. When he says "from my property," he directs payment from estate value generally. Baal HaTerumot says that in a three party arrangement even saying simply "give a maneh to so and so" can suffice. Gidulei Terumah asks that Rav Pappa's reading seems to require a specific maneh. The answer depends on the mechanism. In a deposit, the ordinary commercial enactment can operate on the money actually held, even if the formula was general. In a loan, the three party arrangement is a special enactment without ordinary acquisition logic and Rosh therefore requires the words "the maneh I have in your hands." Rav Zevid's reading can apply the arrangement to a deposit, where the enactment has an intelligible commercial basis and a general reference to the maneh in the holder's hands is enough. Rav Pappa, who applies it equally to loan and deposit, treats the mechanism as a special halachah and therefore requires the more precise formulation. This resolves the competing passages.
