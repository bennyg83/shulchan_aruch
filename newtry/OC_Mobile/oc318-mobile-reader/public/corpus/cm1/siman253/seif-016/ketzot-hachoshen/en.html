Give two hundred zuz to so and so for her dowry. At the end of Perek Na'arah the Gemara discusses one who directed that his daughter's dowry be given. Rav Idi bar Avin ruled that later appreciation or depreciation belongs to the heirs. Rashi explains that the customary dowry articles were known and fixed. Rosh prefers the textual version stating, "four hundred zuz as a dowry for his daughter," because the novelty is that even though a monetary amount was mentioned, the donor intended the ordinary dowry articles themselves. Therefore any increase or decrease in their value belongs to the heirs. Tur similarly rules that where he directed specified articles worth two hundred zuz, the heirs bear the gain or loss.
The Shulchan Aruch distinguishes between saying, "Give two hundred zuz to so and so in her dowry," and, "Give two hundred zuz to so and so for a dowry." Sema questioned the distinction and suggested the main difference was whether the word "give" was used. That cannot be correct, because a deathbed gift requires language of transfer in either case. The true distinction is between tangible dowry articles and a sum of cash. "Dowry" can denote the customary garments and ornaments themselves. If he directs that specified dowry articles worth two hundred zuz be given, then she is entitled only to those articles when she needs them, and appreciation or depreciation belongs to the heirs. If instead he says, "Give her two hundred zuz for a dowry," he directs payment of cash, and the reference to dowry merely indicates the intended use. She acquires the two hundred zuz immediately and bears the later gain or loss. Ritva states this explicitly. Where the ordinary dowry articles were known and later became cheaper, the daughter receives only the customary articles, and if she dies before marriage her heirs receive nothing because the father intended the articles for the time she needed them. But if he said, "Give two hundred zuz to so and so for her dowry," she acquires the two hundred immediately. If she dies before marriage, her heirs inherit them. The words "for her dowry" merely indicate purpose, like giving a poor person a dinar to buy a shirt. Thus the decisive factor is whether he gives articles or cash. Tur's wording, although it uses "for a dowry," expressly speaks of specific articles valued at two hundred zuz and therefore follows the first rule.
