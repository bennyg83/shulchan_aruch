But not a loan. Baer Heitev cites the Mordechai’s reason. Netivot HaMishpat writes that if a person directs an heir, using language of inheritance, to inherit two shares of his assets, the heir acquires even loans owed by non-Jews. Although Bava Batra 131 compares designation of an heir to ordinary acquisition in respect of property not yet in existence, the reason here for excluding such loans is that the giver or recipient lacks full reliance and therefore no complete gift was intended. That concern does not apply where one makes an heir generally entitled to two shares of the entire estate. If, however, he appoints him heir only to loans owed by non-Jews, the Darkei Moshe’s concern may apply. At first this appears to mean that the beneficiary acquires by Torah law as an heir, but that would contradict Netivot’s own ruling in siman 281 that one cannot transmit even a Jewish loan by special inheritance because it is not presently in the giver’s possession. Rather, his intent here must be that the beneficiary acquires under the rabbinic law of a deathbed gift, and that language of inheritance does not detract where the beneficiary is legally fit to inherit. His novelty is only that in such a case the Darkei Moshe’s rationale of lack of reliance does not apply, so the loan may be given as a deathbed gift whether the debtor is Jewish or non-Jewish. This still requires study according to the Bach, who explains the Mordechai’s rationale differently, namely that the institution of assignment in the presence of all three parties does not apply to a non-Jew. The Bach explains that because such a debt cannot be transferred by a healthy person through that mechanism, it cannot be transferred by a deathbed gift under the principle that whatever is unavailable to a healthy person is unavailable to one on his deathbed. Even where the dying person holds collateral belonging to the non-Jew, since a Jew does not acquire a non-Jew’s collateral as his own, the underlying debt remains like an unsecured account and cannot be transferred merely by words. If, however, he transfers the collateral itself so that the beneficiary may collect the debt through it, the deathbed gift can be effective, just as a healthy person can transfer the collateral through a valid kinyan. Avodat HaGershuni, siman 11, discusses a will by which Yaakov gave his entire estate to his three sons, including cash and all oral and written debts owed by Jews or non-Jews, subject to an annual payment to a grandson. After five years the sons had taken possession of the estate and paid the grandson according to the will. The grandson then demanded a share of the non-Jewish debts, arguing that such debts could not validly have been transferred even by deathbed gift. He rules for the sons for several reasons. First, once they had already collected the debts, the money is not removed from them. The law of deathbed instructions operates similarly to assignment in the presence of all three parties, and even where that mechanism does not apply to a non-Jew, the Rama in siman 126 rules that if the non-Jew has already paid the recipient, the payment is not removed. This is especially so here, where the grandson knew for years that his uncles had collected and consumed the funds. Second, even before collection, the Mordechai’s rationale may not apply where the dying person gives the loans to his own sons, toward whom his intent is firm. The same distinction can be made even according to the Bach. Rav Papa’s rationale in Bava Batra is that whatever an heir inherits can be given as a deathbed gift. Although the Ran does not accept that rationale generally, it may remain sound where the decedent divides his estate among those who are themselves Torah heirs. Even if he uses language of gift, where there is no subsequent beneficiary one may regard the language as strengthening their inheritance, especially where the will itself calls them “my heirs.” As to written debts against non-Jews, even the Bach would certainly validate the gift because a healthy person can transfer such a document with the formula “acquire it and all its liens.” Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 45, discusses a will giving the residue of an estate, including cash, land, pledges, and oral and written debts owed by Jews and non-Jews, to a named beneficiary. He rules that debts owed by Jews are certainly acquired, and written Jewish debts are acquired even without an express formula assigning all liens. Oral debts owed by non-Jews, however, are not acquired according to the authorities who hold that assignment in the presence of all three parties does not apply to a non-Jew. Since the Shulchan Aruch cites both views and does not decide, the Torah heir, who is presumed in possession of the estate, cannot be dispossessed. As to written debts against non-Jews and debts secured by collateral, the Rama himself expresses doubt, and therefore the beneficiary cannot extract them from the heir; however, if the beneficiary seized a written debt, it is not removed from him, while seizure of an oral debt does not help. Noda BiYehuda further considers whether failure as to the non-Jewish oral debts invalidates the entire gift as a single attempted transfer of property partly transferable and partly nontransferable. He concludes that a deathbed gift is different from a single formal kinyan, because each item is acquired by the force of the deathbed statement itself. Even if the language grouped the items together, the will should not be invalidated, especially since the Mechaber validates non-Jewish debts and the first opinion in siman 203 that would invalidate an entire mixed kinyan is nearly rejected. Nor can the heir invoke kim li merely because he is the heir, particularly where the assets are held by a third party. His final ruling is that all debts owed by non-Jews, whether written or oral, belong to the Torah heir, except that seizure is effective for written debts but not for oral debts.
