(And two equal claimants came.) The Sema explains that if one came first, we say that the deceased certainly owed him. I am astonished, for what relevance does priority have to a debt? Regarding a gift, each claimant might indeed fear that the other would arrive first, because each truly believes that the gift was intended for him. Regarding a debt, however, we do not presume people to be wicked, such that a stranger would come and falsely claim that the dying person owed him. Moreover, according to the Sema, the text should have said “two came simultaneously.” It therefore appears to me that the words mean exactly what they say, namely, that the two were equal in their names. This is similar to the Ran’s formulation cited here by Beit Yosef: “Likewise, one who says, ‘I owe two hundred zuz to Yosef son of Shimon,’ when there are two persons named Yosef son of Shimon.” As to priority, it need not be stated that the first claimant does not take precedence, because this is a debt. Why should the genuine creditor have hastened to present himself? Indeed, it would have been more reasonable to follow an assessment of intent, such as determining which one was his friend and was accustomed to deal with him. Nevertheless, the Ran writes that in the case of a loan we do not follow such an assessment. This appears correct. The Rama’s words, “for there is no assessment of intent,” mean that in a loan no assessment of intent is effective.
