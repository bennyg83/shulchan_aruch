If he says: if they die, others shall inherit in their place. Beit Yosef explains Rashi's reading that the first beneficiaries died without children. Because where the first recipient is himself fit to inherit, inheritance has no interruption and an "after you" gift ordinarily fails, Rashi had to construe the later recipients as heirs who can receive when the first line ends. Maharash Sasson and Shach attacked Beit Yosef from Bava Batra. They argued that the Gemara itself resolves the baraita by using the rule of Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah and that the gift language stated for the sons can support the later inheritance language within the same immediate statement. In my view that is not sufficient. Where the first recipients are children who already inherit by Torah law, even explicit gift language is treated as inheritance. Rif and Ramban say that one cannot convert what is intrinsically inheritance into a gift merely by using gift terminology. Therefore the gift language to the children cannot validate inheritance language for unrelated later recipients. Rashi was accordingly compelled to explain that the later recipients are themselves fit heirs, after the children die without descendants. This also resolves the Gemara's own discussion. The objection from the baraita, "My property to you, and after you so and so shall inherit, and after him a third," can be answered from the legal meaning of "after you." "After you" takes effect at the completion of the first recipient's death. If the second is a true heir whose inheritance has no interruption, then at that moment the first cannot create a later third right, because the second's inheritance is already absolute for his lifetime and beyond. Even if the second later dies without children, the phrase "after your after you" was supposed to operate at the completion of the second's death, and the first donor had no power to interrupt that heir's inheritance at that moment. The Gemara therefore needs the immediate statement principle and can establish the recipients as nonheirs. The present baraita is different. It says that if the children die, others shall inherit. The children inherit without interruption during their lives, and after they die childless the donor's own inheritance line can return and be directed among heirs fit to inherit from him. Therefore Rashi's reading is coherent.
Beit Yosef further says that when the children die childless, the later heirs can be brothers of the original donor because they inherit through the donor. Darkei Moshe objected that brothers of the children also inherit through the donor, so the distinction is obscure. The basic idea, however, is sound. When a son dies childless, the inheritance returns through the father's line. Just as a living father with several brothers can designate one brother to inherit among brothers under Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah, so too he could in principle designate one of those brothers concerning property that returns through his line after the son dies. The harder part of Beit Yosef's distinction, excluding the son's brothers, is indeed difficult. If the son dies childless and his brothers are sons of the original donor, the property likewise returns through the donor's line, and one might think the donor can designate one son among them. Most authorities, however, do not distinguish and appear to hold that even where the son later dies childless the original donor cannot designate a future heir who is not presently fit. The reason is that Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah's power works only with a person presently fit to inherit, not with a person who will become fit only after future events. Bava Batra says that even the special inheritance language does not transfer to one not yet in existence. Property that will be acquired later can pass to a presently existing heir because once he has been made heir, later property falls to him automatically. But a future heir who is not presently within the inheritance relationship cannot be created now. Rashi's position can be explained according to the approach of Raavad cited by Ramban, who gives greater force to inheritance language even for one not yet fully fit, at least where the recipient himself exists. The baraita of "Give a shekel to my sons each week" is also established elsewhere according to Rabbi Meir, who recognizes more future transfer power. Thus Rashi can read the later heirs more broadly. The practical authorities, however, generally follow the narrower rule.
