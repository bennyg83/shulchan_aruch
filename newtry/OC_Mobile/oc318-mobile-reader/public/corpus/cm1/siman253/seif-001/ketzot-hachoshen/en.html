They may write, or they may act as a beit din. Bava Batra derives from the laws of inheritance that three people present when a deathly ill person distributes his estate may, if they wish, record the instructions as witnesses or sit as judges and decide the resulting inheritance dispute. The person receiving the estate cannot insist upon a different beit din. Rashbam explains that the inheritance section itself is treated as judicial law. Tosafot understands that the verse makes the people present at the testament into judges in a true inheritance setting. It does not directly refer to the rabbinic institution of a deathbed gift to strangers. Rosh, citing Rabbi Yonah, extends the rule after the Sages enacted that a deathbed person's words are like written and delivered instruments. They modeled the rabbinic gift upon inheritance, so three people present at such a disposition can likewise become judges. Tur rules this both where the sick person apportions to his children and where he directs all his assets to others. Tosafot's statement that the verse itself does not speak of an ordinary deathbed gift is not a disagreement with that later rabbinic extension. The verse concerns Torah inheritance. Once the Sages created the deathbed gift institution, they could give it the same judicial structure. Tur rejected Rashbam's attempt to establish the case specifically as a partial deathbed gift made with a kinyan, because such a transaction has the full law of a healthy person's gift and should not be called inheritance. Beit Yosef objected that the Gemara proves from this passage how long one can retract from a kinyan, which seems to show that the case does involve a kinyan. Bach attempted to reconcile Tur by saying the sick person gave all his property with a kinyan from now if he died, which still resembles inheritance because final benefit depends on death. This does not fully answer the difficulty. If a present kinyan, even conditional on death, counts as inheritance, the same should be true of a partial gift with that identical structure. Tur's objection shows that he regards any present kinyan as an ordinary healthy person's transfer. The better explanation is that Tur and Rosh understand the principal verse according to Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah, who allows a person to enlarge or reduce the shares of those already fit to inherit by using inheritance language. Such a declaration is a Torah act of inheritance and needs no kinyan. Once the father says, "this son shall inherit such and such," the son becomes heir to that enlarged share. Still, while the parties remain engaged in the same subject, the declarant can retract the enlargement just as one can retract from a sudden formal kinyan while still discussing the transaction. The reason is that his first statement is not yet treated as fully settled intention. After they move on to another subject, the declaration is fixed. At that point the three present can act as judges. This explains the Gemara's proof concerning retraction from a kinyan without requiring the inheritance declaration itself to have been made by kinyan. If such a powerful Torah inheritance declaration remains unsettled while the parties are still engaged in the matter, certainly an ordinary sudden formal kinyan can likewise be retracted during that interval. Once the conversation moves on, both become final. The father may later create a different valid inheritance allocation by a new declaration, but that is not a retraction of the first legal act. It is a new exercise of the Torah power to reallocate among eligible heirs. Rosh's extension to gifts to strangers is rabbinic. There the three present should exercise judicial authority only after the deathbed gift has reached a point at which it is no longer subject to the ordinary retraction relevant to that proceeding. This resolves the principal difficulty in Tur and Rosh.
And to adjudicate every matter of doubt. The Gemara asks why the three present can judge immediately, since the testator may still retract, and answers that the parties had already moved on from the subject. Rashbam says that while retraction remains possible, any judgment is not a final judgment. Tosafot says that judging before the matter is settled would merely trouble beit din for nothing if the testator later changes his mind. It is surprising that Rambam, the Shulchan Aruch, and Rama omit the requirement that the parties have moved on from the subject. Perhaps, according to Tosafot, it is only practical advice to judges not to waste effort; a judgment entered earlier can still be valid if the underlying disposition ultimately stands. That could explain the omission. Tur, however, cites both Rashbam and Tosafot and appears to rule that they should not judge initially until the matter has become fixed. Ramban notes that we do not ordinarily fear that a deathbed donor secretly retracted before death, for otherwise no deathbed gift could ever be enforced. The concern in our sugya can therefore be prospective: so long as the donor presently retains a readily exercisable right to retract because the matter is still under discussion, judgment is premature. This also explains a related question about future acquired property. Shach argues that a person who subjected future property cannot retract before the property comes into existence, because otherwise an after dated document should be suspect for an unrecorded retraction. That proof is not decisive as to a possible past retraction, because halachah generally does not suspect secret retractions without evidence. But it does have force concerning a continuing future right to retract: if the transaction remains openly revocable, writing or adjudicating now can indeed be premature. Thus the omitted rule should be understood as concern with a presently unsettled disposition, not as a general suspicion that the donor may secretly have changed his mind.
At night they write, but they do not act as judges. Rashba explains that when the three people see the testament at a time when they cannot sit as judges, such as at night, their observation has the status of witnessing, not judicial perception. They cannot later judge on the basis of their own observation because a witness cannot become a judge in the same case. If other witnesses testify before a beit din at night, Rashba allows that testimony to be received and the judges can rule the next day. His distinction is that the judges' own nighttime observation never acquired the status of judicial hearing. Since night is not initially fit for judgment, they became witnesses, not judges. Tosafot and Rosh infer more broadly that nighttime judicial proceedings are invalid even after the fact. Rashbam, however, permits judgment at night after the fact where the judges heard testimony from others, while agreeing that their own nighttime observation cannot be transformed into judicial knowledge. Noda BiYehudah considered a marriage document written without the parties' authorization, signed by three people who had witnessed the kiddushin. He suggested they might count as a beit din because judicial seeing is no worse than hearing. But the underlying kiddushin occurred at night. He therefore wondered whether, according to the opinion that nighttime judgment is valid after the fact, the three might still have judicial status and the document require serious concern. In my view there is no basis to combine the stringencies. Even according to Rashbam, who validates an after the fact nighttime judgment based on testimony from others, judges who themselves saw the event at night became witnesses rather than judges. The principle that seeing is no worse than hearing operates only when they see at a time initially fit for judgment. Therefore all opinions agree that these three nighttime observers were not a beit din through their own observation. Any document they later write is testimony in writing, not a judicial record. The Gemara's express rule, "at night they write but do not judge," leaves no room to treat them as judges merely by combining contrary opinions.
