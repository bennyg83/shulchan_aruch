Two who entered. See Sema, seif katan 1, who explains that because the gift does not take effect until after death and the widow's and daughters' maintenance can be collected from it, the matter resembles inheritance and therefore those present can act as judges. This is not precise. Even where a shechiv mera transfers from now and after death, so that the transfer can operate retroactively, those present can still act as judges, as Tosafot, Rosh, and Tur indicate. Tur's objection was only to Rashbam's interpretation that the case concerns a partial gift made with kinyan. Rashbam must mean an ordinary partial shechiv mera gift that cannot be revoked even if the donor recovers. Had he meant a gift "from now and after death," there would be no reason to speak specifically of a partial gift, since the same law applies to the whole estate. Tur therefore correctly objected that a true partial gift, irrevocable even against the donor himself, does not have the law of inheritance and those present cannot act as judges, as Tosafot state. Beit Yosef's question on Tur is therefore itself difficult. At first glance Sema's own explanation, that the case concerns a gift of the whole estate which the shechiv mera may revoke, also seems difficult. The Gemara asks that the donor can retract because the parties are still discussing the matter or because they are still seated. If this is a shechiv mera gift, he can always retract. There is no difficulty. Retraction while they are still occupied with the matter rests on a special presumption, explained by Rashbam, that the donor has not yet finally resolved to let his words stand, because he may still wish to add a condition. That possibility can directly prevent the formation of a final judicial ruling. By contrast, an ordinary shechiv mera gift has already been fully intended to take effect after death; the mere abstract power of later retraction does not cause us to suspect that he will in fact retract. Ketzot HaChoshen later gives the same answer. There is another distinction. In a shechiv mera case, when those present write during his lifetime, they cannot write that the field presently belongs to the recipient. They write that the donor instructed that upon his death from this illness the field shall belong to the recipient. If they act as judges, their judgment can likewise be conditional: if he dies from this illness without retracting, this field belongs to this recipient and that field to that one. If he later retracts before witnesses, the condition in the judgment fails. Even the same judges can testify to the later retraction, because a shechiv mera's retraction is a new event and does not violate the rule against retracting testimony. If no retraction is established, they may testify after his death to the original shechiv mera gift, and beit din may decide the matter on that testimony. There is therefore no reason they could not write such a conditional judgment during his lifetime. By contrast, the Gemara's case of a kinyan from now and after death while the parties are still discussing the matter is different. The baraita says, "If they wish they may write, and if they wish they may render judgment," in parallel form. If they write a transaction deed, they can write that the property is already his, because once the deed is written the donor can no longer retract merely because they remain occupied with the matter, as explained in siman 198. If they instead act as judges and write an immediate judgment that the property already belongs to the recipient, the donor may still retract before them because their judgment writing is not itself a transaction deed. Should he retract, they could no longer testify that the earlier judgment was wrong merely by saying that he later retracted, because that would uproot their own prior testimony and encounter the rule against retracting testimony. The Gemara therefore asks properly why they may render judgment while retraction remains possible. Ketzot HaChoshen further writes that if a shechiv mera gives one son property by inheritance language, relying on the power to designate one heir under siman 281, he cannot simply retract unless he then gives it to another heir using inheritance language. In my view he can retract. Beit Yosef cites Nimukei Yosef that the power to designate inheritance applies only to all the estate, while a partial designation needs a kinyan; otherwise we would have a partial shechiv mera gift requiring no kinyan when expressed as inheritance. By the same reasoning, if such an inheritance designation were irrevocable, we would have a gift of the whole estate by a shechiv mera that could not be revoked merely because inheritance language was used. Necessarily, because the inheritance does not vest until death, he can retract before death. Moreover, if Ketzot were correct, any gift by a shechiv mera to one of his heirs would become irrevocable, because gift language to one fit to inherit is itself treated as inheritance language under siman 248. This cannot be correct.
