(Seif 16, if he said one hundred twenty or two hundred zuz.) The Sema, seif katan 58, writes that the distinction between the first and latter clauses is that in the first clause he did not say, “Give.” This is difficult, for if he did not say, “Give,” there is no effective disposition at all, as stated in the gloss to seif 2 of this siman concerning one who says, “So much to so-and-so,” and as cited in the name of Maharik. We must therefore say that in the first clause too he said, “Give,” and that the wording is merely abbreviated. The principal distinction is whether he said “for her dowry,” using the prefix lamed, or “in her dowry,” using the prefix bet, as written in Ir Shushan. The Sema writes that he did not find such a distinction. In my view, however, this is an explicit Talmudic rule at the end of the chapter Na'arah Shenitpattah concerning the testamentary instruction of a dying person for his wife’s maintenance. If he designated land “for her maintenance,” he increased her maintenance, whereas if he said “in her maintenance,” he fixed and limited her maintenance. Rashi explains there that the phrase means, “in payment of the debt that you have against me, let this serve as payment.” The Ritva likewise indicates that the same distinction applies here. “In her dowry” means that this shall serve as payment of the obligation he owes his daughter, at the time of her marriage. Even if he used this language concerning a woman whom he was not obligated to provide with a dowry, the wording still means that it is to be given when she requires a dowry, for that is the function of the prefix bet. By contrast, “for her dowry” means for the purpose of a dowry, merely indicating the purpose for which he gives it, like one who gives a dinar to a poor person with which to purchase a cloak, as cited by Beit Yosef. It further appears to me that although in the preceding seif there is no distinction between “for her ketubah” and “in her ketubah,” and in either case she receives only two hundred, that case is different. Whenever an apparently superfluous expression can be understood as indicating an intention inherent in the word itself, it is preferable to interpret it that way rather than attribute the intent merely to the grammatical prefix. Therefore, when he says “for her ketubah,” we explain that the gift of four hundred zuz is in addition to what the husband will write for her, as Tosafot explain at the end of Na'arah. By contrast, in the phrase “for her dowry,” the noun itself does not indicate why it was said, so we must interpret his intent from the prefix lamed or bet. Tosafot there ask why there is no distinction between “for her ketubah” and “in her ketubah,” although there is such a distinction regarding maintenance, and they offer other answers. Thus one may still ask why we distinguish between “for her dowry” and “in her dowry.” Nevertheless, the Ritva, who is the source of this ruling, appears to hold as I have written, and his approach is more compelling than the answers of Tosafot there. Although the Tur writes that if one says, “Give my daughter for her dowry such-and-such articles,” and they increase in value, the increase belongs to the heirs, the Tur follows the explanation of Tosafot just mentioned, according to which there is no distinction between “for her dowry” and “in her dowry,” just as there is none between “for her ketubah” and “in her ketubah.”
