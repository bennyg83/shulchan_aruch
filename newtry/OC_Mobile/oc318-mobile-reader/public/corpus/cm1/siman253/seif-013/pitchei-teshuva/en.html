Two hundred complete zuz. Dagul MeRevavah writes that at the least they may delay payment until all the wine is sold. If they manipulate the price upward so that no buyer can be found, they are not heeded. See Responsa Rosh, kelal 93, siman 3. Regarding the Rama’s statement that they must give him another maneh, Netivot HaMishpat explains that the Rama abbreviated. He means only that they cannot say, “Your portion was lost.” The loss must nevertheless be borne proportionately, no better than where one says simply, “two hundred zuz from my assets,” where the loss is shared according to proportion, as explained in seif 19. The Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef states this explicitly. Netivot further explains that this applies where the loss occurred after the testator’s death, because at death the legatee immediately acquired his gift and the heir his inheritance, like two persons who place funds into a common purse, so the loss is proportional. If, however, the property was stolen during the giver’s lifetime after his statement, the law differs. See there. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 135, discusses a widower who married a widow. Most of her property remained outside his control under a waiver document, while property she did bring in was subject to a condition that it be returned to her heirs if she died during the first year. She died in that year after making a will, allocating part to Torah scholars to learn for her soul, part to relatives, and the residue to her husband. Among her assets was a bill against a nobleman for four hundred gold coins. The nobleman withheld one hundred eighty, claiming it should go to the heirs of her first husband, and paid them. Her second husband argued that this loss should be shared proportionately by all legatees. Chatam Sofer distinguishes four categories: payment to Torah scholars for their service, fixed gratuitous gifts to named beneficiaries, the amount brought into the marriage that she released her husband from returning, and the residue of property excluded from the marriage but later given to him as a deathbed gift. The Torah scholars, whose money is wages for service, suffer no loss. The issue concerns the gratuitous beneficiaries. After analyzing the four views concerning the case in Gittin of “four hundred zuz from the wine,” he concludes that this case resembles wine that turned sour, not a creditor who subsequently appeared. She owed nothing; the debt instrument was lost only through coercion and force, so it deteriorated after the gift. If the will did not say that each named person should take “from my assets,” the loss plainly falls proportionately. Even if it did, most authorities and the Shulchan Aruch rule that the words “from my assets” do not grant priority, so the gratuitous beneficiaries bear the loss according to their shares. The husband must bear the loss with them. He cannot claim the Ramban’s minority view that the specific designated asset alone fails and the heir bears no loss. This is obvious as to property she never brought into the marriage, where he stands like any other donee. Even as to property she did bring in, though he may there stand as heir rather than donee, nearly all authorities disagree with the Ramban, so he cannot rely on that view. Thus, the loss is shared by all gratuitous beneficiaries and the husband, but not by the Torah scholars. Since the husband’s precise share is not established merely by his assertion, he must take the oath applicable to partners and guardians as to how much of the loss falls on each share, and he is exempt from paying that amount.
