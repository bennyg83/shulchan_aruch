To my son as his firstborn share. See Sema, who says this means he did not also say "as is fitting for him." His words imply that if he did say "as is fitting for him," then even if he also said "as his firstborn share" or "for his debt," the amount becomes an additional gift. Perishah says the same. I find this difficult. If he says merely, "Give to so-and-so, the firstborn," or, "to so-and-so, my creditor," there may be doubt whether he means an independent gift or satisfaction of the preexisting entitlement, and the extra phrase can reveal his intent. But if he explicitly says, "for his debt" or "for his firstborn share," he has plainly said that this amount is in satisfaction of that entitlement. How can the additional phrase "as is fitting for him" negate what he expressly said? Perishah cites Nimukei Yosef as proof, but the version before us does not say that "as is fitting for him" is equivalent to omitting the words "for his debt." Rather, it says that when he says "as is fitting for him" we do not automatically interpret it as "for his debt," which is exactly the Gemara's question. Likewise, Rosh's wording does not prove Sema's reading, and there is already a textual correction needed there. It should say "and similarly to so-and-so, a creditor," not "for his debt." If Sema's novelty were correct, the Tanna and poskim would surely have stated the much stronger case that even where the donor explicitly said, "for his debt, as is fitting for him," it is an additional gift. Their failure to do so strongly indicates the opposite.
He receives only two hundred zuz. See Shach, seif katan 11. The reference is mistakenly placed and should be attached to the dissenting opinion concerning one who said merely, "to my son so-and-so." See Sema, seif katan 18, who explains that with an ordinary son the father has power to reduce his inheritance share. At first glance this is very difficult. Saying to an ordinary son that he shall receive only two hundred zuz is no better than saying, "My son shall not inherit more than two hundred," which is ineffective. The father can reduce one son's share only by validly increasing another heir's share through inheritance language. We must therefore be speaking of a case where he distributed his whole estate and said that Reuven shall receive two hundred zuz as his inheritance, while the remainder shall be inherited by the other heirs. This is also evident from Mahara Aharon Sasson, siman 147. The novelty is that we do not say the two hundred are an additional gift above Reuven's normal inheritance share, with the remaining estate afterward divided among all heirs. Rather, we interpret the two hundred as constituting his allotted inheritance share, while the balance goes to the other heirs. A firstborn is different, because the father cannot reduce the firstborn's Torah share merely by using inheritance language toward the other heirs.
Or to so-and-so, my creditor. See Sema, seif katan 22, citing Darkei Moshe that Tur's statement, "his hand is on top," disagrees with Rosh, because Rosh supposedly holds that when one says "to so-and-so, my creditor" it is an additional gift. This is very difficult. Rosh's version of the Gemara is "to so-and-so, a creditor," in contrast to Tosafot in Ketubot who read "for his debt." The Gemara then says that only when the donor adds the phrase "as is fitting for him" is the amount treated as a gift. Without that phrase, the creditor's hand is on top and he can take under whichever characterization benefits him. Therefore Darkei Moshe should read that Tur differs from Tosafot, not from Rosh. The printed reference to Rosh is a scribal error.
