"To so-and-so my son, as is fitting for him." This is also the Tur's wording. In the Gemara, Rif, and the Rosh, however, the text reads, "Give two hundred zuz to my firstborn son, as is fitting for him." In the Perishah I proved that this version is primary and that the Tur should be emended accordingly.
They take an additional two hundred zuz, etc. Since he did not need to say "as is fitting for him" and nevertheless did so, we interpret the extra expression as intending an addition. This is like the Tur and Mechaber in siman 214 regarding one who sells a house: a pit and cistern are not normally included in the sale of the house, but if he says "except for the pit," when he did not need to say so, we interpret the extra exclusion as increasing what he retained, so that he retains even a right of passage to the pit.
"To my son in his firstborn portion," etc. Meaning, he did not say "as is fitting for him."
He receives only his debt. The reason is that if a creditor took more than his debt it would appear like interest. If his debt exceeds two hundred zuz, however, he certainly takes the entire debt. This is specifically where the giver did not say "as is fitting for him." In the first clause, where he said, "to my creditor as is fitting for him," it does not look like interest, for he revealed that he gave the extra amount because it was fitting for the benefit the creditor had done for him. The Tur concludes in the name of Ramah that even if he says, "to so-and-so on account of his debt," it does not look like interest.
Who is an ordinary son with respect to his inheritance, etc. This does not mean that the shechiv mera himself said "he is an ordinary son." Rather, these are the words of the text, meaning that the ruling concerns a son who is not a firstborn.
He receives only two hundred zuz. The reason is that a father has authority to reduce the inheritance portion of his ordinary sons, as derived from the verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit." We therefore say that he intended this son to receive only two hundred zuz as his inheritance, because that is what the phrase "in his inheritance" implies. This differs from a firstborn son, concerning whom the verse says "he may not give the rights of the firstborn," teaching that the father cannot reduce the firstborn portion. Likewise, he cannot reduce a creditor's debt or his wife's ketubah. We therefore say that in those cases his intention was that they have the stronger position.
In all of them their hand is on top. The Gemara teaches as a novelty "in her ketubah" and "in his firstborn portion," to tell us that even in those cases their hand is on top and we do not say that he intended to reduce their rights. Although he has no authority to reduce them, one might have thought that once his intention was to reduce them, at least they would not receive the stronger position. The ruling teaches otherwise. In the Perishah I wrote that Rashbam's text must be understood this way.
Even regarding a creditor, if he said, etc. The reason it does not appear like interest is that he did not say "on account of his debt."
Some disagree and say that if he said without qualification, etc. They hold that the Gemara requires the additional phrase "as is fitting for him" only when he also says, "give to my firstborn son," because mentioning "firstborn" would otherwise imply that he meant it as the firstborn portion or, at most, that the recipient should have the stronger position. But if he does not say "firstborn" and merely says "give two hundred zuz to my son" or "give two hundred zuz to my wife," he need not add "as is fitting for him."
Or "to so-and-so my creditor." In the Perishah I proved that in my view the Rosh does not hold this expression to constitute a gift. The Tur cites the Rosh only regarding the formulation "give to my creditor," not "to so-and-so my creditor." When he says "so-and-so my creditor," the Tur later states explicitly in seif 16 that it is not a gift, but rather the recipient has the stronger position. Darkei Moshe writes that the Tur's latter ruling is unlike the Rosh. This is not clear. If so, the Tur should not have stated it without qualification against his father the Rosh. It therefore appears that the Mechaber omitted this view for this reason. Specifically when he says "to so-and-so my son" or "to so-and-so my wife" do we say that he used the relationship as an expression of affection and to explain why he gives them a gift, namely because he is his son or she is his wife. But when he says "to so-and-so my creditor," the words "to so-and-so" alone would have sufficed. He therefore surely added "my creditor" to indicate that it is not a gift. This is straightforward.
