His son takes first. See Sema, seif katan 27, and Perishah, where it appears that the donor must specifically state the amount given to the son. In my view there is no basis for distinguishing between specifying a sum and specifying a fraction such as one third or one fourth. The true distinction is between explicit gift language and language that can be understood as inheritance. If he says "Give," or, "Let it be given," these are pure gift formulations in the context of "after you," and they are likewise treated as gift here. But if he says, "My property to so-and-so," "It shall be to so-and-so," or, "So-and-so shall take," those formulations can function as inheritance language toward an heir, as Beit Yosef cites from Mahari Weil. Rashba, responsum 887, writes explicitly that even where one distributes his entire estate among persons fit to inherit, if he says, "So-and-so shall inherit," or even, "So-and-so shall take," this can be inheritance language. But if he distributes the estate even among heirs using true gift language such as "Give," it is gift language. Thus the primary distinction is whether the wording can be read as inheritance or is exclusively gift language. Although elsewhere "shall take" can be gift language when said to a non-heir, toward an heir, for the laws of "after you" and the present rule, it can function as inheritance language. Some authorities concerning "after you" require the donor to say explicitly that he does not intend inheritance. Yet where treating the language as gift benefits the heir, the law can give effect to both aspects. Maharit says that where gift language is written to an heir, he is placed on two mounts, meaning he receives the advantages of both gift and inheritance where appropriate.
