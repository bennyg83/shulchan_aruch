But if he said, etc. See Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 44, concerning the will of a healthy person, arranged in numbered clauses. In the sixth clause he wrote that he was fully obligated to pay his sister Leah a certain debt one hour before his death, or alternatively that his heirs should give her one seventh of his entire estate, in which event the debt instrument would be discharged. After his death, many debt instruments for thousands appeared, likewise payable one hour before death, together with his wife’s ketubah and a debt to the community. He rules that the deceased’s sister certainly must bear the debts proportionately with her share. This follows from the explicit Mishna in Bava Batra 138 concerning a person on his deathbed who says, “Give two hundred zuz to so-and-so and three hundred to so-and-so,” after which a creditor appears. At first glance one might argue that in this case she loses everything because the testator numbered the clauses and thereby indicated priority, so that her later clause resembles “and after him.” Yet one should not issue a ruling merely on such an analogy. At the least, however, there is no doubt that she must contribute in proportion to her share. Although one might argue that the beneficiaries whom the testator called “my heirs” should have the full legal status of heirs, and the Rosh, cited by the Tur in siman 253, ruled that where a dying man gave two thirds of his assets to his daughter and left one third to the legal heir, the heir alone must pay the father’s debt, that ruling rests on an assessment of intent. Maharam Padua, siman 51, explains that the Rosh inferred that the daughter’s gift was primary and only the residue was left to the heir. Here the assessment runs the other way. The testator clearly intended the gifts to the named beneficiaries as primary and did not intend them alone to bear all his debts. The sister must therefore contribute at least proportionately. The responsum further discusses her argument based on wording that she should receive one seventh of “all my estate” before any diminution for any reason. The heirs claimed that this wording referred to burial expenses and charitable legacies but not to debts, because property already owed by the decedent cannot properly be called part of the estate. They also sought to deduct expenses incurred to rescue the estate from danger caused by informers. He rules for the heirs. Payment of a creditor is not considered a later diminution of the estate, since that sum was already deficient during the decedent’s lifetime even before collection. He cites the Nimukei Yosef concerning whether a debt reduces the estate for purposes of daughters’ maintenance, who distinguishes a documented loan as already considered collected. Thus, the testator’s phrase concerning diminution must refer to losses such as uncollectible debts, depreciation of merchandise, and similar events, not payment of documented debts. The sister therefore bears her proportionate part of all written debts. Moreover, because the other beneficiaries are true heirs, she must in his view bear her share even of oral debts. Although the law concerning oral debts and daughters’ maintenance remained unresolved, property is presumed to remain with the heirs; where that presumption benefits the heirs, we likewise regard the oral debt as having reduced the estate during the decedent’s lifetime. Any doubts between her and the orphans are resolved in favor of the orphans as possessors. The same applies to unusual losses that reduce the estate, for we assess that the testator did not intend the heirs alone to bear extraordinary losses. This includes losses caused by informers.
