If a deathly ill person says, "My property to Tuvyah," and dies, and one man named Tuvyah comes and claims it, he receives it. Rama: Even if another possible Tuvyah exists, we do not wait for him. If the claimant is commonly called Rav Tuvyah, he does not receive it, unless the deceased was familiar with him and ordinarily called him by his personal name. If two claimants are each known as Tuvyah, a Torah scholar takes precedence. Rama: Unless it is known that the deceased was closer to a neighbor or relative of that name. If neither is a Torah scholar, a neighbor or relative takes precedence. Between a neighbor and relative, the neighbor takes precedence. If both are relatives, both neighbors, or both scholars, the judges give it to the person whom they believe the deceased intended. The same applies when many claim. Rama: "Neighbor" here means not merely one living nearby, but an associate accustomed to dealing with him. Estimating the donor's intent applies to a gift. If he acknowledged owing money to Tuvyah and two equal claimants appear, they divide it, because no estimate of affectionate preference applies to a debt.
