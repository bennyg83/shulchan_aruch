We do not wait for him. Since he hurried and came first, we say that he is certain the giver intended him. We are not concerned that a fraudster rushed forward, because he should be afraid that someone else will later arrive and establish that the giver was closer to him in intent, thereby taking the money from him. This resembles the Tur and Mechaber at the beginning of siman 139 regarding two people fighting over a ship, where the rule is "whoever is stronger prevails," for the reason I explained there.
A Torah scholar comes first. The Gemara explains that a person justifies his deeds at the time of death, and giving benefit from his property to a Torah scholar is a greater mitzvah.
A neighbor comes first. As it is written, "Better a nearby neighbor than a distant brother."
Only a neighbor who is his companion, etc. Meaning, even if he does not live immediately next to him. Residence is not the decisive factor. He is called a "neighbor" because he dwells and associates with him in his affairs. See the law of bar metzra in siman 175, where the Tur and Mechaber likewise say that if there is no adjoining owner and one person is a neighbor and another a relative, the neighbor takes precedence. There they leave the term unexplained; the unstated law there is learned from the explicit explanation here.
And two equal people came. The Ir Shushan writes, "two people whose names are equal came." In my view the Mechaber does not mean that their names are equal, for that is obvious and need not be stated. Rather, both arrived equally, at the same time. One might have thought that since the giver expressed it as an obligation to pay, even if they arrived one after another the second too should receive a portion. The ruling teaches otherwise: when one came first, we infer from his haste that the entire obligation was to him, as explained above.
Since he owed them. Meaning, because he said the payment was on account of a debt, and we do not know whether he owed one or both, we give to both.
