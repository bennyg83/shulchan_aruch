But if he said that he owes Toviah, etc. This ruling requires analysis. Its source is the Ran in the chapter HaKotev and the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 11 of the Laws of Acquisition and Gift. They infer it from the Tosefta at the end of the chapter Get Pashut, which states that if one says, 'Give two hundred zuz to Yosef ben Shimon,' we do not investigate ordinary social language by asking which of the two he loved and which he did not. Rather, the two divide equally. They explain that the Tosefta concerns a debt or deposit, whereas with a gift one follows the donor's intent and gives it to the person whom he loved. In truth, however, this distinction is not self-evident. Where he says that he owes Toviah, the matter is a legal admission, and the court must determine whom he meant from the language and circumstances of the admission. If one Toviah was demonstrably his intimate associate and the other was not, that evidence may identify the creditor. Yet where both fit the designation equally, or the evidence is insufficient, they divide. The same basic rule applies to a deposit. With a pure gift, the donor's probable intent carries greater weight, but it must still be established by reliable circumstances rather than conjecture. The matter therefore depends upon the strength of the identifying evidence, and the blanket distinction between debt and gift should not be applied without qualification.
