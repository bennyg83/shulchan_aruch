It is written and written. This is what he has written to him and has given you all her work and given him the same writing with the biller and the columnist, and the author of R. S. S.S., yes, in selling notes:
The heir cannot forgive. Ordinarily, one who sells or gives a promissory note to another, even after the transferee receives it through writing and delivery, may still forgive the underlying debt, and so may his heirs. A deathbed transfer is different. The heir cannot forgive the debt because such a result would undermine the Sages' enactment that a dangerously ill person's words are treated as written and delivered and could agitate the donor's mind. This is explained in siman 66. The Ir Shushan gives a different reason, saying that the Sages made the deathbed recipient like an heir, and one heir cannot forgive what belongs to another heir. That is not correct. The Sages made him “like an heir” only by analogy and only for certain laws, such as acquisition taking effect at death. He is not a true heir. The Tur later says explicitly that although they treated him like an heir, a creditor still collects before him because an actual heir stands in the father's place in a way a gift recipient does not. In ordinary assignment of a debt, the seller can forgive because he transferred only the monetary lien, while the debtor's personal obligation remains attached to the original creditor and passes to that creditor's heirs. The same reasoning would allow a true heir of the dangerously ill donor to forgive unless the special rabbinic enactment prevented it. Therefore the correct reason is protection of the deathbed disposition.
But not a loan he has in the hands of a gentile. Mordechai cites Avi Asaf in the name of the Re'em, who says that the recipient does not acquire such a debt even through a deathbed gift because the donor does not rely upon collection from the gentile. He suggests that even if the Jew holds collateral belonging to the gentile, the result may be the same, because a Jew's claim against a gentile does not automatically give him ownership of the collateral. Hagahot Maimoniyot states similarly. The Beit Yosef objects: Why should the recipient's confidence matter if the donor's deathbed words are treated as written and delivered? He therefore understands the other poskim, who do not distinguish Jewish and gentile debtors, to disagree. Darkei Moshe answers that the phrase “he does not rely upon it” refers not to the recipient but to the donor. Since the donor himself does not confidently regard the gentile's debt as collectible, he did not fully intend to give it as an absolute gift. Maharam rules accordingly and expresses doubt whether a gentile debt evidenced by a written instrument can be transferred by deathbed gift.
