And some disagree with these laws. Baer Heitev cites Maharshal in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot that where people gave money to assist with a daughter’s marriage, the money may revert if the purpose fails. Darkei Moshe likewise cites those words and notes that they appear contrary to the Ritva, whose view is adopted by the Mechaber, that the recipient’s heirs acquire. Hagahot Maimoniyot derives the rule from Bava Kamma 110b, where money given for atonement may return if the purpose can no longer be fulfilled, because the giver did not give on that understanding. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 147, writes that although the Rama presents a dispute, in his view there is no genuine dispute, because the cases differ. Everyone agrees with the Ritva and Rashba that once a coin has actually been given to a poor person for food, the donor has fulfilled his mitzvah even if the poor person later uses it differently. Likewise, once money has already been given into the daughter’s own hand for her dowry, the donor’s purpose has been fulfilled and, if she dies, her heirs acquire it. Hagahot Maimoniyot speaks where the money was not given to the poor daughter but to her father to give her at marriage. If she dies, or the father does not marry her off, the money returns to the donors. See Choshen Mishpat 125:8. The Mordechai’s case of one who vowed to marry off an orphan girl and she died also concerns a mere vow while the money remained with the donor. Rabbeinu Chaim Kohen held him exempt, whereas Raavyah held that a vow to a poor person is like a declaration to sacred property and is equivalent to delivery, so the heirs acquire. Accordingly, once the money reaches the recipient, whether for food or dowry, the recipient and later the heirs acquire. If it remains with the donor, Rabbeinu Chaim Kohen and Raavyah dispute the matter, and Mahari Weil writes that Rabbeinu Chaim Kohen’s view is more persuasive, so even if the orphan’s heirs seized the money it is removed from them. If it was given merely to an agent collecting on her behalf, all agree it returns to the donors. Where it was placed in the hands of guardians appointed by the donors for minor orphans, Chatam Sofer is uncertain but concludes that such guardians are the orphans’ hand, unlike an ordinary agent, and thus the money has already been acquired for the orphans and passes to their heirs. If no known heirs exist, it should be used for public needs, as the Rosh rules in siman 283. See also Chatam Sofer, Yoreh Deah siman 238, his collected responsa part VI siman 46, and what I wrote above in siman 125, seif 5, seif katan 4.
