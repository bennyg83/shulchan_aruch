(Seif 17, those after them shall inherit in their place.) Beit Yosef asks that the language of inheritance is ineffective for persons who are not heirs, and answers that “shall inherit” is imprecise and means “shall take.” He elaborates somewhat. I am astonished that an entire sugya in Bava Batra 159 escaped the Rav’s attention. The Gemara raises this very question and answers that the beneficiary is one fit to inherit from him. According to the halacha, since we hold that within the same statement the language of gift can validate the language of inheritance where there are two fields and two persons, as stated in siman 281, the Gemara did not need this answer. Rather, the language “give” used for his own heir validates the language of inheritance used for the others. Beit Yosef also explains Rashi’s words, “they died without children,” as intended to answer the rule that an inheritance has no interruption. This too cannot be said. If so, why does the Gemara on page 129 challenge the case where one says, “My property is yours, and after you, so-and-so shall inherit”? The Gemara says it cannot be established where the first is fit to inherit, because then the third would not inherit, and it concludes with a refutation. Why did it not establish the case where the first died childless? Evidently, even then an inheritance has no interruption, since heirs exist in any event. Rashi’s intent is rather to prevent the following difficulty: it is unreasonable that the testator should be more concerned for strangers than for his own grandchildren. Since he did not say, “and if they die,” we assume he was not concerned to preserve the principal for his grandchildren. Why, then, should he be more exacting when leaving it to others? Rashi therefore explains that we say he limited the first beneficiary only when that beneficiary had no children. Ordinarily, all the property would automatically remain with the son, but because the father saw that the son had no children, he limited him to one shekel so that the principal would remain. Rashbam, however, explains in Bava Batra 129 that he was concerned to preserve it for others but not for his grandchildren, and this is also the plain meaning of the Tur. There is therefore no basis for the Sema’s statement in seif katan 72 that where the first has no children the inheritance can be interrupted. He was drawn after the words of Beit Yosef.
