They give it to her heirs. See Responsa Kehillat Yaakov, end of siman 4, who questions the Rama here. The Rama derived this ruling from Terumat HaDeshen, siman 350. Yet Terumat HaDeshen initially compared the case to the discussion in Beitzah, where the daughter may marry if she wishes, but then cites the Semag and Rif that the cases differ. There, there are two distinct gifts, and one does not depend on the other. In the case before Terumat HaDeshen there was a single gift conditioned upon buying a belt, and if she did not buy the belt she acquired nothing. That is his conclusion. It is therefore surprising that the Rama cited only the beginning of his discussion and not its conclusion. Ketzot HaChoshen already raised this point and ruled in accordance with the conclusion of Terumat HaDeshen, as did Responsa Divrei Rivot. Kehillat Yaakov further discusses one who directed that a fixed sum be given for his mother, that the principal be placed on interest with the guardian, that the income be used for her needs, and that she live in his house with his wife and supervise the children. After the mother died, her heir claimed the principal. He rules that the principal was never given to her absolutely, but only for its produce. She herself could not have given away the principal, only the income, and her heirs cannot possess greater rights than she had. This resembles one who says, “Give a palm tree to so-and-so so that he may eat its fruit,” which grants only a right to the fruit. Although the testator first used language of giving a fixed sum, he then expressly explained that only the income was to be given for her needs. Moreover, the later expression controls, especially in a deathbed gift where the giver may retract. Even the income was given only for her needs, indicating that it was intended for her welfare only so long as needed. This is unlike the case in Beitzah, which concerns two separate gifts. It is also stronger than the case of one who gives two hundred zuz to a woman for her dowry and she dies, where the Rama rules that her heirs do not acquire because she no longer needs a dowry. Therefore, strictly by law, the heir has no right to the principal, although it is proper to compromise and give him some amount as a gift. See there.
