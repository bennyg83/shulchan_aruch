It is as though he stated two separate testamentary directions. Terumat HaDeshen discusses Reuven, who, while deathly ill, directed that twenty litra from his estate be given to his granddaughter and that she buy a fine belt with them. Reuven died, and about a month later the granddaughter also died before receiving the money. Reuven's son argued that the gift was conditional upon her buying the belt, while the husband argued that the monetary gift had already vested. Terumat HaDeshen first compares the case to money designated for an orphan girl to marry, where Rabbi Chaim Kohen ruled that if she died before marriage her heirs do not receive the money, because the donor intended only the mitzvah purpose. He then distinguishes the present case. A person's intention is especially close to his descendants, and the direction to buy a belt can be understood as expressing affection rather than as a condition. He also cites the Gemara's distinction between saying, "Give him four hundred zuz and let him marry my daughter," which can be read as two directions, and, "Let him marry my daughter and give him four hundred zuz," which is one conditional direction. Terumat HaDeshen ultimately explains, following Semag and Rif, that the decisive distinction is whether the wording constitutes two independent testamentary directions or one integrated conditional direction. In the present case the better reading is one direction, and this was also understood in Divrei Rivot. I am therefore surprised that Rama states broadly that it is treated as two directions, apparently relying on Terumat HaDeshen, when the responsum itself concludes otherwise. Still, Rama's practical ruling can be sustained on Terumat HaDeshen's first ground. Where the donor directs money to his own daughter, his intention is especially close to her and we can say he fully gives the money without a condition, while the instruction concerning the belt merely expresses the use he wishes for her. This can explain why Rama formulated the case with a daughter rather than a granddaughter. A granddaughter can be treated like another person according to Rama elsewhere. Thus, where he says, "Give my daughter one hundred zuz and she shall buy a belt," the monetary gift can stand independently because of the special closeness of intention. Where he says the same concerning others, it is one conditional direction. Even so, the matter requires study in light of the Shulchan Aruch's ruling that wording "for a dowry" can be merely an indication of purpose rather than a condition.
