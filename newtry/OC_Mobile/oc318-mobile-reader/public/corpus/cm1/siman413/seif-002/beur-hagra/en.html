The potters, etc.; the first, etc.; and the second, etc. Rif records Rava's ruling that the first person is liable for damage to the second, whether the second was injured by the first person's body or property, while the second is liable for damage to the third only when the third was injured by the second person's body, but not by his property, because he can say, 'I did not dig this pit.' The Gemara contains two major readings. Rashi, Tosafot, and Rosh understand the first version as distinguishing the bodies and property of the damagers, not the damaged objects. Under the second construction the distinction concerns the injured person's body or property, while the damaging object is the fallen person's body. The Gemara then returns, according to their text, to the first construction. Rosh explains that this return is necessary according to Rav, and that even according to Shmuel the same reading should be maintained because one should not interpret Rava differently under the two opinions. Rosh then cites Rif and understands Rif as retaining the second construction, with Rava's ruling addressing the injured objects. According to that reading, Rif's reason, 'I did not dig this pit,' refers to the baraita's later clause about the damagers' property, not directly to Rava. This would distinguish Rif from Rambam. Raavad, Maggid Mishneh, and Beit Yosef, however, understand Rif exactly like Rambam and Rashi, so that the stated reason applies directly to Rava's ruling. The language of Rif then reads naturally. A remaining difficulty is that under the first construction liability seems to include utensils, while Rambam and Shulchan Aruch limit the ruling. Baal HaMaor and Milchamot therefore follow Rabbenu Chananel's reversed text. In that version, Rava's first formulation distinguishes the injured person's body and property, while the second distinguishes the damager's body and property. The Gemara's later objection is directed at the baraita, not at Rava, so the second construction remains. Rif and Rambam then agree with Rabbenu Chananel. Shulchan Aruch's first seif cites the baraita: according to Rav only utensils damaged by utensils are exempt, while according to Shmuel even utensils damaged by a person are exempt, and we follow Shmuel. The second seif cites Rava's second formulation. Lechem Mishneh adopts this reading and resolves Tosafot's objections.
Others disagree, etc. This view appears lenient but is actually stringent. It is Rosh's opinion, following Rashi's second version and Tosafot. They hold that even according to Shmuel the Gemara returns to the first construction. According to Tosafot's understanding of Rabbenu Chananel, Rav Adda's resolution applies even under Shmuel because a person who causes damage is liable even for utensils. Rambam disagrees and understands the resolution as necessary only according to Rav; according to Shmuel all property of the injured parties is exempt, even where damaged by a person's body, because every obstacle has the law of a pit. Rashi, Tosafot, Rosh, and Baal HaMaor explain that the first person's property is treated as his damage because he abandoned it after a negligent fall, whereas the second person fell under compulsion and only his body is deemed a damager because he should have risen. Tosafot and Rosh ask why Rava stated the law according to Rabbi Meir rather than according to the Sages. They answer that under the Sages even property damaged by the first person's body is exempt, because without negligence his body is merely an obstacle, which is a pit and exempt for utensils. The second person under Rabbi Meir differs because some negligence in falling combines with his failure to rise, making his body a direct damager. We follow the Sages, as shown by Rabbi Yochanan and the incident in Bava Metzia 83a, according to Rif's text. Thus both the first and second fallen persons are liable only where one person's body damages another person's body. This is the dissenting view. Ramban in Milchamot explains differently: Rava speaks even according to the Sages. The first person's property is liable because ordinarily a person does not abandon property merely because he fell, whereas the second person is exempt because the first person's obstruction caused his fall. This resembles a person's stone or knife placed in the public domain by another, for which the owner is exempt even after learning of it, since it is unjust for one person to create the pit and another to become liable. Raavad similarly explains Rif and Rambam. According to Rosh's dissent, however, even the second person's property that damages the third person's body may create liability where it was not abandoned, especially once the second person knew and should have removed it. Tosafot leaves part of this issue unresolved. The rulings in simanim 390, 410, and 412 must be compared carefully. See also siman 304 and 50.
