For example, it was in his possession, etc. The Shach in seif katan 30 writes that even with a debt or deposit, and even where the owner says "acquire," the subject matter must enter the agent's possession before the owner retracts. With a gift, "acquire" is effective and nevertheless the property must be in the acquirer's possession. The same therefore applies to a debt and deposit. He cites proof from the Ramban, Tosafot, and Rosh. His reasoning is persuasive on its own: from where would the agent acquire if the property never came into his possession, and why should a debt or deposit be superior to a gift? There is no basis for inventing a distinction not stated expressly. His cited proofs, however, do not establish the point at all. He brings proof from Tosafot, the Rosh, and Ramban that with a deposit the item must be in "his possession." But there "his" refers to the messenger who is himself the depositary. Relative to the person for whom he is instructed to acquire, the property is being transferred as a gift. Their entire discussion concerns maamad sheloshtan. According to the view that maamad sheloshtan was instituted only for a deposit, why was such an enactment necessary? The owner could simply tell the depositary, "Acquire it for him." They answer that sometimes the deposit is not presently in the depositary's possession. Thus their discussion concerns absence from the possession of the messenger, who is the depositary. The beneficiary in maamad sheloshtan is acquiring the property as a gift. They do not discuss at all a person acquiring for himself in payment of his own debt or return of his own deposit. It remains possible that in such a case the property need not previously be in the possession of the acquiring agent. Nevertheless, the Shach's ruling appears correct as a matter of logic.
If the giver does not retract. It appears that the same is true if the giver dies in the interim. Death itself functions as a retraction. This can be proved from Tosafot and the Rosh, who write that Rava was not concerned that Isur the convert would say, "Acquire it for him," because the deposit was not in Rava's possession. Why would that matter? Eventually it would return to Rava's possession and he could then acquire it for Rav Mari. Isur certainly would not retract from a gift to his own son. Surely the property would eventually come into Rava's possession. Rather, Rava could keep the deposit outside his possession until Isur died, at which point death would itself revoke the instruction. One should not answer that just as the person giving the acquisition instruction can retract before the property reaches the acquirer's possession, so too the acquiring agent can retract before it comes into his possession by saying, "I no longer wish to acquire for so and so." Then perhaps Rava, although unwilling to defy Isur openly and therefore agreeing while leaving his presence, could retract privately before the deposit returned to his possession. This is strained, because Rava would thereby make himself appear fickle and untruthful, and "the remnant of Israel will not speak falsehood." The rule stated above therefore appears clear.
