It becomes a vow. See the Sema, who writes that one who says, "I will give to the poor," can ordinarily seek annulment of the vow, but once the property has left his hand he can no longer obtain annulment. The Shach likewise cites Rashba. Tumim writes that he remains uneasy. Rashba speaks where another person actually acquired for the poor. Here the sender merely says, "Give to the poor," which, according to Tumim, may not be equivalent to "acquire." The property therefore remains under the donor's control and perhaps he can still seek annulment. Tumim says his doubt is strengthened by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in siman 258, which specifically speak of property reaching the hand of the charity treasurer. Why specifically a treasurer? This suggests that only the treasurer's hand is like the hand of the poor. Where the donor merely says, "Give," and that phrase is not equivalent to "acquire," perhaps annulment remains possible. There is no real doubt. Responsa of Rabbi Betzalel, cited by the Shach in siman 255, explains why reaching the treasurer prevents annulment: the power of an ordinary person should not be weaker than the power of consecrated property. Once the treasurer acquires through meshichah for charity, the property is fully acquired and the donor can no longer annul. Thus of course an actual treasurer is necessary in that particular law in order to complete acquisition for the charitable fund. Here, however, "give" itself should be treated like "acquire." Regarding emancipation, the reason "give" is treated like "acquire" is that a master would not ordinarily free his slave unless the slave had brought him satisfaction. Emancipation is therefore analogous to payment of a debt, and "give" functions like acquisition. The same logic applies to a vow to charity. Once the donor is already obligated to give, the obligation resembles a debt, and the existing obligation together with the expression "give" makes the instruction operate like "acquire." There is another reason. In siman 9, the Shulchan Aruch rules that regarding a deathly ill person, "bring" is treated like "acquire," because the words of a deathly ill person are as though written and delivered and therefore have unusual force. The same should apply to consecration and charity, where the donor's speech likewise has enhanced legal force. "Give" is therefore treated like "acquire," and once it operates as acquisition, annulment is no longer effective. See what I write in seif katan 6.
