(There: even if he does not have, etc.) Our master writes, "and against the sender he has no migo," etc. This is surprising. He still has a migo that the money was lost through unavoidable circumstances, just as in siman 108 concerning one who deposited with another by document. Here too he should be believed to say, "I gave it to the lender," through a migo that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. Some explain that the meaning is that the claim, "I returned it to you," is unavailable even though he has some migo, because "bring it" is treated like acquisition for the creditor. This is incorrect, because Baal HaTerumot explicitly writes that he is not believed by a migo that he could have said, "I returned it," showing that the principal claim is "I seized it for my debt," as the Sema explains. One must say that Baal HaTerumot's intention is only to prevent a mistaken argument: just as the agent has a migo against the lender that he delivered the money, perhaps he also has such a migo against the sender and can therefore say, "I seized it for my own debt, yet I fulfilled your agency as though the lender received it." The ruling teaches that the migo of "I returned it to you" is ineffective for that purpose. He needs some other migo, such as that the money was lost through unavoidable circumstances or that he never received it. With such a migo he can claim, "I seized it for my debt." Otherwise he is not believed, because according to the agent's own account he cannot cause loss to the sender for the sake of his own claim. In truth, Baal HaTerumot's position itself requires study. The borrower certainly remains liable to the lender so long as the lender does not admit owing the agent. Even though the agent has a migo against the lender, the lender can say to the borrower: what do I have to do with your agent? You trusted him to send the money, and you still owe me my debt. The Sema writes that in this case the borrower is exempt from the lender, but that is wholly implausible. Why should the lender lose his money because of the sender's mishandling? Rather, the borrower must certainly pay. If so, why should the agent be able to harm the borrower through his own claim even if the lender owes him money?
