Bring. The Shach writes that if one says, "Give this maneh toward the debt or deposit," it is treated like immediate acquisition for the recipient. Some say that with "give" and "bring" this applies only if he hands the money to the agent now. Even where he says "acquire," however, all agree that the property must come into the agent's possession before the giver retracts, as stated in seif 6. Regarding a non-Jew, who is not subject to halachic agency, "give" and "bring" do not operate as immediate acquisition. Mahara Gallico in a manuscript responsum. See Mabit I, 145. Just as the borrower cannot retract, other creditors likewise cannot collect the money. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 16 of Hilchot Malveh. Knesset HaGedolah.
To his heirs. The sender cannot say, "I gave it to you only to deliver to Shimon, not to his heirs," as stated at the end of seif 8 concerning a gift. A loan or deposit is different. See Responsa Maharashdam, end of siman 143. Shach.
At his responsibility. See the Shach, who cites Responsa Rashach concerning one who owed money to another and, during the term of the debt, the lender went overseas. When the due date arrives there is concern that the currency may fall in value. See there whether the borrower can cause the lender to acquire the money through a third party.
Return. The Sema leaves this requiring study. He understands the Ritva as speaking only about a gift, teaching that the word "return" is no more effective than "bring" for a gift. With a debt or deposit, however, "return" should also work. The Shach writes that the Rama is correct and that the Ritva's wording supports him, since he writes that it is no acquisition at all, implying that it does not operate as acquisition in any case. The Taz agrees.
Exempt. This is not comparable to the Rama's nearby ruling that in a certain case the recipient must answer to the depositor. The Sema suggests that there the instruction is only that initially he should not return the money, while here the rule is that if he did return it after the fact he is exempt. He prefers another distinction. There, Reuven appointed Levi as his agent to receive the property, so as soon as Levi received it the property became Reuven's. If Levi returns it to Shimon because of threats, he is saving himself with another's property. See the end of siman 126, siman 292, seif 8, and siman 388, seif 7. The Shach rejects this. Once Levi acquired it for the creditor or depositor, what difference does it make whether he had been formally appointed? The property is now fully theirs. This is explicit in the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri at the end of the first chapter. Moreover, even where he was appointed agent, if he returns it because of actual coercion, or because the threatening party can force him to return it, he is exempt. This is not the rule of saving oneself with another's property. That rule concerns an external coercion unrelated to the property itself, such as a pursued person who breaks another's utensils, as stated in siman 380, seif 3. Here he is merely a bailee of the money, and unavoidable coercion exempts him. This also explains why the nearby ruling differs: there the coercion is not directed at this money but consists only of the other party threatening to deny his own debt, which is not halachic coercion. See the end of siman 126. Shach.
Both. Meaning that even if Reuven did not become poor, Shimon may collect from whichever he chooses, Levi or Reuven. Sema. See the Shach and Taz.
Reuven. The Sema concludes from the source that even though Shimon had not previously been established as a denier, once Reuven appointed him as agent to receive the money he acquired it for him. Levi's claim, "I did not acquire it for you on condition that I lose my own claim," is merely an unexpressed intention, and unexpressed intentions have no legal effect.
