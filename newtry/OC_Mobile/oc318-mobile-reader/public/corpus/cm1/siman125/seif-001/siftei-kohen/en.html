Who was indebted, etc., regarding a deposit, etc. With a gift, however, the instruction “take” is not equivalent to “acquire,” as explained below in seif 5.
Take this maneh, etc. Likewise, if he said, “Give this maneh” in payment of a debt or return of a deposit, the instruction is equivalent to acquisition for the recipient. Some say that with “give” and “take,” the money must actually be handed to the messenger at that time, as explained below in seif katan 28. Even when he says “acquire,” however, all agree that the property must enter the messenger’s possession before the giver retracts, as explained below in seif katan 30.
This maneh, etc. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that the same applies if one gives his messenger a deed of gift or another document to deliver to another person, for the recipient acquires it. Beit Yosef cites this. Darkei Moshe explains that this means where he says, “Acquire it for so and so,” as stated below in seifim 5 and 6, and the Sema writes likewise. The Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri cite a Tosefta listing “give this maneh to so and so,” “take this maneh to so and so,” “give this deed of gift to so and so,” “acquire this deed of gift for so and so,” and “receive this deed of gift for so and so,” and state that the sender may retract. Avi Asaf objects concerning “acquire this deed of gift,” and notes another version of the Tosefta stating that he may not retract. According to that version, Darkei Moshe and the Sema must be referring only to the end of Rabbeinu Yerucham’s statement, where he hands the messenger a different document. But if he gives the messenger the very deed of gift to deliver, the recipient acquires just as in the case of a debt. This also appears from the Yerushalmi cited by Beit Yosef in the name of the Ritva, which equates delivery of a debt payment and delivery of a deed of gift, and where the intended recipient is found to have died, directs the messenger to give it to his heirs.
Whether the borrower died, etc. Although in ordinary agency, when the principal dies the heirs can revoke the agency, as explained at the beginning of siman 122, here it is different because the messenger already acquired for the recipient. See Responsa Maharshal at the end of siman 143.
Borrower or creditor, etc. Although the discussion began with both debt and deposit, the ruling concludes with borrower and creditor because that case is categorical. With a deposit there are circumstances in which retraction is possible, as explained in seif 2.
Or the creditor. The sender cannot argue, “I gave it to you only so that you would deliver it to Shimon himself, not to his heirs,” as he can at the end of seif 8 concerning a gift. A loan or deposit is different.
Nevertheless, Reuven remains responsible, etc. The Sema explains that according to this view Levi becomes liable only if Reuven later becomes poor; otherwise, even if Levi returned the money, Shimon can claim only against Reuven. The Bach adopts this reading. I see no basis for that conclusion. The opposite is more reasonable. Since the messenger acquired the money for Shimon and the borrower could no longer retract, the money became Shimon’s. When Levi returned it to Reuven, he effectively gave Shimon’s money to Reuven, and Shimon may pursue whichever of them he chooses. In my view, the first opinion agrees. Otherwise the Tur and Mechaber should have stated expressly that liability exists only when Reuven became poor. It is difficult to say that the second opinion disputes an implication of silence, especially since no earlier authority is cited for such a limitation. Hagahot Ashri, citing Or Zarua, expressly rules that the creditor may collect from whichever party he wishes. The Tur’s mention that Reuven became poor states the usual case, because otherwise the creditor would normally prefer to collect directly from Reuven. The Tur then contrasts the Rashba’s rule that if the messenger returned the money because of threats he is exempt with the Rambam, who states without qualification that when it is returned both are responsible. This implies that according to the Rambam even threats do not exempt the messenger.
He remains responsible, etc. Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, book 2, siman 99, discusses a borrower whose creditor traveled overseas before the due date, while there was concern that the currency would later be devalued. He rules that when the debt matures the borrower may acquire the money for the creditor through another person by saying “take” or “acquire,” thereby exempting himself from the later governmental devaluation. He compares this to a borrower of an object who, after the borrowing term, returns it without the owner’s knowledge and is thereafter exempt from unavoidable accidents. He further reasons that once the money has been removed from the borrower’s control and acquired for the creditor, the concern that the borrower might use it for a business opportunity no longer exists; and since a currency devaluation does not physically alter the coin, the borrower can say, “Here is your property,” as in the case of a stolen coin that later becomes invalid. I do not accept this. A borrowed object returns in its own form, and when the term ends, wherever it stands it is the owner’s property. A monetary loan, however, is given to be spent. The authorities state without qualification that the borrower remains liable even after he hands the money to another and says “take” or “acquire,” which necessarily includes unavoidable accidents. The money therefore remains legally at the borrower’s risk until it reaches the creditor, and the same applies to a governmental devaluation. Nor is this comparable to a stolen coin that remains physically intact, because that rule applies to the very coin that was stolen. A monetary borrower ordinarily repays with other money, and even if he sets aside the very coins originally borrowed, the character of a consumptive loan keeps the risk upon him. The Beit Yosef’s additional reasoning in siman 74 concerning a creditor who refuses to take money offered to him is needed only because the creditor there was actually told to take it and refused. That is not our case.
But if he said, “return,” etc. The Sema leaves this ruling unresolved, arguing that the Ritva said only that “return” is no stronger than “take” in a gift, but for a debt or deposit perhaps “return” works like “take.” In my view the Rama is correct. The Ritva writes that Rachel did not acquire the deed, even though a kinyan had been made, because the deed did not reach her during her lifetime and the sender did not use any language of acquisition, saying only “return,” which is not language of acquisition at all. He proves this from the first chapter of Gittin and from the Yerushalmi, which treats a maneh and a deed alike. The end of his discussion makes clear that saying “take this deed of gift to so and so” can result in delivery to the recipient’s heirs, whereas “return this deed” is weaker. If so, the same distinction applies to money, since the Ritva expressly equates money and a deed. The phrase “return” is simply not language of acquisition. I wrote similarly above in siman 124, seif katan 44, in the name of Rabbeinu Eliezer ben Natan, that saying, “Go pay my creditor,” is not equivalent to “acquire,” and “return” is certainly no stronger.
And if because of coercion, etc., he is exempt. This does not contradict the Rama’s nearby ruling, based on the Mordechai, that in another case the law favors the depositor. The Sema distinguishes by saying that there Reuven appointed Levi as agent to receive the money, so once Levi received it, it belonged to Reuven, and returning it to Shimon because of threats would be saving himself with another’s money. I do not accept this distinction. Once the messenger acquired for the creditor or depositor, the money is fully that person’s, whether or not he was formally appointed as agent. The Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri themselves apply the rule even where the messenger was not appointed but merely acquired the money through the instruction “take.” Moreover, even an appointed agent who returns the money because he is actually coerced to surrender those very funds is exempt. This is not the rule of saving oneself with another’s property, which concerns an external danger, such as a pursued person breaking another’s vessels. Here the agent is merely a custodian and is forced to return the very money he holds; the Torah exempts one who acts under such compulsion. The Rama’s nearby ruling concerns a different case, where the pressure is not directed at the specific money but consists of the other party’s threat to deny a separate debt. That is not considered coercion. This distinction is also reflected in the Ir Shushan and appears again at the end of siman 126.
And some disagree, etc. See seif katan 8 for the precise point of disagreement, and see Responsa Maharshal, book 2.
The law favors Reuven. The messenger acquired for him because the money represented a debt. Even if it was a deposit, where the depositor himself instructed the depositary to send it and the depositary said “give,” the messenger acquires and the depositary cannot retract. The Gemara’s concern that a depositor does not want his property in another’s hands applies where the depositary independently says “take.” Where the depositor himself requested that the deposit be brought and the depositary then says “give,” it is equivalent to acquisition. The messenger’s statement, “I did not acquire for you on condition that I would suffer a loss,” is merely an unexpressed intention and has no legal effect. So rule the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri.
