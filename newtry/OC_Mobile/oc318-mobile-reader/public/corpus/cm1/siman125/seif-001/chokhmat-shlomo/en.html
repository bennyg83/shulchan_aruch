Seif 1: Reuven owed a maneh to Shimon, etc. Note: Gittin 43b asks whether Rav is uncertain if the instruction “Take this to him” has the force of “Acquire it for him.” The Gemara challenges this from Rav’s ruling: if a debtor says, “Take a maneh to so-and-so whom I owe,” the debtor remains responsible if the money is lost, but he cannot retract. The Gemara answers that in monetary doubt we rule leniently, whereas in prohibitory doubt we rule stringently. This is very difficult to me. Why should the monetary doubt be resolved leniently for the debtor? The money has a prior-owner presumption. Until now it belonged to the debtor, and it is uncertain whether the creditor acquired it through the agent. The original owner should therefore be considered the party in possession. I later saw that Pnei Yehoshua noticed part of this difficulty and wrote that the agent is considered the possessor because his position resembles one who seizes for a creditor where others are harmed, whereas in a gift case the property remains in the prior owner’s possession. At first glance this is difficult to understand. If the agent has not actually seized the money on behalf of the creditor, in what sense is he like one who seizes for a creditor? If he has in fact seized it for the creditor, there is no need for the wording “Take it to him” at all, because seizure on behalf of a creditor is effective where it does not harm other creditors. The intention may be understood by comparison to the rule concerning a non-Jew who received money for land but before the buyer took possession another Jew took possession of the land. The non-Jew, having received the money, removed himself from the property, while the Jewish buyer did not yet acquire until taking possession. So too here, when the debtor gives money to an agent to deliver to his creditor, knowing that the agent would have the legal power to seize it for the creditor where no other creditor is harmed, the debtor removes his own dominion from the money. The money therefore leaves the debtor’s possession even if the agent has not yet affirmatively seized it for the creditor. For that reason the debtor no longer has a prior-owner presumption. Although he may still have a legal power to retract, the money is no longer in his possession. If we said that the ability to retract itself meant that the money remained in his possession, the original question would return: since it is uncertain whether he can retract, we should follow the prior-owner presumption. We are therefore compelled to say that even if he can retract, his delivery to the agent removed the money from his possession. A new rule appears to follow. If a debtor gives money to an agent to deliver to a creditor in a form that does not yet cause the creditor to acquire it, so that the debtor technically retains a right to retract, nevertheless if the debtor later tries to transfer that same money to another person by an act of acquisition, the later transfer is ineffective because the money is no longer in his possession. It resembles property that belongs to a person but is not in his possession, which he cannot sell or give away. This is not comparable to a deposit or ordinary collateral, which remains legally in the owner’s possession. Here the Gemara’s treatment itself shows that the money is considered to have left the debtor’s possession, since he no longer has the prior-owner presumption. If the agent’s premises were still considered the debtor’s domain with respect to these funds, the debtor would remain the possessor and the Gemara could not call the agent the possessor. It therefore is not his domain. A further practical consequence is that in such a case the debtor would not violate the prohibitions of owning chametz on Pesach with respect to those funds or property, even according to the Rosh, because they are no longer considered in his possession. Although he can retract, this resembles ownerless property: a person may be able to reacquire it, but until he does so it is not his in a way that permits him to transfer it to another. It is not literally ownerless, since strangers cannot simply acquire it, but it resembles stolen property before the owner has despaired, which belongs to the owner yet is outside his possession and therefore cannot be sold. So too here. Pnei Yehoshua writes that according to Shmuel, because the debtor remains responsible for the funds, the agent cannot seize them for the creditor, since that would be seizing for one creditor in a way that harms another. But we do not rule that way. In siman 105 we rule that one who seizes for a creditor where no other creditor is harmed acquires for him, even though the debtor is thereby disadvantaged because he remains responsible for the funds. It follows that this is not considered the type of harm that invalidates seizure. Since the agent has the power to seize the money for the creditor and the debtor knew this when he handed it over, the debtor relinquished his dominion over it. It is therefore his property outside his possession and cannot be conveyed to another. This appears to me a new ruling, but it is compelled by the Gemara’s reasoning. I later saw that the work Tov Gittin also noticed the underlying question and answered with difficulty. His explanation is problematic, because if the agent had actually seized the money for the creditor, the debtor could not take it back even without the phrase “Take it to him,” so the entire inquiry whether that phrase means “acquire” would be unnecessary. The explanation I have given is therefore compelling, with Hashem’s help.
There, in the gloss: All these expressions are like “Take it to him” and are equivalent to “Acquire it for him.” Note: Regarding whether the expression “Return it to so-and-so” is equivalent to “Acquire it for him,” the Sema and Taz disagree with the Shach. Urim VeTumim decides like the Sema. See my eighth-edition responsa to Even HaEzer, siman 85, where I concluded like Urim VeTumim that if the speaker mentions that the item is being returned “for his debt,” even the Shach agrees, because the language itself acknowledges the debt. Thus “return it” is comparable to an admission of indebtedness. I also wrote there that if one says, “Take to so-and-so what I owe him,” he cannot retract even within the period immediately following the statement. See there for the reason.
