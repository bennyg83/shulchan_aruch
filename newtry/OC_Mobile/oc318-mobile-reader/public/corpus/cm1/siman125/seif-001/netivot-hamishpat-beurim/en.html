This maneh. See Shach seif katan 2 through his statement that where the sender gave the messenger a deed of gift to deliver, the rule is like a debt. There is a textual omission. It should read, in substance, "acquire this deed for so and so," as he also writes in seif katan 9. The primary ruling here is as Tumim writes: a deed of gift has the law of a gift itself, and we do not say that the words "give" or "bring" operate as immediate acquisition. Tumim proves this from Tosafot and the Rosh, who needed a special reason to compare a deed of emancipation to a debt, namely that the slave presumably rendered service to his master. This shows that an ordinary deed of gift, although like a deed of emancipation the disposition is written in the document itself, is not treated like a debt, because the rationale that the recipient has already provided benefit does not ordinarily apply to a gift, as the Rosh writes in Gittin. I can further prove this from Rashi, Rif, and Rambam, who explain the Mishnah that certain documents are not delivered after the giver's death. Even though "give" can operate like "acquire," the get is not effective until it reaches the recipient. In Gittin 9b, when the Gemara asks whether there are no further cases in which gittin and emancipation documents are alike, it raises the rule that they are not delivered after death and answers that the Mishnah did not list a law that applies to ordinary documents. According to Shach, however, in a deed of gift given by a healthy person "give" would operate like "acquire," unlike a get or deed of emancipation, and the Gemara's answer would be difficult. Rather, a deed that itself effects a gift is like the gift, and "give" does not mean immediate acquisition. To avoid making Shach's words entirely difficult, however, it appears that he is speaking not of an instrument that presently conveys the land, such as one that states "my field is given to you," but of an evidentiary deed for a gift already completed by kinyan sudar. This is indicated by Ritva's language cited by Shach in seif katan 9, that "Rachel did not acquire the body of the document although there was a kinyan." In such a case the issue is only acquisition of the physical evidentiary document, similar to the rule in siman 243 concerning one who says to another, "acquire the document itself." Regarding that document Shach is correct that "give" is equivalent to "acquire." Since the underlying gift recorded in the document was already completed, the recipient has presumably already provided the donor the benefit that explains why a gift may carry creditor-like rights. Moreover, only where the command itself removes property from the donor's estate do we refuse to treat "give" as "acquire." Here the gift itself has already passed, and delivery of the evidentiary deed merely gives the recipient proof of a gift already his. Therefore "give" can operate as "acquire" with respect to the document itself. The difficulties from a deed of emancipation then fall away. A deed of emancipation presently effects the emancipation, so it resembles a gift and would not ordinarily be governed by "give as acquire" except for Tosafot's special rationale that every slave is presumed to have provided benefit to his master. This is the clear intent of Shach.
And Reuven became impoverished. See Shach seif katan 7 and Taz. Taz's position is that the messenger is negligent only where he returned the money to the borrower so that it would once again be held as a loan, since loan money is given for expenditure. The same applies where the recipient of a deposit had already established himself as a denier. In those cases the messenger is liable even if Reuven did not later become impoverished, because the matter began with negligence and the eventual loss resulted from that negligence. But if he returned the money merely to be held as a deposit by the borrower, in a form in which the borrower had no right to use it, the messenger is exempt. The creditor cannot say, "I do not trust him," because the borrower was already someone with whom the creditor was accustomed to entrust property. If the borrower later became impoverished, liability depends on the dispute in siman 291 seif 24 regarding one who entrusts property to his wife or children and they later have no means to pay.
And if it was because of coercion against him. See Shach seif katan 10 through his statement that there the coercion is not directed against this specific money, because the coercer cannot force him to return the very money he received but merely threatens to deny a separate debt, and this is not called unavoidable coercion. At first glance these words are difficult. Although siman 388 says that financial coercion is not considered coercion, that rule concerns one forced to disclose another person's property that was not entrusted to him. With a deposit, where the coercion arises because of the deposit, the custodian is exempt even when the coercion is financial, as explained in siman 292 seif 9 and siman 126 seif 22. What difference is there whether the coercer takes the custodian's own money until he returns the deposit, or threatens to deny a debt owed to him unless he returns it? It therefore seems that Shach means that financial coercion excuses the guardian only where, had the coercer not found the guardian's own funds to seize, he would have searched until he found the deposit or would have subjected the guardian to bodily coercion until he revealed it. In such a case the deposit itself would have been lost if the guardian's own money had not been available, so when the robber takes the guardian's money and leaves the deposit, the guardian is effectively saving the deposit with his own money, somewhat like a shepherd who hires other shepherds or uses staffs to save the flock. But where the coercer has no power to force him physically over the deposit, such as where the sender can only threaten to deny a debt he owes the messenger, then if the messenger uses the deposit money in order to prevent denial of his own debt, he is using the deposit to save his own property. This appears to be Shach's distinction. There is a second resolution as well. I wrote in siman 121 that an agent appointed by the sender merely to carry money to the creditor may not use the money, even if he is a shopkeeper and the money is loose, as Beit Yosef writes in Yoreh Deah siman 169. All the more so where the sender says "bring" or "acquire," making the money the creditor's, while the creditor himself never deposited loose money with the messenger. But an agent appointed by the creditor to receive the money may use loose funds. Accordingly, the Mechaber here speaks of a delivery agent, who is only a shomer chinam, and when he is coerced over the deposit he is exempt, like siman 292 seif 9. Rama, however, speaks of a receiving agent who may use the money, and who may even pay the creditor with other funds. According to the view that such an agent is liable even for unavoidable accidents, he is certainly liable, just as with loan money in siman 126 seif 22. Even according to the view that he is only a shomer sachar, he remains liable where the coercion is directed at the guardian rather than at the object. A shomer sachar is exempt only where the unavoidable accident befalls the guarded object itself. If the guardian becomes ill or sleeps at a normal time, which are extreme circumstances affecting the guardian personally, he can nevertheless remain liable. For practical halachah, both distinctions are correct. Where the messenger has a right to use the money, he is certainly liable to replace it. Where he may not use it and it has the law of a deposit, the rule follows the distinction I gave in explaining Shach.
