That he owed, etc., a deposit, etc. But regarding a gift, the instruction 'take it to him' is not equivalent to acquisition on his behalf, as stated below in seif 5. See Shach; this is straightforward.
Take this maneh. Likewise, if he says, 'Give it to him,' with respect to a debt or deposit, this is equivalent to acquiring it on his behalf. Some say that with the words 'give' and 'take,' it is effective only if he is presently handing the object to the messenger. So writes the Shach. Regarding a gentile, who is not subject to the laws of agency, whether 'give' or 'take' is equivalent to acquisition, see above siman 121 and Mabit, part 1, siman 145. The Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 16 of Hilchot Malveh that just as the borrower can no longer retract, so too other creditors cannot seize this maneh in such a case.
This maneh, etc. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that if one gives his agent a deed of gift or another document to deliver to a certain person, the recipient acquires it. The Sema, in the name of the Darchei Moshe, explains that this refers to where he said, 'Acquire it for him.' The Shach, seif katan 3, writes that the Darchei Moshe and Sema refer only to the latter part of Rabbeinu Yerucham's statement concerning an ordinary document, but where one gives a deed of gift, delivery itself is effective just as with a debt. He supports this from the Mordechai and Ritva, who cite the Yerushalmi. See there at length. See Tumim, seif katan 1, where I showed that the Sema is correct, that there is no proof at all from the Mordechai, Ritva, or Yerushalmi, and that the Shach's words are difficult.
Whether the borrower died. The borrower's heirs cannot say that the agency was terminated by his death, as in ordinary agency above at the beginning of siman 122, because here the messenger already acquired the money on behalf of the recipient. So write the Sema and Shach, correctly.
Or the lender, etc. The sender cannot say, 'I put it in your hand only to give it to Shimon, not to his heirs,' because the moment the messenger acquired it for Shimon, Shimon's heirs automatically inherit his right. See Sema.
But if he said, 'Return it,' etc. The Sema leaves this law requiring study and writes that the Ritva said this only regarding a gift, whereas concerning a debt or deposit the expression 'return it' is effective like 'take it.' The Shach, seif katan 9, disagrees and rules in accordance with the Rama. See Tumim, seif katan 2, where I clarified that the Sema is correct.
Because of compulsion, etc. See what I wrote in Tumim, seif katan 3, resolving the difficulty on the Rambam from the end of the first chapter of Gittin. One cannot properly answer, as the Taz does, by distinguishing among degrees of threats, for who can set a measure for a threat when the coercer has the power to carry it out? See there for my correct explanation.
And some say that when he returned the maneh, etc. The Sema, Bach, Lechem Mishneh, and other authorities understand that according to this opinion, even if the borrower did not become poor, the creditor may collect either from the first debtor or from the messenger. According to the first opinion, however, if the first debtor did not become poor, he may collect only from Reuven. The Shach, seif katan 7, disagrees. See Tumim, where I agreed with the Shach that even if the first debtor did not become poor, the creditor may demand payment from the debtor whose money remains in his possession. I nevertheless wrote that there is a distinction: according to the first opinion, if the messenger returned the money to the original borrower and it was an oral loan, and the money was afterward lost by unavoidable accident in the borrower's hands, the messenger is exempt. According to the alternative opinion, which is the Rambam's view, the Rambam writes without qualification that both are liable for responsibility, implying that both are equal. Just as the borrower is liable, so too the second party is liable. See Tumim, seif katan 3, where I explained the reasoning.
The law is with Reuven. The Sema writes that this case differs from the Mechaber's earlier ruling concerning compulsion. Here, Reuven appointed Levi as his agent to receive the money, so as soon as Levi received it the money became Reuven's. If Levi returns it, he is saving himself with another person's property. The Shach rejects this and writes that here the coercion is not directed at this particular money. Rather, the other party threatened to deny Levi's own debt, and this is not considered coercion concerning the money in his hand. See there. In Tumim, seif katan 4, I wrote an additional distinction. In the Mordechai's case, the other party expressly demanded return of the money because he had made a mistake, so the loss was certain, and therefore the threat did not exempt Levi. By contrast, the Mechaber and Gemara discuss a case where the loss was not certain. See there.
