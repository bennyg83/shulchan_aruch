Take this maneh to Shimon. The Sema writes in the name of Rabbenu Yerucham that if one gives an agent a deed of gift, or some other document, to deliver to another person, the recipient acquires. The Sema, following the Darkei Moshe, explains that the case is where the sender said, "Acquire it for him," and this is correct. The Shach in seif katan 3 cites the Mordechai, who quotes a Tosefta: "Give this maneh to so and so, etc.; give this deed of gift to so and so; receive this deed of gift for so and so. If the giver wishes to retract, he may retract." The Mordechai says: "I wonder why, with a deed of gift, he may retract. There is a version of the Tosefta that says he may not retract, but it is not accepted, etc." The Shach concludes that according to the version reading "he may not retract," the Darkei Moshe and Sema's explanation applies only to the end of Rabbenu Yerucham's words concerning an ordinary document, but when he gives a deed of gift it is effective like a debt even without saying "acquire." He also seeks support from the Ritva's citation: "Give this maneh to so and so, bring this maneh, etc.; give this deed, etc.; and he went and found that the sender had died, he gives it to the heirs." These words are astonishing. How could one imagine that a deed of gift is treated like a debt? If "give" does not mean "acquire" with a gift, why should giving the instrument of gift itself suddenly mean "acquire"? Tosafot in Gittin 12a, beginning "Anyone who says," clearly treat the two as one matter. From here Ri brings proof against Rabbenu Tam from the Tosefta, which says, "Give this deed of gift to so and so," and rules that the giver may retract. The Rashba and Rosh already answer the implication from a deed of emancipation: a deed of emancipation indeed resembles a gift, but they explain that emancipation is treated like payment of a debt; see there. The matter is therefore clear as I have written. The Mordechai actually says only: "I wonder, when he says 'acquire this deed of gift,' why can he retract?" His question refers to the end of the Tosefta, which states, "Acquire this deed of gift for so and so; if he wishes to retract he may retract." That is what troubles him. He therefore notes another version of the Tosefta that correctly reads "he may not retract," as indeed appears in our printed Tosefta. His difficulty is then only why the baraita did not already list the law of a gift in its earlier clause together with loans and deposits, where even "bring" prevents retraction. In the Mordechai's version the Tosefta did not contain a separate phrase "acquire the gift" beyond "acquire this deed of gift." The apparent extra wording in our text is likely a printer's addition. Hence the Mordechai asks only about a deed of gift and not about an ordinary gift. This is simple. The Shach's proof from the Yerushalmi cited by the Ritva is likewise surprising. The Yerushalmi plainly follows the opinion that a gift is treated like a debt and that even "bring" is equivalent to "acquire," as the Yerushalmi states expressly. We do not rule like that view. We rule like Rabbi Shimon HaNasi that "bring" is not equivalent to "acquire" with a gift. The same therefore applies to "give" according to Rabbenu Tam. All the Shach's discussion here is a serious error.
It is not equivalent to "acquire." The Sema in seif katan 7 challenges the Rama, and the Sema is correct. I would add that even according to the Ritva, who holds that the phrase "return it" is weaker than "bring it," this distinction applies to a gift. What meaning does "return" have in a gift, when the recipient never previously possessed the item? But with a loan, what expression could be more appropriate than "return," since a loan is inherently subject to return? Why should "return this maneh to so and so" be weaker than "bring this maneh to so and so"? The Shach in seif katan 9 attempts to defend the Rama, but his words are not understandable. He writes that the Ritva learned from the Yerushalmi that money and a document have the same law, because the Yerushalmi teaches: "Bring a maneh to so and so; give a deed of gift; bring a deed of gift to so and so; if he dies, return them to his heirs." The Shach concludes that this proves one cannot retract after saying "bring a deed of gift," whereas the Ritva rules that one can retract after saying "return." He therefore infers that even with a debt, "return" is a weaker expression than "bring." He did not examine the passage sufficiently. He assumes that the Ritva rules like that Yerushalmi that "bring" is equivalent to "acquire" for a gift, so that retraction is impossible, and this assumption leads him to distinguish a deed of gift as though it were like a debt. None of this is correct, as explained above. The Yerushalmi holds that a gift is like a debt, and we do not rule like that Yerushalmi. We rule that a gift is not like a debt. The Ritva cites the Yerushalmi only to establish that money and a document are governed alike. Thus according to our law, where "bring" and "return" are not equivalent to "acquire" for a gift, the same is true of a deed of gift. The only lesson taken from the Yerushalmi is the equality of the instrument and the money. The Shach wrote this without sufficient examination.
Both of them are liable for its loss, etc. The Sema, Bach, Lechem Mishneh, and other poskim understand that the practical difference between the two views is where the original borrower has not become impoverished. According to one view, the creditor may collect from whichever of the two he wishes; according to the other, he may not. The Shach in seif katan 7 writes that this distinction is not understandable, because perhaps one of them will not have sufficient assets for the creditor to collect as he wishes. His words are correct. It is also obvious that where the borrower has not become impoverished, the creditor should claim against the borrower, in whose hands his money now rests. Why should he instead claim against the second person who no longer has the money, as the Shach writes. In my view there is another distinction. This messenger, after receiving the money, has no more than the liability of a paid guardian if the money was loose and available for use. We rule like Rav Nachman that if it is lost through unavoidable accident he is exempt. If an accident had occurred while the money was still in the messenger's hands, he would have been exempt. Accordingly, if he returned the money to the original borrower, and the original debt was oral, and the money was then lost through unavoidable accident in the borrower's hands, the messenger who returned it should be exempt. Although the borrower himself is immediately liable for unavoidable accidents, because once the money was returned to him he again became a borrower of it even before he actually spent it, nevertheless, from the messenger's perspective there is no difference whether the accident occurred in his own hands or in the hands of the second holder. If one says that this is a guardian who transferred to another guardian, the usual claim that "I trusted you but not him" does not apply here. The lender originally lent the money to this very borrower and trusted him not to deny the debt at all. How then can he now say that he does not trust him? Thus the messenger is exempt from unavoidable accidents. Nor was the messenger's initial conduct negligence. If his own liability was only that of a paid guardian, giving the money back to the borrower is no more negligent than giving it to another guardian whom the lender trusts. Therefore, in such a case the messenger is exempt. But if no unavoidable accident occurred after the money was returned and the only problem is that the borrower later lacks assets with which to pay, the messenger is plainly liable. Returning the money in a manner that leaves the lender exposed to the borrower's later insolvency is complete negligence. The deposit itself did not suffer an accident; rather, the borrower spent it, and the messenger is liable for that type of loss, namely theft or loss. This is especially so according to the Tur's view below in siman 291 that the first guardian is liable for the second guardian where the second has no means to pay. All the more so here. The first opinion therefore correctly emphasizes the case where the borrower became impoverished. In that case, because the second person acted negligently by consuming the money and becoming unable to pay, the first messenger is likewise liable. But if the oral borrower suffered a genuine unavoidable accident, since the claim "I trusted you but not him" does not apply, it is like a guardian who transferred to another trusted guardian, and the messenger is exempt from unavoidable accidents. This appears to me the clear intent of the first view. The Rambam, however, writes without qualification that both are liable for its loss. His language implies that the first and second have equal liability. Just as the borrower is liable for every type of loss, so the messenger who returned the money is likewise liable for every type, because the Rambam equates them. The reason must be that the Rambam holds that a guardian may transfer to another guardian only sealed money or some other deposit that the second guardian has no right to use. But loose money that the guardian may use cannot be entrusted to another person. Who knows what the second person will do with it? The owner may suddenly come to demand his money and find that it is no longer available. This is especially true where the messenger returns it to the original borrower, who already spent the money once before and will now likely do so again, making collection difficult. The case is therefore one whose beginning was negligence and whose end was an unavoidable accident, where the accident resulted from the negligence, and the messenger is liable. This explains why the Rambam writes simply that both are liable without distinguishing one type of liability from another. Even unavoidable accidents are included. This appears also to explain the Raavad's objection. He writes that there is no liability here except where the borrower denies receipt. The Knesset HaGedolah and other commentators already explain that the Raavad likewise agrees where the borrower becomes impoverished. His intent is only to exclude liability for unavoidable accident, as I explained above, because in his view the messenger's conduct was not complete negligence. This is precise. In my view this is the straightforward meaning of the Tur's presentation. The Shach, however, seeks to say that according to the Rambam, even if the borrower threatened the messenger, the messenger is not thereby exempt from liability. The Rambam's unqualified wording indeed suggests this, although the Maggid Mishneh writes that the Rambam agrees that genuine coercion exempts. We must still understand the Gemara, which says regarding the messenger who returned the silver cup under threat, "You acted properly." The Taz attempts to distinguish one threat from another, but this is strained. Who can establish a measure for the degree of threat when the person has the power to carry it out and is making a serious threat? Such a case is like the one in the Gemara. It therefore appears that the Rambam holds that a paid guardian remains liable for many events that are called "unavoidable" only because they happen to the guardian personally rather than to the deposited object. Even if the guardian becomes ill or the like, the commentators cited in Or Zarua write that any accident that does not affect the deposit itself, although it affects the guardian, is not classified as an unavoidable accident for a paid guardian but rather as ordinary theft or loss, for which he is liable. This distinction applies to a paid guardian. A gratuitous guardian, however, is certainly exempt, because there was no negligence and the Torah exempts him from theft or loss. Accordingly, the matter is resolved. A threat to beat or flog the messenger is entirely an event affecting the body of the guardian. No accident has occurred to the deposited property itself. A paid guardian therefore remains liable according to the Rambam, because he was obligated to protect the owner's deposit and not surrender it in a way that causes the owner loss. This personal threat does not count as an unavoidable accident affecting the deposit. Money sent through a receiving messenger places him in the status of a paid guardian, because he may use the money. In such a case, where he is an agent to receive the funds, he is certainly permitted to use them, as the Rashba and Ran write above regarding one who collects through authorization. He is therefore a paid guardian and remains liable. The Gemara's case, by contrast, involved a silver cup. The messenger is only a gratuitous guardian of such an object. Since returning it to the original holder was not negligence, he is exempt. This reasoning is sound and clear in itself, but it is difficult to attribute to the Rambam the view that the second holder may use the money whenever he is a private individual. One could perhaps compare it to lost-property funds, where the finder has expended effort and may therefore use the money. Yet the Rosh and Mordechai's rule that in our times everyone has the status of a moneychanger is not mentioned by the Rambam. There is another possible explanation. In the Gemara's case the subject was a deposit. By what basis could the messenger be liable after returning it? The rule of a guardian who transfers to another guardian cannot be invoked through the claim "I trusted you but not him," because the owner himself originally deposited the object with that first holder. Admittedly, once the owner told the messengers to bring it to him, he revealed that he no longer wished his deposit to remain there. That makes it improper initially to return it. But after the fact, the messenger is not thereby fully liable. All the poskim write that the mere statement "I do not want my deposit in that person's hands" does not transform return to him into complete negligence. The Gemara's statement "You acted properly" may mean that even initially, once the messenger faced threats, he was permitted to return it. The owner's earlier instruction, "Bring it to me," showed that he preferred the deposit not remain with the original holder, but it did not obligate the messenger to endure beatings and suffering in order to enforce that preference. A loan is different. According to the Rambam, as explained above, returning the money to the borrower is not treated merely as one guardian transferring to another guardian. It is complete negligence. Once the messenger has incurred a payment obligation as a complete negligent party, a threat to his own body cannot exempt him from that already applicable liability. This is precise. The matter still requires study, because these laws require careful consideration.
The law is with Reuven. See Sema and Shach regarding the distinction they draw between the threat mentioned by the Mechaber, where the agent is exempt, and the threat mentioned here in the Rama, where he is liable. Anyone who examines Hagahot Mordechai in its place will see an additional distinction. In the incident stated in the Shulchan Aruch, based on the Gemara, the depositor's loss was not certain. The parties sought return of the deposit because the messenger did not wish to accept liability for unavoidable accidents on the road at all, as the Gemara explains. Had he agreed to accept that liability, they would have left it in his hands. Thus returning the deposit did not clearly cause a loss to the lender or depositor, and under threat he could return it. In the Mordechai's incident, however, the person expressly stated that he sought return of the money because he had made a mistake and Reuven was not entitled to anything. Returning the money would therefore certainly and completely destroy Reuven's property. In such a case a threat does not exempt the agent for returning the deposit and thereby directly causing his fellow a definite loss. This is simple.
