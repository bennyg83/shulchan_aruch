To retain. See siman 83, end of seif 2, where Rama discusses an agent who retains money because of a debt owed by the principal. The Mechaber discusses this at the end of siman 58. In siman 99, seif 3, Tur and Shulchan Aruch discuss one who seizes what an agent handed him, claiming that the agent or broker owes him money. Sema.
He has. Shach writes that at first glance Tur and Shulchan Aruch here appear to hold that where the first debtor has other property, the second debtor is not subject to Rabbi Nathan's rule. This cannot be correct, however, as Shach proves at length in siman 86 seif 2, for even when the first debtor has property, Rabbi Nathan's rule applies. Only where collection from the second debtor would cause him a loss did the Sages enact that one does not collect from him when the first debtor has assets, because the second debtor is treated like encumbered property where unencumbered property is available. See there at length.
Compels. One should not object from what was written above, seif katan 105, that if the depositary handed the deposit to the creditor he is exempt. There the meaning is: since you certainly owe him and in any event must pay him, whether from this deposit or from other property, what loss have I caused you? If you wish to pay him from other assets, the deposit is ready to be returned to you. Here, however, the case is an agent who seized the money for himself and insists upon taking these very funds rather than collecting from other assets. This is the Derishah, and Taz rules likewise.
Others. Although the agent is believed against the creditor to say, I am retaining these funds for my debt, by a migo that he could have said, I repaid you, nevertheless, until the funds actually reach the creditor his agency has not terminated. The agent therefore remains the litigant of the principal, and against the principal he has no migo, since he is not believed to say, I returned them to you, because the instruction bring is treated like acquire. Tur. Sema explains that if he did have a migo against the principal as well, for example if the money was delivered to him without witnesses, he would be believed with a rabbinic oath that the creditor owes him, by a migo that he could have denied receipt. Bach disagrees, and Shach accepts Bach's position: once he admits receiving the funds, he cannot rely on such a migo to establish that the creditor owes him, because he has no claim against the principal. See Responsa Maharashach III, simanim 77 and 96, and Maharit siman 105. See Taz. If it is doubtful whether the creditor has other assets, Tur appears to treat him as though he has them, and Divrei Rivot siman 320 likewise indicates this. Knesset HaGedolah.
