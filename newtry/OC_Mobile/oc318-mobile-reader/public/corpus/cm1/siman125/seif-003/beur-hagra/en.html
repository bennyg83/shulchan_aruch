If he said, etc. As stated in Bava Kamma 40b, "Let him say to him: hand over the ox to me," etc.; "This is satisfactory," etc. The reason is as explained above in siman 86, seif 2.
But if he has, etc., for so long as this, etc. Meaning, the funds still remain legally in the sender's domain, as the Gemara and Yerushalmi state, that he remains responsible until the recipient receives them. Accordingly, the agent's attempted collection is from the sender, and he has no right to collect from these funds so long as the debtor has other property available to the creditor, as explained above. Even if he already seized them, he must return them, as is proven from the passage there that says, "This is satisfactory," etc., because, etc. Where the debtor has other assets, Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply, even though the property is already in the possession of beit din. This is not contradicted by the ruling at the end of siman 105, "And if the bailee delivered it," etc., where it appears that even if the debtor has property and Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply, the bailee is exempt. There the ruling only says that the bailee is exempt after the fact; initially he may not deliver it, as stated there. Here the issue is whether the agent is legally required to return it. If he does not return it, the sender is indeed exempt, as stated there and in Bava Kamma: "Let him say, Had you returned it," etc., because the answer is, "In the end," etc. This shows that whenever the creditor is obligated to pay and recovery can be had from him, the sender is exempt, even though the property should not have been given to him under Rabbi Natan's rule because the debtor has other property.
All the more so if, etc. The creditor may claim from the sender, as above, because the funds have not yet left the sender's domain. Although the agent is holding them on account of his own loan, he remains liable, as there: "Let him say, hand over my ox to," etc. The response there is, "Had you returned it," etc., unlike a case where he denied the debt and would have been believed. To the objection that he could hide the property, the Gemara answers with Rabbi Natan's rule, because otherwise he would not be acting lawfully; but to the earlier objection it answers, "He can say to him," etc. Likewise at the beginning of Bava Metzia, "What claim do I have against him? You trusted him," which shows that even though the shopkeeper said that he paid him, the sender remains liable. The same applies to the case of the shopkeeper. See Shach. As against the sender, the agent has no defense. See the gloss to siman 58, seif 1, and what I wrote there that the matter depends upon a dispute among the authorities. See also Rivash, responsum 393.
