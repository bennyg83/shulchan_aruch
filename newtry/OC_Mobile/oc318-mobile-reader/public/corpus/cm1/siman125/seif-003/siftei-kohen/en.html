And he has no other assets, etc. The continuation, “but if he has other assets, etc.,” appears at first glance to reflect the view of the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch that when the creditor has other assets, the second debtor is not subject to the lien of Rabbi Natan. This cannot be maintained, however, as I proved at length above in siman 86, seif 2, seif katan 5. Even when the creditor has other assets, the second debtor is subject to Rabbi Natan’s lien. Rather, where collection from the second debtor would cause him a loss, the Sages instituted that collection should not be made from him when the creditor has other assets, for the debt in his hands is treated like encumbered property, from which one does not collect while unencumbered property is available. Accordingly, it would appear that the ruling of the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch here is incorrect, and that even when the creditor has other assets, the sender cannot compel the agent to return the money. The sender is in any event obligated to pay it to the creditor, and the agent is willing to give it voluntarily, so the sender suffers no loss. All the more so, the Bach’s comment on their phrase “and the time of the loan has arrived” is untenable. He writes that this teaches that where the creditor has other assets, the sender may compel the agent to return the money even when the due date has arrived, because beit din does not collect from this person for the benefit of that person under Rabbi Natan’s lien when the creditor has other assets, even inferior land. This debt is like encumbered property, from which collection is not made when unencumbered property exists, even after the due date; he therefore rejects what the Beit Yosef wrote in siman 86, that once the due date has arrived the debt is not treated as encumbered property. This is not correct. I already proved above in siman 86, seif 2, seif katanim 5 and 7, that where the second debtor suffers no loss, collection is made from him even when the creditor has other assets. I wrote there that this is also the view of the Beit Yosef, and the Bach himself at the beginning of siman 86 cites the Beit Yosef’s words there. Here, the sender, who is the second debtor, has already paid out his money and suffers no loss from collection, even if the creditor has inferior property. The agent may therefore collect from this money. Even if the creditor has ordinary property, the agent may collect from the money, because a creditor’s proper mode of collection is cash. All the more so where the agent has already seized property of the sender. Maharshal already ruled in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 4, siman 19, that if Reuven seized property from Levi on account of a debt owed to him by Shimon, and Levi owes Shimon, the property is not removed from Reuven even if Shimon has other assets. Although I wrote above in siman 86, seif katan 5, that Maharshal overlooked the words of the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur here, that was only according to an interpretation of their words which, in truth, cannot be sustained as their view. As a matter of law, I concluded there in accordance with Maharshal. It therefore appears that the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch here also agree that even when the creditor has other assets, if the sender wishes to pay, the agent may retain the funds whenever he could have collected from them had they remained in the hands of his own debtor. Even if his debtor has movable property, and all the more so land or inferior land, he cannot discharge the agent with that property. Since, had this money been in the debtor’s hands, the debtor would have had to pay the money itself, he must likewise pay it now if he has no other money. The case here concerns only whether the agent must first return the money to the debtor and then collect it again. Since the first debtor is present and says that he will discharge the agent with his own money, as the law permits, the agent must return the sender’s money to him. Until it is delivered to the creditor, the sender has not been discharged from the debt. Although Rabbi Natan’s lien applies even when the creditor has assets, as explained, if the second debtor pays the first debtor, the first debtor is not necessarily obligated to give this particular money to his creditor. The practical difference may be where the creditor wants the money merely to display it and will later pay the agent, or where the creditor specifically desires the very coins or article sent by the sender and wishes to discharge the agent with his own money, as he is entitled to do. If their words are not interpreted in this manner, but rather literally, as indeed the language of the Baal HaTerumot appears to indicate, their ruling is not correct. The law is as I have written and as I proved at length above in siman 86, seif katan 5. See there.
But if he has other assets, etc. The Bach writes that this distinction applies only where the sender transmitted payment of a loan. With respect to a deposit, even if the creditor has other assets, collection is made under Rabbi Natan’s lien, as stated by the Rashba, the Mechaber above in siman 86, seif 4, and the Rama in siman 101, seif 4. The Bach follows his position stated above. According to what I wrote immediately before this, however, there is no distinction here between a loan and a deposit. Examine this carefully.
"And thou shalt deliver them to death." “And he shall be given to him, and he shall be given to him, and he shall be given to him, and shall be given to him, and he shall be given to him, and that he shall be exalted to him, and that he shall be exalted to him, and that he shall be given to him, and that he shall not be ground.”
Even if he has no other assets. Although the agent would be believed against the creditor to say, “I am holding the money for my own debt,” through a migo that he could have claimed, “I paid you,” he is nevertheless not believed here. So long as the money has not reached the creditor, his agency has not ended, and the agent remains the litigant of the sender. Against the sender he has no migo, because he cannot claim that he returned the money, since “take it” is treated as “acquire it.” This is the Tur’s language. The Sema explains that if the agent also possessed a migo against the sender, such as where the sender delivered the money without witnesses, the agent would be believed, with a Rabbinic oath, that the creditor owes him, through a migo that he could have denied receiving the money. The Bach, however, writes that the agent cannot say, “I never received it from you,” because once he admits receiving it, he is no longer believed to deny the receipt. At first glance, the Tur’s words appear to support the Sema, and the Baal HaTerumot likewise appears to say: “He is not believed through a migo that he could say, ‘I returned it to you,’ because he cannot return it, since ‘take it’ is treated as ‘acquire it,’ and the money stands under the recipient’s responsibility.” The Bach’s point also appears difficult, because every migo involves a present admission, while we believe the claimant because he could have lied. On closer examination, however, the Baal HaTerumot’s words are correct. Otherwise, why should a migo against the sender establish that the creditor owes the agent? The agent has no claim at all against the sender. Since the sender is not discharged from the creditor until the money is delivered, the sender may compel the agent to perform his agency. Until he gives the money to the creditor, he has not performed it. The Baal HaTerumot did not previously say that the agent is believed that the creditor owes him, as the Tur did. His intent may therefore be that if the agent claims that the sender owes him, he is not believed through a migo that he returned the money. Yet through a migo that he never received it, he would be believed when claiming that the sender owes him. The Tur, however, appears to say that he is not believed to claim that the creditor owes him. In that case, even a migo that he never received the money does not help, as the Bach explains, because once he admits receipt he has no claim against the sender. The Tur’s statement that he is not believed through a migo that he returned it should be understood similarly, and the same applies to a migo that he never received it. Alternatively, the Tur too may be discussing a claim that the sender owes him. Although the earlier discussion concerned a claim against the creditor, the present point may be that if he claims the sender owes him, he is not believed through a migo that he returned the money. In such a case, however, he would be believed through a migo that he never received it. But when he claims that the creditor owes him, no migo available against the sender can establish that claim. This also appears from Responsa Maharit, siman 105, and accordingly the Tur’s words here, in the name of the Baal HaTerumot, do not contradict Responsa Rosh, beginning of rule 106, cited above in siman 58, seif 1. This is contrary to Responsa Maharam ibn Sasson, siman 101. Maharam ibn Sasson further asks in siman 106 why the agent is not believed through a migo that the money was lost through unavoidable accident, and leaves the matter unresolved. There is no difficulty. The present passage is not discussing those laws, and may concern circumstances in which such a claim is unavailable, as explained below in siman 296, seif 3. Alternatively, it may be known that he already used the money, in which case he is liable even for unavoidable accidents, as stated below in siman 292, seif 7. Examine this carefully. See also Responsa Maharash Kohen, volume 3, simanim 77 and 96.
