Even if he has no, etc. The Sema in seif katan 19 cites the Tur: "So long as the money has not reached the lender, the messenger's agency has not terminated. Thus he remains the sender's litigant, and against the sender he has no migo, because he is not believed to say, 'I returned it to you,' since 'bring' is equivalent to 'acquire.'" The Sema explains that if the messenger did have a migo against the sender, such as a migo that he could have denied receiving the money or a comparable claim, he would be exempt and believed. The Shach in seif katan 21 disagrees, writing that even if the messenger has a migo, the sender remains liable to the lender and can compel the messenger to carry out his agency. The Shach follows his own position above at the beginning of siman 58, rejecting the Ritva and the second view in the Shulchan Aruch, according to which even though the messenger thereby loses and must reimburse the borrower, the lender can still claim that the money was received on account of another debt. According to what I wrote there, however, the Ritva's view is clear, and especially the Rosh in Ketubot in the incident of the cattle keeper proves like the Ritva. Only where Shimon delivered Reuven's movable property to Levi as a deposit is Levi unable to seize it, because Levi must make a complete return as a depositary to the depositor through Shimon. But if Levi seizes property belonging to Shimon himself, he may retain it even though Shimon thereby loses and must pay Reuven. See there. Here likewise, the sender did not give the money to the messenger so that it should later be returned to the sender, which would require a complete act of restitution. On the contrary, the sender cannot retract at all and the messenger's obligation is to complete the agency. If the messenger's assertion is true that the lender owes him money, then he has fulfilled the agency. What difference is there whether he physically places the money into the lender's hand or retains it himself against the lender's debt? Especially where the lender has no other assets, the messenger's hand is legally like the lender's hand and the agency is fulfilled either way. If the sender will consequently have to pay twice, that does not concern us, just as where another party seizes the sender's own property under the Ritva's rule. These statements apply, however, only if the lender truly owes the messenger. In that event the messenger can say, "I carried out your agency. It is no different whether I gave the funds to the lender or to one of his creditors, and indeed Rabbi Natan's lien gives that creditor a primary claim." The sender's response is that he does not believe the messenger. The messenger cannot simply assert that the lender owes him and thereby say that he fulfilled the agency. We do not allow him to seize and cause the sender loss merely through an unsupported statement. But where the messenger has a migo against the sender, the sender is compelled to believe that his assertion is true. The messenger has then fulfilled the agency as described above, and the sender has no claim against him even though the sender suffers a loss. This is exactly like seizure according to the Ritva. It is simple. There is an additional basis even if one were to say that the Rosh in responsa, klal 46, disagrees with the Ritva, as the Shach argues above in siman 58. The Rosh's reason there is that the first depositary is obligated to repay the depositor. Therefore, how can the second person seize the first depositary's own property? He is not thereby seizing the depositor's property, because the depositor will receive full payment from the first depositary. That reasoning applies specifically where the depositor has other assets and the first depositary is not himself liable to the second person. The first can say, "You are not my litigant. I owe you nothing, and you cannot seize my property on account of the depositor when I will still be compelled to pay the depositor." But where the depositor has no other assets and the first depositary knows that the depositor owes the second, Rabbi Natan's rule makes the first depositary directly liable to the second. He is then fully his depositary and all his property is subject to the second's claim. The second may say to the first, "You are my litigant." The Rosh's argument that the second has nothing to do with the first no longer applies, because the first is directly indebted to him by Rabbi Natan's lien. If the first later suffers unjustly because the depositor denies the matter and forces him to pay a second time, that is merely a false claim against him. The second party is still entitled to collect from his direct obligor. This resembles the Rama's ruling above that where the first party seeks return of the deposit specifically in order to cause the claimant to lose his money, the holder need not return it because the claimant has already acquired a right in it. Here too, the first depositary became obligated through Rabbi Natan's lien. If the depositor later denies the debt and makes a false claim, why should that affect the second creditor's right? Accordingly, in our case where the messenger has a migo and is therefore believed that the lender owes him, the sender is already directly obligated under Rabbi Natan's lien. It is clear that the sender must pay that creditor, and the messenger may retain the money in satisfaction of the claim. The words of the Baal HaTerumot and Tur are therefore upright and correct. This also resolves the question of Maharit and Shach, noted above in siman 58, from the Rosh in the chapter HaKotev. The Rosh holds there that if one seizes property belonging to the cattle keeper, even though the keeper must still pay the orphans, the seizing party retains what he seized. See what I wrote there, that the Rosh distinguishes in accordance with the Ritva. According to what I have now written, the matter can be resolved even without that distinction. The cattle keeper's case concerns orphans who have no other property. Rabbi Natan's lien therefore applies directly, and the Rosh agrees that the seizing creditor may retain the property because the keeper is himself his obligor. However one difficulty remains, both according to the Sema's explanation and according to the Bach and Shach, who understand the Tur's statement "and against the sender he has no migo" as an independent point. If the messenger claims that the sender himself owes him, why does he have no migo that he performed the sender's instruction and paid the lender? The messenger was not required to make payment in the presence of witnesses. Thus even if the lender denies payment, the messenger should be believed through that migo. The same should be true whether he says, according to the Bach, "You, the sender, owe me," or according to the Sema, "The lender owes me." He also has a migo that the money was lost through unavoidable accident. Perhaps one must explain with some strain that the Tur's primary purpose is only to teach that this particular migo, that he could have said "I returned it to you," is not a migo because he had no legal right to return it. In truth, if he has another migo, such as "I paid the lender" or "it was lost through unavoidable accident," he is believed. The discussion thus concerns a case where no such alternative migo exists, for example where the sender expressly said, "Do not pay except before witnesses," and the messenger used the money so that he cannot claim accidental loss. The Tur then teaches that the only remaining supposed migo, "I returned it to you," is not a migo for the reason stated. This excludes the Ritva's view in Kiddushin, who holds that even return to the sender can serve as a migo against him, because the rule that "bring" is equivalent to "acquire" is for the lender's benefit, whereas the borrower cannot complain if the messenger says, "In any event I returned your money to you, so what is your grievance?" The Tur teaches otherwise. Such a migo is weak. Why would the messenger voluntarily return the money and place himself under liability? Even according to the Sema above, who holds that the messenger is liable only if the original borrower became impoverished, why would he enter such a dispute? It is therefore a weak migo and he is not believed. The matter still requires study.
