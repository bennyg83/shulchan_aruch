If he has property. See Shach seif katan 15. I wrote above in siman 86 that the direct lien of Rabbi Natan has only the law of an arev in every respect. Only where the first borrower is violent or refuses to pay does the second debtor become liable directly. But where the first borrower is willing to pay, even with inferior land, the second debtor is not directly encumbered at all, even if the second debtor owns intermediate land. This is demonstrated in Ketubot 110, concerning two people who each hold a debt instrument against the other. Each collects from the other even though each is effectively subject to Rabbi Natan's lien against himself, yet one cannot take his own intermediate land when the other debtor is prepared to pay with inferior land. The Mechaber's ruling is therefore correct.
Even if he has no property. See Sema seif katan 19 through his words, "against the recipient he has no migo." At first glance these words are difficult. Why is this not a migo against the sender, since the messenger says, "I paid the creditor and fulfilled your agency"? Sema's conclusion that in such a case even the sender is exempt is also difficult from siman 121, where even though the messenger says "I paid the creditor" and has a migo against the sender that he could have said "I returned it to the borrower," the sender is not thereby discharged from the creditor. It seems to me that the Perishah explains the relevant migo as follows. When the messenger says to the creditor, "You owe me money," he thereby creates litigation with both creditor and debtor, because the debtor says, "You did not perform my agency." It would be easier for the messenger to deny receipt altogether, in which case he would litigate only with the debtor. This is a strong and superior migo, and it supplies affirmative proof that the messenger is telling the truth when he says the creditor owes him. Therefore even the sender is exempt. By contrast, in siman 121, where the messenger says "I paid the creditor," we suspect he is lying and that the money remains in his hands. His migo is that he could instead have said "I returned it to the borrower." That is not a superior alternative claim and does not affirmatively prove the truth of the payment claim, because from his perspective there is little difference between claiming payment to the creditor and return to the borrower. Rather, the point is evidentiary: since he could have lied in another way to obtain the same benefit, we do not regard him as an interested witness merely because his testimony produces a benefit. His credibility is thus based on witness status, not on migo. But the Torah says that a witness who himself requires an oath is not valid testimony. By contrast, where the migo is to a materially better claim and operates as a true migo, it can establish credibility even against the sender and can work despite an oath obligation, just as "I returned it" is believed by a migo of unavoidable accident. This also explains Sema's opening words. A migo of "I paid the creditor" is not the relevant migo, because that assertion itself appears as testimony about another person's receipt. Indeed, we believe that claim only by a migo of "I returned it to the borrower," showing that "I paid the creditor" is the less comfortable claim because it resembles testimony. This is how Sema can be reconciled. Nevertheless, it appears that the sender is not actually discharged, as Taz writes.
He has no other property. See Shach seif katan 21, who rules that even if the messenger has a migo against the sender, he remains liable because the sender has not been discharged from the creditor. Shach questions the Tur and Sema. At first glance Shach's ruling itself is difficult, for it resembles siman 58 seif 1, where the messenger may say, "What do I have to do with you?" However, as I explained in siman 58, that argument applies only where the creditor is seizing directly from the messenger's or custodian's possession. Movable property in a custodian's hands resembles encumbered movables that a creditor cannot seize from a purchaser. But if Reuven independently undertook responsibility for Shimon's movable property lying in a meadow, Shimon's creditor may seize that property even though doing so causes Reuven a loss. Here, where the sender says "acquire" or "bring," with "bring" operating like "acquire" according to the first opinion, the money becomes the creditor's property. The messenger may therefore seize it for a debt owed by the creditor, and the argument "what do I have to do with you" does not apply, because he is not seizing property that remains in the custodian's domain. According to the alternative opinion cited by the Mechaber, however, "bring" is only a condition and the creditor has not yet acquired the funds, so the messenger cannot seize them because they still belong to the sender. Furthermore, even where the messenger claims "you owe me," this is substantively the same as saying "I paid the creditor," because setting off the creditor's debt is itself a form of payment. There is no difference whether he says "I paid you" and the creditor denies it, or "I paid your wife" or "I paid your creditor" and the creditor denies it. All are treated as payment to him. Therefore, where the messenger has a genuine migo it works. Only a migo that he could have claimed ordinary payment to the creditor is ineffective for the reason explained immediately above. The primary ruling is therefore that of Sema and the Tur, as Tumim also concludes.
