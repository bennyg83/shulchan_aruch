If the transferor was a deathly ill person, the issue depends on the dispute at the end of the first chapter of Gittin concerning whether "bring" is equivalent to "acquire." Tosafot explains that even for a deathly ill person, "bring" is not itself immediate acquisition. Rather, the recipient's heirs can sometimes receive because the special gift of a deathly ill person is treated as written and delivered. Ran questions this, since such a gift takes effect only after death, and if the intended recipient already died there is no acquisition for a dead person. The answer can be developed from the dispute in Perek Mi SheMet concerning a deathly ill person who says, "My loan shall belong to so and so." Rav Pappa explains that since a loan passes by inheritance, the Sages likewise empowered a deathly ill gift to transfer it, for such gifts were structured like inheritance. According to that approach, even if the recipient dies before the transferor, the recipient's heirs can receive through the inheritance character of the deathly ill gift. Rabbi Acha, however, grounds the rule in the fact that a loan can be transferred by a three party arrangement during life. Ran says Rav Pappa's reason is not the final one. On that approach, what cannot be acquired by an ordinary living person cannot be acquired through a deathly ill gift merely because inheritance could reach it. Rashi explains the Gittin passage differently. He says that because the words of a deathly ill person have unusual force, "bring" is itself treated as "acquire." Rashba explains that the special strength of the deathly ill person's words turns the delivery instruction into immediate acquisition. According to this approach, if the recipient was already dead when the instruction was given, the acquisition fails, because there is no acquisition for the dead. Rav Zevid's statement that the failure is an erroneous acquisition can be understood according to his own position that a three party arrangement does not transfer a loan and that the deathly ill transfer therefore works only through the inheritance model. Practically, according to Rashi and the Shulchan Aruch, "bring" is treated as "acquire" only when the transaction truly has the legal character of a deathly ill gift, such as one who distributes all his assets or commands because of impending death. If he gives only part of his property and does not command because of death, his words do not have the special force of a deathly ill gift, and "bring" is no stronger than in the case of a healthy person. This resolves Tumim's question from the Gemara, which asks why a partial deathly ill gift needs a kinyan even though a baraita says, "Give one hundred to so and so," and then the giver died. The Gemara establishes the baraita as a command because of death or a distribution of all assets. There was no need to answer that "give" is immediate acquisition, because outside the true deathly ill framework it is not. The same analysis suggests that if a deathly ill person says, "Give a gift document to so and so," we do not automatically treat "give" as "acquire" according to Rashi, because the special rule that his words are as though written and delivered was enacted for monetary gifts, not for a direction to create and deliver a document. Rashbam makes this distinction in Perek Mi SheMet. Rashba, however, goes further. Rivash reports in his name that because the deathly ill person's speech is so strong, "bring" can count as "acquire" even if the giver later recovers. Rashba himself writes that when the deathly ill person actually hands the property to the messenger for delivery, the messenger acquires immediately for the recipient even though the gift is framed as a present gift rather than one taking effect after death. On that approach, the special force of his speech can extend even to a partial gift and perhaps even to an instruction concerning a document. Tumim objected to Rivash, noting that Rashi too says "bring" is like "acquire" for a deathly ill person, yet the Shulchan Aruch rules that if he recovers he may retract. But Rashba's own novellae show that Rivash understood him correctly. Rashba expressly distinguishes between the ordinary deathly ill gift, which takes effect after death, and a present gift handed to a messenger, where the messenger acquires immediately because the giver's speech is unusually strong. Thus according to Rashba, once the transfer was made to the messenger the giver cannot retract even if he recovers. There remains a separate reason why the Gemara did not answer its question by saying simply that "give" is like "acquire." The baraita there could not be established according to Rabbi Shimon Shezuri, who treats even the words of one in danger as though he expressly said "give." If that special assumption of hurried speech is absent, we cannot presume that "give" means "acquire" merely because the person is ill. Under our accepted ruling like Rabbi Shimon Shezuri, however, there is stronger basis to treat the language generously. Accordingly, where the recipient has already taken possession, there is at least substantial ground to say that he may retain the property by saying that the law follows Rashba.
