And the giver cannot retract so long as he has not recovered. Rashi clearly indicates that although regarding a person on his deathbed "bring" is equivalent to "acquire," nevertheless on 15a, beginning "And some say," he writes expressly: "He gives it to the recipient's heirs so long as the sender has not retracted." Thus Rashi holds that retraction is effective. His understanding must be that although "bring" is equivalent to "acquire," it is as though the giver said, "Acquire it for him on condition that I do not retract." I therefore wonder at the Rivash in responsum 346. He writes that because the Rashba explains that the words of a person on his deathbed are especially effective, so that even "bring" is equivalent to "acquire," one may infer that even if the giver recovers he cannot retract, contrary to the Ramah cited by the Tur. What proof is there? Rashi too expressly writes that for a person on his deathbed "bring" is equivalent to "acquire," precisely the Rashba's principle, yet he still holds that the giver may retract. The reason is that it is treated as an acquisition on condition. The same can therefore be said where the giver recovers: his original intent was, "Acquire it for him on condition that I do not recover," and if he recovers the acquisition is retroactively void. The Mechaber and Ramah therefore acted correctly in omitting the Rivash's view and ruling in accordance with the Tur. I later saw that the Ramah's rule, that if the giver recovers he may retract, is derived from the Yerushalmi at the end of the first chapter of Gittin. The Yerushalmi teaches: "Give this maneh to so and so, and the giver died. If the heirs wish to withhold it, they cannot. Needless to say where he said, 'Acquire it for him,' or 'Receive it for him.'" The Yerushalmi then asks whether the baraita must be interpreted specifically where he said "acquire," because otherwise why can the heirs not retract. Rabbi Abba bar Mamal answers that the Mishnah concerns a person on his deathbed. The Yerushalmi asks: if so, why is it necessary to say that the heirs cannot retract where he expressly said "acquire" or "receive"? Rabbi Mana answers by establishing it through Rav Huna in the name of Rav, that the words of a person on his deathbed are treated like those of a healthy person who wrote and delivered the instrument. This is exactly the same explanation as in our Gemara, that because the words of a person on his deathbed are especially effective, even "bring" is equivalent to "acquire." The Yerushalmi then concludes: "And this is only where he died from that illness, but if he recovered, not." Thus it is explicit that although "bring" is equivalent to "acquire," the acquisition depends on his not recovering. The Ramah's ruling is therefore fully explained. I am surprised that Hagahot Mordechai remained uncertain about this and did not consult the Yerushalmi. This is precise.
