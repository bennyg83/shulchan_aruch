There, seif 5: If the recipient is poor and does not possess two hundred zuz, the promise becomes a vow and he cannot retract. Note: See Sema, who challenges Beit Yosef and maintains that even a gift to the poor is considered property of Heaven from which the poor acquire their shares. In my poverty I have been unable to understand his words. If charitable funds were literally property from the table of Heaven in that sense, a poor person who used his charitable share to betroth a woman should not effect kiddushin, just as a kohen who attempts to betroth a woman with his portion of sacrificial meat does not effect kiddushin because the kohen receives his portion from the table of Heaven. By contrast, terumah and maaser can be used for kiddushin because we do not say that the recipient receives them merely as a consumption right from the table of Heaven. If charity funds functioned as Sema describes, a poor recipient likewise could not use them for kiddushin. We have never heard such a rule. One could answer based on what I wrote in my work Bigdei Yom Tov on Beitza. Perhaps even terumah and maaser are received from the table of Heaven, but there is a distinction between property that Heaven immediately gives the recipient as a full gift, after which it is entirely his, and property that remains continuously Heaven’s while the recipient is given only a right to eat from the divine table. Terumah and maaser may be of the first kind: upon receipt, the kohen acquires full ownership from Heaven. Sacrificial portions are of the second kind: even in the kohen’s hand they remain sacred property and he is merely permitted to eat them. According to this distinction, Sema could mean that Hashem gives the charitable funds to the poor as their complete property. They could then use the funds for kiddushin. Even so, a serious difficulty remains. Tur and Shulchan Aruch siman 95 state that property dedicated to the poor does not have the legal status of hekdesh. According to Sema it should possess that status, since it initially belongs to Heaven. One could try to resolve this through the same distinction and say that we follow both aspects to produce the appropriate result: for purposes of making a verbal dedication binding, the property initially becomes Heaven’s and the principle that a declaration to Heaven is equivalent to delivery operates; then the poor acquire it from Heaven. For purposes of guardianship and ordinary monetary law, however, once Heaven has granted it from His table to the poor it becomes their private property, so the laws of guardians apply. But another difficulty seems decisive. Shulchan Aruch siman 212 states that although with consecrated property an intention in the heart can be effective because Scripture speaks of “every generous-hearted person,” charity for the poor does not become binding through an unspoken intention. It requires verbal expression because it has the law of ordinary private property. According to Sema, however, charity would first become property of Heaven, and an intention of the heart should therefore suffice just as with hekdesh. This expressly indicates that the law is not like Sema, but like Beit Yosef: the obligation arises through the law of a vow. For that reason it must be expressed with the lips. Beit Yosef’s explanation of the Tur is therefore correct. See my work on Yoreh Deah, siman 258, where I explained this point more fully.
