He cannot retract. The Sema in seif katan 15 cites the Maggid Mishneh, who explains that when the owner ordinarily entrusts property to the second person, the first guardian cannot demand its return because he has already been discharged from liability. This requires precision. In the Gemara the owner instructed Rabbi Dostai and Rabbi Yosi to bring him his deposit. According to the Maggid Mishneh's reasoning, the original depositary should plainly have been discharged once the owner instructed that it be brought to him. Yet those holding the deposit demanded that the agents make a kinyan accepting liability for the hazards of the road. This implies that the original depositary remained liable. It appears that where the messenger is taking the deposit on a journey, as in the Gemara's incident, the original depositary certainly remains liable. The depositary himself is not entitled to expose the item to travel, because all roads are presumed subject to accidents. Thus, even if he transfers it to a trustworthy person, he remains liable for the travel risk. Tosafot state this expressly in Bava Kamma in the discussion of an agent appointed before witnesses. See what I wrote above in siman 121. The Maggid Mishneh, however, speaks of a depositary who transfers the object to a messenger in the same city, so that the messenger can bring it to the owner without travel. In that case the first guardian is plainly exempt, under the law of a guardian who transfers to another guardian where the owner cannot say, "I trusted you but not him." The Maggid Mishneh's words are therefore simple and correct. His practical distinction in the Rambam's rule, which makes the first guardian's ability to retract depend on continuing liability, concerns a case where the second guardian is a person whom the owner trusts and the transfer takes place within the same city. The Rambam himself does not mention travel at all, and this fits well. There is consequently no contradiction between the Maggid Mishneh and the Rashba and Ran, who explicitly say that the first guardian remains liable. They discuss the Gemara's actual case involving travel, whereas the Maggid Mishneh discusses a transfer without travel. This is simple. Yet a new law emerges. If the Rambam does not speak of travel according to the Maggid Mishneh, then the first guardian's liability is based on the rule of a guardian who transferred to another guardian. Suppose the first guardian has been established as a liar and transfers the item to a second guardian who has not been so established. The Rambam nevertheless says that the first remains liable. Why? If the basis is merely a guardian transferring to another guardian, how can the owner say, "I trust you but not him," when the first guardian has been established as dishonest and the second retains a presumption of fitness? It is very strained to say that the owner can argue, "Although you are established as a liar to everyone else, I personally trust you more than all others." If we instead explained that the Rambam too speaks of transporting the item on the road, the first guardian's liability would be obvious, not because he transferred to another guardian but because he exposed the item to travel danger. That would resolve this difficulty, but then the Maggid Mishneh's explanation requires clarification. Perhaps the Maggid Mishneh merely intended to state a practical difference that arises where there is no travel, while the Rambam's primary case itself may involve travel. The matter requires study.
