Even if the deposit is in the agent's courtyard. The recipient of the gift does not acquire it. From this it is evident that the Mechaber agrees with the Sema that when the owner says to the agent, "Acquire it for him," and the deposit is already in the agent's courtyard, the recipient does acquire. We must therefore explain the Mechaber's statement, "But if he said to him, 'Acquire it for him,' while the object was in his hand, he acquires for him," not as requiring the object literally to be in his hand, but merely within his possession. If the object enters his possession only later, then an act of lifting may be necessary, but where it was already within his possession at the time of the instruction, the Ramah's position agrees with Rabbenu Tam. The Shach in seif katan 16 holds that the agent's courtyard is not sufficient merely because the owner said, "Acquire it for him." Rather, the owner must expressly say, "Let your courtyard acquire it for him." The Shach therefore creates two categories: according to Rabbenu Tam, a general instruction "Acquire it for him" suffices; according to the Ramah, the agent must specifically say that his courtyard should acquire. This distinction does not appear correct from Tosafot and the Ramban in Bava Batra, chapter HaSefinah, in the sugya of the four rules governing sellers. The Rashbam explains that the four rules cannot mean four distinct domains, because the seller's domain and the depositary's domain are legally the same: if the seller accepts the acquisition upon himself, the buyer acquires. Tosafot and Ramban object that a seller cannot use his courtyard to acquire for another person. See there. Tosafot themselves expressly hold, in the same passage beginning "This is the correct version," that merely saying "Acquire it for him" is effective. According to the Shach, there would therefore be an obvious difference between a seller and a depositary. With a depositary, his mere acceptance of the instruction would suffice, while with a seller, saying "Acquire it for him" plainly cannot work, because who would be acquiring on the buyer's behalf? If so, why did Tosafot need to bring a proof from the chapter Eruvin to refute the Rashbam? The same applies to the Ramban's chiddushim there. It is therefore clear, contrary to the Shach, that when the owner says, "Acquire it for him," and the depositary accepts, the meaning is that the depositary's courtyard should acquire for the recipient. Otherwise, by what mechanism would he acquire for his fellow? The object is not in his hand so that his lifting could acquire it for another. The only possible meaning is that his courtyard acquires for the recipient. This is clear. Therefore, if a seller likewise had the power to say, "Let my courtyard be acquired to the buyer for the purpose of acquiring the goods," the Rashbam's explanation would be correct, because the seller's domain would be legally the same as a depositary's domain. Tosafot and Ramban therefore had to prove that a seller has no power merely to cause his own courtyard to acquire for the buyer unless he actually rents the place to him. That is why they needed their proof. This establishes that Tosafot understand the phrase "Acquire it for him" as operating through the courtyard, meaning, "Let your courtyard acquire it for him." They never contemplated the Shach's distinction. If they had, there would have been an obvious difference between seller and depositary requiring no additional proof. The Rosh in the first chapter of Gittin cites Rabbenu Tam's rule that saying "Acquire it" with a gift effects acquisition, and he brings proof from the baraita concerning the four rules governing a seller. The baraita teaches that where the goods are in the domain of a person with whom they are deposited, once that depositary accepts the instruction, the buyer acquires. From this the Rosh derives that "Acquire it" is effective. Yet in the chapter HaSefinah the Rosh expressly writes: "When a person says, 'Let my utensil or my courtyard acquire for my fellow in a purchase that he is making from another,' his fellow acquires, because one's courtyard is like one's hand and he can acquire for his fellow. This is what the baraita means by 'in the domain of the person with whom it is deposited, until he accepts.' We see that acceptance alone effects acquisition, etc." The Rosh's words cannot contradict one another. We must therefore say that Rabbenu Tam's rule is the same in both places: the agent must cause his courtyard to acquire for the beneficiary. The phrase "Acquire it for my fellow," which the Rosh derives from the case of goods held by a depositary, means precisely that his courtyard should acquire for him. There is no verbal distinction at all. With due respect to the great sanctity and stature of the Shach, his distinction here is not supported.
Now in truth, whether a general instruction "Acquire it for him" is effective when the deposit is merely in the agent's domain and was not physically handed to him depends on how one understands the Rambam. The Maggid Mishneh and Kesef Mishneh disagree. The Rambam cites only the Rif's version of the baraita: "In the domain of the person with whom the goods are deposited, until he rents the place or removes them from their place." He omits the phrase "until he accepts upon himself." The Maggid Mishneh explains that this phrase was not in the Rif's text, but nevertheless the Rambam agrees with the rule in practice. The Kesef Mishneh writes that the Rambam disagrees as a matter of law. According to him, the Rambam holds that "give" is equivalent to "acquire," but that acquisition by "acquire" requires delivery from hand to hand. Mere presence within the agent's domain is insufficient. This dispute, however, has nothing to do with a distinction between a general phrase "Acquire it" and an express phrase "Let your courtyard acquire it." No such distinction appears among the poskim. See also the Nimukei Yosef in chapter HaSefinah, and the matter is simple. I have one unresolved doubt in the case of an agent who acquires for a recipient through his courtyard by saying, "Let my courtyard acquire it for him." If the object lies in his domain mixed together with the agent's own movable property, does the recipient acquire according to the view below in siman 200, seif 1, in the Rama, which requires a courtyard to be guarded for the benefit of the acquirer? Perhaps there is a distinction. Where the seller rents his courtyard to the buyer, the courtyard must be guarded for the buyer. Here, however, the agent is acquiring on behalf of the recipient. The recipient has not personally lifted the object. The only mechanism is that the agent lends the use of his courtyard to the recipient. Yet the courtyard is guarded by the agent, and that should count as guarded for the recipient because the agent acts on his behalf, under the principle that one may acquire a benefit for a person in his absence. If one asks that this itself would create a difference between the domain of a depositary and the domain of a seller, and therefore the baraita could indeed have meant four distinct domains because the former need not be guarded specifically for the recipient while the latter does, the answer is that according to the view that in a sale a courtyard need not be guarded for the buyer where another person's intent effects the transfer, there would be no such distinction. It would be strained to say that the identification of the baraita's "four rules" itself changes according to that dispute. The poskim therefore state the rule here without distinction. This is precise. The Shach in seif katan 28 writes that Tosafot, the Rosh, and Mordechai imply that even Rabbenu Tam agrees that with a debt or deposit the instruction does not acquire for the recipient unless the property actually reaches the agent's hand from hand to hand, just as the Rambam and Ramah require. I do not see any decisive proof from the sources he cites. It is true that Rabbenu Tam writes that even if "give" is equivalent to "acquire," that is only where the owner actually hands the object to the agent. But Rabbenu Tam wrote this to answer the questions: why does the Gemara ask "but he did not pull it," if "give" means "acquire"; and why was maamad sheloshtan instituted if "give" already effects acquisition? According to Rabbenu Tam's second answer, however, that with a gift "give" is not equivalent to "acquire," those questions are already resolved. What basis remains to invent the additional rule that delivery from hand to hand is necessary? With the word "acquire" itself, hand-to-hand delivery is not required, as the Shulchan Aruch rules here. Why should the equivalent instruction "give," in a context where it legally means "acquire," be weaker and require hand-to-hand delivery? That would be an extremely forced distinction. Tosafot, the Rosh, and Mordechai in chapter HaSefinah discuss this only according to Rabbenu Tam's first answer. Once Rabbenu Tam answers that "give" does not mean "acquire" in a gift, there is no obvious reason to distinguish between forms of "acquire." The language of Tosafot in Gittin 13a, beginning "One who says," likewise suggests this. They give two explanations of the Mishnah depending on whether the text reads, "Give this get." According to the first, the Mishnah follows the Sages in a case where the owner does not physically hand the get to the agent but merely points it out and instructs him to take it, and the agent does not take it until after the master's death. This implies that had the agent taken it during the master's lifetime, he would have acquired it for the slave even though it was not handed from hand to hand. Tosafot then give another explanation: perhaps the agent took it immediately and nevertheless the slave did not acquire, because "give" is equivalent to "acquire" only when the master physically hands the document to the agent. These are plainly two different resolutions. It seems likely that these two resolutions correspond to the two explanations in Tosafot on the preceding page, beginning "Anyone who says," namely, either "give" requires hand-to-hand delivery to count as "acquire," or, alternatively, "give" simply is not equivalent to "acquire" in a gift. According to the Shach, however, how could Tosafot write in the first answer that the agent did not take the document during the master's lifetime, as though taking it would have been enough? Tosafot had already concluded that hand-to-hand delivery is required. We must therefore say that according to the answer that "give" is not equivalent to "acquire" in a gift, there is no need to maintain the separate requirement of hand-to-hand delivery. Nevertheless, I have seen that the Shach's practical ruling is compelled by the Tur. The Tur unquestionably follows Rabbenu Tam, in agreement with his father the Rosh, that with a gift "give" is not equivalent to "acquire." Yet in Yoreh Deah, siman 267, in the laws of slaves, the Tur cites Rabbenu Tam that regarding emancipation, where "give" is equivalent to "acquire" because emancipation resembles payment of a debt as the Rashba and Rosh explain, the document must actually be handed to the agent. Thus the Tur accepts both propositions simultaneously: with a gift "give" is not equivalent to "acquire," and even where "give" does mean "acquire," hand-to-hand delivery is required. The Shach's rule is therefore explicit. It is strained to say that the Tur ruled stringently regarding emancipation because he took into account Rabbenu Tam's first answer, for then he should not have stated the matter unqualifiedly in a way that permits the freed slave to marry a maidservant. We still need to understand the source for Rabbenu Tam's requirement of hand-to-hand delivery once all the questions in Tosafot can be resolved through the other answer, that with a gift "give" is not equivalent to "acquire." I discussed this at length in my chiddushim to the sugya. It appears that although Tosafot treat as a settled principle that with an ordinary gift "give" is not equivalent to "acquire," the hand-to-hand rule is independently compelled. The Gemara at Gittin 14b says that where a person on his deathbed says, "Bring a maneh to so and so," even if the intended recipient dies first, the money is not returned to the sender's heirs. Rashi explains that for a person on his deathbed, "bring" is equivalent to "acquire." See Tosafot there, beginning "Here, with a dying person," the Rashba's chiddushim, and the Tur at the end of this siman in the name of the Ramah. This raises a difficulty regarding Rav's earlier ruling that if one says, "Give a maneh to so and so," the money is given after the sender's death only if the coins are gathered and lying together, which Rav Pappa explains as a case of a person on his deathbed because of the problem of a buried maneh. Why force the case into the uncommon concern of a buried maneh? The Gemara could have said simply that the dying person instructed, without a deathbed gift formula, that a maneh be given to so and so. We rule that "bring" for a dying person, and certainly "give," is equivalent to "acquire." The recipient would therefore acquire, subject only to the general rule that with "acquire" the property must be within the possession of the agent who is acquiring, as the Shulchan Aruch rules in this seif. Rav would then require that the coins be gathered and lying in that agent's possession. If they are not within his possession, the acquisition fails. One cannot answer that eventually the property will enter the agent's possession and he will then acquire for the recipient, so why must it be there at the moment of instruction? As discussed above, the giver may die in the interim, which itself can terminate the incomplete acquisition. This also illuminates the Gemara in Bava Batra 151a. It challenges the opinion that a partial gift by a person on his deathbed requires a kinyan from this Mishnah, according to the view that the Mishnah concerns a dying person and not the other interpretations offered in Gittin. The Gemara answers by interpreting it as a person giving instructions because of impending death. Why did it not answer more simply that it concerns a dying person who gave an ordinary gift, where "bring" is equivalent to "acquire"? Even if the property was not currently in the agent's possession, the Shach's reading would say it could enter later. We must conclude that although "give" can be equivalent to "acquire," it is effective only when there is delivery from hand to hand. The Mishnah does not state such delivery. Therefore the Gemara, both in Gittin and Bava Batra, never gives that simple interpretation. This establishes the Tur's rule. Tosafot's statement containing two apparent answers can be reconciled as follows. Their concluding legal rule is indeed that even if the agent received the document during the master's lifetime, "give" does not function as "acquire" unless the master physically handed it to him. But if so, why does the Gemara ask, "If the case is a dying person, why specify 'give'? Even 'write' should suffice"? Perhaps the Mishnah specifically teaches that even with the stronger term "give," the document may not be delivered after death because it lacked hand-to-hand transfer during life. Tosafot therefore first explain that the Mishnah can be read as a case where the agent did not take the document during the master's lifetime at all. Thus the Mishnah itself cannot be the source for the special hand-to-hand rule. If the agent had received it, one could have thought he was already freed. The hand-to-hand requirement must be known from elsewhere. Once that is established, the Gemara properly asks why a dying person's instruction should not be effective in the ordinary way. This is precise. The Rambam still requires explanation. In the laws of slaves he follows the Rif, who rules that although we follow the Sages that if one says, "Give a deed of emancipation to my slave," he cannot retract, nevertheless the slave is not actually freed until the document reaches his hand. This is based on the Rif's question from the Mishnah that says, "Give a deed of emancipation to my slave," and rules that after the master's death it is not delivered. This proves that "give" did not itself complete emancipation. The Rambam must therefore either have the text "Give this get," or hold that hand-to-hand delivery is not required for "give" to function as "acquire," yet he still distinguishes between preventing the master from retracting and actually freeing the slave. Otherwise the Mishnah would pose no difficulty, exactly as Tosafot explain. According to the Rambam's approach, however, Tosafot's question returns on the later clause: if one says, "Give a maneh to so and so," and then dies, why does the Gemara ask, "But he did not pull it"? If "give" functions as "acquire," why should meshichah be necessary? How can we distinguish the first clause from the second? For the Rambam, the first clause must concern a form of "give" effective enough to prevent retraction. The second clause is worded in the same way, so why assume a different mechanism? It is strained to say that the Rambam adopts the Rosh and Rashba's distinction that emancipation is like payment of a debt, so in a debt no hand-to-hand transfer is needed but with a gift it is. From where would the Rambam derive such a distinction, especially when he constructs an entire framework upon the Rif's distinction between the effect of "give" for retraction and for emancipation? It is more plausible that the Rambam's version of the Mishnah specifically read "Give this get" in the first clause, while in the second clause the word "this" was absent. The Rambam would understand "this" to indicate actual immediate identification and delivery, enough to activate the stronger legal effect. The Gemara then notes that the second clause lacks that element and therefore asks why the gift should be acquired without meshichah. His commentary to the Mishnah appears to support this, though the explanation remains somewhat strained and requires study.
