And some say that "give" is like "acquire" even with a gift. Rashba writes in his novellae to Perek HaTekabel, Gittin 63: where one says, "Bring it to her," "Give it to her," "Acquire it for her," or "Receive it for her," he cannot retract. Rashba is uncertain whether, according to the view that "bring" is like "acquire," it is literally equivalent to immediate acquisition, so that even if the messenger never actually brings it to her she is already divorced and the instruction "bring" merely leaves the details to the messenger's discretion, or whether it is only like acquisition for purposes of preventing retraction. Under the latter possibility, the messenger must still bring it and give it to her, because that was what the sender said. It would then be as though he stipulated: acquire it for her now on condition that you later bring it and give it to her. Only when that condition is fulfilled does the acquisition become complete. Therefore, until the get actually reaches her, she should not remarry initially, lest it never reach her. Rashba leaves the matter unresolved. It appears that Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia, and likewise the opinion cited in seif 4, holds that "bring" and "give" are not literally immediate acquisition. They are treated like acquisition only regarding the sender's inability to retract, but a condition remains that the property must actually be delivered to the recipient. So long as that condition has not been fulfilled, the recipient has not fully acquired. That is why the messenger cannot retain the property for a debt owed to him by the lender, as explained above. Rambam also appears to follow this approach. In Hilchot Avadim, chapter 5, he writes: if the master says, "Acquire this get of emancipation for the slave," the slave becomes free immediately even if the get does not reach his hand before the master's death. But if the master says, "Give it to him," then although the master cannot retract, the get is not delivered after the master's death. It appears Rambam means that because he said "give," even though it has the effect of "acquire" concerning retraction, it still contains a condition that it reach the slave's hand. Until it reaches him, the slave is not fully emancipated. Rashba differs in one respect. Since the delivery is merely a condition, he allows the condition to be fulfilled even after the master's death, just like other conditions. Rambam holds that this condition is unlike an ordinary external condition, because the condition concerns the very body of the document: the instruction was that the document itself be given to the slave. If we deliver an emancipation document after death, it appears like a get operating after death. Although technically the acquisition could be understood as having taken effect earlier subject only to fulfillment of the condition, the act would resemble an invalid posthumous get. Therefore Rambam says it is not delivered after death. Since it is not delivered, the condition is never fulfilled and the slave remains fully enslaved. Bach in Yoreh Deah similarly explains Rashi and Rambam regarding a slave. He says that although "give" is treated like "acquire," the law that the slave does not become free after the master's death is a decree lest people permit a get after death. His explanation, however, is still difficult. If the slave was already fully emancipated during the master's lifetime because "give" is immediate acquisition, what relevance is there to giving the get after death? And if so, why is the case of "acquire" different, where Rambam says the slave becomes free immediately? According to what I wrote, Rambam is much clearer. The instruction "give" still requires the get eventually to reach the slave because that delivery is a condition of the acquisition. Therefore an observer seeing the get delivered after death could think a get can operate after death. This is why the later delivery is barred. Since the condition cannot then be fulfilled, emancipation never becomes complete. This also resolves Tosafot's question in the first chapter of Gittin 11. Tosafot asks how Rabbi Yochanan can say that "give" is like "acquire," since in the first chapter of Bava Metzia Rabbi Yochanan says that one who lifts a found object for another acquires it for him, and the Mishnah says, "Give it to me," rather than "Acquire it for me." Tosafot uses this to argue that "give" is treated like "acquire" only in a debt. According to Rambam and the authorities who apply "give" as "acquire" even in a gift, there is no contradiction. When one says, "Give it to me," the instruction means: acquire it for me now, on condition that you later give it into my hand. If the finder refuses to hand it over, the condition is not fulfilled and the requester never acquires. Thus the lifter can say, "I acquired it for myself and I will not give it to you." Because he does not deliver it, the condition fails and the requester never acquires. This is precise. However, a further difficulty remains from Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch in seif 1. They rule that if one said, "Bring" or "Give," and the messenger improperly returned the money to the payer, and Reuven later became insolvent so that the creditor lost, the messenger must pay because he was negligent in returning it. But if the recipient does not acquire until the money actually reaches his hand, because the sender's instruction contains the condition that it reach him, then when the messenger returned it to the sender the condition was never fulfilled. The recipient should never have acquired retroactively, and the money should be treated as never having left the sender. Why, then, is the messenger liable? There is another difficulty from the end of the first chapter of Gittin. One baraita says that where a person said, "Bring one hundred to so and so," and they searched for the recipient but did not find him, the money is returned to the sender. Another says it is given to the recipient's heirs. The Gemara establishes the dispute as depending on whether "bring" is like "acquire." But if "bring" is only acquisition subject to a condition that the money actually reach the recipient, then when the recipient died before receiving it the condition was never fulfilled and he should not have acquired. The same problem arises from Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, who rule in the case of a deathly ill person that "bring" is like "acquire," and even if the recipient dies the money is given to his heirs rather than returned. Perhaps that sugya follows Reish Lakish, who holds that when a condition says "to me," it can include the person's heirs. But Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 143 rule that "to me" means to me and not to my heirs, while here the Tur and Shulchan Aruch nevertheless rule that the heirs receive because "bring" is like "acquire." It appears one must distinguish between a condition formulated as, "on condition that you give it to me," which means specifically to me and not to my heirs, and a condition created by the sender's instruction that the messenger "give it to him." In the latter case, the condition is fulfilled by giving it to the recipient's heirs, because delivery to his legal successors counts as delivery on his behalf. Thus if the recipient dies, the messenger can still fulfill the condition by giving the money to the heirs, and the acquisition becomes effective retroactively. This resembles a distinction stated by Mishneh LaMelech in Hilchot Zekhiyah. This also explains the final sugya of the first chapter of Gittin concerning whether "bring" is like "acquire" and whether there is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. One opinion says they divide, while in Bavel they said the messenger may do whatever he wishes, which the Gemara identifies with shuda deDayanei. Rashi explains that the Sages were uncertain both whether "bring" is like "acquire" and whether there is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. Rashi's wording requires explanation. It is clear why he says they were uncertain whether "bring" is like "acquire." If they were certain it was not, and the only doubt was whether there is a mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words, then while the sender was still alive the money would certainly return to him. Yet the Sages' ruling appears to address all the cases in the baraita, including a living sender. But why did Rashi also need to say they were uncertain about the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased? Perhaps they were certain there is no such mitzvah and were uncertain only whether "bring" is like "acquire." According to what I have written, Rashi is precise. The Sages who say they divide are uncertain whether "bring" is like "acquire." The Babylonian ruling that the messenger may do whatever he wishes is shuda. If "bring" meant immediate absolute acquisition, the messenger would have no discretion at all. Once the recipient had acquired, the messenger would be forced to deliver to him. But under the present explanation, even according to the view that "bring" is like "acquire," the recipient acquires only subject to the later delivery condition. Until delivery, the messenger can choose to return the money to the sender, thereby preventing fulfillment of the condition and leaving the recipient without acquisition. If so, the Babylonian ruling of shuda does not actually contradict the view that "bring" is like "acquire." The only point on which it can disagree is the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words. If such a mitzvah applies, then after the sender's death the messenger is compelled to carry out the instruction and give to the recipient. If the law is shuda, the messenger retains discretion. Rashi therefore explains that the Sages were uncertain both about whether "bring" is like "acquire" and about whether there is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. Their ruling to divide differs from the opinion permitting full delivery to the recipient, while the Babylonian ruling of shuda differs regarding the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words. This is a satisfactory explanation.

Alternatively, even according to the Sages, the case can be where the master does not presently hand the get directly to the messenger but only points it out and commands him to give it, and the messenger does not actually take it until after the master's death. Or even if he takes it immediately, the slave still does not acquire, because "give" is treated like "acquire" only when the sender transfers the object directly from his hand to the messenger's hand. Were it not for Tosafot, it would appear to me that even if "give" is treated like "acquire" without direct hand to hand delivery, the messenger still cannot acquire the emancipation document for the slave if he merely takes it from the ground. This would be a case of, "Take your get from the ground," which is ineffective. Gittin teaches that if a husband says, "Take your get from the ground," he has done nothing, because the Torah requires that he give it into her hand. Emancipation is compared to divorce in all these laws. Therefore, if the slave himself took the emancipation document from the ground, he would not acquire freedom. So too, one who acquires it on behalf of the slave cannot do so if he does not receive it from the master's hand. It remains equivalent to taking the get from the ground. At first, however, the opposite difficulty arises. If "give" is treated like "acquire," why does the baraita say that it should not be given after the master's death, implying that giving it during life would work? Since "give" is supposedly only an instruction to acquire and not an appointment as a delivery agent, perhaps taking it from the ground should never work. The answer is that when immediate acquisition for the slave is impossible because the document was not transferred from the master's hand, the messenger does not intend at that moment to acquire for the slave. He instead understands the instruction in its literal sense as appointing him to deliver the document. "Give" means actual delivery in that situation. By contrast, with an ordinary debt or deposit, where no rule of "take your get from the ground" exists, there is room to say that even if the sender does not presently hand the object to the messenger, the command "give" can function as "acquire."
