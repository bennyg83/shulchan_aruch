(See there: If because of coercion). It is difficult, for we hold in HaKones that one may not save himself with another's property. Since another's money is in his hands, how may he save himself by returning it? One may answer that here the coercion arose specifically because of that money, as the Tur writes at the end of siman 126. Yet what novelty does this teach, since even if unrelated robbers attacked him he would be exempt? The answer is that here even a mere credible threat is sufficient to excuse the return. This explains the incident in the first chapter of Gittin. Rabbi Aha bar Yoshiyah had a deposit and sent Rabbi Yosi and Rabbi Dostai to retrieve it. After they received it, the depositaries demanded its return. Rabbi Yosi refused, while Rabbi Dostai agreed. Rabbi Aha asked whether the depositaries had horses and mules or connections to the government. When told they did, he said that Rabbi Dostai had acted properly. Why were these questions necessary? Because a credible threat alone suffices, and Rabbi Aha was determining whether the threat had substance. This also answers Sema's question from Rama's nearby ruling that judgment favors Reuven, where there was no bodily coercion, only Shimon's threat to retain Levi's money. Financial pressure alone is not coercion; bodily blows and suffering are. Nor is there a difficulty from the end of siman 126, which says that even if the depositary spent his own money to save the deposit he may recover all expenses from the deposit. That case is different. Sema's answer, that there he was an agent, is not persuasive. Rabbi Dostai and Rabbi Yosi were themselves Rabbi Aha's agents, yet the rule of coercion was learned from their case. Mordechai's discussion underlying Rama's present ruling likewise does not involve a formally appointed agent; it compares the case to one who says, “Take a maneh to so-and-so,” where agency matters only to prevent retraction after the recipient has become established as a denier.
