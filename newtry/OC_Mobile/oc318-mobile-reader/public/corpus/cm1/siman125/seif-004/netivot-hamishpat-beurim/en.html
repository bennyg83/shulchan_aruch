If the sender died. This case must be one in which the creditor has other property from which his own creditor can collect, while the sender has no property. If the creditor also had no property, even the dissenting opinion would agree that the recipient may seize the money. For even if the transfer did not itself effect an acquisition and the money technically remains the sender's, the recipient has a claim against the sender by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien. The Shach writes in siman 107 that even movable property inherited by heirs may be collected through Rabbi Natan's lien. Although I wrote above that Rabbi Natan's lien is only analogous to the liability of a guarantor, and the Rama in siman 129, seif 8, rules that where a borrower on an oral loan says that he paid, he takes a hesset oath and both he and the guarantor are exempt, nevertheless seizure from the guarantor is effective where the guarantor has a migo. Accordingly, the case here necessarily concerns a creditor who has other property. Even according to the Shach, who holds that Rabbi Natan's lien applies notwithstanding that the creditor possesses other assets, the recipient here is considered to suffer a loss because the loan is outstanding and the creditor is not demanding payment. The heir can therefore retain the money, as in other cases where the recipient would otherwise suffer a loss.
And there is an authority who says. See Shach, seif katan 23, who asks why the creditor should have no right to the money. The objection from the law of seizure of movables can be answered as follows. A direct debtor owes each creditor the full amount of his own debt, so each creditor may seize the entire sum necessary to satisfy his claim. A subdebtor who is obligated only through Rabbi Natan's lien, however, owes in total only the amount that he owes his own creditor. If, for example, that amount is one hundred and there are three creditors sharing it, each effectively has only one third of that hundred against the subdebtor. One of them therefore cannot seize the entire hundred for himself.
