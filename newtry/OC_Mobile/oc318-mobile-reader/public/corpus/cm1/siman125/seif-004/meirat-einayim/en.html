And he left no property, etc. Had the sender left property, the lender would proceed against the estate, and the situation would be treated as though the sender were still alive, because the lender could compel the sender through his estate to require the agent to deliver the money. Where nothing was left, the lender has no claim against the heirs and can only demand from the agent, "Give me the money Reuven sent me through you." The agent can then take a hesset oath and say, "You owe me money," through a migo that he could have said he already delivered the funds to you.
Some say that if, etc. The Tur explains that this view holds every instruction "give" or "carry to so-and-so" to be like "acquire for him on condition that you deliver it into his hand." So long as the condition was not fulfilled, the sender's right has not fully disappeared. Although with a deposit or loan "carry" and "give" ordinarily prevent the sender from retracting, the sender's intent is still that the recipient acquire only in a manner that actually results in delivery. If the money never reaches him, it remains in the sender's legal sphere for this purpose.
