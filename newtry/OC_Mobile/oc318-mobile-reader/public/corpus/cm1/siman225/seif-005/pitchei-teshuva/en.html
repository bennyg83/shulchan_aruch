Reuven bought land from the community without assuming liability for governmental seizure and sold it to Shimon on the same condition. Their first sale was later cancelled after disputes, and they then made a new settlement at a higher price. The witnesses wrote only a short deed, adding that a full customary deed would be written when necessary. Later governmental claims caused Shimon expenses, and he demanded reimbursement, arguing that the renewed settlement did not repeat the exclusion of governmental liability and that customary full deeds include warranty against claims by Jews and non-Jews. Reuven answered that the original condition remained understood, and the witnesses testified that their shorthand reference to a fuller deed concerned only ordinary clauses and protections, not cancellation of the prior exclusion. Panim Meirot ruled for Reuven. Rama, siman 207:1, holds that where parties first stipulate a condition and later write a deed without repeating it, the deed is presumed written subject to the original condition. Here the witnesses' explanation confirms that understanding. Moreover, siman 61 recognizes that conditions are interpreted according to the parties' intent rather than mechanically by wording. Reuven himself had bought without governmental warranty and expressly imposed the same condition in the first transaction, so absent evidence of a change the later settlement is presumed to rest on it as well. Even ordinary warranty against a non-Jew means a lawful private claim proving debt or theft of the land, not arbitrary governmental confiscation, which is an accident generally outside a seller's warranty. On the combined force of these considerations and the witnesses' testimony, Shimon has no claim against Reuven.
