Because of the seller, etc. In the Derishah I wrote that there are three kinds of responsibility. The first is a creditor who removes the property because of a debt of the seller. The second is a person who comes with witnesses proving that the field had been stolen from him. The third is responsibility arising from the seller himself, as the Tur and Mechaber write in the following siman, seif 2. See Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, who writes that these three forms of responsibility rank one above the other, and all are included in ordinary responsibility even if not expressly stated. But if the seller expressly limited responsibility to claims "arising from me," that wording excludes claims by outsiders of a higher category. See Darkei Moshe, siman 111, seif 2, and siman 226.
All the money that he paid. The creditor also collects half of the appreciation from the purchaser, and the purchaser then recovers that amount from his seller, as the Tur and Mechaber write above at the beginning of siman 115. This is not discussed here because the subject is different. Moreover, ordinary movable property, which is also included in this seif, does not ordinarily appreciate in the same manner. See the Perishah.
Even if he sold the land by deed. Land is specified because movable property is not ordinarily transferred by deed. The novelty is that one might have thought this rule applies only when no deed is before us, allowing us to say that responsibility was orally stipulated at the time of sale. But where a sale deed is before us and contains all the other terms of the sale while making no mention of responsibility, one might have thought the seller certainly stipulated that he would bear no responsibility. The ruling teaches otherwise. See the Perishah for further discussion.
It is a scribal error. The reason is that there is a presumption that a person does not throw away his money for nothing by purchasing property subject to the possibility that a creditor will immediately seize it. With movable property, where ordinary creditor seizure from purchasers does not apply, there is still concern that another person may bring witnesses proving that the item was stolen from him. The purchaser's ordinary intent is surely that if the item is removed from him for any reason arising from the seller, the seller will return his money. The scribe was therefore expected to write the responsibility clause even if the parties did not specifically instruct him, and its omission is treated as the scribe's error.
