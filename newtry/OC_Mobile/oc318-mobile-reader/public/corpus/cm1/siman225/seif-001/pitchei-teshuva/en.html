The same law applies to every sale. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 4, and Avodat HaGershuni, siman 107, discuss Reuven and Shimon who exchanged houses, paid the difference in value, registered each house to the new owner, and took possession, but wrote no deed between themselves. Half a year later a prior creditor of Reuven appeared with a deed and lien exceeding the value of both houses. The creditor sought to collect first from the house now held by Reuven as unencumbered property and also from the house now held by Shimon as property previously owned by Reuven. Shimon argued that if the creditor seized the house he had received, he should recover the house he had originally given Reuven because sellers ordinarily bear implied warranty. Tzemach Tzedek and Avodat HaGershuni ruled for the creditor. They held that the rule that warranty is an implied scribal condition applies to an ordinary purchase, because a person does not throw away his money for nothing. In an exchange, however, each party is simultaneously purchaser and seller, and absent an express undertaking of warranty each may be understood to accept the transaction in consideration of the other's likewise not assuming warranty. Tzemach Tzedek adds that payment of a cash equalization does not alter this, because the exchange remains one transaction. Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 116 disputes them, citing Ri Migash as indicating that implied warranty applies to an exchange as well. Their proof from Bava Batra 107 is distinguishable because there both brothers stand equally in doubt concerning their father's property, while in an exchange each party knows that his own property is clear of defects and liens and therefore has every reason to insist that the other also accept warranty. Netivot HaMishpat agrees and calls the matter straightforward. Shevut Yaakov likewise rejects the no-warranty theory, but adds that even if each party does have warranty against the other, the earlier creditor may still initially collect from the property subject to his lien, leaving the exchanging party to pursue his warranty claim. He then argues on a different basis that one creditor should not be able to collect both houses, since when the debtor transferred one parcel and received another parcel in exchange, the creditor's security was effectively replaced rather than diminished. Beit Meir similarly questions the original ruling and suggests that where land is exchanged directly for land, the creditor never lacked a parcel on which his lien could rest; the transferee may therefore say, "What loss did I cause you? I placed other property under your lien in its place." He leaves the point requiring study.
