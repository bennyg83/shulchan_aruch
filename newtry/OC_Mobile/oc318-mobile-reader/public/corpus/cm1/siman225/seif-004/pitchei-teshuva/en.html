See Responsa Panim Meirot, part 2, siman 21, concerning Reuven, who purchased land from the community without warranty against governmental interference and then sold that land to Shimon subject to the same condition. The sale was later annulled because of disputes between them, and each waived claims against the other. They subsequently compromised again, with Shimon adding ten Reichsthaler to the original price. The original witnesses wrote only a short deed of sale and concluded that, when needed, a full deed of conveyance would be written according to custom. After many years disputes arose over the property because of the government, and Shimon incurred expenses and had to compromise with the authorities. He then demanded reimbursement from Reuven, arguing that when they made the later compromise the seller did not repeat the earlier condition excluding governmental warranty. The new deed was brief and expressly contemplated a later full deed in the customary form, and customary deeds include warranty against claims by Jews and non Jews. The seller therefore accepted the risk. Reuven replied that there was no need to repeat the condition because he had already stated it at the first transaction. The witnesses also testified that their reference to a later full deed concerned only the other customary clauses, enhancements, and the seller's personal warranty, not governmental warranty. Panim Meirot ruled for the seller. Rama, siman 207, seif 1, rules that where parties first make a condition and afterward write an unqualified deed, the deed is presumed written subject to the first condition. Although here one might question this because the witnesses wrote that a full deed would later be written according to custom, and custom is to include all warranties against Jews and non Jews, the witnesses themselves explained that their intent was only to include the remaining strengthening clauses of the deed and not to annul the condition already made. We follow their explanation of their intent. The end of siman 61 cites an opinion that a condition between parties is interpreted according to intent rather than merely the written wording. Here the intent is especially clear, because Reuven himself had purchased the land from the community without governmental warranty and at the first sale expressly excluded governmental warranty. The later transaction arose only after a quarrel annulled the first sale, so it is presumed that he again sold on the same condition. This also resembles Rama, siman 221, that where the parties first fixed a price and one later performed meshichah without restating it, the transaction is presumed to be on the original price. Moreover, even if one interprets the phrase "a full deed according to custom" as incorporating customary warranty against Jews and non Jews, that refers to a non Jew who proves that the seller owed him money or had wrongfully taken the land from him. It does not include arbitrary governmental seizure by force. No one would reasonably undertake such liability, as explained in siman 225, seif 2, because this is an unavoidable event and the seller does not bear liability for accidents. Although there are arguments against this last point, the practical ruling rests primarily on the strong circumstantial evidence and the witnesses' own testimony that the first condition remained intended.
