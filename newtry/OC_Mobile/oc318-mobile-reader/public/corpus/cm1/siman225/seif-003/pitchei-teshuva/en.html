Whether by the law of the king. See Baer Heitev, Kesef Mishneh, Hilchot Mechirah, chapter 19, halachah 3, and Bach. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, who discusses this at length in connection with a case mentioned above, siman 69, seif 2, seif katan 4. He then writes that Rambam's statement that a non Jew came and removed the land still requires study. He had suggested that the case might be one where the law of the king provides that land purchased from a non Jew reverts to that non Jew without payment, or something similar. Yet Rambam's unqualified language implies that in every case the purchaser has no recourse against the seller, even where the non Jew's claim is not merely a monetary debt but a legal claim to the land itself. Why should we not say that once the law of the king clearly recognizes the land as belonging to the non Jew, this is equivalent to a seller who sold property that was found not to be his? It is difficult to distinguish between the king himself having a legal right in the property and some other person empowered by royal law to dispossess the purchaser. If that person's legal right is enforceable under the king's law and attaches to the land itself, the seller has effectively sold property that was not truly his. Beit Ephraim therefore concludes that if, at the time of sale, the royal law already applied to that parcel and required that it be taken by another person or by the king himself, the transaction is a mistaken sale. The seller should have disclosed that fact. This is not an unforeseeable coercive event arising after the sale. The rule concerning one proscribed by the government should likewise be understood as a case where, at the time of sale, he was already under governmental proscription. But if at the time of sale no such royal law or decree existed and the seller validly owned what he sold, and only afterward a royal law arose authorizing the king or another person to take the land, even if the reason traces back to the seller, such as unpaid taxes from prior years for which a new decree is now issued against the land, this is an unavoidable event arising after the sale. Rambam's rule that the purchaser has no recourse where he is dispossessed under the law of the king applies in this latter situation. From this follows that the exemption applies specifically when the governmental claim was unknown at the time of sale. If the royal rule affecting the land was known then, it is plainly not an unforeseeable event. If the government has a claim to the land itself, the transaction is a mistaken sale. If the government's claim is only a debt that can be discharged with money, the sale remains valid, but the matter is at least included in the seller's accepted warranty, because this is not a newly arising unavoidable event. All the more so under our customary deeds, which state everything for the purchaser's benefit and provide that if anyone from anywhere in the world, near or far, Jew or non Jew, comes against the purchaser, the seller bears responsibility. It is obvious that this warranty includes dispossession by a non Jew. There is no reason to say that it applies only if the dispossession occurs under Jewish law. The additional wording plainly comes to include even removal under their courts, requiring the seller to indemnify the purchaser. This is all the more so in our deeds, which state that every expression in the document shall be interpreted to strengthen the rights of the holder, and certainly where in the judges' assessment the non Jew is not acting by mere fabrication but is asserting a genuine debt.
