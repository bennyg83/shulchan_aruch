The accident of prohibited libation is upon the seller. Sema explains from the Rosh and Tur that because the seller accepted responsibility for external accidents to the wine, specifically breakage and spillage, he is presumed to have accepted all comparable external accidents, those two being mentioned because they are common. The Rosh, however, first gives another reason: the wording is ambiguous, and because the buyer has not yet paid, money should remain with its possessor. Panim Meirot II, siman 162, therefore rules that this first reason is primary. If the seller already received the money, it should not be extracted from him on the basis of the Rosh's second rationale alone. Indeed, if ritual contamination or prohibited libation is uncommon, that itself suggests it was not included in a general undertaking of liability, especially where the seller expressly mentioned only breakage and spillage. Panim Meirot accordingly ruled in a case of four barrels of wine that became prohibited on the road through the wagoners. The seller had undertaken to deliver the wine intact and to bear damage to hoops or barrels. Since the seller had already received payment, he retained it; moreover, even if payment had not yet been made, an undertaking limited to bringing the wine intact from breakage and spillage does not necessarily include ritual prohibition. He supports this from the Mishna in Tevul Yom, where "arrive safely" means safe from breakage and spillage but not from impurity according to the first Tanna. Derishah had already addressed the objection that an uncommon accident should not be included, distinguishing a case where the purchaser expressly refuses all responsibility except for the wine's own inherent condition. Pitchei Teshuva concludes that Panim Meirot's monetary ruling is persuasive, particularly because the Rosh joined two rationales and one should not rely on a single auxiliary rationale to extract money. He also cites Responsa Beit David concerning a shipowner. Although a paid carrier may generally be liable for theft and loss, established commercial practice was that the shipowner did not personally sail but appointed a captain, and the cargo owner's claim for theft or loss was therefore against the captain. A clause excluding liability for fire and water did not necessarily imply acceptance of every other liability, since particular exclusions may be inserted merely to strengthen the document. See there and above, siman 216.
