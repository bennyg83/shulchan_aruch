See Sema, seif katan 5, who explains that the ruling excludes a case where the purchaser voluntarily surrendered the property to the claimant solely on the strength of that claimant's testimony. Responsa Mayim Chaim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 4, distinguishes its case. Where the buyer simply surrendered the entire property, he did the seller no favor, and the seller may demand proof of lawful dispossession before paying under warranty. But where the purchaser compromised with heirs and thereby protected the seller from the possibility that a civil court might have awarded them the entire house, even Sema may agree that the seller should reimburse the buyer according to the benefit received. This resembles Rama, siman 264:4, that where property was saved only through another's efforts, one cannot refuse payment merely because the claimant's underlying position was legally correct; he must pay according to the benefit obtained. The responsum therefore recommends a judicial compromise between seller and purchaser according to the circumstances.
Whether under the law of the king. See Baer Heitev, Kesef Mishneh, Hilchot Mechirah 19:3, and Bach. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, explains Rambam's rule concerning a non-Jew who removes land under the king's law. If, at the time of sale, the royal law already applied to that land and entitled the king or another person to take it, the transaction is a mistaken sale because the seller was obligated to disclose that existing defect. This is not a later-arising accident. But if at the time of sale no such royal claim existed and only afterward a decree arose authorizing seizure, even where it resulted from the seller's past conduct, such as newly imposed collection for old unpaid taxes, it is a later accident and falls within Rambam's rule that the purchaser has no recourse. Thus the distinction is whether the royal right existed and was known as a legal burden at the time of sale. If the claimant has a direct right in the land itself, the sale may be void; if the claim is merely a debt that can be discharged with money, the sale remains effective but ordinarily falls within warranty. This is especially true under contemporary deeds drafted broadly for the purchaser's benefit, expressly protecting him against claims by Jews and non-Jews alike and directing that ambiguous clauses be construed in favor of the holder. Such language includes even a dispossession under non-Jewish law, particularly where beit din sees that the claimant's demand is genuine rather than a fabricated pretext. See there.
