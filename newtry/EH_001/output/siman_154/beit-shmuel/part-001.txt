(ב) <b>And if she knew of them.</b> This is the view of Ramah. According to this, with foul breath and other such matters, if she knew of them we do not compel him. If so, according to the tanna in the first Mishnah who taught, "a man in whom blemishes arose," etc., and the Gemara says that if they arose after marriage we do not compel him, all the more if they existed beforehand, because she saw and accepted them. It means: all the more if they existed beforehand, for then even with foul breath, etc., we do not compel him. According to the one who reads the Mishnah as "they existed beforehand," the difficulty is that even foul breath should not compel him. Perhaps according to that view the second Mishnah, "these are those whom we compel," speaks of foul breath even if it existed beforehand, and nevertheless we compel him; R. Meir and the Sages disagree only where he stipulated explicitly. But we rule like the one who reads "they arose after marriage," all the more if they existed beforehand, and then we say the dispute concerns cases that existed beforehand. What the Mishnah teaches, "these we compel, whether they existed before she married," Bach explained to mean that they existed but she did not know. However, in the first Mishnah regarding blemishes, one can say that if they existed and she did not know, we compel him too; the difference between the two Mishnayot is where they arose after marriage: with foul breath we compel, but with other blemishes we do not. If she did not know, it is a mistaken acquisition and we compel him to divorce, as written in siman 39 and 117 regarding blemishes found in her. Do not ask why the Gemara did not establish the dispute this way: one view says "they existed" means existed and she did not know, all the more if they arose after marriage, while the other says that if they existed beforehand it is a mistaken acquisition. One can answer that all agree in such a case that it is a mistaken acquisition. Bach explains differently; according to him, following Ramah, we do not compel for blemishes even if they existed and she did not know, and all the more according to the other poskim. According to their approaches, all cases speak where she knew, and the case where they existed and she did not know is not heard at all; one may say that all agree we compel even for ordinary blemishes. Now, according to several poskim, even if she knew of them she can say, "I thought I could tolerate it, but now I cannot." Therefore it appears that even according to Rama, who wrote that we do not compel him to divorce, nevertheless she does not have the law of a moredet if she does not wish to live with him, for she can say kim li like those poskim. The reason Rama ruled like Ramah and not like those poskim is that Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef wrote that Rambam holds we compel him only if the blemish arose after marriage, but if it was there originally we do not compel him. Yet it is not necessary to say so. One can say that Rambam wrote this only regarding the ketubah: if the blemish arose after marriage, he must give her the ketubah, but if it was there from the beginning, he need not give her the ketubah, for otherwise every woman would scheme and marry such a man, as Tosafot wrote in Yevamot 64, in Perek HaMadir, in Mordechai, and in Agudah. A proof is that regarding one afflicted with boils Rambam also wrote, "he became afflicted with boils," even though according to all opinions, even if he stipulated and it existed originally, we compel him to divorce. Rather, he wrote so because of the ketubah.

(ב) <b>And she says that he is the cause, etc.</b> According to Tosafot and the Rosh, the reason she must say that he does not shoot like an arrow is that this must speak where she stayed with him ten years; otherwise we would be concerned that she set her eyes on another, since she demands a get. Since it speaks where she stayed ten years, if he has not yet fulfilled peru u-revu, we should compel him to divorce her by the law of the Shas so that he can fulfill peru u-revu. It must therefore speak where he already fulfilled peru u-revu, and we do not compel him except because of her claims. Therefore she must say that he does not shoot like an arrow, for since he already fathered children it is reasonable to assume that she is the cause of the failure to have children, unless she makes that claim. But for us, since we do not compel because of failure to fulfill peru u-revu even after ten years, then even if he does shoot like an arrow, nevertheless when she comes with the claim that she needs a support in old age, we compel according to the relevant opinions. Beit Shmuel then compares Tosafot, Ramban, Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, and the gloss, and distinguishes between monetary ketubah claims, coercion to divorce, and the evidentiary weight of her claim.

(ח) <b>And the same applies if he remained ten years, etc.</b> In the Talmud it states: he says the impediment is from her, and she says it is from him; she is believed. If he says, I will go and test myself, he is permitted. I have already written the various approaches, and according to each approach, just as in the first clause where he says the impediment is from her, so too in the latter clause if he says I will go and marry another woman. According to Rambam's approach that I wrote, every woman must say that he is not capable of fathering children; this is what the Talmud means when it says perhaps he did not merit to be built from her. If he says I will marry another woman, then if she bears a child it becomes clear that the impediment was from the first wife, and we do not say perhaps he did not merit to be built from her. The Tur cited a responsum of the Rosh, who wrote that if he married a second wife and she bore a child, the first wife has not lost, and we say perhaps he did not merit to be built from her, until she is established as not bearing children to three men. Afterward the Tur wrote this law: if he says I will marry another, and if she bears a child the first wife leaves without a ketubah. In Darkei Moshe he distinguishes between these two laws and writes that here the case is where she did not bear children to two men, then married a third and did not bear children, and she says he is not capable of fathering; she is believed and has a ketubah. If he married another and she bore a child, then we no longer say that he did not merit to be built from her, but rather she is established as one who does not bear. This follows the answer of the Rash in Tosafot that I cited above: the sugya of he says the impediment is from her, etc., speaks of the third husband. Accordingly, what the sugya says there, that he says I will go and test myself, necessarily refers to the third husband. But according to Rif's approach, that the sugya speaks even of the first and second husband where he claims with certainty, necessarily the phrase I will go and test myself also speaks where he claims with certainty. Then if the second wife bears a child, the first wife leaves without the ketubah. But where he does not claim with certainty, even if the second wife bears a child we say perhaps he did not merit to be built from the first. If he claims with certainty that the impediment is from her, meaning that she emits semen, and he is believed in his claim, while she claims that he too is not fit to father because he is not capable of erection, then when the second wife bears a child her first claim is contradicted and his certain claim remains. With this the words of the Tur are well resolved, unlike Darkei Moshe, who rejects this distinction; his difficulty there does not appear to me. Maharshal and Bach also explained that the Tur speaks where he claims with certainty. Likewise according to Tosafot's answer, where he already had children she must say that he is not capable of fathering, for otherwise we do not attribute the failure to merit, but attribute the defect to her. What it says, I will go and test myself, also speaks in such a case, to contradict her claim that he is not capable of fathering, meaning that now his strength has weakened; and if the second wife bears a child, it is clarified that his strength has not weakened. However, according to this answer of Tosafot, if he already had children we do not attribute the failure to merit, and therefore she must say he is not capable of fathering. Necessarily, even if he had not previously had children but now fathers with the second wife, the first wife leaves without a ketubah, for she cannot say his strength weakened; and since he fathered, we do not attribute it to his not meriting to be built from her. The Rosh ruled in responsum that even if the second wife bore a child, the first wife does not lose, because in that responsum he rejects this answer, and in his rulings too it implies that he does not accept this answer; he treated as primary the Rash's answer above, that as long as she has not been established with three men, we say he did not merit to be built from her, even though he fathers children from other women. According to this, Rama's words in the gloss are astonishing to me, for nearby he wrote like Tosafot's answer, that when he had children she must say he is not capable of fathering; otherwise we do not attribute the matter to merit, but say she is barren. If so, how can he say that after ten years he cannot say I will go and test myself? If he fathers with another woman, we no longer attribute it to merit, but to her. It does not seem right to say that he holds that even according to Tosafot, if he previously had children we attribute the defect to her, but if he had not previously had children we do not say he may test himself. First, his language implies that even if he went and tested himself and the second wife bore a child, the first wife would not lose; and according to Tosafot, certainly if the second wife bore a child we attribute the defect to the first, as implied in the Rosh's responsum there. Also, from where would he derive such a distinction? This requires study. The Tur's words too are at first glance astonishing. In the law where she comes with the claim I need a staff for my hand and says he is not capable of fathering, it is difficult: it speaks where he claims nothing against her, and it also does not speak of a third husband, for that was not mentioned. If so, according to Rif's approach, and also according to the Rash's answer in Tosafot, she need not say he is not capable of fathering. If he holds like Tosafot's answer that where he has children she must say he is not capable of fathering, that is difficult: first, the Rosh in the responsum above rejects that answer; second, why did the Tur write even if he has children, when the case is specifically where he has children? Also, the earlier difficulty remains: if so, how could he cite the Rosh's responsum that when he fathers from the second wife we attribute it to his not having merited to be built from the first? If he holds like Rambam, as I wrote above in seif katan 9, it is also difficult: if she must claim with certainty that the impediment is from him, then necessarily the sugya of I will go and test myself also speaks in such a case, and if he tested himself and the second wife bore a child, we no longer attribute it to merit. Regarding the Tur, however, one may say that he rules like Tosafot: when she comes to force him to divorce, we say that since he already had children, one may say the defect comes from her and she cannot force him to divorce. In the Rosh's responsum, however, the case is where he comes to divorce her and reduce her ketubah; then one may say the opposite, that even if he fathers from other women, perhaps he did not merit to be built from her. What he wrote in seif 30, that she claims he is not capable of fathering, and there the case is where he wants to divorce her, may be because he claims with certainty that the impediment is from her; therefore she must claim with certainty that he is not capable of fathering. With this one can explain the Tur's words. But Rama remains difficult, for it is impossible to explain him this way, since he wrote these laws on the case where she wants to force him to divorce.

The rule is: one who marries a woman in violation of a prohibition is compelled even with lashes, as written at the end of Perek HaMadir; likewise if he married a sheniyah, and likewise all those listed at the beginning of seif 7. According to all opinions we compel with lashes, because the Talmud states explicitly that we compel them. In all cases where compulsion is because of them, they are not given the added ketubah. But where the beit din compels him to divorce because she did not bear children for ten years, she does have the added ketubah, for it is not because of her, as written in Tosafot at the end of Perek HaBa Al Yevimto. When she comes with a claim, the Talmud there states that we compel him, meaning with lashes, and Tosafot wrote likewise; however, Rashba wrote in responsum 1192 that we do not compel him with lashes, but add to the ketubah like the law of a rebellious husband. One who stayed with his wife ten years: according to Rif we compel with lashes, and Rambam wrote likewise in chapter 15 law 1; see Derishah. In Hagahot Maimoniyot, Perek HaBa Al Yevimto, he first wrote that it requires study in our time whether he can divorce her against her will, since Rabbenu Gershom decreed not to divorce a woman against her will; afterward he cited a responsum of Rabbenu Gershom ruling that he may divorce her against her will. It is forced to say that this responsum was written before the enactment. In Responsa Rivash, siman 15, he wrote that the sages of the generations turned a blind eye in matters of marriage, because otherwise they would have had to compel everyone and that is impossible; therefore, whenever the prohibition is not intrinsic to the marriage, they turned a blind eye. He also gave a reason according to the custom of his country, that he could marry another woman; even though this one does not bear children, he can marry another, and therefore they turned a blind eye. It follows that according to our custom the second reason is not relevant at all. Even according to the first reason, the prohibition nevertheless remains in place, though the sages of the generation do not compel; the prohibition still rests on the person. Therefore it appears that even in our time, when the cherem of Rabbenu Gershom exists, nevertheless if she did not bear children for ten years he may divorce her against her will. In all these cases it appears that even if a sage ruled leniently and she married, we compel her to leave. This is not comparable to what is written in siman 17 seif 34 in the name of a responsum of the Rosh regarding endless waters, that if a sage ruled leniently she does not leave, because there we hold that most people die and therefore we are lenient; but in these prohibitions there is no permission at all.
