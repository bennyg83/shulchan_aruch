<b>In Even HaEzer 41</b> the Beit Yosef wrote there in the name of the Ran that the intent of the Baal Halakhot Gedolot and the Rambam is that the buyer wants the sale to take effect upon both the inferior and the superior item. The Rosh, however, explained the intent of the Baal Halakhot Gedolot differently: since he said the word "all of you," including everyone in a single word, all agree that one cannot say that the transaction took effect only upon half of them; see there.

In Choshen Mishpat 216 it is written in the name of the Rambam that when one says, "land and palm trees," it is one sale, unlike the view of the Rashbam, who treats it as two sales, so that if the seller has no palm trees the sale remains valid for the land according to the valuation of experts, as much as belongs to the field. According to this, since "land and palm trees" is one sale for the Rambam, there is no reason to distinguish between the case of "you and a donkey" and the case of "all of you." One may say that for this reason the Ran, when explaining the Rambam, also gave another rationale, distinguishing between the buyers and the objects acquired, and did not make the distinction depend upon the word "all of you" being inclusive. For "you and a donkey" is also as though it were in one word, like "land and palm trees," since in the case of "you and a donkey" it was impossible to include them in one word. The Rosh, however, who mentioned only the intent of the Baal Halakhot Gedolot, may more readily distinguish between "all of you" and "you and a donkey," since the latter is not one utterance.

See the Beit Yosef at the end of section 207, in the name of the Rashba, regarding one who fined himself ten for building the city and ten for his fellow, where we do not divide the obligation. The Taz there, in section 216, wrote as practical halakhah that we do not extract from the possessor, for we say that the whole matter is one sale, and when part of it is void the whole is void. He concluded from what is stated there in section 182 that the buyer may retract. On a quick reading this implies that where the buyer does not retract, the seller cannot retract, as explained in the Taz there. Yet it appears that in the case of two places, both parties may retract, for one can say that one place is worth much in his eyes and the other less, as the Taz wrote there. This is unlike the case in section 182, where the agent fulfilled his agency and merely added to it; if he had not added, he would have done only what he was commissioned to do, without alteration. Where he diminished, however, there are reasons to say that the principal does not want two, and the same applies regarding places.

Even if he initially revealed his mind that he wished to sell one place for a certain sum and another place for a certain sum, and afterward it became known that a prior sale had already taken effect upon one place, the later sale of both does not take effect. Even with respect to the second place, the sale does not take effect according to the sum he wanted, for he can retract; his willingness to sell both may have been because he thought that the first sale had not taken effect. [Two words are missing.]

I wrote nearby regarding the Taz in Choshen Mishpat 216, that the sale is void because the intent is that each of them may retract. I remember writing in my youth about what is written in Bnei Yaakov, which contains discussions on the Baer Heitev concerning these laws of palm trees, but now I do not remember it. The words of the Taz have spread as practical halakhah, and one need not be concerned until a contrary ruling is clarified.

A question came before me regarding the lease of several businesses included in what is called an arende. One person leased some of those businesses to another and stipulated that he bring him a certain sum of money by the end of the day. Near the end of the day he said to another, "I leased such-and-such businesses to so-and-so on condition that he bring money today, and he did not bring it; I will lease everything to you." They clasped hands. Afterward, at the end of the day, the first person brought the money, and the lessor did not want to accept it because of the handclasp he had with the second. We said that it is impossible to nullify the first sale, and consequently the sale to the second is void, as stated above in the name of the Taz.

In Even HaEzer 41 the decisors wrote that because he included them all in the single word "all of you," the whole matter is void when part of it is void; alternatively, because he wishes to acquire both the superior and the inferior, the whole is void. So too in the case above, where he included everything in one word. It may be said that even according to the Rashbam this is one sale and all is void, unlike "land and palm trees," where, even though he had to say it in two words, it is nevertheless two sales since he did not specify that it should be one sale. The same applies to "you and a donkey": although he could not include them in one word, the whole is not void as it is in "all of you."

It appears that even according to the Rif, in whose name it is written in Even HaEzer 41 that even with "all of you" in one word the whole is not void, despite the buyer's usual desire for the superior and inferior together, he would agree in the case above that the whole is void. Betrothals of women are not a case in which one person conveys everything, and it is not appropriate that part should be void because of another part. This is unlike one seller selling to one buyer; since part belongs to the owner of the whole, the whole is void. We do not multiply disputes. Since he included it in one utterance and one sale, no one disputes the Rambam there.

So too with "land with palm trees": if there are no palm trees, the sale is void either from the seller's side or from the buyer's side. It is not a matter of saying, "as people appraise it, how much belongs to this," for we hold that when there was no fixed purchase price there is no acquisition, and we do not say that his intent was according to people's appraisal. According to the Rashbam, who says that the sale remains valid for the field according to appraisal, that is because such is people's intent when they include two sales in one sum; it is not so in one sale. The case in Choshen Mishpat 209, of something not yet in existence together with something in existence, concerns a case where there was a known sum for each one by itself.

In Choshen Mishpat 227:35, where they evaluate how much belongs to the wage of the person and how much to the hire of the animal, and there is recoverable overreaching in the animal's hire, even though it was one lease, one can say that for overreaching, which follows the valuation of ordinary worth among people, the value of each item can be assessed independently. The holy book Machaneh Ephraim is not before me now, but I remember that he discussed whether there is a prohibition of overreaching at less than one-sixth. Even if less than one-sixth involves no prohibition, where it is more than one-sixth, which people do not waive, one may say that there is a prohibition even with land and similar matters; Scripture excluded them only from return of the overcharge or cancellation of the sale.

This requires analysis now. Perhaps the return of overcharge or cancellation of the sale is only a rabbinic enactment, which is why there is a dispute whether the measure is one-sixth or one-third. According to the view that what the Merciful One said not to do, if one did it, it is ineffective, one could say that the sale is reversed where people do not intend to waive; the Sages enacted that sometimes the overcharge is returned and the sale remains valid. According to this, one must say that people waive less than one-sixth and there is no prohibition, for otherwise the sale would automatically not stand. It is difficult to say that the Sages enacted that the sale should not be void even though a prohibition was committed. With land and similar matters one can say that Scripture revealed that the overcharge is not returned even though a prohibition was committed, but it is difficult to say that the holy Torah permitted overreaching in land and the like, for consecrated property is also included in the exclusion, and plainly it is more severe to overreach there. If, however, doing the act is effective, one may still say that the sale's reversal is from the holy Torah by reason: since there is a mistake in the sale, and had he known he would not have sold, the sale does not take effect. With land and the like, the holy Torah excluded the need to return the overcharge, though there remains a prohibition to overreach. Or perhaps according to the view that the act is effective, there is some derivation that the overcharge is returned, or perhaps the return is a rabbinic enactment. This needs careful analysis now.

Therefore it is obvious that it is proper to estimate separately the value of a person's labor, which is like land, and the hire of an animal. This is like the proof written by the Terumat HaDeshen there, that one estimates the hire of a worker according to appraisal, because people presumably act according to ordinary practice and do not leave what is fixed among people. All the more so, we presume that they guarded themselves from the prohibition of overreaching. There is no reason to say that both agreed to a deviation from value and that there was no prohibition, since there is a presumption that a person does not throw away his money for nothing.

So too regarding the measure of overreaching, it is proper to estimate the person's wage according to ordinary value, for with respect to the prohibition there is no distinction between a person and an animal, and it is preferable to minimize deviation from ordinary practice. If we were to say that all the overreaching was only in the animal's hire, there would be two deviations: a great overcharge in the person's wage, and a deviation from ordinary practice, where the person's wage is treated, for example, as equal to the animal's hire, although here they greatly separated the person's wage from the animal's hire.

Even if there is no prohibition of overreaching in land, just as one could say that all the overcharge was in the person's wage so as not to presume that he committed a prohibition, and also so that his deceit should succeed and he should not need to return it, so too one could say of the other party that he presumably focused more on the animal's hire because it was easier for him to discern, knowing that overcharge which people do not usually waive is one-sixth or more. One can also say that he trusted that his fellow would not overreach him in the animal's hire, since it is forbidden to overreach there, whereas with land, which is lighter and is not returned, he presumably knew to guard himself from being overreached. Since there is no decisive indication either way, ordinary practice decides that the person's wage and the animal's hire are equal by way of example, and since their unspecified intent followed ordinary practice, one could there return the hire and nullify it. As the Sema wrote there, it appears that automatically the sale is void also regarding the person's wage, and as the Taz wrote in section 216 above, that the whole is one sale and valuation cannot separate the parts. The reasoning still requires careful analysis.

In the holy book Derishat Aryeh on Even HaEzer there, section 41, he wrote in the name of Maharshal a proof from the fact that they proved that when it was stolen from her as well she is not betrothed, from their statement, "and it was the sabbatical year." If the entire basket belonged to the gentile women, there would also have been theft from others; see there. Perhaps this is not conclusive. Although one may not divide partnership property without the partners' consent except with good and bad divided fairly, nevertheless one partner can sell without the knowledge of his fellow, and partners are agents for one another even where damage results. See the Shakh in Choshen Mishpat, who wrote that the partner may later claim from the partner who acted improperly, but the act he performed is not void. If, when it is stolen from her, the betrothal is not void because she presumably transferred it to him, the same is true if she has a partner. Perhaps, however, there is a distinction between a sale, where partners are agents for one another to sell, and a gift, where one partner cannot give away the property. See the Urim there. The reasoning written in Derishat Aryeh, that it is strained, may perhaps not be strained; see there.

Perhaps in the case of the basket it speaks where he did not know at all that there were sisters among them and thought that all of them were unrelated to one another. If so, it is not appropriate to say that the unrelated women acquired the entire basket. Or perhaps the woman who received the betrothal acquired the entire basket, and the betrothal of the other woman took the form of guarantorship, like "give to so-and-so and I will be betrothed." But that does not seem to be the meaning there, as the Derishat Aryeh wrote. This requires much careful analysis.
