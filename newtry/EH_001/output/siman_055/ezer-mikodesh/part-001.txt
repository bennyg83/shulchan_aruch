<b>סעיף א' בהגה (שהביאה החתן לביתו)</b> הר"ן ז"ל כתב שממה שאמר הכתוב אם בית אישה נדרה כו' מבואר שחופה היינו הבאה אל ביתו. אולי כמו שכתבו הפוסקים ז"ל שחצר שכירות אינו קונה לו ע"ש בש"כ ז"ל בחו"מ שי"ג ואינו קונה רק למשכיר אולי כן הוא גם אודות חופה שהיא בית אישה דצריך להיות ביתו לצמיתות עולם ולא על ידי שכירות לבד או על ידי שאלה ואולי אין סגנון חופה כמו לגבי קנין חצר ומועיל שכירות כמו קנין מכירה לגמרי וצל"ע כעת אך במכירה לזמן כבחו"מ רי"ב אולי כ"ע מודי שקונה בו הלוקח קנין חצר וכן הוא לגבי חופה וכל שכן מתנה על מנת להחזיר שהוא כמעט עדיף ממכירה לזמן וצלע"ע כעת היטב:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Section 1 in the gloss, "that the groom brought her into his house."</b> The Ran wrote that from the verse "if she vowed in her husband's house" it is clear that chuppah means bringing her into his house. Perhaps, just as the decisors wrote that a rented courtyard does not acquire for him, see the Shakh in Choshen Mishpat 313, and it acquires only for the lessor, so too regarding chuppah, which is "her husband's house," it must be his house permanently, not merely by rental or borrowing. Or perhaps the form of chuppah is not like courtyard acquisition, and rental is effective like a full sale. This requires analysis for now. With a time-limited sale, as in Choshen Mishpat 212, perhaps all agree that the buyer acquires courtyard rights, and the same would apply to chuppah; all the more with a gift on condition to return, which is almost stronger than a time-limited sale. This still requires careful analysis.

<b>There, "a curtain spread over poles."</b> I wrote elsewhere regarding the chuppah set up on poles. According to what I wrote there, one may say that this is the reason for the custom to make the chuppah specifically under the sky: if it were set up in a house, it might sometimes be set up in her house and be ineffective; or the house may later be found to have been held by the man, or by the one who gave permission to set up the chuppah there, through theft or mistake. It is also so as not to embarrass one who has no house, just as the groom does not recite the blessings so as not to embarrass one who cannot. Thus the essential acquisition should be equal for all. The place before the entrance of the holy synagogue is known to belong to the public.

By setting up the poles like doorframes on all four sides there is also a good and precious remembrance of the house of our father Abraham, peace be upon him, which was open on all four sides, reminding the couple to accept upon themselves that their home be open generously, resembling Abraham's house in love of kindness and good traits. The main hospitality is through the woman, as stated in the holy Zohar regarding the Shunammite woman. This is better than any minimal locking or fencing that would create possession for acquiring land. Even if it cannot be said that it is acquired by each partner when he walks there, since sometimes a guest marrying is not a partner at all and the place is usually like public domain rather than a partners' courtyard, nevertheless the townspeople plainly do not object to establishing a proper chuppah there, and this includes clear consent that the place be acquired to the groom for that time.

In the courtyard of the synagogue itself, these countries do not customarily set up a chuppah, for acquisition of the place is less applicable there, since it may not be sold or rented, especially a synagogue of a city. In places where they do so, the court's stipulation applies according to the custom, and the original custom was presumably made by explicit condition. For a widow, however, since we hold that chuppah does not acquire her, and she is not acquired through bringing her to the husband's house, the custom is to set up a widow's chuppah indoors, without concern that the house may be hers or stolen.

Likewise, the head-covering custom in these countries on the morning of the chuppah seems to have an element of chuppah as bringing into his house. Since the owner of the house grants permission for the groom, the witnesses, the attendants, and others to enter, and a human partition is considered a real partition, the Sages may have intended that it serve temporarily as a human partition to acquire the place as a loan. Because chuppah means covering, as the Derishah u-Perishah wrote, they also make a covering over her head, like a hinuma. Still, the chuppah of poles appears to be like his house itself. There is no deficiency because those holding the tent against the wind are not the husband's, since the body of the tent is his; perhaps they lend themselves to him for the time of holding it, like his hired workers.
