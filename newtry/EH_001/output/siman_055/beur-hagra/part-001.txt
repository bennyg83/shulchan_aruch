<b>And some say that the chuppah, etc.</b> In the Yerushalmi at the end of Sotah: what are "canopies"? Sheets decorated with designs and golden ornaments hanging from them. It teaches that one may make frames and hang on them whatever he wishes. So too in the Bavli there: "it was taught also, etc.; what is chuppah, etc." The Yerushalmi calls the crowns of bridegrooms gilded ornaments, but it is one matter. See Rashi there, s.v. "zehorit," and Tosafot Gittin 7a, s.v. "crowns"; this is our chuppah.

The Beit Yosef in section 61 cites the Ittur, who rejected this opinion from the expression "until he brings her into the chuppah," implying entry into a designated place. The text should refer to the Yerushalmi in Sotah, not Sukkah. Rather, chuppah is the father's handing her over and bringing her into a house containing a distinctive feature, such as decorated sheets. This is the practice with a virgin; with a widow, once he brings her in for the sake of marriage and there is a designated place and a prepared bed, it is like a house containing a chuppah. See the Rosh, Sukkah chapter 2, and the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri in Ketubot.

The main point is that the chuppah which effects acquisition is her departure from her father's house and her going with the groom to his house for the sake of marriage, showing that she is in her husband's domain, as the Ran holds. It is not necessary that he seclude himself with her. Nevertheless, the making of the chuppah is with sheets like a canopy, as in the Yerushalmi, and this is our chuppah too. Since among us, even after marriage they may live in her father's house, the father removes her from his domain to the synagogue courtyard, and there they establish the chuppah with sheets; that is the essential chuppah and effects acquisition for all matters. The synagogue courtyard is the place of acquisition, no worse than her own courtyard. The canopy itself is not indispensable, but since the custom is to make it, their intent for marriage centers under it. The canopy is not an independent domain that acquires; the courtyard is.

<b>And some say, etc.</b> See the Rosh on Ketubot there, Rashi there s.v. "and collects," and Tosafot Bava Metzia 17b s.v. "from." This is the principal halakhah, for their proof is very compelling. It seems to me there is another proof from what is stated in the fifteenth chapter of Yevamot, "one betrothed, etc." Even the Rambam's explanation that it speaks where he wrote a ketubah for one woman and it was lost is not correct. First, the Mishnah does not mention a ketubah claim at all; and when Rabbi Akiva says "a bill of divorce and a ketubah," the mention of the bill of divorce is otherwise superfluous, unless the bill of divorce removes the ketubah claim. Further, the Gemara says "it taught betrothed," etc., and if the prohibition of betrothal is unrelated to ketubah it is not comparable to theft. Nor is there need to speak of a rabbinic prohibition when a Torah prohibition is already present.

Certainly all the sugyot prove that she has a ketubah, as stated in Kiddushin 66a and many other places. In the first chapter of Bava Metzia they ask from where we know that she has a ketubah, and although it is unresolved how we know it, certainly she has a ketubah. Still, the custom recorded by the Rav is also correct, as the Ra'avad wrote: specifically in their times they relied on the court's condition, and even in places where they wrote ketubot, some wrote them for an arusah too; this is unlike the present. See the Mordechai at the beginning of chapter 2 of Ketubot.
