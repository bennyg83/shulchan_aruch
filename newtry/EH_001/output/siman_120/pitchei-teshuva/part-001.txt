<b>שהאשה תתן. </b> כתב בס' ישועות עקב סק"ד וז"ל והנה יש לי מקום עיין היכא שהקנה הסופר הקלף והדיו לבעל כדרך ואח"ז אין רצון האשה לשלם להסופר שכרו אי יכול הסופר לחזור מהקנאה זו או נימא כיון דלא התנה ע"ז והקנה בסתם הקלף והדיו קנוי לו אף שלא נתנה לו שכר כל עיקר ונראה לפע"ד ברור דיכול הסופר לחזור בו והדבר דומה למברחת ולנותן מתנה ושמע שמת בנו במדה"י דאינו צריך תנאי דסתמא דמלתא על דעת כן נותן וה"ה בזה כו' ובזה נתיישב על נכון הסוגיא פ"ב דגיטין דאמר יכילנא למיפסל כולהו גיטי דעלמא כו' עכ"ל ע"ש. ומזה מוכח דס"ל בפשיטות כדעת התו"ג שיובא בסמוך דגבי כתיבת הגט לכ"ע לא אמרינן אומן קונה בשבח כלי ודו"ק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That the woman shall give.</b> Yeshuot Yaakov s.k. 4 writes that there is room to consider a case where the scribe transferred the parchment and ink to the husband in the usual way, and afterward the woman refuses to pay the scribe his fee. Can the scribe retract that transfer, or since he made no condition and transferred the parchment and ink without qualification, are they the husband's even though she paid nothing? In his view it is clear that the scribe may retract. The case resembles one who gives a gift because he heard that his son died overseas, where no explicit condition is needed because the gift is plainly given on that assumption. So too here, the transfer was plainly on the assumption of payment. This resolves the passage in the second chapter of Gittin in which a scribe said he could invalidate all bills of divorce. From this it is evident that Yeshuot Yaakov follows the view, cited nearby, that with respect to writing a get all agree that a craftsman does not acquire ownership through the improvement he adds to the object.

<b>קודם יאוש. </b> עבה"ט ומ"ש אבל אם נותן לו בהקפה ועדיין לא פרע לו לא הוי גזל. עיין בס' תו"ג שכ' דנראה דאפי' לקחו להקלף בהקפ' רק שקבע זמן לפרעון דלא תנשא עד יעבור הזמן דאימר לא יפרע לו ויהי' המוכר עייל ונפיק אזוזי ויתבטל המקח למפרע כמבואר בח"מ סי' ק"צ (סי"ז) ויהיו הבנים ממזרים ובקלף אינו מועיל אף אם תרצה האשה ליתן המעות בעצמה דהא קלף שלה לא אקנו לי' רבנן עכ"ל ועיין בס' גט מקושר בסג"ר אות י"ז שהביא ג"כ דבר זה בשם הרב בית מאיר (אלא דמלשונו שם משמע קצת דה"ה אם הקלף של בטל מתחילה או ע"י מה שהקנה לו הסופר קודם הכתיבה רק שהסופר עייל ונפיק אזוזי דמי כתיבת הגט. ובאמת בכה"ג הדבר תלוי בהא דאומן קונה בש"כ שיבואר בסמוך ודעת הרב בעל תו"ג דלא שייך בזה אומן קונה כמו שיובא בס"ק שאח"ז) והוא ז"ל השיג עליו דליתא לדין זה דהא במוכר שדהו מפני רעתה גם בעייל ונפיק אזוזי קנה כדאי' בח"מ שם סי"א וכ"ש בכה"ג שאין גט שוה להסופר ועיקר נתינתו להבעל על דבר שכר ואינו דומה לחפץ כו' ע"ש עוד שהאריך בכמה פרטים בזה. ומ"ש עוד ואיכא לספוקי אם גזל קלף כו' ע' בזה בס' ג"פ סק"ו ובס' שעה"מ ס"א מה"ג מ"ש בזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Before despair.</b> See Baer Heitev. What he wrote, that if he gives it to him on credit and has not yet paid him it is not theft: see Torat Gittin, who wrote that it appears that even if he took the parchment on credit but fixed a time for payment, she should not marry until that time passes, for perhaps he will not pay, and the seller will repeatedly demand his money, and the sale will be void retroactively, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 190:17, making the children mamzerim. With parchment, it does not help even if the woman wants to give the money herself, for the Sages did not transfer her parchment to him. See Get Mekushar, Seder Get Rishon, letter 17, who also brought this matter in the name of Beit Meir. From his language there it sounds somewhat that the same applies if the parchment belonged to the husband from the beginning, or through the scribe transferring it to him before the writing, but the scribe repeatedly demands payment for the writing of the get. In truth, in such a case the matter depends on whether an artisan acquires through the enhancement of the vessel, which will be explained nearby, and the view of Torat Gittin is that an artisan's acquisition does not apply here, as will be brought in the next seif katan. He disagreed with this ruling, saying it is not correct, for one who sells his field because of its poor quality acquires even where the seller repeatedly demands payment, as stated in Choshen Mishpat there, seif 11. All the more in this case, where the get has no value to the scribe and his main transfer to the husband is for the wage. It is not comparable to an ordinary object; see there, where he discusses several details at length. What Baer Heitev further wrote, that there is room to doubt if he stole parchment, etc., see Ginat Veradim, s.k. 6, and Sha'ar HaMelekh, Laws of Geirushin 1, who wrote about this.

<b>They recognize and know.</b> See Responsa Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 123, where he was asked by the gaon author of Or Chadash about a case in which one agent came with a letter from the rabbi of the holy community of Yass: a man named Binyamin, called Wolf, son of Shelomo HaKohen, had married there a woman Leah daughter of Chaim HaKohen, called Zelig, and because of necessity had left there; therefore wherever the agent would find him, he should request from him a Get. From the rabbi's handwriting it was recognizable that he was precise with names, for he was careful regarding the woman's father to write HaKohen before the nickname, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, siman 129 seif 7 in the gloss. This agent found in Poland a man who had married a woman in Yass. The agent himself did not recognize the man, for he had never seen him; only after he investigated after a man of such a name, they showed him that man. That man had not remained in Poland thirty days, but had spent the previous winter in a certain city, and a man came from there and recognized that this man had been there in the winter. He said that there he was not called Wolf at all, but was called Binyamin by people, and was called to the Torah as Binyamin Ze'ev; in the collection documents in the husband's possession his name was written Binyamin Ze'ev, and so he signs himself. If so, there are two changes here, etc. The rabbi who asked wrote that regarding writing the names we should press and direct matters to write Binyamin Ze'ev called Wolf. But the difficulty is that it is explained in Shulchan Aruch and Beit Shmuel that the witnesses must recognize that this man, as his name is written in the Get, has a wife whose name is as written in the Get. Here there is no facial recognition, and necessarily the witnesses rely on this: since we know this man is called thus, and from the man's words and from the rabbi of Yass we learn that a man with such a name has a wife with such a name. But on the contrary, here we know a man whose name is Binyamin Ze'ev, while the letter from Yass says Binyamin called Wolf, so at least there is no identifying sign. Noda BiYehudah answered him: regarding what you wrote about writing the names, that you would write Binyamin Ze'ev called Wolf, my opinion is not so. Rather, one should write Binyamin called Wolf, demitkarei Binyamin Ze'ev, since in the place of delivery he certainly was not called Ze'ev at all. If time permitted, it would be more correct for him to remain here thirty days, be called to the Torah as Binyamin, and be called so by people; then the place of writing would match the place of delivery. But if the agent is pressed to continue on his way, one should write the first formula. Yet the fact that they do not recognize the husband is difficult. Although in a time of danger one writes even though they do not recognize, we learned this only for a time of danger, not merely for pressing circumstances; and what danger is here? See the new Responsa Bach, siman 92, who did not write so, and it will be cited below in seif katan 15. Regarding what is clear from the questioner's words, that if there had been no change in names at all and the man's name were established here as the name mentioned in the Yass letter, it would be simple to rely on this: to me the matter requires study according to the Tur, which is the opinion of the some say view brought by Rama, that even the scribe must recognize. In my humble opinion the reason is that although the facts are correct and true, if the scribe does not recognize this at the time of writing, it is not called written for her sake, etc. Now, if he recognizes the husband, it is enough that he knows the husband has a wife with such a name; if so, he writes for her sake wherever she may be, for this husband is her husband. But if he knows only that the woman being divorced has a husband with such a name, if he does not know this is her husband, even though he knows his name is such, perhaps he is another man and has no wife with such a name. Even if afterward it becomes clear that this is the man, since it was not clarified at the time of writing, it is not called written for his sake. He then wrote that nevertheless, if there had been no change in names, it would have been proper to combine the fact that sometimes they write even when they do not recognize, especially since I saw at the end of his letter that there is a reliable man there who recognizes that this man married a woman in Yass, though he does not know his wife's name. If so, his name, his father's name, and the city Yass would have sufficed to recognize the man. But now that there is a change in names and the sign works against us, I cannot permit. These are his words; see there. See what I wrote below in seif katan 12.
