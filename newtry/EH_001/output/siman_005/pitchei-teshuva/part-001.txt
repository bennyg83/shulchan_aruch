Not in his sanctity. Baer Heitev wrote and Chelkat Mechokek challenged Rambam, etc.; apparently for his honor since Rambam holds through illness kosher as Shulchan Aruch below — one can say there in Laws of Kehunah discusses through illness and since kosher plain eats kodashim; but Chelkat Mechokek concludes with full verse "from your thigh and belly eat kodshei kodashim" — thus main Chelkat Mechokek's question from full verse — difficult for Rashi and Rosh who hold also through illness disqualified; but not so difficult — still can establish verse pure heavenly cause kosher for all — tzarich iyun. <b>But according to Rashi and the Rosh.</b> See Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel. See Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 3, concerning one who has only one testicle, where there was doubt whether this was from Heaven or caused by smallpox, and whether he is valid. He wrote that even if we assume plainly that it came through smallpox, he should be validated, following Rambam's view, as all the later authorities, Bach, Chelkat Mechokek, and Beit Shmuel, concluded that the essential law follows Rambam, and that Rashi too holds so; the Rosh is a lone view, and we should not impose stringencies where there is no remedy and where mishap may result. What the Rosh wrote, that the Yerushalmi implies like him, was already examined by commentators who infer the opposite, and Beit Ephraim on Yoreh Deah siman 36 cited Yam Shel Shlomo in Yevamot that Rashi's view is like Rambam and so too the Yerushalmi. He also cited this in the name of Tosafot Yom Tov, and it is clear one may rely on all these authorities. Moreover, even where one testicle was entirely removed by human action, many early authorities validate him, and several later authorities strongly adopted leniency based on Rabbenu Tam and ruled so in practice; see the previous s.k. Therefore in our case there is no concern at all, especially where it is only a doubt of illness, and his opinion agrees to permit. Birkei Yosef, ot 5, also holds that we follow Rambam. See Beit Meir, who wrote that it requires study if he became disqualified by Heaven through illness and the organ then had to be removed by human hands to prevent further illness: since it stood to be cut, perhaps it is like something already cut, as we hold according to R. Shimon, and is considered by the hand of Heaven and valid. In a manuscript responsum from the gaon R. Chaim of Volozhin, I saw that he too concluded that the main law follows Rambam, that an injury caused by illness is considered by the hand of Heaven and is valid. He also agreed that if it had rotted before being cut, then even if cut by a person we are not concerned, for something dissolved is treated as absent everywhere. He further argued that one may rely on Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, who strengthened the view that Rabbenu Tam and his supporters permit even where the remaining testicle is the right one; the primary Yerushalmi reading is one word, "sheleimin." He further proved that even wholeness is not required, because our Talmud does not distinguish as the Yerushalmi does. Sefer HaTerumah states explicitly that the world relies on the other teaching from Kerem BeYavneh: anyone who has only one testicle is like a natural saris and is valid; on this they relied to validate men from whom one testicle was cut. We learn that the halachah follows R. Yishmael son of R. Yochanan ben Beroka, that there is no distinction between right and left or between whole and not whole, and that the practical custom already spread to be lenient. Nevertheless, the responsum concludes that one should not come to decide lightly; in Torah matters the poor in knowledge should generally follow the stringent view, though one who wishes to be lenient has great pillars to rely on, including Rabbenu Tam, his supporters, and Sefer HaTerumah. Chatam Sofer, siman 17, later discussed a case where the right testicle became diseased and enlarged, physicians removed it, and a bean-sized remnant remained. He wrote that Rabbenu Tam's view gives no support there either way, but one can argue it is not called human-caused, since the main law follows Rambam that illness is from Heaven and valid. Semag's wording also implies that even when a person cuts, disqualification applies only where he cuts negligently, not where he cuts for healing. He concludes with care according to the circumstances. The later discussion continues with further distinctions, but the practical thrust is that illness-caused removal has strong grounds for validation.
