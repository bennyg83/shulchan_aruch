<b>יצא עמנו</b>. משמע אפי' אינו מזכיר שמו כלל מ"מ כשידוע שלא יצא שום אדם אלא זה מותרת ונראה דהכל לפי הענין אם העיר משומר דא"א לצאת בלי ידיעה וידוע דלא יצא שום אדם אלא זה מותרת אבל אם אפשר לצאת בלי ידיעה אסורה אא"כ מעידים שיצא אחד מתושבי העיר הזאת אז מחפשים אם אינו חסר שום אדם אלא זה מותרת וע' תשוב' מהרי"ו סי' מ' ותשו' מ"ב סי' מ"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He went out with us.</b> This implies that even if he does not mention his name at all, nevertheless when it is known that no one left except this person, she is permitted. It appears that everything depends on the circumstances: if the city is guarded so that one cannot leave without it being known, and it is known that no one left except this person, she is permitted. But if it is possible to leave without it being known, she is forbidden unless they testify that one of the residents of this city left; then we investigate, and if no one is missing except this person, she is permitted. See responsum Mahari Weil siman 40 and responsum Mishnat Binyamin siman 44.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>We do not examine witnesses concerning women.</b> The reason is that the matter is one that is likely to become revealed; alternatively, because she investigates carefully and then marries. The Rambam follows the first reason; see the Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 13. If the witness is before us, we ask how he saw and how he knows, as stated above.

See siman 11, where we rule that to forbid a woman to her husband requires formal interrogation and examination. Here, however, interrogation and examination are not required. Since she collects the ketubah, the case resembles monetary law, even though permitting her to another man is like capital law. In any event, capital cases do not apply in our time. But regarding forbidding her to her husband there is no monetary issue at all: if she is forbidden, she has no ketubah; and if she is permitted, she nevertheless does not collect the ketubah. Therefore it is treated as capital law. As for joining witnesses of impropriety to forbid her to her husband, witnesses are not joined in capital matters, as I wrote in siman 11. See the Beit Yosef there and the responsum of the Rosh.

<b>והכירוה בהם</b>. וכאן לכ"ע די ט"ע בלא סימנים ועסי' קל"ב שם מבואר דלא סמכינן על ט"ע אלא כשיש לו מיגו וכאן לא שייך מיגו וי"ל שם החשש שמא משקר מחמת שכירות שלו או דאל יתרעם עליו אבל כאן ליכ' חשש זה לפ"ז אם יש איש אחר שאינו שליח אמר דיש לו ט"ע בגט מהני אפילו לשיטות הרמב"ם שם דלא מהני ט"ע כלל והיינו יש שפסקו שהבי' שם נראה דוקא שם מחמיר משום דאפשר בגט אחר אבל כאן מודה, ומצינו חילוק זה בהרא"ש אליב' דהרי"ף גם י"ל ט"ע בגוף אדם עדיף טפי, אבל א"ל דכאן איירי דשני עדים הכירוהו אבל ע"א אינו נאמן בט"ע, דא"כ איך ס"ד דהעכו"ם היה נאמן יותר מע"א גם לקמן סעיף כ"ח מבואר אפילו ע"א נאמן בט"ע אם לא ראה הטביעה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And they recognized him by them.</b> Here, according to all opinions, visual recognition without signs is sufficient. See siman 132, where it is explained that we do not rely on visual recognition unless he has a migo, and here no migo applies. One may say that there the concern is perhaps he lies because of his wages, or so that the other not resent him; but here that concern does not apply. According to this, if another man who is not the agent says that he has visual recognition of the get, it helps even according to the Rambam's approach there, that visual recognition does not help at all, namely according to the authorities brought there. It appears that only there are we stringent because another get is possible, but here he agrees. We find this distinction in the Rosh according to the Rif. One may also say that visual recognition of a person's body is stronger. But one cannot say that here it speaks where two witnesses recognized him, whereas one witness is not believed through visual recognition; for if so, how would one have thought that a gentile would be more believed than one witness? Also below, seif 28, it is explicit that even one witness is believed through visual recognition if he did not see the drowning.

בגופו ובכלים דין א' והיינו כלים דלא מושלי דמהני בהו ט"ע מ"מ סימנים אמצעים לא מהני וכל זה איירי כשנמצא בבגדיו אשר לבוש ארנקי או נמצא בידו טבעות אבל אם נמצא ארנקי סמוך לו לא מהני כי יש לומר הארנקי מונח מאז ומקדם ואין לדמות דינים אלו להא דאיתא בפ' אלו מציאות אם נמצא פירות סמוך לכלי כמ"ש בת"ה סי' רס"ב ופשוט בעיני:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For we are concerned that the item may have been borrowed.</b> In the Talmud, according to the first answer, identifying marks are biblical proof, and the Mishnah's ruling that marks found in his clothing are ineffective is because we are concerned that the clothes may have been borrowed. The return of a get when the husband has marks in the container in which the get was found, without concern for borrowing, as written in siman 132, refers to objects that people do not ordinarily lend, namely a purse or rings.

According to the second answer, the Talmud establishes that identifying marks are rabbinic, and therefore an exceptionally distinctive mark is required. On that approach one need not say that we are concerned for borrowing; rather, the Mishnah can refer to marks that are not exceptionally distinctive. This is also implied by Tosafot: when the Talmud does not prove that we are concerned for borrowing, we do not assume such a concern. Tosafot write, according to the latter answer, that the Mishnah's statement about marks in his clothing refers to weak marks, such as white or red color. They add that on this approach we no longer say that we are concerned for borrowing. Thus, since borrowing is not proven, they do not assume it. If so, according to the answer that marks are rabbinic, we also should not say that we are concerned for borrowing; so write the Ran and Nimukei Yosef in accordance with Tosafot.

Although we rule that marks are rabbinic and nevertheless are concerned for borrowing, one may say that we are stringent for both talmudic explanations: according to the first, we are concerned for borrowing; according to the second, marks are rabbinic. Still, there may be a practical difference. If intermediate marks are found on the body and exceptionally distinctive marks are found on the utensils, they may suffice either way: if marks are biblical, as in the first answer, and we are concerned for borrowing, the bodily marks that are not exceptionally distinctive suffice; and if marks are rabbinic and such marks do not suffice, then we do not suspect borrowing. I heard this in the name of the Gaon Maharal of Prague.

Yet this is not compelled, for one may say that when the Talmud establishes marks as rabbinic and requires exceptionally distinctive marks, the concern for borrowing follows automatically. Since an exceptionally distinctive mark is required, any possibility that the item was borrowed prevents the mark from being exceptionally distinctive. Many authorities indeed hold that we are concerned for borrowing. At first glance it is difficult: whence do we know to suspect borrowing? According to the answer that marks are rabbinic, this concern is not necessary according to the Maharal; and according to the answer that marks are biblical, there are also two possible explanations, for one may say that the Mishnah refers to white or red clothing, and then there is no proof that borrowing is a concern. According to what I have written, this is resolved.

The Talmud says that purses and rings are not lent. The Beit Yosef, however, ruled in a responsum that we are concerned for borrowing even there, while the Chelkat Mechokek remains uncertain. This too must be explained as above: according to the answer that marks are rabbinic and exceptionally distinctive marks are required, even these utensils are not sufficiently distinctive, for although it is uncommon to lend them, people sometimes do lend them. The talmudic statement that we are not concerned for borrowing in these utensils applies specifically according to the view that marks are biblical; then no exceptionally distinctive mark is required, and we need not suspect borrowing in such utensils. But according to the view that marks are rabbinic, we suspect borrowing even in these objects. This is the Ritva's view cited by the Beit Yosef.

Still, this is difficult, for the sugya challenges the Mishnah, which does not rely on marks in clothing, from a get that is returned by marks in its container. This implies that the law of a get and the law of permitting the woman are the same. Yet below, in siman 132, the ruling is that we do not suspect borrowing in the case of a purse. Perhaps by a get the principal party is before us, and since he does not say that he lent the object, we do not suspect borrowing; here, however, we fear that the husband may have lent it. Even so, the sugya remains unsettled.

In my view the main ruling is that we do not suspect borrowing with purses and rings, in accordance with the plain sense of the sugya. Also according to Tosafot, the Ran, and Nimukei Yosef, we do not suspect borrowing at all. Although generally one should be stringent, as I wrote, with these utensils one may be lenient. I later saw in Chut HaShani, responsum 98, in the name of R. Naftali, several points like those I wrote. This also answers the Maharal's question: if, under the answer that marks are rabbinic, one says that we suspect borrowing, then visual recognition of the utensil would not help. Why then did the Mishnah teach that marks in utensils do not help? Even visual recognition, which is biblical, would not help; why mention marks, which are rabbinic? Rather, under that answer one must say that borrowing is not suspected. According to what I wrote, this is no proof: the Mishnah teaches that non-exceptional marks do not help, and the law of marks on the body and marks on utensils is the same. It refers to utensils that are not lent, where visual recognition would help, but intermediate marks do not help.

All this concerns a purse found in the garments he was wearing or rings found on his hand. But if a purse was found near him, it is ineffective, for one can say that the purse had been lying there from earlier. These laws should not be compared to the case in Bava Metzia, chapter Elu Metziot, of fruit found near a vessel, as explained in Terumat HaDeshen 262; this is clear to me.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And even a mole.</b> This is the Rambam's language. The general rule is that signs such as being tall or short and the like are weak signs and do not help, even when several signs are combined; if she married on the basis of such signs she must leave. Intermediate signs are called "distinctive signs," and the matter depends on whether signs are biblical or rabbinic: if signs are biblical, such signs help, and if signs are rabbinic, such signs do not help. In the conclusion, Rava holds that signs are rabbinic, and the Rif and Rosh cite only this conclusion. If she married on the basis of intermediate signs she need not leave, as R. Yerucham writes in the name of the Ramah, and so too the Darkei Moshe writes. The most distinctive signs are biblical, and therefore visual recognition is biblical.

Regarding a mole, according to the Gemara's first answer Rava holds that signs are biblical, and R. Eliezer and the Sages dispute whether a mole is liable to change. It follows that according to the Sages a mole is not even an intermediate sign, for if signs are biblical, an intermediate sign would help, yet a mole does not help. Thus it is not considered a distinctive sign at all. According to the later answer, however, signs are rabbinic, and the dispute is whether a mole is an especially distinctive sign; if so, one may say that it is an intermediate sign. The Rambam clearly implies that a mole is an intermediate sign, and therefore he writes "even a mole." So too Responsa Mas'at Binyamin, siman 63, and the Bach in Kuntres Acharon.

The Tur writes that a mole is not an especially distinctive sign because it is liable to change. This implies that it is not even an intermediate sign, for according to the first answer the dispute is whether a mole is an intermediate sign, and the Sages hold that it is not, because it may change. Still, one may say that the Tur holds it is an intermediate sign, following the later answer: they dispute whether a mole is an especially distinctive sign, and the Sages hold it is not especially distinctive because at times it changes, but it remains an intermediate sign. This is also implied by the Tur, for he is speaking about especially distinctive signs and writes that a mole is not such a sign; hence it is not especially distinctive, but it is an intermediate sign.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>However, if a report had circulated, etc.</b> The Bayit Chadash and Chelkat Mechokek explain that this refers to the first opinion, which permits her. One should not say that this case is worse than one in which the report came out before three days; the ruling teaches that the report itself does not worsen the matter. Either way: if the report is true, he is dead; and if the report is false, it is as though no report had emerged. Still, the statement that she is permitted according to all opinions is not well settled. The Taz writes that the author decided this on his own reasoning: since a report also circulated, she is therefore permitted according to all.

One should not object from Maharam Mintz, who writes that if one does not know when his relative died, we presume that he died recently, because we place him in the status of being alive, as stated in Yoreh De'ah 397. Why then do we not say here that he was killed within three days? The Taz raises this difficulty there. It may be answered that here Reuven went away from this place and we do not know where he is. We therefore leave Reuven in his presumption of life, and say that this dead man, although he appeared to them to be Reuven, had died more than three days earlier and changed, and is some other unknown person. The presumption of life does not attach to that unknown body, while Reuven remains alive.

If the facial form is whole and uninjured, there is room to permit, and in such a case one combines Rabbenu Tam's view when there is doubt whether the identification was within three days. It is possible that even the stringent authorities agree in this case; see Maharshdam, responsum 56. Likewise, if the body is whole and there is doubt whether it remained after being brought up from the water, one may permit on the basis of a double doubt: perhaps it was within the first hour, and even if it was after the first hour, perhaps the law follows Rabbenu Tam, the Rosh, and the Tur, who hold that if the witness did not see the drowning he may testify even after several days. See Knesset Yechezkel, Rashakh, part 1, responsum 182, and Masat Binyamin, responsum 45.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And it is whole.</b> The Rosh did not write this; rather, the Tur wrote it, combining Rabbenu Chananel's answer with the Rosh's answer and ruling stringently in accordance with both. According to Rabbenu Chananel's answer, the sugya concerns a body that is not whole, and therefore we require that it was seen immediately. According to the Rosh's answer, the witness saw the drowning, and that is why the Tur rules stringently: when he saw the drowning, visual recognition is ineffective even if the body is whole; and if he did not see the drowning but the body is not whole, visual recognition is also ineffective. If he did not see the drowning and the body is whole, however, he need not see it immediately.

Accordingly, if the body is not whole, one needs non-exceptional identifying marks, visual recognition, and immediate viewing, for according to Rabbenu Chananel that is the case discussed in the sugya's phrase "and they saw them immediately." Rema did not write that one who did not see the drowning may testify even if he did not see the body immediately. This implies that he is stringent in this matter like the Baal HaMaor, who establishes the sugya of the man who drowned in the Tigris as a case in which the witness did not see the drowning, and nevertheless he must see the body immediately.

See Mahari ben Lev and Radakh, responsum 21; they dispute the interpretation of the Rosh. According to Mahari ben Lev, the Rosh holds that even one who did not see the drowning must see the body immediately. He writes that perhaps the reason is that the dead body changes and can no longer be recognized; therefore even where the witness did not see the drowning and there is no concern that he speaks by assumption, he must still see it immediately, because later he may be unable to recognize it. On this explanation, even two witnesses must see it immediately. Or perhaps even one who did not see the drowning speaks somewhat by assumption, and therefore he must see it immediately, while with two witnesses there is no such concern. See R. Eliyahu Mizrachi, responsum 39, the responsa She'erit Yosef, Masat Binyamin 45 and 47, and the Chelkat Mechokek, who also wrote a responsum on this matter.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or they shot arrows at him.</b> So writes the Rambam. According to the Maggid Mishneh's explanation, the Rambam did not mention the law of one cut to pieces. The Bayit Chadash likewise writes that the Rambam's statement, "if they stabbed him or shot arrows at him," is learned from the sugya at the beginning of HaIshah Shalom, where it says that sometimes they strike a man with an arrow or a spear and she assumes he certainly died, while there are people for whom a healing salve is made and they live. But if it appears that they cut both signs, witnesses may testify; and likewise if one saw that they wounded his brain, witnesses may testify about him. If, however, arrows were shot at him and we did not see what happened to him, we say that perhaps nothing happened to him, because a salve may have been prepared for him and the weapon did not prevail over him.

The Beit Yosef, however, explains that when the Rambam writes "they stabbed him," he means the Mishnah's case of one cut to pieces, about whom one may not testify, because the Rambam rules stringently like Abaye's explanation there, which says that we are concerned that the knife may have been heated. Still, it appears that even according to the Beit Yosef, if the wound was in a place from which the soul departs, witnesses may testify. After all, where both signs were cut, the Rambam and Tur rule that one may testify. It must be that if the wound is in a fatal place, witnesses may testify; but if it is in a place from which the soul does not depart, even if he became a tereifah, witnesses may not testify even after twelve months, because we fear that the knife was heated and then he may live even longer than twelve months. This is unlike the Bayit Chadash, who wrote that after twelve months one may testify; that is incorrect, and the Chelkat Mechokek also objects to him. But where the soul departs, a heated knife does not help.

Nimukei Yosef distinguishes differently: a heated knife helps where he was not rendered a tereifah but was merely cut to pieces; where he was rendered a tereifah, witnesses may testify, and a heated knife does not help. Shmuel's statement, "they taught this only where his soul does not depart," applies also to one cut to pieces. It follows that a heated knife does not help where he was rendered a tereifah. Similarly, in the Rambam's words one may distinguish: where he was rendered a tereifah, it helps, but where his soul departs, it does not.

All this concerns one who is not moribund. If he is moribund, Tosafot write that if he is both moribund and cut to pieces, witnesses may testify about him; but if he is moribund and not cut to pieces, witnesses may not testify. If he has been moribund for three days, witnesses may testify about him, as stated in Yoreh De'ah; so too writes the book Beit Hillel.

The Ran raises a difficulty from the sugya in the second chapter of Yevamot, where it says that if one testifies about a man that he died or was killed, she may not marry. <b>Some say that she need not leave.</b> This is the view of the Ran, meaning where she married before the prohibiting witness came; if she married afterward, it is explained above that she must leave. The reason the Shulchan Arukh writes "some say" is that from the responsa of the Ramban and Rivash one may infer that only permission granted by a court is effective, not her own marriage. See the responsum of Maharshaldam, who brings proof that if she married without court permission it is ineffective.

From the Tosafot cited above, who hold that even if the court permitted her it helps only with respect to the rule that if she married she need not leave, one cannot infer that they hold that if she married without permission she must leave. They may mean as follows: if the court permitted her, then even if a witness later comes and says he did not die and she afterward married, she need not leave. But if they did not permit her and the second witness came before she married, she must leave. If, however, she married before the second witness came, she need not leave even without permission. See Mordekhai at the end of Ha-Ishah Shalom and see siman 7.

At first glance, according to the Ran's approach that her marriage without court permission is effective and she need not leave, the Gemara's question in Ha-Ishah Shalom is difficult. On the Mishnah, "one witness says he died and she married," it infers: the reason is that she married, but if she did not marry she may not marry; yet Ulla says that wherever the Torah believed one witness, he is like two. The Gemara answers that "married" means that she was permitted to marry. Thus it is proved that Ulla too speaks where she was permitted; why not answer that the Mishnah means actual marriage without permission? One should not bring proof from the Rambam that he holds she need not leave, for he writes that if the second witness came before they permitted her, she must leave unless she married the witness who testified that he died. This shows only that if she married that witness, even without permission she need not leave, because he says with certainty that the husband died. If she married someone else, who does not know whether he died, perhaps only court permission helps; without court permission she must leave.

<b>And the bet din must be three and valid, etc.</b> Chelkat Mechokek discusses this at length. The general point of his words is that no bet din is needed here; it is like other rulings of prohibition and permission, and one judge is enough. Also, no bet din is needed to receive the testimony, for testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid even if the first witness did not state his testimony before bet din. The reason a bet din is generally needed to receive testimony is so that it not be testimony from the mouth of another witness; here, where testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid, a bet din is not needed to receive the testimony. We have not found explicitly the source of this law written by the Rama, that they must be three and valid. What is cited as responsum Rashba siman 790 is not there, and it can properly be rejected as Chelkat Mechokek wrote. But one can bring proof for this law from the Gemara at the end of Yevamot, where there is a dispute about testimony for women, whether it requires derishah and chakirah. Rabbi Akiva holds that it requires derishah and chakirah, and they dispute whether permitting women resembles monetary law or capital law, for there are arguments both ways: permitting her to another man resembles capital law, while her collection of the ketubah because of the permission resembles monetary law. According to all opinions, we hear that this is actual adjudication and is not like other rulings of prohibition and permission, where there is no legal judgment; here there is judgment. Although sometimes she has no ketubah, nevertheless they did not distinguish, as Nimukei Yosef wrote. Although testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid for testimony concerning a woman, we do not derive the possible from the impossible. Know this, for Rabbi Akiva holds that derishah and chakirah are required, and he also holds that testimony from the mouth of another witness is valid; but the first witness spoke without derishah and chakirah. Rather, one must say that the first witness is not comparable to the witness who comes before us: this one requires derishah and chakirah, and likewise must give his testimony specifically before bet din. So too it implies from Nimukei Yosef, who holds that a valid bet din is required, for he gives the reason that testimony concerning a woman is valid before lay courts because it is common and severe because of monetary loss, and therefore agency applies to this testimony. If you say no bet din is needed at all, it is obvious that a lay court is valid. Rather, certainly a valid bet din is required. What Chelkat Mechokek brought as proof from the rulings of Mahari, from the permission issued by Maharar Tevel, is no proof at all, for there a bet din was with Maharar Tevel, only the permission was called by his name. Also, if she already married, that is different.
