<b>אם שנים מעידים ששמעו מפי א'</b>. היינו כ"א שמע מפי א' כן הוא בתשו' הר"ן וכ"כ בתשובת מהר"מ מלובלין סי' קל"ג וכ"כ בח"מ אבל אם רבים שמעו מא' שמת וא' שמע מא' שחי הוי כחד וחד ומבואר שם דחיישינן שמא שמעו הרבים מאחד כשלא אמרו דשמעו מרבים ומשמע שם אם אחד שמע מרבים אפילו הרבים אינן כאן מ"מ עדיף מאחד שאמר דשמע מאחד והיינו אותו אחד ששמע מרבים אמר דרבי' בעצמם ראו ולא ששמעו ולא כח"מ שכתב ע"א מפי הרבים וע"א מפי יחיד שקולים הן כל שאין הרבים לפנינו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If two testify that they heard from one.</b> Meaning, each one heard from one person. So it is in responsum Ran, and so wrote responsum Maharam Lublin siman 133 and Chelkat Mechokek. But if many heard from one that he died, and one heard from one that he is alive, it is like one against one. It is explained there that we are concerned that the many heard from one person when they did not say that they heard from many. It is implied there that if one heard from many, even if the many are not here, nevertheless this is better than one who said he heard from one, provided that the one who heard from many says that the many themselves saw it, not that they heard it. This is unlike Chelkat Mechokek, who wrote that one witness from the mouth of many and one witness from the mouth of an individual are equal whenever the many are not before us.

<b>ואם נשאת תצא</b>. כלו' אף על גב דנשא' בהיתר ע"פ  שנים הראשונים תצא מהיתרה ע"פ עדים האחרונים דלא אמרינן לא תצא אלא בפסולים אבל כשאחרונים כשרים מוציאים אותה דתרי ותרי ספיק' הוא והיא בחזקת א"א עומדת וכ"כ בח"מ ועיין בתוספת בסוגי' בפ"ב דכתובות בתשובת ריב"ש סי' שע"ט כתב אפילו אם התירוה אותה ע"פ שני עדים ואחר כך באו שני עדים ואמרו לא מת אסורה להנש' ול"ד להיתר ע"פ עד אחד ובא אחר כך עד אחד ואומר שהוא חי דשם ע"י ההיתר נעשה העד הראשון כשנים והוי כאלו אין ספק בדבר וכן כל מקום שנ' לא תצא אם התירו' לא תנש' מיהו בשבויה מהני ההיתר אף על גב שבאו עידי השבויה אחר ההיתר מותרת כי עדותם אין סותרת ההיתר דיכול להיות אף על גב דהיתה בשביה לא זנתה לכן מהני ההיתר שם אם לא באו עידי טומאה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if she married, she must leave.</b> Meaning, even though she married permissibly on the basis of the first two witnesses, she must leave that permission because of the later witnesses. We say that she need not leave only with invalid witnesses, but when the later witnesses are valid they remove her, for it is a case of two witnesses against two witnesses, which is a doubt, and she remains in her presumption as a married woman. So too wrote Chelkat Mechokek. See Tosafot in the sugya in chapter 2 of Ketubot. In responsum Rivash siman 379 he wrote that even if they permitted her on the basis of two witnesses and afterward two witnesses came and said he did not die, she is forbidden to marry. This is unlike permission based on one witness followed by another witness who says he is alive; there, through the permission, the first witness becomes like two, and it is as though there is no doubt in the matter. Likewise, wherever it says she need not leave, if they merely permitted her, she should not marry. However, with a captive woman the permission helps: even though witnesses to captivity came after the permission, she remains permitted, because their testimony does not contradict the permission, for it is possible that although she was captive she did not commit zenut. Therefore the permission helps there, unless witnesses to defilement came.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And they give her the basic ketubah.</b> So the Rambam writes in chapter 16 of Hilkhot Ishut regarding a woman who says to her husband, "You divorced me." I already wrote at the beginning of siman 7 that the reason she collects the principal is the derashah of the ketubah, for it is written in the ketubah: "When you marry another, take what is written for you." This derashah is not applied to the additional ketubah payment. So writes the Maggid Mishneh there, and so writes the Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ritva in Ha-Ishah Shalom, on the Mishnah that Beit Shammai say she may marry and collect her ketubah. All the more so, she does not collect nichsei tzon barzel. Likewise, if they permit her on the basis of a gentile speaking in the course of conversation or on the basis of one witness, she does not collect the additional ketubah payment.

See Responsa Maharshaldam, siman 253. In Responsa R. Yosef of the house of Levi, siman 17, he writes that this law depends on the reasons given there for why Beit Hillel retracted to rule like Beit Shammai that she may marry and collect the ketubah. One reason is the ketubah derashah, and a second reason is an a fortiori argument: if we permit the severe matter of a forbidden married woman, should we not permit the monetary matter? According to the ketubah-derashah reason she does not collect the additional payment, but according to the a fortiori reason she collects even the additional payment. This is also implied in the Tur in this siman and in siman 100.

Nevertheless, money is not extracted unless, when they permit her and his name has disappeared, the heirs may descend to the estate as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 284; then she too collects the additional ketubah and nichsei tzon barzel. As long as she is not permitted she does not collect the basic ketubah or the addition; so hold the Rambam and Ra'avad. Although the ketubah derashah does not apply to the additional payment but only to the principal, so the addition should be like the rest of the estate and the heirs descend to the inheritance when he drowned in water that has no end, nevertheless she does not collect it. This is unlike the Hagahot Maimoniyot cited in se'if 35.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And her intention is not to marry.</b> So wrote the Rambam, chapter 17 halachah 1. At first glance, according to his approach, the sugya of the Gemara is difficult to reconcile. It appears that he holds that we are not concerned that she will marry in prohibition, as the Gemara says, because of the stringency imposed at the end; rather, the concern is that her intention is only to extract the money. All the give-and-take in the Gemara concerns the ketubah and not her permission to marry. Therefore he holds that if she said, give me my ketubah and permit me, the question was not resolved regarding whether we give her the ketubah; but if she wishes to marry, this was not in question, and it is obvious that she is permitted. Therefore he ruled that she is permitted to marry but we do not give her the ketubah. If she married and afterward claims the ketubah, one may say that we give her the ketubah; what he wrote, that we do not give her the ketubah, speaks only as long as she has not married. Here, however, we do not permit her because she herself did not say, permit me. But what Beit Yosef and Bach wrote, that the Rambam holds she is permitted because it is a rabbinic doubt, since the question ended in teiku and is only rabbinic, is surprising. If so, why did he rule in the teiku cases of one witness in war and one witness in a quarrel that she may not marry? There it is even more reasonable to be lenient, for there is one witness testifying that he died.

<b>Or that he apostatized.</b> So wrote Shiltei Gibborim in the name of some who say, but he disagrees with them, writing as follows: it appears to me that they said she is not believed only in a quarrel, etc.; but if he apostatized and rebelled against her, or her husband was a denier of the fundamental principle, she is believed and we do not presume that she lies. The Rama ruled like the some-say view. One may say that because this is stringent he was concerned for that opinion. One may also say that the Rama holds that these two views in Shiltei Gibborim depend on the dispute between the Rambam and the other poskim whether, when he initiates the quarrel, we say she lies, as written nearby. <b>And even if one witness came.</b> The Talmud asks: what is the law of one witness in wartime, and likewise of one witness in a quarrel? Rashi explains that the question is whether we fear that one witness speaks by assumption. Ordinarily he is believed because she investigates carefully before remarrying; here she does not investigate carefully and may also speak by assumption, so he is not believed. On this reading, the sugya of the two Torah scholars who drowned is well explained: they were permitted on the basis of one witness in water, which is like wartime, and the Talmud establishes that the permission was by identifying marks, where speaking by assumption is not relevant.

Tosafot, however, explain that with one witness there is no concern that he speaks by assumption. The question is instead whether one witness is not believed even when he says "I buried him," because he is believed only where she investigates carefully, and in wartime and similar cases she does not. According to them it is difficult to explain the sugya of the two scholars: how were they permitted on one witness who gave marks? There remains a concern that he is lying and that the man is not dead. The Maggid Mishneh raises a similar difficulty. If there were an exceptionally distinctive mark, one could say it was in a hidden place, impossible to know except at death when the witness saw him; so write Nimukei Yosef and the Bayit Chadash. But Tosafot write that it was a non-exceptional mark and that the witness had visual recognition. If so, he must have seen the mark while the man was alive and through it recognized him visually. Since he himself says that he saw him alive, why is he believed? Perhaps he saw the mark and the man is actually not dead. This requires study.

The Rif writes that although this question was not resolved here, it was resolved from the sugya at the end of Yevamot concerning the man who drowned in the Tigris. Nimukei Yosef explains that, according to the Rif, the question concerns one who said "I buried him," as Tosafot explain, and the Talmud initially tries to prove from the story of the two scholars, but rejects the proof by saying they were permitted through marks. These must be exceptionally distinctive marks, in a hidden place, as stated above. The phrase "and they saw them immediately" must then be stated as an added point: even though they saw them immediately, marks were still required.

Accordingly, the Rif's statement that the question is resolved from the end of Yevamot is well understood. There it cannot be rejected by saying that the witness gave exceptionally distinctive marks, for if so why would the Talmud say that this applies only when he saw him at once? If he gave such marks, immediate viewing would not be needed. In our sugya one could say the phrase is an added point, but there, since the text explains that he saw him at once, it must be that he did not give exceptionally distinctive marks. Thus the question is resolved: one witness does not lie even where there is concern that she will not investigate carefully.

Nevertheless, all this concerns a witness who says "I buried him," for the whole sugya deals with that case. If he did not say "I buried him," then although it has been resolved that he does not lie, there is still concern that he speaks by assumption. In this the Rif does not agree with Tosafot, who hold that we do not fear such assumption. Rather, according to the Rif the ruling applies specifically where he said "I buried him"; without that, we fear that he speaks by assumption.

The Rambam may also hold this way, for he rules like the Rif. Therefore he writes that if one witness says "I buried him," he is believed, because the question was resolved; but if he did not say "I buried him," she should not marry ab initio, because we fear that he speaks by assumption. If she did marry, she need not leave, because it is only a concern, not because the question was unresolved. The question itself concerns one who said "I buried him." This also answers the Maggid Mishneh's objection to the Rambam, who explains the trustworthiness of one witness by saying that the matter is likely to become known, rather than by saying that she investigates carefully. Since the question was resolved that, when he says "I buried him," the witness is believed even in wartime where she does not investigate carefully, it follows that one witness is believed even where she does not investigate; it must be because the matter is likely to become known.

On this basis, the Rambam's ruling about one witness in a quarrel is also understandable. We fear that she may have hired him, although this reason is not stated in the Talmud's question. It is nevertheless implied: according to the Rif, the wartime question was resolved, while the question of one witness in a quarrel was not. The difference must be that in a quarrel there is concern that she hired him. The Maggid Mishneh, however, explains the Rambam as holding that the question concerns a witness who did not say "I buried him," and whether we fear that he speaks by assumption; it was not resolved, and therefore the Rambam rules that she need not leave if she married. He writes that the Rif also holds this way. This is astonishing to me, for the Rif writes that the question was resolved and also writes that the witness is not believed if he did not say "I buried him."

If one witness in a quarrel says "I buried him," the law also depends on these explanations. According to the Rambam's approach, where the concern is that he lies because she hired him, he is not believed even if he says "I buried him." But according to the other authorities, he is believed when he says "I buried him." See Kesef Mishneh, who cites a responsum that even according to the Rambam we do not suspect that she hired him unless she brought him. Although this reasoning is not fully compelled, one may be lenient, since according to the other authorities we do not suspect that she hired him.

<b>And she came and said: "My husband died in war."</b> Thus writes the Rambam. In Rashi it is explicit that even if she says simply, "My husband died," when war has been established it is as though she said that he died in the war, unless she states explicitly that he died on his bed. The Bach wishes to infer that the Rambam does not agree with Rashi. It is possible that Rashi learned this law from the difficulty he raises against the questioner who asks whether, when she herself established that there was war, she is believed by a migo that she could have refrained from mentioning the war. Rashi asks that this should be resolved from the Mishnah: when there is peace in the world she is believed; necessarily, then, it speaks where she says he died in war, for if she says he died on his bed she is believed even when there is war in the world. Rather, it must be that she said he died in war, and this is called "she established war." Rashi answers that the Mishnah speaks where she says merely that he died; if there is peace in the world she is believed, but if there is war in the world she is not believed, for it is as though she said he died in war.

According to the Rambam one may say that the Mishnah speaks where she says he died in war and "I buried him"; then she is believed by a migo, since she could have said there was peace in the world. For when she says "I buried him" the concern is not that she is speaking by conjecture, but only that she may be lying, as the Ran and Beit Yosef write. Therefore, where she herself established the war she is believed by a migo. The question arises only where she did not say "I buried him"; then one may say that she speaks by conjecture, and perhaps even a migo does not help. But when she says "I buried him" and she herself established the war, she is believed. Where war is otherwise established she is not believed, for she has no migo. If she merely says he died, however, one may say it is as though she said he died on his bed.

There remains a slight difficulty: how can the sugya seek to resolve the matter from the baraita, "robbers fell upon us," etc.; perhaps there too she says "I buried him." This can be answered, though it is not compelled by the Rambam's language that he disagrees with Rashi. Although his phrase "my husband died in war" suggests disagreement with Rashi, his continuation suggests the opposite, for he writes that if she says he died on his bed she is believed, implying that if she says simply that he died she is not believed.

<b>Even if she says, "I buried him."</b> The reason is explained in the Ran's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef and in the responsum of R. Eliyahu Mizrachi cited by the Kesef Mishneh. Although when she says "I buried him" the concern that she is speaking by conjecture does not apply, we nevertheless suspect that she is lying, because it is not normal to wait during war and bury him; therefore we are more concerned that she is lying. Thus when she says he died on his bed, meaning in his house and not in war, she is believed, for then she is neither lying nor speaking by conjecture.

This differs from famine. In famine, when she says "I buried him," she is believed; but if she says he died on his bed, we suspect that she speaks by conjecture, thinking that since he had no food he certainly died on his bed. There is also another reason: since it became fixed in her mind that he died, she may lie and say "I buried him." A practical difference between these reasons would be a collapse, or where a snake or scorpion bit him and he died. According to the first reason she is believed; according to the second she is not. Likewise, if she says he drowned in water that has no end, was cast onto dry land, and she buried him, she is not believed according to the second reason; so writes the Bach.

What the Bach writes, however, that even if she married she must leave, seems not to accord with the Rambam; according to him she would not leave, for she has a migo that she could have said he died on his bed, as in the case where she herself established war. Still, if it was known that he drowned and she says that the water cast him up and she buried him, then according to the other authorities she is believed, while according to the Rambam, for the reason stated above, she is not believed. According to this, however, it is not resolved why "I buried him" helps when she says he died in famine. See Responsa R. Eliyahu Mizrachi. It follows from there that if during a famine she says he did not die from famine, she is believed, whereas according to the Maggid Mishneh she is not.

<b>וי"א דאינה נאמנת</b>. כן היא גירס' הר"ן ברמב"ם כמ"ש בתשו' שהביא הב"י נשמע מזה דיש חילוק בין תיקו לבין תרי לשונות כי כאן איכא תרי לשונות בש"ס אם דבר דומה למלחמה ולהרמב"ם אינו נאמנת משמע דתצא ובתיקו ס"ל לא תצא ומזה יש להביא ראיה גם בפלוגתת הפוסקים יש להחמיר מיהו י"ל כאן בדבר ע"פ דין תורה ליכא היתר כשהיא אומרת מת בעלי אלא חז"ל הקילו בעגונה לכן בתרי לישנא או בפלוגתת הפוסקים יש להחמיר אבל כשנטבע במים שאל"ס אז מדאורייתא מותרת וחכמים החמירו י"ל בפלוגתא יש להקל, מי שנשרף וצעקה אשתו ואומרת ראו בעלי שנשרף נאמנת ודוקא דליכא שום דבר שמור' שהיה שם עוד אחר אבל אם נשאר יד ואנו מכירים שאין זה מאיש שנשרף אף על גב דאמר' בעלי נשרף אינה נאמנת עכ"ל רי"ו נתיב כ"ג ח"ג בשם הרמ"ה ונלמד מש"ס יבמות ריש דף קט"ו ועיין סעיף ל"ב מבואר אף על גב דזה נפל למים והעלוהו רגל אם לית ברגל ס"מ אמרינן זה האיש שנפל למים עלה והלך לדרכו ורגל זה הוא מאיש אחר וכאן צ"ל הא דלא חיישינן שמא אדם אחר היה כאן ונשרף משום דהיא צעקה בעלי נשרף לכן לא חיישינן אלא היכא דמוכח דהיה שם איש אחר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some say that she is not believed.</b> This is the Ran's version in the Rambam, as written in the responsum brought by Beit Yosef. It follows from this that there is a distinction between a teiku and two versions, for here there are two versions in the Gemara whether plague is comparable to war, and according to the Rambam she is not believed, which implies that she must leave; whereas with a teiku he holds that she need not leave. From this one can also bring proof that in a dispute among poskim one should be stringent. However, one may say that here, regarding plague, by Torah law there is no permission when she says my husband died; the Sages were merely lenient for an agunah. Therefore, with two versions or a dispute among poskim, one should be stringent. But when he drowned in water that has no end, she is permitted by Torah law and the Sages were stringent, so one may say that in a dispute one is lenient. If a person was burned and his wife cried out and said, see, my husband has been burned, she is believed, specifically where there is nothing indicating that another person was there. But if a hand remains and we recognize that it is not from the man who was burned, even though she said my husband was burned, she is not believed. End quote of Ri Vav, Netiv 23 part 3, in the name of the Ramah. This is learned from the Gemara in Yevamot at the beginning of folio 115. See seif 32, where it is explained that even though this person fell into the water and they brought up a leg, if there is no distinctive sign on the leg, we say that this man who fell into the water came up and went on his way, and this leg belongs to another man. Here one must say that the reason we are not concerned that perhaps another person was here and was burned is because she cried, my husband was burned; therefore we are not concerned except where it is proven that another man was there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Rather, she was only betrothed.</b> The rule is that where she married and thereby committed a prohibition, they penalized her so that she is forbidden to the first husband. According to the Rif this is only a penalty, while according to the Rosh it is not only a penalty; rather, because of the penalty we are concerned that people will say that the first husband divorced her and she married the second. But with kiddushin, where she committed no prohibition, they did not penalize her and we are not concerned that people will say this; rather, they will say it was mistaken kiddushin. A practical difference from this dispute is the law written in siman 159, as written in siman 15. If she secluded herself after the kiddushin, it is like marriage, all the more so if she was betrothed as a concubine, where only delivery to chuppah is lacking. See the responsum of Rashba brought by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 67. If the betrother gave her a get, she is forbidden to the first husband, for now people will say that the get was valid and the first husband is taking back his divorcee after betrothal; responsum Rashakh, part 1, siman 132. Responsum Maharshdam siman 62 disagrees with this.
