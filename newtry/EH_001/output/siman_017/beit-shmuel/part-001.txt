<b>חזקה אין אשה מעיזה</b>. הרמב"ם פ"י ה"ג כתב כשהבעל אומר לא גרשתיך והיא אומרת גרשתני נאמנת ואם הבעל אומר גרשתיך והיא מודה לו אינה נאמנת ולא אמרינן בכה"ג אין האשה מעיזה כיון דהוא מסייע, ואם היא התחילה לומר גרשתני ואח"כ מסייע לה ואומר גרשתיך אין מפורש הדין ובח"מ מסופק בזה מיהו לפי הטעם שכתב הרמב"ם ובטור סימן קנ"ב הביא לשונו וחד טעמא כתב אם אומר גרשתיך י"ל שמא בגט פסול גירש לפ"ז אפי' אם התחילה ואומרת גרשתני והוא אומר גרשתיך עדיין יש לחוש שמא גט פסול היה ואינה מגורשת אלא באומר לא גרשתיך לפ"ז גם אם קבלה הקידושין מאחר אינה מקודשת אא"כ באומר לא גרשתיך דאל"כ אכתי יש לחוש שמא גט פסול היה ועיין סימן קנ"ב, כתב ברי"ו אם היא קבלה קידושין מאחר בפניו מותרת לבעלה אחר שגירש המקדש, ולכאורה תמוה הא יש לחוש שמא יאמרו שמחזיר מגורשת אחר הארוסין וכן הקשה בד"מ ותירץ אם היא קבלה ולא אמרה דגירש אותה בעלה מותרת לבעלה הראשון אחר הגירושין של זה ולא מצינו חילוק זה גם בש"ס סוף גיטין לא משמע כן גם מ"ש ב"ש ב"ח ליתא וכבר השיג עליו ח"מ:
**** ENGLISH ****
Hazakah: a woman does not act brazenly. Rambam ch. 10 halakhah 3 wrote: when the husband says "I did not divorce you" and she says "you divorced me" — she is believed; and if the husband says "I divorced you" and she admits to him — she is not believed and we do not say in such a case "a woman does not act brazenly" since he supports her. And if she began to say "you divorced me" and afterward he supports her and says "I divorced you" — the law is not explicit; and Choshen Mishpat is uncertain. However per the reason Rambam wrote — and Tur siman 152 brought his wording — he wrote one reason: if he says "I divorced you" one may say perhaps he divorced with invalid get; therefore even if she began and says "you divorced me" and he says "I divorced you" — there is still concern perhaps it was invalid get and she is not divorced; only when he says "I did not divorce you" — therefore even if she accepted kiddushin from another she is not betrothed unless he says "I did not divorce you" — for otherwise still concern perhaps invalid get; see siman 152. Binyamin Ze'ev wrote: if she accepted kiddushin from another in his presence — she is permitted to her husband after the betrother divorces her; and apparently wonder — behold concern perhaps they say he returns a divorcee after betrothal; and so Darkei Moshe challenged and answered: if she accepted and did not say her husband divorced her — permitted to first husband after this one's divorce; and we do not find this distinction; also end of Gittin does not imply thus; also what Beit Shmuel and Bach wrote — not so; and Choshen Mishpat already challenged him.

**** ENGLISH ****
In our time. See Bach wrote bedieved if another betrothed her while betrothed to him we do not remove her; and Choshen Mishpat challenged him; and Be'er Heitev wrote in our time the custom is plain — the rabbi arranging the get stores the get after giving it — then she is not believed to say "you divorced me" for it is possible to clarify; and it appears what Ran and Maggid Mishneh wrote that forbidden to marry — does not speak of betrothal in husband's presence but if she says in husband's presence "you divorced me" — they wrote forbidden to marry; but if betrothed in husband's presence — permitted to betrother. <b>חוץ מה' נשים</b>. ואם מת בעל זה ונשאת לאחר מעידים נשים אלו ואף על פי שהיו שונאים קודם לכן כ"כ בכ"מ פי"ב וברמב"ם פ"י מבואר צרת'  אינה מביאה לזו הגט אף על גב דצרתה נשואה עכשיו לאחר וכ' הרב המגיד דלמד דינים דשם  מדינים דכאן לפ"ז משמע לכאורה דאינה נאמנ' אפילו היא עכשיו נשואה לאחר ולקמן סי' קמ"א פסק המחבר דאינה נאמנת אפילו אם היא נשואה לאחר, ואפשר לאותו בעל אינה מעידה דרצונה שצרת' תהיה לו ג"כ אסורה, וכתב בתשו' שער אפרים סי' ק"ה צרתה אינה מעיד' אפילו אם היא מסל"ת וכן מוכח מתשוב' ריב"ש דהא מביא ראיה דכותי אם אומר פלוני מת הוי מסל"ת אפילו בלא קישור דברים מדאית' בש"ס סוף יבמות נשמע קול האומר פלוני מת משיאים אותה והקשו בש"ס דלמא שד היה דלמ' צרה היה ולא הקשה דלמא כותי היה ש"מ אפי' אם היה כותי מותרת משום דאצ"ל קישור דברים ושפיר הוי מסל"ת ואי אמרת צרה מסל"ת נאמנת וא"כ מה מקשה דלמא צרה היה אלא ש"מ צרה אינה נאמנת אפי' אם הוא מסל"ת ועיין עוד מזה לקמן:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Except for the five women.</b> If this husband died and she married another, these women may testify, even though they hated her before; so wrote Kesef Mishneh, chapter 12. But in the Rambam, chapter 10, it is explained that her co-wife may not bring this one's get, even though her co-wife is now married to another man. The Maggid Mishneh wrote that he learned the laws there from the laws here. According to this, it apparently implies that she is not believed even if she is now married to another man. Below, siman 141, the Mechaber ruled that she is not believed even if she is married to another man. Perhaps she may not testify regarding that husband because she wants her co-wife to be forbidden to him as well. Responsum Shaar Ephraim siman 105 wrote that a co-wife may not testify even if she is speaking innocently. This is also proven from responsum Rivash, for he brings proof that if a gentile says, so-and-so died, it is considered innocent speech even without connected contextual words, from what appears in the Gemara at the end of Yevamot: if a voice was heard saying, so-and-so died, they allow her to marry. The Gemara asks: perhaps it was a demon; perhaps it was a co-wife. It did not ask: perhaps it was a gentile. This shows that even if it was a gentile she is permitted, because contextual words are not required and it is properly innocent speech. If you say that a co-wife speaking innocently is believed, then what is the Gemara asking, perhaps it was a co-wife? Rather, this proves that a co-wife is not believed even if she is speaking innocently. See more on this below.

<b>And these are: her mother-in-law.</b> The reason is explained in the Gemara: she says, this woman will consume all my labor and toil; the daughter of her mother-in-law says, this woman will inherit my father's and mother's labor; her yevamah says, this woman will become my co-wife; and her husband's daughter says, this woman comes in place of my mother. One might ask: if her yevamah is afraid that she may become her co-wife, what does her testimony accomplish, since we hold that when a woman underwent yibbum by mistake she is permitted to her husband, as written in siman 156? Tosafot in Yevamot 117 answers that nevertheless she has embarrassment, and also perhaps her husband will perform chalitzah and she will marry another, becoming forbidden to both. Shiltei Gibborim wrote that males corresponding to these women mentioned, such as her father-in-law, the son of her mother-in-law, and her husband's son, are believed. However, the yavam is not believed to testify in order to permit her to himself; but if his brother had children, he is believed to permit her to others. As for the unresolved question of a mother-in-law afterward, where her thought is that her husband has already died, Rashi already answered that the pain of a yevamah is bodily pain, while the pain of a mother-in-law is only because of money. One may also say that when the Sages said she shall not testify, we say they did not distinguish, as written nearby in the name of the Maggid Mishneh. But regarding men, the Sages said nothing; therefore we say that where there is suspicion he should not testify, but where there is no concern he may testify. Chelkat Mechokek is uncertain nowadays, when we do not perform yibbum, whether the yavam is believed to testify that his brother died in order to perform chalitzah for the yevamah. Although the yevamah cannot testify even nowadays, as implied by the Tur, who wrote in this siman that his yevamah does not testify, implying even nowadays, and the Maggid Mishneh in siman 287 also wrote that her yevamah who is her sister may not testify, which shows they did not distinguish between one yevamah and another, nevertheless one should not compare the yavam to the yevamah, as I wrote. However, there in Chelkat Mechokek it implies that even if he had children he would not rule permissively. At first glance it appears that even one who fears ruling should have no concern in this, as Shiltei Gibborim wrote explicitly. מן המים ונהרג שם בתשובת מהר"מ:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One witness says he died, etc.</b> The Tur cites a responsum of the Rosh that where two witnesses testify that he died, even though there is a contradiction between them, the woman is permitted. That responsum concerned a contradiction about whether a certain person was present at the time of death, and the Rosh ruled that since both testify that he died, this is complete testimony. From there alone one would not know the case where they contradict one another as to whether he died or was killed, for perhaps that is a contradiction. Yet the Rosh learned his rule from the Mishnah in Yevamot: one woman says he died and one says he was killed; Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon say that since both acknowledge that he is no longer alive, they may marry. It follows that even when the witnesses contradict one another in this manner, saying died or was killed, they may marry.

The Beit Yosef asks why the Tur wrote this law in the name of the Rosh, when it is explicit in that Mishnah. This is not difficult, for in the Mishnah one might say that only rival wives do not create a contradiction, since they cannot truly refute one another. But where witnesses contradict one another in this way perhaps it is a contradiction. From the Rosh we learn that even such witnesses are not considered contradictory, since both agree that he is not alive. The Derishah and Chelkat Mechokek write similarly. The Rosh's ruling even regarding witnesses appears to rest on Tosafot in the name of R. Yoel, who explains that the case is where we see that both women come to permit themselves and not to contradict one another. According to the first answer there, then, even contradictory testimony of this kind is effective; this follows R. Yochanan's answer there, not R. Eliezer's first answer. Examine the sugya and it will be clear.

I have seen in Tzemach Tzedek, siman 59, that he wrote differently. See also Choshen Mishpat siman 30, where it is explained that when witnesses contradict one another in bedikot their testimony stands, but when they contradict one another in chakirot their testimony does not stand, even in monetary matters. If one says he lent barrels of wine and the other says barrels of honey, the Rambam treats this as a chakirah and the testimony is void. The Chelkat Mechokek understood that the contradiction here, whether he died or was killed, is like wine or honey according to the Rambam, and therefore asked how the Mechaber could rule simply like the Rosh.

It is more reasonable that the Mechaber holds there is no dispute here between the Rambam and the Rosh. The contradiction here is like "a black maneh" and "a white maneh," which all agree is not a contradiction, because once he saw that the man was not alive he was not so exacting about the cause of death. Moreover, in this matter one witness who says he is no longer alive is sufficient; therefore even if two contradict one another in the investigative details, one of them is nevertheless saying the truth that he is no longer alive, and she is permitted. This differs from monetary law, which requires two witnesses. I saw this also in Penei Moshe I, siman 64. The Shiltei Gibborim, however, writes that if one says he died and one says he was killed, it is a contradiction, while Maharam Lublin, siman 130, ruled like the Mechaber. If one testifies that he drowned and the other says he was killed, the matter depends on this dispute: according to the Shiltei Gibborim she is forbidden, and according to the Mechaber she is permitted. In any event, if the drowning was in water that has an end, it is like one saying he died and one saying he was killed; but if it was in water that has no end, it is not a contradiction, for he may have emerged from the water and been killed, as appears in the responsum of Maharam.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>They found it written in a document.</b> Even if it was written in a gentile script, it is effective if it is known that a Jew wrote it, as Terumat HaDeshen, siman 247, writes in the name of Or Zarua. Even if he wrote to the court that he heard it from another, it is effective. But where he writes only as a memorandum and not for practical action, we do not rule leniently on the basis of testimony from the mouth of another witness, as stated in Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 2 of Yevamot. If, however, he writes as a record of what he himself saw, there is room to be lenient.

The Yerushalmi distinguishes whether one may rely on writing, and the Ran explains that the dispute concerns a writing made for memory. The Rif, Rashba, and Ran infer from the fact that the Rif in chapter Mi She'achazo cites the baraita, "Just as they examine for gittin, so they examine for testimony," and establishes it with regard to testimony that a woman's husband died but not other testimony, because of "from their mouths and not from their writing," that he holds one may rely on writing in this matter. At first glance it is difficult to derive this from there, because the Ran explains that the Yerushalmi's dispute concerns a memorandum, while the case in Mi She'achazo is not one who writes merely for memory. The Chelkat Mechokek also asks how one can derive the law from there, since there the writer is known, whereas here he is unknown.

The Tur writes in this siman that the Rosh did not decide the Yerushalmi's dispute whether writing may be relied on, although in Mi She'achazo the Rosh rules like the Rif. Therefore one must explain as above: the Yerushalmi speaks of a writing for memory, and for that reason the Rosh did not decide it; what he rules in Mi She'achazo is where it was not written merely as a memorandum. From the Rambam, however, it seems that he learns these laws from one another, for he writes both in one clause: they found written that so-and-so died, and likewise if a mute person was examined and wrote that so-and-so died. There the baraita teaches that just as they examine for gittin, they examine for testimony that a woman's husband died. Since regarding gittin we rule that one does not rely on writing, as in siman 120, it would seem that the same should apply to testimony about a woman. The Rambam, who rules that one may rely on writing here, follows his own view that writing also helps by a get when the man became mute. Regarding the Mechaber, it is at first difficult how we know to be lenient here for a mute person, when below he seems to hold principally like the authorities that writing does not help for a mute person. See siman 142.

<b>אם י"ל שכ"כ</b>. ל"ד שכתב ה"ה אם הגיד בע"פ אמרי' שמא שמע שטבע במים שאל"ס כמ"ש בת"ה סי' ר"מ ובפסקים סי' קמ"א ובתשו' ב"י אית' בשם רב א' ה"ה אם יצא קול שנהרג פלוני וא' אמר שנהרג אמרי' שמא שמע הקול שנהרג והוא לא ראה שנהרג והב"י חולק ע"ז דיש לומר דוק' כשטבע במים שאין להם סוף דעל פי רוב הוא מת אז חיישינן דזה סמך על הרוב לכן אמר שמת אבל אם יצא קול שנהרג פלוני ואחד אמר שמת לא אמרי' שסמך על הקול שיצא ועיין תשו' מהרשד"מ סי' מ"ז, ויש לומר שם בתשובה לא יצא הקול שנהרג אלא אמר לא חזר לביתו זמן רב וא' אומר שמת י"ל בכה"ג סמכינן על זה שאמר שמת דאם לא ידע שמת איך אמר שמת דהא לא יצא קול שמת אלא לא חזר לביתו ומנין הוא אומר שמת אבל אם יצא קול שנהרג או שמת וא' אומר שמת י"ל מחמת הקול שיצא אומר שמת והוא א"י שמת וכן הוא דעת המ"ב סי' ק"ה, ובט"ז מדייק מת"ה אם יצא קול על א' שמת ואח"כ כ' א' שמת אף על פי שאותו קול לא היה בירור מותרת:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If one can say that he wrote so.</b> It is not specifically that he wrote; the same applies if he stated it orally: we say perhaps he heard that he drowned in water that has no end, as written in Terumat HaDeshen siman 240 and in the rulings siman 141. In responsum Beit Yosef, in the name of one rabbi, it appears that the same applies if a rumor emerged that so-and-so was killed and someone said that he was killed: we say perhaps he heard the rumor that he was killed and did not see that he was killed. The Beit Yosef disagrees with this, for one can say that specifically when he drowned in water that has no end, where according to the majority he is dead, we are concerned that this person relied on the majority and therefore said he died; but if a rumor emerged that so-and-so was killed and one person said he died, we do not say that he relied on the rumor that emerged. See responsum Maharshdam siman 47. One may say that in that responsum, the rumor did not emerge that he was killed, only that he did not return home for a long time, and one person says that he died. In such a case, one may say that we rely on the one who said he died, for if he did not know that he died, how did he say he died? After all, no rumor had emerged that he died, only that he did not return home, so from where does he say that he died? But if a rumor emerged that he was killed or died, and one person says he died, one may say that he says he died because of the rumor that emerged, while he himself does not know that he died. So too is the view of Mishnat Binyamin siman 105. The Taz infers from Terumat HaDeshen that if a rumor emerged about someone that he died, and afterward one person wrote that he died, even though that rumor had no clarification, she is permitted.

<b>וכן אם אמר כו'</b>. הריב"ש הביא ראיה לזה מדהקשה דלמא צרה היה דלמא שד היה ולא הקשה דילמ' כותי היה נשמע אפילו אם היה כותי היא מותרת משום דהוי מסל"ת אפילו לא אמר שאר דברים ובט"ז כתב דלא חיישינן שמא כותי כי כותי אינו מכשיל לישראל בחנם כמ"ש בי"ד לכן לא חיישינן אלא צרה היה או שד ולא ק"מ כי אף על פי שכותי אינו מכשיל מ"מ מי אמרי' כל שלא אמר קישור דברים לא הוי מסל"ת  ולא מהני א"כ אף על גב דאינו מכשיל לישראל מ"מ כל שאינו דרך מסל"ת לא מהני א"כ שפיר  קשה שמא כותי היה אלא ש"מ אף על גב שאינו אומר קישור דברים הוי מל"ת ובזה מיושב נמי  קושית הב"ח על הריב"ש ובתשובת שער אפרים הביא קושי' זו בשם מ"ו הגאון מו"ה העשיל ז"ל איך מיישב הריב"ש הסוגי' בגיטין דאית' סוגי' זו גם כן שם ולא הקשה שם דלמא כותי היה ואמר השומע קולי יכתוב גט לאשתי ושם לא שייך הישוב של הריב"ש עכ"ל ולא ק"מ כי שם שפיר י"ל דלא חיישינן שמא כותי אמר השומע קולי יכתוב גט לאשתי וע"כ אמר זאת להכשיל את ישראל ולא חיישינן שמכשיל בחנם אבל כאן קשה דלמא הכותי אומר פלוני מת דידע לו ואמר אלא כשאומר בלא קישור דברים לא מהני אף על גב דלא חיישינן דאומר  להכשיל את ישראל ועיין ב"י כתב בשם מורי כל בתי דינים בסאלני"ק הורו להיתר וכ"פ בתשובת מהרא"מ סי"ג ותשובת מהרשד"ם ותשו' רש"ך ותשו' ר"י לבית הלוי ותשו' מ"ב סי' ק"ה ואם נכרי מספר לנכרי לכ"ע א"צ קישור דברים תשובת רש"ך ס"ב סי' מ' וב"ח כ' במקום עיגון יש לסמוך על המתירים וכן נ"ל מיהו ראיית ב"ח הנ"ל דהבי' ראיה מסוגי' זו דלא חיישינן שמא צרה משום במקום דחק לא חיישינן בכל דבר אשר ספק הוא ויש לדחות שאני החששא שמא צרה היה דחשש' רחוקה היא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And likewise if he said, etc.</b> The Rivash brought proof for this from the fact that the Gemara asks perhaps it was a rival wife, perhaps it was a demon, but does not ask perhaps it was a gentile. This implies that even if it was a gentile she is permitted, because it is treated as speech in the course of conversation, even without his saying additional contextual words. The Taz writes that we do not fear lest it was a gentile, because a gentile does not gratuitously cause a Jew to stumble, as is written in Yoreh De'ah; therefore we are concerned only that it was a rival wife or a demon.

This is not difficult. Even though a gentile does not cause a Jew to stumble for nothing, who says that without connected contextual speech it is considered speech in the course of conversation? If it is not in that manner, it is ineffective; thus the question would properly remain, perhaps it was a gentile. Rather, it must be that even without contextual words it is still considered speech in the course of conversation. This also answers the Bach's difficulty against the Rivash. In Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim this question is brought in the name of my teacher, the gaon R. Heschel, asking how the Rivash explains the parallel sugya in Gittin, where the Gemara also does not ask perhaps it was a gentile who said, "Whoever hears my voice should write a get for my wife"; there the Rivash's answer does not apply. This is not difficult, for there it is reasonable to say that we do not suspect a gentile said such a formula merely to cause Jews to stumble. Here, however, perhaps the gentile simply says that so-and-so died because he knows it; if such a statement is ineffective without contextual speech, the question should stand.

See the Beit Yosef, who writes in the name of his teachers that all the courts of Salonika ruled permissively, and so ruled Mahar'am, Maharshal, Rashach, R. Yosef of the house of Levi, and Mas'at Binyamin. If a gentile relates the matter to another gentile, all agree that connected contextual speech is not required. The Bach writes that in a case of agunah one may rely on the lenient authorities, and this appears correct. Still, the Bach's proof that in pressing circumstances we are not concerned about a rival wife, because every remote doubt is disregarded, may be rejected: the concern that it was a rival wife is different, for it is an especially remote concern.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But other gentiles speaking incidentally.</b> So writes Terumat HaDeshen. The Chelkat Mechokek discusses this at length and objects from Terumat HaDeshen's ruling about one who drowned in waters without an end: when a gentile, speaking incidentally, says that so-and-so died, we say that his incidental statement means only that he assumes the man died, not that he knows it. Here too, perhaps the gentile heard from the courts that the man was executed by judgment, assumed he had died because he trusted them, and therefore said that he died.

In truth, these concerns are not alike. Terumat HaDeshen rules that a gentile who says a man was sentenced in the courts is believed. He also rules that a gentile speaking incidentally from the mouth of another gentile is believed, and we do not suspect that the first gentile was not speaking incidentally. He further rules that when one gentile asks another gentile outside the presence of a Jew, this is considered incidental speech. These three rulings all follow one approach. Since he holds that a gentile speaking incidentally from another gentile is believed, we do not suspect that he asked him, because he holds that even if one gentile asked another outside a Jew's presence, it is incidental speech.

The general rule is that we do not entertain any concern where there is no basis for it, as written in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot. Therefore we do not suspect that a Jew was present when this gentile asked the other, or that he asked in the name of a Jew; such concerns are not entertained, just as we do not suspect that he said it merely to frighten him. The concern that the second gentile asked the first, however, is not inherently remote, because that is the normal way people converse: sometimes one tells the whole matter on his own, and sometimes one asks his fellow. It would therefore be a concern, except that Terumat HaDeshen holds that even if he asked him, it remains incidental speech when the question was asked outside the Jew's presence. We do not suspect that a Jew was present, for the reason stated above.

For the same reason we also do not suspect that he heard it from the courts; that is like other unsupported concerns. But if a report had circulated that so-and-so drowned in waters without an end, and a gentile said that he died, this is not a mere concern. Rather, we say that presumably the gentile knows no more than everyone else; he knows only the report that the man drowned in waters without an end, and on that basis he says that he died. Likewise, if a report had circulated that the man was sentenced in court and the gentile said he died, we say that he spoke because of the report. But if no report had circulated and he said the man died, we do not suspect that he heard it from the courts, and we do not suspect that he drowned in waters without an end. Such a possibility is only a general concern, and we do not entertain it unless there is a necessity for that concern.

The Ran, who disagrees with Terumat HaDeshen and holds that a gentile reporting from another gentile is not incidental speech, also agrees that we do not suspect anything without a basis. Otherwise there would be concern that he asked in a Jew's name or that a Jew was present. Rather, the Ran must hold that when one gentile asks another, even if no Jew was present and he did not ask in a Jew's name, it is not incidental speech. Therefore a gentile from the mouth of a gentile is not accepted, because we suspect that he asked him; and this kind of case is a real concern, as I have written. Thus the Beit Yosef's inference from the Ran's responsum, that when one gentile asks another it is not incidental speech, is correct. This is how these disputes appear to me.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Provided that there were not established.</b> According to the Rif, the Rosh, and the Tur, even if there was an established same-name case, she is permitted if caravans are not common. The Perishah explains that this means two people named Yosef ben Shimon were not established in this city; rather, it was merely established that somewhere in the world there was another Yosef ben Shimon. The Bayit Chadash explains that two people with that name were established in the same place, but one had gone on his way many days earlier and one had gone recently. The case is that witnesses say the one who went recently died, and we do not say that the one who went many days earlier returned and then went recently. One may also say that one of them went to the city where the court told the messenger to say that so-and-so had died; so writes the Taz.

At first glance this is difficult: why do we not suspect that the second man, who went on another road, returned and then went to this city, as in siman 132, where we suspect that perhaps he came on a "flying camel"? Perhaps here we are lenient even about this, just as we are lenient and do not require two favorable factors, both that the name was not established and that caravans are uncommon, as the Rosh writes. But it is difficult: what source allows this leniency? Granted, the fact that we do not require both favorable factors can rely on the Talmudic wording, since there are two versions in the Talmud: according to one, both favorable factors are required; according to the other, one is enough, and here we are lenient. But whence do we know to be lenient and not suspect that the other man came, as we suspect by a get?

One may answer that there, two favorable factors are required: caravans are not common and the name was not established. Therefore we suspect that that other man may have come, for the name is still established. But here, one favorable factor permits her. Therefore, if caravans are not common she is permitted. Only if the name was established in that place does "caravans are not common" fail to help, since there are two Yosef ben Shimon there. But if one went on his way, we permit with one favorable factor, namely that caravans are not common. Although there is a concern that the other man may have returned, since two Yosef ben Shimon are not present here, she is permitted.

The language of the Talmud in Yevamot 116b, "Rava said," still requires explanation. The reason we are stringent by a get is that it is possible to write another get. According to the Ramban and Rashba, the reason by a get is that it was found away from the place where it was written; had it been found at the place of writing, we would say "here it was found, here it was," and would not say it fell from someone else. Here the case is that Yosef ben Shimon went from here to that city where the court told the messenger that so-and-so had died, and therefore we say "here it was found, here it was."
