One witness says "he died," etc. The Beit Shmuel and Chelkat Mechokek understood, etc. Here too, since the witness saw that he was no longer alive, he is not careful about the precise cause by which he died. Even when one witness says that he died by the sword and another says that he died by an ariran, their testimony is invalid only because this is treated as a matter of derishah, formal examination. In my humble opinion, that is because liability or exemption depends on the matter: if the death was by a sword no medical assessment is needed, whereas if it was by another instrument assessment is required, as is stated in Hilkhot Rotzeach 3. The same may explain the Gemara's answer in Sanhedrin 41a regarding a cloth with which he was strangled, if it was fit to strangle with. The words of Tosafot in Chullin 11b, who write that sword and ariran are not so closely connected with the body of the testimony, require examination.

This is also explicit in Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilkhot Edut: the chakirot and derishot are the matters that constitute the essence of the testimony, by which one becomes liable or exempt. Here, by contrast, there is no practical difference between "he died" and "he was killed," so it is not called derishah. Accordingly this must also be Rambam's reason for treating wine and oil as a contradiction in derishah: according to one witness the defendant is exempt from wine, and according to the other he is exempt from oil.

The same is clear from Ran in Sanhedrin, in the fifth chapter, where he writes that the case in which one says "barrels of wine" and the other says "oil" is a contradiction. Although in chapter Chazakat HaBatim we say that people do not distinguish carefully between wheat and barley, that is where one says that he consumed wheat and the other says barley; there the contradiction is only in bedikot, for the matter on which the chazakah depends is equally significant whether it was wheat or barley. See there. Thus there is no real difference between Ran and Rambam except that Ran calls the contradiction of wine and oil a severe bedikah that invalidates even in monetary cases, while Rambam includes it among the chakirot. When Rambam also lists, among the chakirot, the food with which one ate on Yom Kippur, it must be because there too there is a practical distinction, for example whether the food was unfit for eating. So too, after analysis, each matter that he mentions has legal consequence.

At first glance there is support for Rambam's view that whatever defines the substance of the testimony is called derishah. At the end of Yevamot the Gemara asks whether Rabbi Tarfon does not require derishah from the baraita that examines the witness: did the lion speak about the person concerning whom he testified, or about the village? The Gemara establishes it according to the one who requires derishah. Thus that question is within the class of derishot, because through it we know about whom he is testifying. Ran's responsum 47, however, writes that even a contradiction about the name is considered a contradiction in bedikot and is valid in monetary law. That is because the identity of the person about whom they testified was known apart from the name: everyone knew that the husband had no more than one brother, and both witnesses testified to the death of the husband's brother. The name therefore made no practical difference.

In this light the words of the Shulchan Aruch are correct. The second answer brought by the Beit Shmuel in the name of the Penei Moshe, that in testimony permitting a woman to remarry one witness suffices even if the witnesses contradict one another in chakirot, is difficult. The Shev Yaakov, responsum 18, agrees with the Beit Shmuel himself, who in section 21 rules decisively that when there is contradiction in chakirot the testimony is void. Also, Bach in responsum 94 thought that in testimony concerning a woman, where the sages were lenient because it is a matter likely to become known and because of the woman's own careful inquiry, even contradiction in chakirot does not void the testimony. But the Shev Yaakov already wrote that the language of Ran and of Shulchan Aruch section 21 does not imply this.

The Baer Heitev, however, writes that if one witness says he died in one place and another says he died in another place this is not a contradiction, citing Mahari Karo. This requires checking in the work itself, for it is proved in Bach.

Know also that against the Penei Moshe's reasoning the Noda BiYehudah, responsum 41, objected from Sema, Choshen Mishpat 30:9, who writes that the testimony is void and that no oath is even required, although one witness also suffices for an oath. He answered that in monetary law real testimony is required, and without a witness judgment cannot be rendered; truth of the matter alone does not suffice. The Torah invalidates the testimony of relatives even in the case of Moses and Aaron, although the matter would certainly be true. Since these two witnesses contradict each other and one of them is lying and invalid, the other's testimony is also void. But in testimony permitting a woman we do not require formal witnesses; we require knowledge that the husband died. Therefore, if many witnesses testified and one was found disqualified by sin, it appears to him that the woman is nevertheless permitted. So too, when they contradict each other in chakirot, since according to both of them he is at least dead, and one of them is telling the truth, she may be permitted through the true witness, whoever that is.

His statement that in monetary law testimony rather than truth is required needs examination, for we rule that witnesses were created only against falsehood, as appears in Choshen Mishpat 191:1. Only in bills of divorce and betrothal do the acts have no force without witnesses; in monetary matters, the truth of the matter is plainly the essence. He must mean that a Torah-level oath can be imposed only by a valid witness. As to the decree concerning relatives, the decree must mean that their testimony is considered as though it had not been given, so that the truth of the matter is not known through them. From the Itur cited by Shakh, Choshen Mishpat 33:9, it also sounds as though the decree even treats Moses and Aaron as suspect of lying; otherwise what does he mean by saying that once the ruling has been issued, it stands, and that a person is inclined to maintain his prior statement? If he was never suspected of lying but was merely disqualified at the time of testimony by the royal decree, and now that he has become distant the decree no longer applies and his testimony begins and ends in fitness, why invalidate him? It would seem that the decree is to suspect even Moses of falsehood.

Yet that cannot be correct, as is clear from Choshen Mishpat 46:35, where others testify that they recognize the signatures. The reason is that disqualification through kinship is not because one is suspected of forgery, but because of the King's decree. If one is not suspected of forgery, he is certainly not suspected of testifying falsely. The words of the Itur therefore require examination. We must explain with difficulty: since a person is inclined to maintain his prior words, and we know even before he testifies again that he will repeat his first testimony, it is not considered a new testimony but one continuous testimony; once the matter has been ruled on, it has been ruled on. This is satisfactory if judgment has already been rendered. But if judgment has not yet been rendered, why follow the first testimony? Let us follow the second, which begins and ends in fitness, and rule by it. This explains Shakh's statement that there is no reason to distinguish whether judgment has already been rendered. According to his implication, that there is suspicion of falsehood, he wrote well; according to what we have said, it is resolved.

If so, because the decree is that their testimony is treated as nonexistent, and the truth of the matter is therefore not known, one might also say in testimony concerning a woman that through the rule that when one witness is found a relative or disqualified the entire testimony is void, the testimony is deemed nonexistent and the truth is not known. Nevertheless the Noda BiYehudah spoke well that in testimony concerning a woman the rule of "one of them was found a relative or disqualified" does not apply. According to those who hold that by Torah law one witness is valid in testimony concerning a woman this is obvious, for the analogy "just as two..." does not apply to such testimony. Even according to most authorities who hold that by Torah law two witnesses are required, and therefore the analogy could have applied by Torah law, once the sages validated all those who are Torah-disqualified except one disqualified by a Torah transgression, a person disqualified only by combination is no worse than a woman or slave, since he himself committed no transgression. The author of the responsum, in responsum 45, wrote this as obvious.

Thus, if Sema's reason were clearly based on "one of them was found disqualified," the answer would be good. In my humble opinion, however, this requires examination, for it appears that his reason is that with respect to the testimony in which they contradicted each other we treat both of them as disqualified. This is like Rosh in chapter Zeh Borer, law 34, who writes on Ramah's words that at first glance even a Torah oath should not be required, because according to the lender one of them is testifying falsely; the testimony is contradicted, and we treat both as disqualified for that testimony. If his reason were "one of them was found disqualified," what was difficult? To invalidate through that rule they must have intended to testify; perhaps Ramah was speaking where they saw the loan without intending to testify. Although Sema had to distinguish this way, on further examination Sema 30:25 writes on Rosh's words that we do not say these witnesses are considered contradicted and their testimony void, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in the following siman. This shows clearly that he understood Rosh's reason also to be that no Torah oath should result because it is like "one of them was found a relative or disqualified," for in the following siman, subsection 2, he explicitly explains this reason, as he writes there and as appears in Rashba's responsum 322.

Still, this is difficult for me, because Rosh rules that two requirements are needed for "one of them was found disqualified": they must also have intended from the outset to testify, as is clear in siman 36 in his name. Moreover, even if they intended to testify, that is not enough here, for one of them was contradicted only at the time of testimony by the lender's own words; at the time of seeing the event both were presumed valid. What has this to do with invalidating the valid witness by his combination with the invalid one? This is especially so according to Shakh, siman 36:9, who writes that intent to testify is of no use when they did not see the event together, for how did they join? That certainly does not apply to Ramah's case, where they testified about different times. One should not force the matter by saying that Rosh wrote according to Ramah's view in Tur siman 36 that testimony in court alone suffices, for Rosh never brought that view of Ramah even in tractate Makkot. Furthermore, even according to Ramah there is no difficulty according to Rif's view in siman 36 that the valid witness must recognize the invalidity of the other. Even according to those who disagree, the witness who is contradicted and invalidated only at the time of testimony was truly valid beforehand, and the valid witness was an anus, not even inadvertent. Why then did Rosh answer by distinguishing between witnesses who contradict each other and witnesses contradicted by the lender? He could simply have said that even if the witnesses contradict each other in chakirot, the valid witness should not be invalidated by combination with the invalid one because he was an anus. It therefore seems to me that Rosh plainly means that for the testimony for which they were invalidated through contradiction they are entirely void in their own right, though the source for this still requires examination.

This is surprising regarding Sema: according to his own reasoning, how could he rule that law simply in siman 30:9, when almost all decisors agree that intent to testify at the time of seeing is required? Tosafot in Zeh Borer 31, s.v. "she-beit Shammai," although they imply that intent to testify is not required, answered in other ways, and in Bava Batra they indeed changed the reason to lack of intent. According to my reading of Rosh, the Penei Moshe's answer does not stand. See, however, Urim VeTumim, who undermines Sema's very rule on the basis of the sages of Lunel; the matter requires examination.

I later found a great novelty in the book of the Noda BiYehudah, responsum 46. He brings a clear proof for the Shiltei Giborim from the Yerushalmi on this mishnah in Ha-Ishah Shalom. The Yerushalmi records: Rabbi Yaakov bar Acha in the name of Rabbi Yochanan, and Rabbi Chila in the name of Rabbi Eliezer: Rabbi Meir admits in the first case. He found a teaching: even that case is in dispute. What is the difference between the second and the first? In the first they did not make this woman's statement count at all against the other. Rabbi Elazar said: Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses. What is the difference between witnesses and a rival wife? They did not make the rival wife's words count at all against her fellow. Rabbi Yochanan said: if Rabbi Elazar said it, he heard it from me.

The Penei Moshe explains that "Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses" means the case of one witness saying "he died" and one saying "he did not die," and likewise explains "from whom did he hear it." The Noda BiYehudah writes that he erred, for Rabbi Elazar in the Yerushalmi says that Rabbi Meir admits in the first case. If so, the opening case, where one says he died and one says he did not die, is unanimous; Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon are not mentioned there. How then can one say that they "admit regarding witnesses," which implies that although they disagree regarding rival wives they admit regarding witnesses? They do not disagree regarding rival wives at all in that case. Rather, "they admit regarding witnesses" means where one says "he died" and the other says "he was killed." Even if we emend the text to align it with our Talmud, so that Rabbi Yochanan says Rabbi Meir admits in the first case and Rabbi Chila in the name of Rabbi Elazar found that even it is in dispute, nevertheless the conclusion remains. The Yerushalmi says that Rabbi Elazar heard it from Rabbi Yochanan, and according to Rabbi Yochanan Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon did not disagree regarding rival wives in the case of one saying "he did not die." It must therefore mean that they admit regarding witnesses where one said "he died" and one said "he was killed." The Noda BiYehudah concludes that, with due respect to Rosh, Tur, Beit Yosef, and all the later authorities, the Yerushalmi was hidden from them and the words of the Shiltei Giborim are primary.

In my humble opinion, although his explanation is clear, as I will explain, the reason remains difficult. Since Rabbi Yochanan holds that Rabbi Meir admits in the first case because the contradiction of a rival wife counts for nothing, and according to the Yerushalmi Rabbi Elazar also holds this way, Rabbi Meir's disagreement where one says "he died" and one says "he was killed" must follow Tosafot's reason: since both women come to permit themselves, we cannot treat either as intending only to ruin the other; rather, they may have spoken by conjecture. Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon's reason must be that their words agree on the essential matter, that he is not alive. If so, why distinguish and say that regarding witnesses they admit? What is different? Their words still agree on the essential matter. The reason of contradiction by a rival wife is one Rabbi Meir himself admits, according to Rabbi Yochanan; he holds only that where both come to permit themselves that reasoning does not apply. These are precisely the words of the Beit Shmuel on Rosh, that this follows the answer of Ri. See the sugya and you will understand. Thus, contrary to the Noda BiYehudah's astonishment at Rosh from logic, the simple explanation is as Beit Shmuel wrote: according to Rabbi Yochanan, the matter is proved intrinsically, for there is no reasoned basis to distinguish for Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon between witnesses and rival wives. According to the Noda BiYehudah's explanation, this difficulty falls on Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Elazar in the Yerushalmi.

Moreover, I will bear my shame before all our masters from Rosh onward: shall we say that the Yerushalmi escaped even Rif and Rambam? They are the ones who illuminate our eyes in both Talmuds by extracting law from them. Would they have ignored this Yerushalmi set out in its place? Rather, later in the Yerushalmi it says: we learned there, one who had, etc. Rav said that contradiction of testimony within testimony does not void it; Rabbi Yochanan said it does void it. Rabbi Yochanan follows his own view, for Rabbi Chiyya said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: if the counter was established and one says "from the purse" while the other says "from the bundle," this is contradiction of testimony within testimony, and even Rav admits that the testimony is void. Where do they disagree? Where there were two groups, these saying "from the purse" and those saying "from the bundle": is contradiction of testimony within testimony void, or has it not been voided? Rav's strength is from the mishnah in which Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Yehudah say, since both admit that he is not alive, they may marry. But did he not hear what Rabbi Elazar said, that Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses? What is the difference between witnesses and a rival wife? They did not make the rival wife's words count at all against her fellow. The mishnah then challenges Rav: both chakirot and bedikot, when they contradict one another, their testimony is void. Rabbi Mana answers for Rav that this refers to one witness against one witness.

From this it is explicit, as the Noda BiYehudah explained, and it is surprising that Penei Moshe did not look from the end of the passage back to its beginning. Yet it is also explicit that Rav and Rabbi Yochanan both hold that with one witness against another, when one says "from the purse" and the other "from the bundle," the contradiction is considered in the body of the testimony and the testimony is void. They disagree only about two groups. This is exactly what our Talmud calls contradiction in bedikot, such as one saying a black money pouch and the other saying a white one, which is valid in monetary cases, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 30. This shows that the Yerushalmi disagrees with the conclusion of our Talmud and holds that even in monetary law, contradiction in bedikot voids the testimony. Therefore Rabbi Yochanan, following his own view, properly rules that Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses; and Rav, following his own view, who did not wish to distinguish between rival wives and witnesses, properly derives from that mishnah a distinction between one group and another group. But according to the conclusion of our Talmud, just as we judge in the case of the money pouch that the testimony remains valid in monetary law, the same is true for testimony concerning women, which is treated like monetary law: even with one witness against one witness, if one says "he died" and the other says "he was killed," their testimony remains valid for all opinions. I have already shown that even for Rambam this is only bedikot. For this reason they excluded the Yerushalmi from practical law and stated the mishnah simply, to teach that there is no distinction, even in one witness against one witness, as Rosh rules.

However, Rosh and Tur in Choshen Mishpat 145 distinguish between wheat and barley on the one hand and wheat and legumes on the other, saying that the latter does not establish a chazakah because people do not confuse all such things. According to him it is somewhat difficult, for "he died" and "he was killed" should perhaps resemble legumes and barley. This can be answered with difficulty and requires further study. In any case, we learn from the Yerushalmi that since Rabbi Yochanan, following his own view, says Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon admit regarding witnesses, although the witnesses are really comparable to one group against another, and because even in one group against another he holds that the testimony is void, it follows that he agrees with Rosh that with respect to the testimony for which they contradicted each other we treat both witnesses as invalid for that testimony. Since the law is known to follow Rabbi Yochanan against Rav, it follows for us as well that in contradiction in chakirot, like wine and oil, Beit Yosef wrote correctly that even for a single witness's oath the witness does not stand. See the Urim VeTumim cited above, for his words are not compelling, since Ran in Sanhedrin holds exactly like Rambam that monetary witnesses who contradict each other in chakirot are entirely void, unlike the ruling of the sages of Lunel. Examine this carefully.

<b>They found it written.</b> The Beit Shmuel wrote that even if he wrote to the bet din that he had heard from another, it is effective; only where he writes as a memorandum and not for practical action do we not rule leniently with testimony from the mouth of another witness, as written in Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 2 of Yevamot. His words are brief, so I will copy the language of Hagahot Maimoniyot. It says there: many times I wondered how we permit a woman to marry on the basis of writing, and I found in the Yerushalmi, etc., as in the Rif. Now one must wonder according to Rabbi Bun: how do we permit them on the basis of writing? Even according to Rabbi Yirmiyah the matter requires study, for perhaps the Yerushalmi speaks of witnesses who signed based on their own sight, like a loan document, which it cites there; but these witnesses wrote based on testimony heard from another, or from a gentile, or from a woman. I would distinguish in order to uphold the custom: the Yerushalmi speaks where they wrote on their own, not for a need and not for a practical case, but to stand as evidence; a document of that kind does not have the law of a shtar. He further wrote at the beginning of chapter 23, joining this distinction, that the writings we send in order to determine the truth of the testimony are different: if they had testified so before bet din, the bet din would have permitted her, and we too act as their agents and permit her. In a similar way Riva answered in Ketubot, chapter 2, regarding the law that a person may write his testimony in a document and testify from it if he remembers it on his own, but if he does not remember it, he may not. Riva asked: why should he not send his testimony to bet din, so that it be like a document and they validate it, as is said regarding an erased debt document? He answered that there it was made at the command of the borrower and lender and has the status of a shtar; but a document that a witness makes on his own has no status of a shtar, and is considered from their mouths and not from their writing. He brought the Midrashim that I cited, and he inferred from the phrase: now we allow marriage based on testimony from the mouth of another witness, that likewise based on writings we allow marriage. This implies that if not for the rule allowing testimony from the mouth of another witness, we would not allow marriage based on writings. Why should they not validate the signatures? Rather, in such a case it is not a shtar. End quote. From this the Beit Shmuel derived that even if he wrote to the bet din that he heard from another, it is effective, for that does not belong to the dispute in the Yerushalmi: it is a shtar, and there is no leniency of from their writing. But when he writes as a memorandum and not for practical action, then it belongs to the Yerushalmi's dispute, as the Ran also explained. Even though we rule like the one who permits, the Beit Shmuel accepts definitively what Hagahot Maimoniyot left requiring study, namely that perhaps the Yerushalmi speaks only of witnesses who signed based on their own sight, and that we do not rule leniently for testimony from the mouth of another witness because that would be two leniencies, testimony from another witness and testimony from writing. In my humble opinion this truly requires study, for the one who permits brought support from the baraita that now we allow marriage on the basis of one witness, and the same should apply from writings. With one witness we do not distinguish, and even two additional leniencies, such as from another witness and from a slave, are accepted. The same should be true of from writing; one should not distinguish between one leniency and two. As for Hagahot Maimoniyot's answer that when he writes testimony to bet din it is not considered from their writing because it is a shtar, although we rule in Choshen Mishpat siman 28 that one may not send testimony in writing because it is considered from their writing, one must say that this is only when testifying for the benefit of one litigant and it was not made at the command of both litigants, or with one witness, where it is not considered a shtar. See what I brought in siman 142 in the name of Rivash. Here, however, the consent of litigants is not relevant, for who is the opposing party? And one witness in this testimony is considered like two. Whenever he writes his testimony to bet din, it is considered a shtar and not from their writing. One must also press the phrase, we too act as their agents, to mean that we accept this writing as a shtar; otherwise the words are not understandable. It is known that the reason a shtar is not considered from their writing, even if the witnesses later died, is that at the time of writing it is considered as though their testimony had been investigated in bet din, and then they were able to testify. But a mute witness plainly cannot testify even if he signed his testimony in a shtar; what does it help that it is considered investigated, when even if he had then been investigated in bet din, the Torah disqualified him? He is one of the disqualified witnesses, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 35 seif 11. It is therefore obvious that even if he signed a shtar it is nothing. I therefore do not know what difficulty the Beit Shmuel saw when he wrote: at first glance it is difficult how this law is derived from there, since the Ran explains the Yerushalmi's dispute as speaking of writing for remembrance, and with this the Tur is resolved when he wrote that the Rosh did not decide. What difference does it make that he is mute? If it is written for remembrance, it is still from their writing, and the whole defect of writing for remembrance is that it is considered from their writing. This is unlike writing in order to testify, which can be considered a shtar as explained above. Therefore, since the Rif and the Rosh ruled that a mute person may testify by writing, it follows that even writing for remembrance also helps for testimony about a woman. In my humble opinion it is more plausible that the Tur wrote only that from its own place it would appear to rule this way, and then the Rambam wrote otherwise, and he concedes to him. Know also that aside from the Ran's precision that the language they found written indicates writing for remembrance, in my humble opinion the matter is proven from its place. If it were a full shtar signed by proper witnesses in order to testify, I wonder what room there would be to disqualify it for testimony about an agunah. Could one have thought to be stricter in testimony about an agunah than in monetary law? Even witnesses disqualified for monetary testimony are valid for testimony about an agunah, all the more a full shtar, which in monetary law is not considered from their writing. What we are stricter about regarding identifying signs than in monetary law is, as explained in Bekhorot, because signs are the beginning of testimony; but at the end of testimony we are lenient. Necessarily the Yerushalmi speaks, as they explained, of writing for remembrance or of one witness. See Choshen Mishpat siman 46 seif 10 in the gloss. Unless we say the opposite of the reasoning of Hagahot Maimoniyot, namely that because this shtar was not made in the ordinary way between two litigants who customarily write such documents, it is considered from their writing. However it may be, it is at least resolved from the case of the mute person that from their writing is valid here, and the same applies in all forms. Know that according to their interpretation, that the Yerushalmi is not speaking of a full shtar, one must press the language of the supporting baraita, for in its first clause it says that the power of witnesses is greater regarding testimony about an agunah, and that speaks of a full shtar; in the latter clause it says that the power of a shtar is greater regarding gittin and encumbered property, and that too must speak of a full shtar. Still, this is not so difficult, for the witnesses whose power is greater for testimony about an agunah also refer to inferior witnesses, and this is clear. The Beit Shmuel further wrote: there it teaches that just as they examine, and according to what we rule, etc., it appears, etc. This is also the question of Knesset HaGedolah, by Maharach Shash, against Rashba himself. He writes that Rashba inferred from the Rif's conclusion, that in other testimony the rule is from their mouths and not from their writing, that in testimony about an agunah from their writing is effective. According to this inference, one must say that the Rif holds the reverse of Rashba's own view regarding gittin, for according to this the Rif holds that writing is effective for gittin; yet Rashba proved that writing is not effective for a get, even by a sound person. In my humble opinion this is not difficult. Although in a get nodding the head is better than writing according to the Yerushalmi, on the contrary, the difficulty is stronger: why did the baraita exclude a mute person from liability for an offering because he cannot testify? He can testify by nodding. Although Tosafot in Gittin 71, s.v. Amar Rabbi Zeira, pressed to answer the difficulty only according to Rav Kahana, nevertheless according to Abaye's answer, that testimony is different because the Torah said from their mouths, this is certainly also the reason why he cannot testify by nodding, even though nodding is better than writing: the Scriptural decree requires from their mouths specifically. This is what the Rif concludes: but elsewhere not, for the Torah said from their mouths, meaning that just as writing is excluded by the decree of from their mouths, so too his nodding is excluded. Once we see that regarding testimony about an agunah nodding is effective and we do not insist on from their mouths, it follows that the same is true of writing. But with a get, where the insistence does not emerge from the decree of from their mouths, one can distinguish as the Yerushalmi does: nodding the head, which is an action of the body, is more like his voice than handwriting is. The phrase just as they examine for gittin was not stated to equate the law of testimony about an agunah with gittin, but only to equate the law of examination for agunah testimony to the examination used for gittin. This is clear. According to this, even without the above, the difficulty raised against the Ran is not difficult: in Perek Mi She'achazo the case does not concern writing for remembrance. What of it? The main proof is that since nodding is effective for testimony about an agunah, we see that in this testimony we are not precise about from their mouths, and automatically from their writing is valid, namely writing for remembrance.

What connection is there to testimony from the mouth of another witness, which would be invalid even with two witnesses? By contrast, validation for testimony permitting a woman to remarry is no better than the body of that testimony itself, and therefore one witness is sufficient. This is the plain intention of the Maggid Mishneh.

In Knesset HaGedolah of Maharach Shash he pressed himself to distinguish between witnesses who validate by recognizing the signature, where two witnesses are required, and one witness who says that the document was signed before him, where one suffices. This is not correct; the matter is as understood by the Mishneh LaMelekh and by our later great authorities.

Regarding Rambam's requirement, the Beit Shmuel writes that this means the writing was composed in the manner of a person speaking innocently, and that the Maggid Mishneh wrote the same, making this a novelty. The Beit Yosef objects that this has no reason: if they wrote it in the manner of innocent speech, why should it be inferior? In my humble opinion, the Maggid Mishneh follows his own view in law 13, where he maintains the position of Rivash, which is the first opinion in section 14 of the gloss. He therefore interpreted Rambam to mean that even without introductory casual words, any statement made without being asked is called innocent speech, and was forced to explain this rule as a novelty.

But according to the second opinion, derived from Rambam's language, that specifically requires introductory casual words, one may explain simply that Rambam wrote this to explain the Tosefta's case, which he copied in its own wording: "they found written in a document, So-and-so died." That sounds like nothing more than a notification without a question. Rambam therefore teaches that such writing is not innocent speech, and one must insist that it be the writing of a Jew. Yet he would agree that where there were introductory casual words, even a gentile's writing is valid. It follows that if a woman has a gentile's written statement made innocently with introductory casual words, and also a gentile who testifies orally without being asked and without introductory casual words, she is permitted either way, without hesitation.

He continues that according to Rambam the Mishnah certainly speaks of every minor, and it is effective only when the child says, "we were coming," etc., even if he has not reached that stage. But where he has reached that stage Rambam agrees that he is effective if he says explicitly, "he died"; at any rate, our master the Tur is worthy of reliance once the child has reached that stage. End quote.

Although in my humble opinion Rambam's language does not imply that he agrees once the child has reached that stage, I am nevertheless uncertain whether Rambam requires an actual adult with pubic hairs, or whether for testimony permitting a woman to remarry the rule is like the gloss in Orach Chayim 55: once he has reached the years of adulthood we presume him to be an adult and say ordinarily that he has brought hairs. The source of that custom appears to me to be Massekhet Soferim 16, which says that a minor does not count for Barekhu, Torah reading, or Kaddish until he is thirteen, and that we do not examine them for these matters.

One may raise an even greater doubt: perhaps Rambam at least agrees that when he has reached the years of majority he does not need hairs at all, since Rambam makes his disqualification depend on lack of understanding. In Hilkhot Edut 9:7 Rambam writes that even a minor who is perceptive and wise remains invalid until he brings two hairs, which implies that here the matter does not depend on hairs but on understanding.

See the responsum of the Noda BiYehudah, responsum 41, who decides that actual hairs and examination are specifically required. According to his approach, he writes that the minor is believed about himself by an a fortiori argument: if he is believed as an adult to testify that a bride went out with a hinuma because most women marry as virgins, all the more should he be believed that he brought two hairs, since the majority supports him; and certainly if he is examined now and hairs are found. It appears that at first he argued by a fortiori even without any examination at all. In my humble opinion this a fortiori argument can be refuted. Evidence that we rely on a majority when he testifies as an adult about others is no proof that he may testify about himself, especially where perhaps at the very time of testimony he is still a minor, and if he is a minor his words have no force. We have not found testimony about oneself anywhere in the Torah, except for a woman regarding the death of her husband.

Apart from this, the law itself requires examination in my opinion, and it seems more likely to me that hairs are not required at all. See Yoreh De'ah 65 in the gloss, which writes that he is called a minor until he is thirteen years and one day old, excluding the view brought in Darkhei Moshe that permits a child entering his thirteenth year. This is difficult, for we rule that "within the time" is as before the time. Rather, it must be that all agree that hairs are not required for testimony in which one witness suffices. The Kereti u-Peleti presses the language of the Shulchan Aruch, and I do not know how he would answer the permissive view cited in the Darkhei Moshe.

Here, by contrast, the only concern is that he too came there. Perhaps, however, one may still say that the disappearance of this man from the world is comparable to the fall of the get. This is the reasoning of our teacher the Beit Yosef z"l in responsum, as the Beit Shmuel and Sefer Even HaEzer reported in his name. However, what he also wrote, that this is also the view of the Ritva cited in the Beit Yosef, in my poverty I do not understand. The Beit Shmuel asked from what is ruled in siman 132, that these utensils have a presumption that he does not lend them. He answered: perhaps with a get, where the litigant is before us and does not say that he lent it, we are not concerned for borrowing; one may say this even where he did not specify that he knows he did not lend it. Certainly in such a case we are not concerned; see there.

**** HEBREW ****
אבל אם טבע במים וכו' אפילו אחר כמה ימים ל' הרי"ף אבל במים אפשר שישהא ד' או ה' ימים ואינו משתנה וע' בק"ע דמהרח"ש דף מ"ד ע"ב יש נסתפקו אם דוקא ולא יותר או ל"ד ועיין בהרמ' ה' סוכה בדין אתרוג המנומר ובכ"מ ומ"ל שם ויש ראיה משם דל"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
But if he drowned in water, etc., even after several days. The language of Rif is: "But in water it is possible that he remains four or five days and does not change." See Knesset HaGedolah of Maharchash, fol. 44b: some were uncertain whether this means specifically four or five days and not more, or whether the number is not exact. See Rambam, Hilkhot Sukkah, regarding a speckled etrog, and Kesef Mishneh and Mishneh LaMelekh there; there is proof from there that the number is not exact.
