**** ENGLISH ****
Hazakah: a woman does not act brazenly. Beit Yosef brings in the name of Eliyahu Rabba: if it is known through witnesses that she is his wife — this hazakah does not apply; rather it applies specifically when he came from elsewhere and there is no clear testimony that she is his wife but only through hazakah — the later hazakah comes and nullifies the earlier. Beit Yosef rejected this. In my view Tosafot Yevamos also follow this line on 116b s.v. ba-oto she'ah — they wrote: if so why don't we believe her from a migo that she could have said "he divorced me" per Rav Hamnuna even not in his presence — answer, etc. Question: it seems from that version that some explain that view — even not in his presence she is believed when she said "he divorced me"; how can one think thus? We learned: if there are witnesses she was a married woman and she says "I am divorced" — Eliyahu Rabba went this way; specifically if there are witnesses; but against hazakah she is not believed even not in his presence. And likewise when she married and afterward witnesses came — because she married permissibly she is again believed even against witnesses due to agunah. They challenged from R' Yehudah's mishnah of the woman who went — applies even to a husband via hazakah alone without witnesses — due to agunah she is believed; they challenged: let us believe from migo. Nevertheless for law the essence is as Beit Yosef wrote and Tosafot there, as Ran also explained in Gittin — if so there is no proof for Eliyahu Rabba's view; Shulchan Aruch — they do not distinguish.

**** ENGLISH ****
See Choshen Mishpat — what Beit Yosef wondered on R' Yonah: it seems if he contradicted her she is not believed — but this is certainly not so regarding hazakah, etc. In Hagahot Rama it states: some say further in this time when brazenness is common — or rather for stringency. It appears to me that when there is a convincing excuse proving she speaks truth — even in our time she is believed, as stated siman 154 seif 7 gloss 2 — one law and one reason; both stringencies of Maharam emerge. One may say R' Yonah also already adopted Maharam's stringency for nowadays — therefore he did not believe her except when an excuse joined because he did not contradict; and he has good support from the incident of Rabbenu Tam in Tosafot Gittin 92b s.v. halakhah — see there. In my view this is correct for law.

See Beit Shmuel — "and if she began," etc. Choshen Mishpat is uncertain; however one may say invalid get to divorce — therefore if she began, etc., there is still concern, etc. See siman 152 where he repeated the matter: per Taz even if she began not in his presence — Rama wrote law as stated siman 152 in Tur in his name — "or he divorced her with invalid get and she does not know" — if so wonder: even when he says "I divorced you" and he contradicts her — also concern, for in any case we believe her only that he divorced with get; let us say both speak truth — she because she accepted get brazenly and he because he knows he always said "I did not divorce you." And what R' Yonah explained — from silence and no contradiction she is betrothed and permitted — Beit Yosef agrees. But in my humble view Rama never intended his reason for his ruling; rather in the Gemara regarding husband who said "I divorced" or not — early authorities questioned and answered: if so that he divorced her — a rumor would reach him; question: if so a woman who said "he divorced me" — also say if so, etc.; in Bava Batra we need whether we say migo in place of hazakah — at least it seems migo is not weaker than hazakah and they are balanced; if we nullify migo due to married woman we nullify hazakah that she is not brazen due to married woman — perforce strained that hazakot are not equal. But apparently Rama poses the question initially — there is no migo here; therefore he wrote in this language: even if she admitted to him that he divorced or perhaps he intends to ruin her or divorced with invalid get and she does not know, or perhaps she will act brazenly since he believes her — meaning perhaps entirely he said he divorced only to ruin her and there is no migo at all; he again explains modes of ruin: mode one — or he divorced with invalid get to ruin her and what she admits because she does not know; mode two — or perhaps he did not divorce at all and what she admits — perhaps she will act brazenly, etc.; all this we concern; because he intends ruin — thereby removes "since it is in his power to divorce her" — all this applies only to one who says "I divorced you" coming to permit her now in his lifetime; one may say his intention that they marry again his words will clarify for ruin; but if he has sons it does not help her except after death — no intention to ruin. Again I saw Beit Yosef already brought clear proof from Rama himself unlike my view — "and if she married not in husband's presence although," etc. — if so there is support, etc., or he precisely wrote "or not" and not "kiddushin do not take effect in her" as above from what he wrote: but if another betrothed her not in husband's presence kiddushin do not take effect in her; and in Tur siman 152 he wrote also this — no get needed from second in my view — because Rashba responsum siman 1253 disputes this reasoning on the question: Reuven betrothed woman and another came and betrothed her not in his presence — reasonable no get needed from second, etc.; but in our case specifically when she does not now say he divorced her; but if she says he divorced — forbidden from Rav Hamnuna; and even though she began not in his presence we do not say initially she was not believed and now establish her as liar — as proven in chapter Ha'ishah sheNitarmelah — we learn: two say, etc., and we challenge: what difference — Rava said, etc., and we challenge: if brazen — but Rav Hamnuna said, etc. — meaning even though she began brazenness not in his presence she is not established liar now in his presence; it appears from his proof even if she married — similar to our case Rosh also is not established liar, etc.; nevertheless it appears her law is not like beginning in his presence per Shulchan Aruch above — rather if she married forbidden to both and leaves both with get; if only betrothed — first divorces and second marries — meaning stam "or not" is for leniency that she be permitted to second; but certainly needs get. At that stage he judged that most who are goses do not live three days, and most die; but the minority may remain goses for four or five days, some die afterward, and some even recover from this illness. In any case, we clearly hear from this that one cannot derive from his ruling any permission for testimony regarding a woman. All the more so regarding what the Beit Shmuel wrote in s.k. 94, that if he is a goses for three days they may testify about him, whose implication is that the witness testifies that he saw him as a goses for three days and left him as a goses. If so, he has left the majority according to Maharam, who wrote that most who are goses do not live three days; one may still say that he is from the minority and recovered from this illness. Tosafot in Yevamot 120b, s.v. lemeimra, and 121a, s.v. velo hi, decided that we do not testify concerning a goses. They even pressed themselves in their question and did not establish that which was taught, and even a goses, whose implication is that he conveys impurity but is not alive, as referring to a goses of three days. This proves that there is no distinction at all. Although the Rama z"l in Yoreh De'ah added the language that he certainly died, this is necessarily imprecise. For if not, we would infer that in the case mentioned there, where they left him as a goses for one hour and three days have now passed, his wife may remarry; Heaven forbid to say so, as is explained in the above Tosafot on the basis of an explicit Tosefta. Rather, because there was a difficulty: since we rule that in mourning we follow the lenient view, and we do not mourn for a child who dies within thirty days because we are concerned for the minority of nonviable infants, here too let us be concerned for the minority and not let her mourn. Therefore he wrote that he certainly died, meaning that this is an uncommon minority and we do not concern ourselves for it even according to Rabbi Akiva. But in testimony to permit a woman we are stringent because of the severity of forbidden relations, as Tosafot wrote in Yevamot 36b. Nonviable infants, by contrast, may be considered a common minority. One may also say, as I wrote, that in Maharam's case there was no concern for an ervah stumbling block. Tosafot in Yevamot 109, s.v. mechuverta, wrote that what we rule regarding nonviable infants, not to mourn, is because of concern for the stumbling block of chalitzah; if so, in our case, where there was no concern for a stumbling block, he ruled to mourn. However it may be, the Beit Shmuel's words in this matter are not halakhah in my humble opinion, and one cannot learn anything at all from Yoreh De'ah. The Beit Hillel, whom he brings as support, is actually a help against him, and this is astonishing. I later saw that Yam Shel Shlomo, Perek Kohen Gadol, siman 8, also learned from Maharam's ruling to permit a woman based on testimony that they left him as a goses. It seems to me that all the above words of Tosafot were then hidden from his honor's eyes. In Kuntres Iggun of Maharachash he raised many difficulties against the very ruling concerning mourning; see there, folio 56. What emerges from his words is that with a goses, even if she married, she must leave. It never entered my mind to learn from there as the Beit Shmuel distinguished between a goses for three days and a goses for one hour. However, from the above Tosafot, and likewise from Bekhorot 20b, s.v. chalav, it appears in my humble opinion not like Maharachash z"l in this point; rather, its law is like water that has no end regarding if she married. But to marry initially, it is clear that she is forbidden in every case.

Empty and reckless apostates who returned in repentance, but not in complete repentance, should not be relied upon for their testimony. I saw in a manuscript responsum of the late gaon Rabbi Chaim Rapoport z"l, av beit din of Lemberg, which is under my hand, concerning an agunah where testimony was collected by the bet din of London from a man who had been an apostate and afterward returned to the Jewish faith. Now, in Shulchan Aruch Choshen Mishpat siman 37 seif 22, the Rama brings that an apostate who retracted and accepted repentance upon himself is valid immediately, even though he has not yet completed it. The Sema wrote as follows: in Terumat HaDeshen he explains the reason, because the apostate had been mixed among the gentiles, who have all desires, and this person comes to separate himself from all of them and accept repentance; therefore they were not stringent with him, and we establish that he certainly made complete repentance. At first glance these two laws contradict one another. It appears to me that here in Even HaEzer the case is where it is known that he did not return in complete repentance, such as where they saw that he still treated prohibitions lightly and returned only from idolatry. In this case one may not rely on his testimony. In Choshen Mishpat, however, it speaks of an ordinary case, as implied by the phrase, we establish that he certainly made complete repentance. Since he wrote, we establish, this implies that ordinarily we establish him with a presumption of full fitness. He also concluded accordingly that he wrote only as halakhah and not for practical ruling, and for this he is not to be faulted at all.

Gloss: and the bet din need not, etc. The Chelkat Mechokek z"l challenges this, and his view is that according to the Maggid Mishneh the same applies here: where it is possible, they are obligated to send after him. The Beit Shmuel z"l explained that the case is where the second witness explains his testimony, and he even strained to explain the sugya of the children this way. But one who studies Mahari Weil will see explicitly that his case was that they testified in the name of Rabbi Aharon and Rabbi Moshel, how they told them that Rabbi David Ganz was killed, and no more. This certainly is not called explaining, for they did not say how they knew: whether he was killed before them, or whether he was found killed and they recognized him. One may doubt that perhaps they spoke about matters whose majority end in death, more than if one testifies, so-and-so died before me and I saw that he died. Nevertheless, he did not require asking Rabbi Aharon and Rabbi Moshel themselves. Necessarily, the Chelkat Mechokek understood correctly that the words of Mahari Weil z"l do not accord with the words of the Maggid Mishneh. In truth, Mahari Weil's proofs are very weak. He wrote that it is forced to say that all those cases speak where the original witnesses are unavailable; but certainly according to the Maggid Mishneh one must indeed say so, and this is not forced at all. He further brings proof from the first chapter of Makkot: wherever two stand and say, we testify about so-and-so that his verdict was finalized in such-and-such bet din, he is put to death. Why do we not say, let us send to the bet din and ask them? I do not understand this proof at all. He concludes: rather, we do not scrutinize after witnesses, and here too there is no difference. In my humble opinion the cases are very different. There, there are two witnesses about a clear matter in which there is no uncertainty. But in our case, according to the Rambam, even regarding the one who testifies before us, since it is possible we are concerned that he testified about matters whose majority end in death. Why, by Torah reasoning, should we not be concerned where it is possible to ask the first witness the cause of death? Moreover, it is likely that even the Rambam said that we ask only with one witness, not with two witnesses, as he distinguishes in the case of war; and in our case each one testified about what he heard from one person. Therefore it appears to me, at least to defend the Rama z"l, that since without this the Chelkat Mechokek was precise that the words of Mahari Weil apply only where the first witness is in another country. Although in my humble opinion one should not distinguish between another city and another country where it is possible, nevertheless it is not the Rama z"l's way to add extra words. One should also note that this gloss is not in its main place here, but should have appeared after seif 3. Therefore perhaps one may say that the matter did not emerge from Mahari Weil alone, but from the Ran on this seif in Shulchan Aruch. He wrote in Perek Shevuat HaEdut: we read in the Gemara that all agree regarding a witness of death that he is exempt where he said it to her and did not say it to bet din, for we learned, a woman who said, my husband died, may marry. Meaning: one witness who knows about a woman that her husband died, and she adjured him to testify so that she could collect her ketubah, is exempt, because he caused her no monetary loss. Once he told her outside bet din, she no longer needed his testimony, but could go to bet din and say, my husband died, etc.; see there. What he wrote, that she can say to bet din, my husband died, is necessarily imprecise. Heaven forbid that she should lie and say, I testify that my husband died, whose implication is that he died before her, for this is false. This is not only according to the Chelkat Mechokek above, who holds that even the woman herself is asked the cause of death; if so, if we ask her, what will she answer? Rather, even according to what I thought, that the woman herself is different in this regard, as above, still it does not appear that she is permitted to lie and use language implying that he died before her when he did not. All the more so may she not lie and say, I heard that he died, while not knowing from whom, for that is almost included in mere rumor. Rather, she must come to bet din and tell the truth: so-and-so told me that my husband died. The Ran's language is imprecise. Certainly the implication of Rav Pappa's language is that the informant was in the city, only outside bet din. If the law were that bet din must send after the first witness, what did her report in his name help? We would send after him, and she had properly adjured him. Rather, it implies that even in the city one need not send after him. Therefore the Rama z"l placed it here, for it emerges from this. If so, if what I thought above in the Rambam's view is correct, that the woman herself is stronger and one need not ask her the cause of death, because the presumption is that she investigates carefully and ordinarily does not testify based on matters whose majority end in death, it is also possible that the Rama z"l agrees that with testimony from one witness heard from another, there is an obligation to ask the first witness, if possible, the cause of death. Why should he be better than a witness who says, I saw that he died? But where the woman herself testifies by way of witness from witness, we rely on her careful investigation, assuming that she ordinarily questions the witness so that he is not testifying about matters whose majority end in death. In this way the placement here would be more fitting: it refers specifically to the woman's testimony from witness to witness. What he concludes, that we rely on witness-from-witness initially, is not a general rule for every witness-from-witness, but teaches that there is no concern that the woman is witness-from-witness while it is possible to ask the first witness, who is the actual witness. The concern regarding the cause of death is removed only because she is the woman herself. Nevertheless this still requires study, for in truth even the Ran's language may be pressed and explained as the Beit Shmuel explained, namely where the witness explained to her the clarified death, and she can say all this in his name. As law, certainly the words of the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel are correct together: wherever the second witness does not testify that he heard a clear matter from the first, and it is possible, and in my humble opinion even by letter from another country, there is an obligation to ask the first witness the cause of death. But if the second testifies in his name to a clear matter, then even if the first is in the city, there is no need to send after him, and we rely on witness-from-witness initially. See Lechem Mishneh and the Kuntres Iggun of Maharachash, who were astonished that the Rambam did not copy the above law of Rav Pappa. See also Responsa Pnei Moshe part 2 siman 113.
