And he remained there long enough for his soul to depart. See Be'er HaGolah, who cites in the name of the Rivash that the implied measure is two hours. The words of Maharit that he cites are astonishing, for according to him each person's law is in his own hand. See also Responsa Bach, no. 79, who wrote that since she stood near the opening into which he had fallen for approximately an hour and saw that at the place where he drowned, etc., she therefore stood and waited more than long enough for his soul to depart, and we permit his wife to marry. This requires study: from where did he derive a measure of one hour? It appears that he too estimated according to his own judgment. As for the incident of R. Hanina b. Dosa, from which the above responsum learned a measure of two hours, he was forced to explain it differently; see there. In practice, therefore, the matter requires study.

He found him killed, etc.; some say that we attribute. The gaon Noda BiYehudah wrote in siman 28, s.v. furthermore it appears to me, that one should wonder about the incident of the Diglat, where the Gemara states as a novelty that she married after five days, and asks, according to whom did he act? See there. The difficulty is: how is this after five days case understood? If they saw that this woman's husband drowned, and after five days they found someone in the Diglat and recognized him as this woman's husband, what does it matter that it was after five days? Whichever way you look at it, she is permitted. If the drowned man did not come up again, then he remained there longer than the time required for his soul to depart. Rather, we must be concerned that he came up. If so, I say: if we had not known anything about drowning, and we found in the water a man recognizable by visual identification as this woman's husband, she would be permitted even if the water did not constrict the face, for according to Rabbenu Tam we attribute that it was within three days. If so, if someone drowned before us and then came up alive before us, and afterward it happened that they found in the water a man whose visual identification was like the person who drowned and came up before us, his wife would be permitted. For although he came up then, nevertheless since he is now recognizable by visual identification as so-and-so, we say that he drowned again and died in the water. If so, in the Diglat case, where you say it was after five days and her husband drowned, even if we say that he came up, nevertheless since he is recognizable by visual identification as this man, we say he drowned again and not yet three days have passed, and visual identification properly applies. And if you say that it was after five days because this person drowned five days earlier and had been lying there since then, then it is obvious that she is permitted. See Shulchan Aruch seif 27 and examine carefully. Based on this, he explained that the case was where there were already witnesses by signs that the one who had drowned was the one who was brought up, but they did not know whether he was this woman's husband, and the shushvin recognized by visual identification that he was her husband, though he did not know from the sign. In my humble opinion, this is a remote explanation. As the incident is stated, there is no difficulty at all; the essential element needed for the difficulty is missing. It is also difficult: from where did he know to ask this? Perhaps it was simply as stated. Rather, in my humble opinion, what he wrote, that it should not be better than if we saw with our own eyes that he came up, is certainly better. If we saw that he came up and now someone is found and recognized by visual identification, we ordinarily say that he drowned again within three days and that the identification is true. But where this person drowned five days ago, although if he were not found we would be stringent and be concerned for the minority possibility that he came up, why should we say this leniently by Torah law, when the majority do not come up? If so, when he is found and recognized, we say: how is it possible that this identification is true and he is the person who drowned five days ago? After three days there is no recognition. Rather, this identification is imaginary and false, and there is no testimony, similar to Bekhorot 28b regarding a practical difference to contradict witnesses. What remains is the remote concern that perhaps he came up; but one cannot say that through this testimony it is clarified that he did not come up, for if he did not come up how can they identify him after three days? To say that their identification is true is impossible unless he came up and then drowned again, and for leniency we do not say that he came up. Examine carefully. In the Kuntres Iggun of Maharachash, folio 64, it is stated that the custom is to be lenient like the first opinion, and the second opinion is the view of the Rashba, who was uncertain, as stated in the Beit Yosef. Without doubt those who practice leniency have their reasons. To strengthen the matter I will add support for the custom and say that the Rashba z"l is properly uncertain according to his own view, but according to us there is proof for leniency. It is also stated in the above Kuntres Iggun, folio 45b, in the name of a responsum of the Ran, that what the Rambam wrote, if he remained after being cast from the water onto dry land and became swollen, does not come to be lenient by requiring that he certainly became swollen. Rather, he means that ordinarily, if he remained there, he certainly became swollen even if it is not noticeable. He wrote that according to the Rambam's words it appears that the Rambam is stringent because he holds that he certainly became swollen, whereas the Rashba wrote only that perhaps he became swollen. Perhaps in this they follow their own views, for it is stated in the Shach, Yoreh De'ah siman 110, in the name of Responsa Rashba, that in a rabbinic doubt where a prohibition has an established presumption, we do not rule leniently; whereas Pri Chadash and Minchat Yaakov ruled leniently on the basis of the Rambam. Therefore the Rambam had a difficulty: since he already drowned, she is permitted by Torah law, and only a rabbinic prohibition remains, so why should we be concerned that perhaps he became swollen? He was forced to explain that he certainly became swollen. The Rashba does not need this, because in any case she remains in the presumption of a married woman. It appears at first glance that since all the poskim equate after three days on dry land with remaining after being brought up from the water, according to the Rambam, after three days on dry land he too certainly became swollen, unlike the implication of the other poskim that it is only a doubt. The reason he did not explain this regarding dry land is because it was not needed for the above. However, according to his view, the difficulty is: what does the Gemara ask in the Diglat case, according to whom did they act? Perhaps in truth the reason that one does not testify after three days is only because of doubt; but with a drowned person, where the remaining prohibition is rabbinic, we rule leniently. One must say that the Talmud holds that necessarily the reason is that after three days he certainly changes. For if it were only a doubt, even in a Torah matter it would not be proper to suspect perhaps he became swollen and thereby establish the witnesses as having testified by imagination, especially where there are two witnesses. On the contrary, reason dictates that the testimony of the witnesses clarifies for us the doubt that he did not become swollen. Rather, after three days he certainly became swollen, and the question is sound. It also appears that although the dispute of Tosafot and Rashba concerns the meaning of the Mishnah, nevertheless the main conceptual dispute depends on this: according to Tosafot, the testimony removes us from the doubt of after three days, and we ordinarily say it was within three days; according to the Rashba, because of the doubt we are concerned and establish the witnesses as testifying by imagination. If so, the Rashba, following his view, can remain uncertain, and nevertheless the Gemara's question is proper. But according to the Rambam, the above difficulty arises, and one must say as above: he plainly holds that because of doubt we do not establish witnesses as testifying by imagination. From this he proved that after three days he certainly became swollen. Consequently, in a doubt whether it was within three days or after three days, we also do not establish the witnesses as testifying by imagination, and we say it was within three days. If so, according to the agreement of Pri Chadash and Minchat Yaakov with the Rambam, there is room to be lenient.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>From the knee and above, we allow his wife to remarry.</b> The first clause is stated in order to infer that at the knee itself we do not allow his wife to remarry. Although the Mishnah also says that from the knee and below we do not allow her to remarry, from which one could infer the opposite, since the inferences contradict one another we must rule stringently. This is not considered two contradictory stringencies, as is evident from what we rule in Yoreh De'ah 55, that if an animal was cut at the knee it is a treifah. So the Rosh z"l answered his son in responsa, klal 20 siman 16, in these words: there is no known decisive ruling here, but because of doubt we act stringently in every place. However, although in Yoreh De'ah we also have a doubt based on the dispute between Rashi and the Rif whether the lower or upper knee is meant, that is only with an animal, for in the Gemara we find that it has two knees; but with a human being there is no doubt. The knee is above the shin, the place between the shin and the thigh bone, as explained in the first chapter of Ohalot, and it is called in the vernacular kenia; a person has only one. What certainly requires explanation is what is called at the knee and what is above the knee. According to what the Shulchan Aruch and Rama adopted, based on Rivach, that within the knee means only between the ankle-joint and the shin, everything below that is from the knee and below; if so, here too above the knee means only within the thigh bone itself. But according to the view of the Shach and Pri Chadash, who accepted as primary the Rosh's explanation in responsum that the ankle-bones are called the knee, and Pri Chadash writes explicitly that according to this the main view is the Rosh's first explanation, so if it was cut between the lower joint and the ankle-bone it is kosher. It is clear that in his view the Mishnah's phrase from the knee and above is as though it said from the ankle-bone and above is treifah, and from the ankle-bone and below is kosher. If so, between the lower joint and the ankle-bone is certainly included in from the knee and below, and consequently cutting in the upper joint between the ankle-bone and the shin is certainly treifah; the doubt is only if it was cut within the ankle-bone. It would then emerge here leniently that if the foot is found with the ankle-bones attached to it, since in any event it was cut above the ankle-bones, we allow his wife to remarry. In my humble opinion this is not so, for two reasons. First, Pri Chadash rejected the words of Mahariva, but in my view they should at least not be rejected. The Shach wrote: in truth it is proven from the Rosh in his rulings that if it was cut within the ankle-bone it is treifah, but he does not explain how this is proven. Pri Chadash explains the Rosh's words: in the first path, where he derived the law from the inferences, he certainly discusses the ankle-bones and calls them the knee, as he does in the responsum; in the second path, where he derives it from the interpretation that the lower bone is called the knee, he is even more stringent, so that even if it was cut within the joint between the lower bone and the ankle-bones it is also treifah. I say that this cannot be attributed to the Rosh's intent. If so, according to the second path the difficulty of Tosafot there certainly applies: what does the Gemara ask from the case where the tzomet hagidin was removed? According to this interpretation, it is explicit in the Mishnah that immediately above the lower bone it is treifah, and there no one holds that there is tzomet hagidin. According to his explanation in the responsum, that he discusses the body of the ankle-bones, one cannot raise this difficulty as Yam Shel Shlomo does. Although it is not reasonable that Ulla holds like the opinion that the ankle-bone itself is the place, but rather like what we rule, the place where the butcher exposes it, meaning from the beginning at the end of the ankle-bone, and although according to the inference from the latter clause we rule that wherever it was cut within the ankle-bone it is treifah, which is below the tzomet hagidin, nevertheless they properly rejected his question, because that is not heard at all from the phrase from the knee and above, which indicates only above the ankle-bone, where the tzomet hagidin is present. Thus the Gemara properly asks: since it taught that if the tzomet hagidin was removed it is treifah, why do I need from the knee and above, as Rashi explained; that is the main question. But according to the second path the difficulty surely applies, and in my view there is no one who can untie it. Necessarily, according to that path the entire ankle-bone area is considered the head of the lower bone, as the Taz wrote in the name of Hagahot Issur VeHeter, and the discussion is only about the connective joint between the ankle-bones and the shin. According to this, it is proven that within the ankle-bone itself he holds it is kosher, for according to that explanation they are called the lower knee like the lower bone itself. If so, if in the first path he followed his way in the responsum and called the ankle-bone the knee, why did he write further to support the main law? On the contrary, according to the first path within the ankle-bone is treifah, while according to the second path it is kosher. Rather, the ruling and his responsum are not identical. In the rulings, in the first path, he called the connective joint between the ankle-bone and the shin the knee, and about it he ruled treifah from the inferences; in the second path too he ruled that same place treifah because he called the lower bone together with the ankle-bones the knee. In any case, according to his rulings, within the ankle-bone itself is kosher. On this Rivach relied, unlike the responsum, because the rulings are primary. Consequently here as well we do not allow the wife to remarry. Second, even according to Pri Chadash's interpretation and the responsum, I am not satisfied. Although the responsum once says that Rav Pappa and Ulla call the knee the small bones between the middle bone and the third, which sounds like Pri Chadash's understanding that the Mishnah's knee is the ankle-bone, this is impossible. First, the proof itself that he brings for this interpretation from Ohalot, which lists five bones in the knee, indicates only that knee is the general name of the place where the five bones lie, not that those bones themselves are called the knee. Further, immediately after that language he writes that anything that bends and kneels is called berekh and knee; thus he calls the entire bending place the knee, not only the bones. He further wrote that according to all views, the joints connecting the bones are called knee. In my humble opinion he means that the whole place between the lower bone and the shin is included in the name knee, with its bones, because that whole place bends and kneels. He came only to exclude what his son had thought, which is the second path in the rulings, where he began by writing: know, my son, that the lower bone is not called the knee. On this he proceeds throughout; but every place above the lower bone until the head of the shin, together with the bones, is called the knee. In all these places there is doubt: the joint below the ankle-bone, the ankle-bone itself, and the joint above the ankle-bone, and because of doubt it is treifah. This is also proven from what he answered his son above, to be stringent here too because of doubt. According to Pri Chadash, this is a remote circumstance, that the leg would be found cut in the middle of the ankle-bone; if the ankle-bone is attached to the foot, she may remarry, and if not, she certainly may not. It is forced to say that he discusses finding a foot with some ankle-bones attached and some missing, and likewise in this very responsum, where he writes regarding the question if the middle bone was separated from the lower bone: this is the doubt whether its legs were cut between the joints, and not merely separated, meaning with skin and flesh covering. For it is no worse than the whole middle bone, where if skin and flesh cover it, it is kosher; but if it was separated and not covered, it is treifah, and that too is because of doubt. According to Pri Chadash one would again have to press that he means it separated in the middle of the ankle-bones, with some attached to the lower bone and some to the upper. This certainly is not the implication of the language. The main interpretation in my humble opinion is as I explained. In my view, even Hagahot Issur VeHeter cited by the Taz, in the language that the connective joint is called the knee, means that the entire place between the lower bone and the shin is called the connective joint, which in Hebrew is perek, not as the Taz understood, specifically the joint between the ankle-bone and the upper bone; this still requires checking in the text of the book, which I cannot do. This is also the intent of the Tur for one who reads his language carefully, unlike the Pri Chadash's pressed explanation; see and examine carefully. If so, with an animal one certainly rules treifah even within the joint between the lower bone and the ankle-bone, based on his words in the responsum. Here, however, we allow the wife to remarry only when the cut is actually within the shin. Do not say that if the foot was found with the ankle-bones attached, then in any event the tzomet hagidin was severed, since we rule at the beginning of Yoreh De'ah 56 like the opinion that the place of the tzomet hagidin is above the ankle-bone, and Rashi explains that this measure is the lowest of them all, and if it was cut between the middle bone and that ankle-bone it is treifah; if so, here we should allow the wife to remarry. This is not so. First, there too we are stringent only because of doubt, as explained in Rashi there, and consequently here we must be stringent in the opposite direction, like the opinion that it is at the thickening and upward. Further, in my poverty I do not know whether in a human being the tzomet hagidin is in that place of the knee, for in an animal it is at the end of the shin bone, which is the lower knee, whereas here we are discussing what in an animal is called the upper knee, namely the end of the thigh bone, as explained above. Know that the language of Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh De'ah there, end of seif 1, where it says: and likewise if it was cut within the joint between the ankle-bone and the shin it is treifah according to this opinion, requires study according to Rashi's explanation, for if it was cut in that place without this reason it is treifah according to all because of severing the tzomet hagidin. What then is meant by according to this opinion? Necessarily, cut is imprecise; it means dislocated, with the tzomet hagidin attached to the ankle-bone and no skin and flesh covering, and he also follows the view of the responsum of the Rosh cited in the gloss there, seif 3. Examine carefully.

One witness said, etc.; if he said, I buried him. From this the Maggid Mishneh inferred that the Rambam does not agree with the Rif to require, I buried him, even with two witnesses. But it is difficult for me according to how the Kesef Mishneh and the Chelkat Mechokek explain that what he wrote, or he drowned in the great sea and died, means that he remained there long enough for his soul to depart. First, why did he change the language, writing here, and died, whereas in seif 34 he wrote, and did not emerge? Further, regarding seif 34 one can say that he mentioned one witness as a novelty, that even so she may not marry ab initio; but here, how can he include it together with war, or he drowned in the great sea and died? Regarding war, he precisely says one witness to teach that with two witnesses, I buried him is not required. But regarding drowning in the great sea, if two witnesses said he drowned and died, which according to their explanation means he remained there, would she be permitted? Therefore it appears to me that the word died means: I saw him after he came up, lying dead. With two witnesses she is permitted; but with one witness there is concern that he is testifying about something whose majority ends in death, yet perhaps he was alive and the water confused him. This is like war, where he must say, I buried him; that is, according to the Rivash's explanation, they brought him up before us, which is like I buried him, or actual burial. If he says, I buried him, he is believed. For the poskim who are not concerned for approximation through mistaken substitution, but only for approximation whether he died or did not die, which is the method of the Mordekhai in the name of Ri and of Yam Shel Shlomo, here if they saw him immediately after he was brought up, so that there is no longer concern for swelling, then saying I buried him suffices to remove the concern that perhaps he did not die. According to the Rosh's method, however, here where he also testifies about the drowning and knows of it, the recognition must be joined with an intermediate sign. So too according to his method, even in war with one witness, even within three days, if he says, I buried him, based on recognition, saying that he recognized him after the war, signs must be joined because of the concern that perhaps he speaks by approximation: since he relies on himself that all those others were killed and he escaped, he says by approximation, this one who was in the war is the one found dead. So it is explicit in Shiltei Gibborim at the beginning of the chapter, and it is in Darkei Moshe at the end of s.k. 38 regarding a woman who testifies about her husband in war; he learned war from waters that have no end. The same applies according to the Rosh's method regarding one witness. As for what Shiltei Gibborim there on the second side rules in this language, and likewise if the witness testified that her husband drowned in water and they brought him up from there dead, and the witness recognized him, he is believed, even though he did not recognize him by signs but by visual recognition, for the witness does not speak in error, that is because he rules like the Rif, Ramban, and Yam Shel Shlomo, that with one witness the question was resolved that if he said, I buried him, he is believed. But according to the method of the Baal HaMaor and the Rosh, where if he knew of the drowning, signs are also required with one witness, the same applies in war. The words of the Chelkat Mechokek are brief, and the words of the Beit Shmuel require study. He wrote: if he drowned in water, etc., he must have a sign in him, which implies that he wishes to exclude war, where I buried him alone suffices. This is not so, for war and water have one law, as explained in Shiltei Gibborim and as appears to one who examines the sugya. Also, what he wrote about waters that have no end is not precise, for the same applies to waters that have an end where he did not wait long enough for his soul to depart; according to the Rosh's method, he too must reach solid recognition with an intermediate sign, because of concern for approximation through mistaken substitution, and to I buried him because of concern for approximation that perhaps he did not die. Gloss: "Or even if he merely drowned, but they did not testify that he remained in the water long enough for his soul to depart." We learned this point from the teaching of Moshe Rabbenu, namely Ramban of blessed memory. In truth, however, it requires study what benefit this waiting accomplishes, for one must still suspect that he emerged within that period at a place beyond the observer's sight; and if so, even if she married she should have to leave, as in the case of waters that have an end where he did not wait. Rather, one must say that emerging from waters that have no end is not common, and therefore she need not leave. But where he saw this person fall into the sea, perhaps no waiting is needed, for the reason just stated. One must then press the matter greatly: certainly it is unlikely that he swam underwater to a distance beyond the observer's sight and emerged there, and therefore if she married she need not leave; but at the place of the fall it is not so unlikely that he might surface immediately, and therefore one must wait the above measure. But with disqualified witnesses, etc. In Mahari Weil, no. 164, he raised a difficulty against Terumat HaDeshen, who held that Maharam treated the "detestable" person only as rabbinically disqualified and therefore valid for testimony permitting a woman. If so, from where did Maharam know that we follow the majority of opinions, namely three gentiles speaking incidentally against the Jews? Perhaps we follow the majority to permit only where those who permit are disqualified in the same way as those who prohibit, such as two gentiles saying he died and one saying he did not die, or two women and one woman. But in Maharam's case, if this person is fit to testify by Torah law, from where do we know that we follow three gentiles leniently, when they are Torah-disqualified, as the language of the Gemara implies: "R. Nechemiah says: wherever the Torah believed one witness, follow the majority of opinions, and they treated two women like one woman," etc. It mentions only cases where their disqualification is equal. In my humble opinion, the unqualified language of all the decisors implies that they did not descend to such a distinction. The language of Shulchan Aruch, se'if 37, which is Rambam's language, also seems unlike him, for it says: "When does this apply? When two women or rabbinically disqualified witnesses came and said he did not die, etc.; but if a valid witness says he died and many women or disqualified witnesses say he did not die, this is like half against half." This law derives from the answer: "Wherever a valid witness came first, even one hundred women are considered like one witness." According to Mahari Weil this too is difficult: let us say that only women, who are Torah-disqualified, are one hundred like one, but rabbinically disqualified witnesses, since by Torah law they are valid and for testimony concerning a woman they are valid even rabbinically, should count as one against two and not as half against half. Rather, it seems that the text did not insist specifically on "women," but used the Gemara's expression "disqualified witnesses," meaning that all disqualified witnesses who are valid for testimony concerning a woman are equal; and the same applies to minority and majority: since both are disqualified witnesses, even though one is Torah-disqualified and the other rabbinically disqualified, they are equal and we follow the majority. This requires study.

Even so, where she claims certainty, the children are valid; see there. It must therefore rest on another reason, and if so perhaps she too is always fit for the priesthood. This requires study.

{Rama: We do not remove her, etc.} This law appears in Yevamot 92: Rav Ashi said, we are not concerned for a rumor. Rashi explains: if she married by permission of bet din and afterward a rumor emerged that her husband is alive, and he is not before us. The Gemara asks, what rumor is meant? If it is after marriage, Rav Ashi already said this once. It answers: lest you say that since she came to bet din and we permitted her, it is like a rumor that preceded marriage and she should be forbidden, he teaches us otherwise. From this we hear that if she did not marry, but they merely permitted her on the basis of one witness, and afterward a rumor emerged that he is alive, she leaves her permitted status and is forbidden to marry; and if nevertheless she married, she must leave. For if not, and even for this matter she would not lose her Torah permission and we would not be concerned for the rumor, then Rav Ashi would have taught us a much greater novelty. So too it appears from the fact that he stated his law only on the Mishnah that teaches, she must leave, in the case where her husband came, meaning after she married. For this reason the sugya also did not establish the case as a rumor that preceded marriage, after permission but before she married, where she would not have to leave; necessarily, this is because in truth in such a case she must leave. Although in Mahari Weil siman 81, regarding one witness who testified to a widow that she was not bound to yibbum because he saw the daughter of her husband alive, he is believed against the rumor that emerged that the daughter died in her father's lifetime, and he brings proof from Ketubot 26, where the Gemara speaks of one who was presumed by us to have a father who was a kohen and a rumor emerged about him that he was the son of a divorced woman, etc., and concludes that a single witness is believed by Torah law against everything, end quote. There the case is different, as explained there in Tosafot, s.v. ve-ata: since the witness supports the presumption, one witness together with a presumption is stronger than the rumor. But where the presumption supports the rumor, the rumor is stronger than one witness. If so, however, it is difficult: what did the Gemara ask, what does this teach us? There it is different, as Rashi explains, for it speaks of a rumor about a woman married to a kohen that she is divorced or a zonah, a rumor that weakens a presumption; whereas here the presumption supports the rumor, so I might have thought that even after marriage we should be concerned. One may answer with difficulty that the question is from the language, every rumor, which sounds inclusive, and the answerer says that one might have thought this is not in the category of a rumor after marriage. Or perhaps here, even when she married, the presumption also does not support the rumor, as Tosafot wrote in the sugyot of Ketubot concerning two against two and in Yevamot 88, that the presumption that a woman investigates carefully weakens the presumption of being a married woman whose husband is alive. Nevertheless, it is not comparable: there the rumor opposes the presumption, while here at least it is not against it, for in any case Tosafot wrote that one who has relations with her brings an asham because the prohibition is considered established through the presumption of married status. Therefore I was forced to answer with difficulty as above. I am astonished at all our early authorities who omitted this statement of Rav Ashi, although the sugya concludes that it is a novelty and is not learned from Rav Ashi's general rule.

Although we do not rule this way, that is because the Mishnah distinguishes between a Jew and a gentile only where he intends to testify; this implies that where he does not intend to testify, he is equal to a Jew. But regarding a woman, where we find no more than this language, and she came and said, my husband died, she is believed, whose plain meaning is that he died before my eyes, there is room to doubt whether she is believed through identification by visual recognition. Perhaps, because she is anxious to free herself from being an agunah, we are concerned that she will testify by approximation. Although my answer is not compelled, for my question can be settled simply, as explained in the Kuntres Agunot of Maharachash, that regarding concern for lying, women are certainly preferable, while regarding approximation, one who speaks innocently is worse; nevertheless his law is certainly true and we do not examine further, etc. So it appears from the plain words of most poskim, and so it appears from Shiltei Gibborim that I will bring in seif 48. One can also infer this somewhat from the language of the Gemara on 116: Rav Shimi said, because she says by approximation. The plain implication is that in peacetime we are not concerned at all for her regarding any concerns of approximation, and this is the simple understanding.

שמא משנשאה וכו' ע' בהה"מ פי' הירושלמי שמביא ולדבריו ע"כ פי' ל' זה הכי דלהכי לא מוקמינן הדין שבתר שנשאת המעידה שתנשאה צרה ואין אומרים כו' כי אלו כן הי' החשש שמא משנאתה כו' משא"כ עתה שיודעת שאין בדבריה לגבי חבירתה כלום אף היא אומרת אמת וכ' הב"ש דאם קיבלה הצרה קדושין תלי' בס' התו' אם אכלה בתרומה דאוריי' ולע"ד בל"ז היא ס' מקודשת מכח השגות הראב"ד בה' תרומות שאינו גורס דהלכה כר"ט ותו צ"ע דמפי' רש"י ד"ה צערה דגופה שכ' ואפילו שהיא נשאת תמות נפשה כו' נראה שמפרש דינא אף בתר שנשאת אכלה הצרה בתרומה ולפי' הירושלמי זה תימ' שהרי מעתה שיודעת שאין בדבריה כלום אף היא אומרת אמת ואיך תאכל הצרה בתרומה דהא כל שלא נשאת הצרה אינו כ"א מדרבנן כדמשני דא"נ חזרה לקלקולא הראשון אבל מעיקרא דדינא ידעינן דמת ומל' הרמ' בה"ת משמע דאינו מעתיק דינא דר"ט בההיא חידוש דאף בתר שנשאת המעידה דתאכל ואולם ל' המשנה ודאי משמע כפירש"י וצ"ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
Perhaps after she married, etc. See Maggid Mishneh's explanation of the Yerushalmi that he cites. According to his words, the phrase must be explained as follows: for this reason we do not establish the law as a case where, after the testifying woman married, her rival-wife may marry, and we do not say, etc. For if so, the concern would be "perhaps because she hated her," etc. By contrast, now that she knows her words have no effect regarding her fellow woman, she too tells the truth. Beit Shmuel wrote that if the rival-wife accepted betrothal, the matter depends on the doubt of Tosafot whether she ate terumah by Torah law. In my humble opinion, even without this she is doubtfully betrothed because of Ra'avad's glosses in Hilkhot Terumot, since he does not read the text as ruling in accordance with R. Tarfon. Further study is needed: from Rashi's explanation, s.v. "the pain of her own body," where he wrote "even though she married, may her soul die," etc., it appears that he explains the law as applying even after the testifying woman married, so that the rival-wife may eat terumah. According to this explanation of the Yerushalmi, that is astonishing: since from now on she knows that her words are of no effect, she too tells the truth; how then may the rival-wife eat terumah? For as long as the rival-wife has not married, the concern is only rabbinic, as the Gemara answers, lest the matter return to its original ruin; but at the root of the law we know that he died. From Rambam's language in Hilkhot Terumot it sounds that he does not copy R. Tarfon's law in that novel respect, namely that even after the testifying woman married the rival-wife may eat. However, the language of the Mishnah certainly sounds like Rashi's explanation, and the matter requires study.

זאת אומר' כו' צרת' מכחישה ואומר' ל"מ ה"ז תנש' והצרה ל"ה ואפי' רוצה לחזור ולומ' מת עם אמתל' דלהכי אמר' ל"מ לעגן הצרה מ"מ ל"ת תו' ד"ה סד"א וכתבו ול"ד לאשה האומרת א"א אני דנאמנת לחזור ולומר פנויה אני ע"י אמתלא ור"ל דלהכי ל"ד דשם א"צ האמתלא אלא לעקור דבריה הראשונים וממילא שרי' משא"כ בזו שאף אחרי עקירות דבריה אינה נתרת בעדות צרתה אלא ע"י עדותה באחרונה וזה מהיפך להיפך א"נ:
**** ENGLISH ****
This one says, etc., and her rival contradicts her and says, he did not die. This one may marry and the rival may not. Even if the rival wants to retract and say that he died, with an explanation that she said he did not die only to leave the rival an agunah, nevertheless she may not leave. Tosafot, s.v. Salka da’atach amina, wrote that this is not comparable to a woman who says, I am married, who is believed to retract and say, I am unmarried, with an explanation. Meaning, it is not comparable because there the explanation is needed only to uproot her first words, and she is automatically permitted; but here, even after uprooting her words, she is not permitted by her rival’s testimony, but only through her own later testimony, and this is a reversal from one extreme to the other, so she is not believed.

**** ENGLISH ****
And they contradicted her. The Beit Shmuel wrote: in our time, etc. Although this is obvious to him, in my humble opinion the implication of the Gemara is not so. For were it not for the question, let us believe her from the rule of Rav Hamnuna, it implies that if the Gemara had held that she is not believed, it would not have been difficult at all. It would have been satisfactory that with such a quarrel, once she said, you divorced me, which is not what all women say, she is no longer believed to testify, even though she has not been established as lying. The same would follow for us as well. And where he apostasized, too, she has not been established as lying, and nevertheless the Rama was concerned because it is a great quarrel. With this, what the Chelkat Mechokek wrote on the law of an apostate is resolved, that the sugya does not imply these distinctions; according to the above, it strongly implies that we require only a great quarrel. As for what the Maggid Mishneh also asks from the words of the Ran, I do not know what is difficult: one witness in a case of quarrel, if she married after the fact, she need not leave, whereas for the reason of famine, even after the fact she must leave; he took a reason sufficient even to remove her. All the more according to the Taz's view there is no difficulty, for he holds that only regarding her own testimony is an apostate comparable to a quarrel, but not regarding one witness, and she is permitted even ab initio to marry on his word. The Beit Shmuel did not copy it this way, and perhaps it is a scribal error, though the Taz's proofs require study; the Chelkat Mechokek's view appears not to distinguish. However, according to the Rosh in seif 2, that there is another case where she is not believed when she says, you divorced me, namely when she claims her ketubah, one may infer like the Beit Shmuel, since the Gemara did not establish the quarrel case of you divorced me that way, so that we would not mistakenly say that only where she was contradicted is she not believed. Rather, it implies that it teaches the truth: whenever she was not contradicted, even though she said, you divorced me, and she would not have been believed, nevertheless she is believed to say he died. But according to the other poskim, the implication is more as above. In my humble opinion, the very law of an apostate depends on this: if being contradicted is not specific, and the main matter depends only on a great quarrel, then one may say the same of an apostate, for ordinarily there is great quarrel and hatred. But if in the case of you divorced me she must specifically have been contradicted by witnesses, I wonder how an apostate can be compared to this. The Rama's language, where he concludes that this woman has been established as a liar, indicates the Beit Shmuel's view, and one may learn from it even leniently regarding an apostate. In any case, it appears to me that one may be lenient regarding an apostate if she says, I buried him, and the like; for according to the plain sugya this depends on the dispute of Rav Chisda and Rav Sheshet, and although we rule stringently because of doubt, nevertheless regarding an apostate there is a double doubt. This follows the method of the Sephardic sages who use a double doubt, and this itself requires study. Even according to the Re'em, that even for Rav Sheshet saying I buried him does not help, that is specifically because of his reason at the end, that she is established in this as lying; this does not apply to an apostate. And it is clear.
