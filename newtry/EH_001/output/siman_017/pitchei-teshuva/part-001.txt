<b>תופסין בה. </b> עבה"ט מ"ש בשם כנה"ג אם מתה אשה אחת תחת בעלה כו' ועיין בס' ברכי יוסף שחקר כה"ג לגבי בעל שמת היום מיתה ודאית ולמחר חיה כההיא דאמרו באגד' עיין פ"ק דמגיל' קם רבה ושחטי' לרבי זירא למחר בעי רחמי ואחייה אי כשמת ר' זירא הותרה אשתו לשוק וכי חיה למחר היה צריך לחזור ולקדשה או דילמא הא דמיתת הבעל מתרת דוקא כשנשאר מת אבל כשלא נקבר וחי ע"י נביא או חסיד איגלאי מלתא דאותה מיתה לא היתה כמות כל אדם לא פקעי קידושי קמאי ולא תפסי בה קדושי אינש דעלמא ובעלה כאשר חי מותר בה מיד. וכתב דלפום ריהטא יש ראיה מירושלמי גיטין פ' מי שאחזו הלכה ג' גבי מתנה מעכשיו אם לא באתי ומת בתוך הזמן מהו שתהא מותרת ר' יוסי אומר אסורה לינשא אני אומר נעשו לו נסים וחיה ע"ש ואף דהפוסקים לח חיישי כלל לירושל' הזה (עיין לקמן סימן קמ"ד ס"ב) היינו דלא חיישינן למעשה נסים מ"מ למדנו דאם אחר שמת נעשו נסים וחי אכתי אגוד' ביה אשתו זאת ולא מהני לה מה שמת ודאי כיון דלבסוף חי ויש להתיישב בדברי הירושלמי הזה והראשונים עכ"ד (עיין בנ"צ מ"ש בביאור דבריו בזה) . ומ"ש הבה"ט אשת צדוקי כו' עיין לעיל ס"ס ד' בהגה ובהתשובות שרמזתי שם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Kiddushin take effect in her.</b> See Baer Heitev, who wrote in the name of Knesset HaGedolah: if one woman died while under her husband, etc. See Sefer Birkei Yosef, who investigated a similar case regarding a husband who certainly died today and tomorrow came back to life, like the case mentioned in aggadah, see the first chapter of Megillah, where Rabbah rose and slaughtered Rabbi Zeira, and the next day he prayed for mercy and revived him. If, when Rabbi Zeira died, his wife became permitted to the marketplace, and when he came back to life the next day he had to betroth her again, or perhaps the husband's death permits only when he remains dead; but when he was not buried and came back to life through a prophet or saint, it is revealed that this death was not like the death of ordinary people, the first kiddushin did not lapse, kiddushin of any other man would not take effect in her, and when her husband lives he is immediately permitted to her. He wrote that at first glance there is proof from Yerushalmi Gittin, chapter Mi SheAchatzo, halachah 3, regarding one who gives a get from now if I do not come, and he died within the time: what is the law, is she permitted? Rabbi Yose says she is forbidden to marry; I say miracles may have been done for him and he lives. See there. Although the poskim are not concerned at all for this Yerushalmi, see below siman 144 seif 2, that is because we are not concerned in practice for miracles. Nevertheless, we learn that if after he died miracles were done for him and he lived, this wife is still bound to him, and the fact that he certainly died does not help her, since in the end he lived. One must consider the words of this Yerushalmi and the early authorities. These are his words. See Nechalat Tzvi, what he wrote in explanation of his words on this. What Baer Heitev wrote regarding the wife of a Sadducee, etc., see above at the end of siman 4 in the gloss and in the responsa I indicated there.

<b>גרשתני נאמנת. </b> עיין בתשובת מנחת עני סימן ס"ד שנשאל בהא דקיי"ל אשה שאמרה לבעלה בפניו גרשתני נאמנת משום חזקה דאינה מעיזה אם יש לחלק בזה בין למפרע ולהבא כמו דמחלקינן בבעל שאמר גרשתי את אשתי דנאמן רק להבא (ר"ל בגמרא דב"ב דף קל"ד ע"ב וע' לקמן סימן קנ"ב ס"א ובב"ש שם) או דילמא שאני התם דהנאמנות משום דבידו לגרשה וטעם זה ל"ש אלא לגבי להבא אבל הכא דהטעם הוא משום דאינה מעיזה י"ל דלהקדים הזמן ג"כ אינה מעיזה. והשיב דלכאורה קצת ראיה דמזק' זו דאינה מעיזה מועלת אפילו לגבי למפרע דאל"כ למ"ד בב"ב הבעל שאמר גרשתי למפרע דא"נ אפילו להבא דלא פלגינן דבוריה. וכי היכי דא"נ למפרע ה"ה להבא א"כ גם באשה שאמרה לבעלה בפניו גרשתני למפרע נימא הכי דלא פ"ד ולא תהיה נאמנת כלל אפילו להב' וכל כי הא ה"ל לש"ס ופוסקים לפרושי. אולם לאחר העיון נראה דלמפרע אינה נאמנת והטעם פשט ומוכרח מלישנא דרב המנונא דקאמר חזקה אין אשה מעיזה פניה בפני בעלה מבואר דדוקא בפני בעלה אבל בפני שאינו בעלה יכולה להעיז ולפ"ז בשלמא בעיקר הגירושין דאי נימא שהיה משקרת ולא גירשה הרי הוא בעלה ובפני בעלה א"י להעיז מש"ה נאמנת אבל לענין הקדמת הזמן י"ל שפיר שהיה משקרת ולא גירשה בזמן שהיא אומרת רק עכשיו גירשה זה יום או יומים (ע' בס' מה"צ סק"ו) ואפילו תימא דזה חשיב ג"כ העזה להקדים הזמן מ"מ אין זה העזה בפני בעלה דהא עכ"פ עכשיו אינו בעלה וכאחר דמי. ובזה ניתחא ג"כ אפילו למ"ד בב"ב שם לא פ"ד הכא נאמנת שפיר לגבי להבא ולא אמרינן כי היכי דלגבי הקדמת הזמן למפרע אינה נאמנת ה"נ להבא דז"א דהא עיקר מה שא"נ למפרע אינו אלא משום דאמרינן עכשיו היא גרושה ואינו בעלה מש"ה יכולה להעיז א"כ איך תאמר שא"נ להבא דא"כ הרי הוא בעלה ואיך תוכל להעיז ואינו דומה כלל להא דב"ב בבעל שאמר גרשתי דהתם חסרון נאמנות דלמפרע אינו תלוי בנאמנות ולכך אמרינן שפיר לפ"ד וא"נ בשניהם אבל הכא עיקר מה שאינה נאמנת למפרע אינו אלא מכח נאמנות דלהב' כו' ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>You divorced me; she is believed.</b> See responsum Minchat Ani siman 64, where he was asked regarding what we rule, that a woman who says to her husband in his presence, you divorced me, is believed because of the presumption that she would not be so brazen: is there a distinction here between retroactive effect and future effect, as we distinguish regarding a husband who says, I divorced my wife, where he is believed only for the future? This refers to the Gemara in Bava Batra 134b; see below, siman 152 seif 1 and the Beit Shmuel there. Or perhaps there it is different, because the credibility is based on his power to divorce her, and that reason applies only to the future; but here the reason is that she would not be brazen, so one could say that she would also not be brazen to advance the date. He answered that at first glance there is some proof that this presumption, that she would not be brazen, helps even retroactively. Otherwise, according to the view in Bava Batra that when a husband says retroactively, I divorced her, he is not believed even for the future because we do not divide his statement, and just as he is not believed retroactively so too he is not believed for the future, then when a woman says to her husband in his presence, you divorced me retroactively, we should say the same: we do not divide her statement, and she should not be believed at all, even for the future. Something like this should have been explained by the Talmud and the poskim. However, after analysis it appears that she is not believed retroactively. The reason is simple and compelled by Rav Hamnuna's language, for he said: there is a presumption that a woman does not brazenly face her husband. This shows that it is specifically before her husband, but before one who is not her husband she can be brazen. According to this, regarding the essential divorce, if we say she is lying and he did not divorce her, then he is her husband, and she cannot be brazen before her husband; therefore she is believed. But regarding advancing the date, one may well say she is lying and he did not divorce her at the time she says, but rather only now, a day or two ago; see Sefer Me'orei Tzedek s.k. 6. Even if you say that advancing the date is also considered brazenness, nevertheless it is not brazenness before her husband, for in any case now he is not her husband and is like another man. With this it is also resolved that even according to the view in Bava Batra that we do not divide a statement, here she is properly believed for the future. We do not say that just as she is not believed retroactively regarding advancing the date, so too she should not be believed for the future. That is not so, for the main reason she is not believed retroactively is only because we say she is now divorced and he is not her husband, and therefore she can be brazen. If so, how can you say she is not believed for the future? For then he is her husband, and how could she be brazen? This is not comparable at all to the case in Bava Batra where the husband says, I divorced her, for there the lack of credibility retroactively does not depend on credibility for the future; therefore we properly say that we do not divide his statement and he is not believed in either respect. But here, the main reason she is not believed retroactively is only because of the credibility for the future, etc.; see there.

<b>Disqualified persons.</b> Noda BiYehudah siman 45 was asked about three witnesses who testified together in an agunah case, identifying the husband by bodily marks, and one was then found to be a thief, biblically disqualified because of wickedness even for testimony concerning a woman. The question was whether the two valid witnesses are invalidated by the rule that when a group testifies and one is found related or disqualified the whole testimony is void, and if the first testimony is void whether they may at least testify again. Noda BiYehudah answers that this rule does not apply here. Those disqualified for testimony concerning a woman are disqualified only because they are suspected of lying; if we knew that the particular statement was true, the witness would be accepted, as with incidental speech. Therefore the valid witnesses, who are not suspected of lying, are not invalidated by joining him, and they need not testify again.

R. Akiva Eiger siman 87 is unsure of this rule and cites Ran in the first chapter of Gittin as evidence that even in testimony concerning a woman the entire testimony may be void when one witness is invalid. In simanim 124-125 he raises the issue against the Gaon of Lissa, who had written simply like Noda BiYehudah, and then records the Gaon of Lissa's answer as correct and reasonable. Berit Avraham discusses it as well.

Yeshuot Yaakov s.k. 6 was asked whether the rule of "a complete matter, and not half a matter" invalidates testimony concerning a woman. Some contemporary scholars thought it did; he responds that this is no worse than relatives, who are biblically invalid for ordinary testimony but accepted in testimony concerning a woman. Only a disqualification rooted in suspicion of lying invalidates such testimony. Pitchei Teshuvah notes, however, that Responsa Mabit, as cited by Chatam Sofer, seems to support the stricter view, though he personally finds Yeshuot Yaakov more persuasive.

<b>Except for the five women.</b> See Ba'er Heitev, who writes that even a statement made incidentally in conversation is ineffective. Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 15 discusses this at length and likewise concludes that these women are not believed even when speaking incidentally; Birkei Yosef records that this is the consensus of most later authorities, and Yad HaMelekh infers from Shevut Yaakov that even a fully incidental statement, preceded by connected conversation, is not accepted. The reason is comparable to the rule concerning gentile courts below, seif 10: since they are personally connected to the matter, and these five women are presumed to lie because of their hostility, even incidental speech is not a sufficient ground for reliance. Their hatred is so strong that the Sages suspected a willingness to harm themselves while harming the other woman.

On this basis, Pitchei Teshuvah considers whether a yevamah and the related doubtful categories, who are believed to forbid her, might be believed to permit her when speaking incidentally. Machatzit HaShekel's wording, however, implies that even those women who are believed to forbid are not believed to permit by means of incidental speech, unless the phrase "to testify about her" means specifically to permit her. Beit Shmuel's proof that incidental speech is ineffective was already given in Shevut Yaakov and earlier in Responsa Ra'anach; Mishneh LaMelekh notes that this proof is decisive only with respect to a rival wife, leaving the other four categories still in question.

Ba'er Heitev's further discussion, drawn from Beit Shmuel, concerns a rival wife whose own later husband has died. Beit Shmuel suggests that she still may not testify about her former husband if her motive is to make the other rival forbidden to him as well. Pitchei Teshuvah explains that this applies where the witness remarried and the other woman remained in her place; but if the woman about whom she testifies had already left that household through divorce or death and married another man, earlier hostility is not suspected. Maharash disagrees and invalidates even after both women separated and remarried, while Machatzit HaShekel supports Beit Shmuel's distinction. Maharchash limits the concern for former hatred to rival wives; after separation, a mother-in-law and the other listed women are fit to testify.

Chelkat Mechokek cites Mahar'a that all five women are also not believed to forbid her. Machatzit HaShekel writes that a yevamah and similar unresolved cases are certainly believed to forbid, but that a sister who is also a yevamah is not believed even to forbid. Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 64 is also cited. Mishneh LaMelekh is unsure whether, when one of the five testified, the woman remains a definite married woman, so that one who betroths her would be forbidden to her relatives and a child born from adultery would be a definite mamzer, or whether the testimony creates doubt because perhaps it is true. He connects this to the dispute of Rambam and Ra'avad in the laws of terumah. The fact that they are not believed even to forbid her is not proof, because one who is not believed to permit is likewise not empowered to forbid.

Mishneh LaMelekh adds that where valid testimony has already removed the biblical prohibition, as when a qualified witness testified that the husband fell into endless waters and remained long enough that he would have died, and one of these five women later gives testimony sufficient for permission ab initio, perhaps she may be relied upon because women are suspected of corrupting matters before human courts but not before Heaven. Since, if she remarried, she would not be forced to leave, the hostile witness cannot fully ruin her; and although incidental speech by such women is generally ineffective, in a case already removed from biblical prohibition there may be room for leniency. The same may apply to the doubtful or disputed categories, but the matter still requires study.

<b>Whose presumption is that they hate.</b> See responsum Knesset Yechezkel at the end of siman 83, where he asked: if so, even one valid witness should not be believed if he is an enemy, since one witness is not believed by Torah law and the Sages were merely lenient; with an enemy they did not act leniently. <b>Now her mother-in-law.</b> See Ba'er Heitev and Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 18-20, who defends Chelkat Mechokek both regarding the first rule, where she is not fit for levirate marriage, and the second rule, in our countries where levirate marriage is not practiced. He concludes this as practical law, against Beit Shmuel, Avodat HaGershuni siman 67, and Tzemach Tzedek siman 88. Responsa R. Akiva Eiger siman 86 also upholds Chelkat Mechokek, and Berit Avraham and Chemdat Shlomo discuss the same matter.

Machatzit HaShekel cites Tzemach Tzedek, also brought by Beit Shmuel below in siman 158, that even according to Rif, Rambam, and Shulchan Arukh, who hold that a yevamah is believed to testify only where she is not fit for levirate marriage, the reason is that there is no hatred. Where hatred exists, she is not believed even if she cannot actually fall to levirate marriage. He then considers cases of a mother-in-law, a daughter of the mother-in-law, and other relatives after the concrete basis for hatred has ceased. If the woman married the son during the father's lifetime, then even after the father died without leaving assets, the mother-in-law may already have fixed hostility in her heart because she once feared that the daughter-in-law would consume her son's wealth. If, however, the son married her only after the father's death and the father died poor, there is no apparent cause for hatred, and similarly with the daughter of the mother-in-law where her brother married only after both parents died and inherited nothing. Still, perhaps the Sages made no distinctions within these categories. Such reasoning may support stringency, but not leniency without care.

And the daughter of her mother-in-law means even if she is not the daughter of her father-in-law, as is also explicit in Mishneh LaMelekh there. In the Talmud, 117, the question was asked: what is the law regarding the daughter of her father-in-law who is not the daughter of her mother-in-law? Is the reason for the mother-in-law's daughter because there is a mother who hates her, etc.? The question was not resolved. All the decisors omitted it. Even the Rosh, Tur, and Rama, who brought the unresolved question of the mother of her yavam and ruled stringently, nevertheless did not mention this unresolved question of the daughter of her father-in-law, which is very surprising.

In Kuntres Maharchash, p. 33, he wrote that although all the decisors omitted it, implying that their view is to be lenient, nevertheless it is proper to be stringent, like the view of Rabbenu Yerucham and the Maggid Mishneh. See Tosafot Yom Tov, Yevamot 15:4, who wrote that this is also the view of Rashi and Nimukei Yosef, since they used the wording "this one will inherit all the toil of my father and mother," carefully writing "the toil of my father" to say that it is not specifically her mother-in-law's daughter, but the same applies to her father-in-law's daughter who is not her mother-in-law's daughter. See Machatzit HaShekel, who wrote at length to give a good reason for the decisors who omitted it, but concluded that in practice it requires analysis to be lenient unless there is some additional ground.

Maharchash further wrote there, and it is also cited in the addenda to Machatzit HaShekel, that the daughter of her rival wife, the daughter of her yevamah, and the others may testify for her. Although their mother hates her, we are not concerned for that. When the Talmud was uncertain whether the reason for the mother-in-law's daughter is that there is a mother who hates her, this means because there are two causes: her mother provokes her, and that mother herself hates her. But where there is only the fact that the mother hates her, we are not concerned.

<b>And likewise she may not testify for them.</b> See Machatzit HaShekel, s.k. 24, who wrote the total of women presumed hostile who do not testify: sixteen. They are: her mother-in-law; her mother-in-law's daughter; her rival wife; her yevamah; her husband's daughter; her daughter-in-law; her brother's wife; her father's wife; her father-in-law's daughter; her brother's wife from the father but not from the mother; the mother of her yavam; the daughter of the mother of her yavam; the daughter of her yavam; the wife of her son's paternal brother; the wife of the brother's brother, where they are brothers from the father but not from the mother, even if she is a sister from the mother and not from the father; and the wife of her father's brother.

All this is explained according to the words of Rama above, that she too may not testify for them. If so, to the count of the five women mentioned above, another three are added: corresponding to her mother-in-law, her mother-in-law's daughter, and her husband's daughter, their inverses are added, namely her daughter-in-law, her brother's wife, and her father's wife. According to the view of Maharchash and Machatzit HaShekel above in s.k. 28, that one should be stringent regarding the unresolved question of the daughter of her father-in-law who is not the daughter of her mother-in-law, two more are added: her father-in-law's daughter and its inverse, her brother's wife from the father but not from the mother. According to the view of the Rosh, Tur, and Rama, who ruled stringently regarding the unresolved question of a future mother-in-law, two more are added: the mother of her yavam and its inverse, the wife of her son's paternal brother. According to Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, Tzemach Tzedek, and Beit Shmuel, whom I cited above in s.k. 27, the same applies to a future mother-in-law's daughter and a future husband's daughter, adding four more: the daughter of the mother of her yavam, the daughter of her yavam, and their inverses, the wife of the brother's brother, where they are brothers from the father but not from the mother even if she is a sister only from the mother, and the wife of her father's brother. In all, they are sixteen; the details of each law have already been mentioned above.
