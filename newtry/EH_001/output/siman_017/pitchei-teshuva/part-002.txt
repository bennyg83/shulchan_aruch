<b>That the witness said.</b> In Responsa Maharival, part 1 siman 1 and part 2 simanim 12 and 14, he wished to derive a leniency regarding water and war, where the witness must say "I buried him." If the witness did not say "I buried him" and is not before us, or if it is testimony from the mouth of another witness and the first witness is not before us, we may be lenient and assume that if the first witness were before us he would clarify how he knew with certainty that the man died, for example that he moved him from place to place, which is considered like "I buried him" as Rama wrote in seif 17, or the like; only at first he did not think it necessary to mention it. The same would apply where there is doubt whether enough time elapsed, and where there is doubt whether it was within three days: one may be more lenient with testimony heard from another witness than with the first witness himself. He supported this from Terumat HaDeshen's rulings, simanim 220 and 223. Some later authorities followed him in leniency. However, Responsa Raanach siman 65, cited in Responsa Maharit part 2 siman 31, and Maharit there and in part 1 siman 48, strongly rejected this reasoning. Kuntres HaRosh also objected to Maharival and cited Responsa Maharam Alashkar siman 25. Later he concludes in the name of Rashdam siman 51 that where the concern is rabbinic, such as endless waters and the like, one may rely on Maharival, unlike Responsa R. Betzalel Ashkenazi siman 21 and the Shach in siman 183, who followed Maharival even for a Torah concern. One who studies Maharival part 2 simanim 12 and 14 will see that he himself did not rely firmly on this reasoning and was unsure about it. See what I wrote above, seif 27 s.k. 123, in the name of Responsa Noda BiYehudah siman 48.<br />
(33) <b>But he said simply that he heard, etc.</b> See Mahaneh Efraim, s.k. 26, who wrote that this law comes from Responsa Binyamin Ze'ev, cited in Darkei Moshe, and it is clear that it concerns a case where he testified explicitly that he heard from another, but did not clarify from whom. In such a case we do not suspect that the first witness was invalid. But where he says only "I heard" and does not say "I heard from another," there is room to doubt that perhaps he heard only a rumor. See Knesset HaGedolah, siman 139, where one rabbi was unsure about this, while Beit Yosef in his responsum, cited there in siman 148, tends to be lenient even in this case, apparently only where the witness is not before us. See Responsa Chatam Sofer siman 70 concerning an agunah whose husband had wandered far away for eighteen years. One testified that while in Odessa with his son he spoke with a man from Bohuslav, who said that in the synagogue in Bohuslav he heard that the man in question, who had a son in Horbate, died in Bohuslav. Chatam Sofer answered that the Mishnah at the end of Yevamot explicitly permits remarriage on the basis of a heavenly voice, and all the more where one says he heard that so-and-so died, even if we do not know from whom he heard; we do not suspect the minority possibility that it came from an invalid source. However, this applies where he at least heard from a known person. A general rumor, like women's talk, is worse for two reasons: sometimes a sick man is near death and people spread a report that he died, though later he lives; and sometimes a person disappeared and people assume by estimation that he died. Yet in the case before him, all agree the woman is permitted, because the report was that people in the synagogue said R. Hirsch Kirshner died there in Bohuslav. If it were only a momentary rumor because he had fainted, why would they say he died in Bohuslav, "here in our community"? It clearly meant that he had died there earlier and one person was telling another. Nor is there concern that it was mere estimation because he had disappeared, for they said he died specifically in Bohuslav. Therefore the report is clear enough, and one who permits this agunah loses nothing. A court of three should sit and permit her. See also Responsa Mahari M. of Brisk siman 23 on a similar matter.

<b>בעת צרה. </b> עבה"ט מ"ש וס"ז מיקל בזה וכתב דוקא רשע גמור כו' וע' במה"צ ס"ק כ"ח שהאריך בזה והביא תשו' מהריב"ל ח"ג סי' ע"ה שהעלה דבעדות אשה א"צ שיהא תחילתו וסופו בכשרות (אפי' ברשע גמור ופלפל בדבריו ולבסוף הסכים עמו לדינא דלא גרע מישראל עוזב דת המסל"ת שבשעת ראיה היה פסול להעיד בתורת עדות ונמצא שלא היה תחילתן בכשרות וגם עתה אין שם תורת עדות שהרי פסול להעיד ואפ"ה מהני גבי עיגונא כיון דעכ"פ אין חשש שקר בדבר וכ"ש בזה שמעיד אחר ששב כו' ובק"א שם הביא שכן מסקנת הרח"ש ז"ל ע"ש ועמ"ש מענין זה בפ"ת לח"מ סי' ל"ד סכ"ט בד"ה חוזר לכשרותו ובסי' ל"ז סי"ח בד"ה שיסלק עצמו. וע' בס' בר"י אות ג' שכ' ג"כ דהסכמת האחרונים להקל דלא בעינן תחילתו וסופו בנשרות נמ"ש מהר"ם ן' חביב בעזרת נשים ס"ק כ"ג וכן הורה הרב כנה"ג ורבני דורו הלכה למעשה וכן הסכימו רבנן בתראי כמ"ש בס' יד אהרן ודלא כמהרי"קש בהגהותיו דנראה דנטה לאסור ע"ש וע' בתשו' דרכי נועם סי' ך"ב וך"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In a time of distress.</b> Ba'er Heitev cites the view that section 7 is lenient in this matter only with respect to a complete evildoer. Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 28 discusses the issue at length and cites Responsa Maharival III:75, which concludes that for testimony concerning a woman it is not necessary that the witness's beginning and end both be in fitness, even where he had been a complete evildoer. Machatzit HaShekel ultimately agrees: this is no worse than an apostate Jew speaking incidentally, who was disqualified as a witness at the time of seeing and still has no formal status as a witness, yet is effective for an agunah because there is no concern of falsehood. Certainly one who testifies after repenting should be acceptable. The addenda cite Maharchash to the same effect, and Pitchei Teshuvah refers to his discussion in Choshen Mishpat regarding one who returns to fitness and one who removes himself from an interest.

Birkei Yosef likewise writes that the later authorities agree leniently that beginning and end in fitness are not required, as Maharam ibn Chaviv wrote in Ezrat Nashim, and as Kenesset HaGedolah and the rabbis of his generation ruled in practice. Yad Aharon and other later rabbis agree, against Maharikash in his glosses, who appears to incline to prohibition. See also Responsa Darkei Noam simanim 22 and 24.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or it is known that they testified without deceit or distortion.</b> This language implies that even without incidental speech they are valid if the testimony is known to be honest. Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 30 questions this, since Maharai himself was unsure whether one who has not fully repented remains biblically disqualified unless he speaks incidentally, like a gentile; if so, what does absence of deceit help, and why should this differ from a gentile? Responsa Amudey Esh also challenges Rema on this point.

Noda BiYehudah, at the end of siman 36, interprets Rema in Yoreh De'ah 69:10 and develops a possible principle: testimony that is not incidental but is supported by strong circumstantial indications may be better than incidental speech without such indications. If a gentile speaking incidentally is believed in testimony concerning a woman even without circumstantial support, then certainly testimony with strong indications might be believed even without incidental speech. He explicitly warns, however, not to rely on this even as a mere additional factor. Pitchei Teshuvah notes that because Noda BiYehudah printed the idea, it seems he meant that a careful reader who finds it persuasive may use it as a supporting factor; indeed, in Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 47 he himself joins it as a supporting consideration.

This may sustain Rema's wording: "or it is known" refers to a case with strong circumstantial indications, which can help even without incidental speech. Rema did not say this earlier regarding a gentile because he would not rely on that consideration alone; here there is an additional factor, namely Maharai's doubt that perhaps the disqualification is only rabbinic and therefore valid for testimony concerning a woman. Chatam Sofer siman 46 later rejects relying on Noda BiYehudah's proposed leniency at all.

<b>But if the witness came.</b> Chelkat Mechokek explains that this means even if he came after she had been permitted to marry; if he came before permission, even a single other witness would prevent her from marrying. Kehillat Yaakov of Lissa disagrees and writes that after the court permitted her, the first witness is certainly not believed to retract. Shiltei Gibborim in the name of Or Zarua says the same, and the reason is that once she has been permitted she has a presumption of permissibility, and one witness is not believed against a presumption. Chelkat Mechokek's question from the case before permission is answered by the consequence that the children would be definite mamzerim rather than doubtful ones. Kehillat Yaakov finds it impossible that a witness could later undo the permission, especially after she remarried, for that would create ruin: the first witness could return after she had children and say he never testified, perhaps out of hatred.

Kehillat Yaakov therefore rules that after permission the witness is not believed, but before permission the main law follows Ramban, Ran, Rosh, and other authorities that the first witness is believed; even if the person whose statement she reported is disqualified, his denial is like saying, "I fabricated it," and for that there is no difference between valid and disqualified witnesses. Pitchei Teshuvah finds the first point difficult. Rambam, cited below in seif 38, holds that even one valid witness is believed against a woman after permission, and Ramban and Rashba agree regarding the woman herself, as Maggid Mishneh explains. Maharchash even says that if the witness came after she had actually remarried, she must leave, though he hesitates somewhat. If so, the case here should be no worse than another witness. Pitchei Teshuvah suggests that Chelkat Mechokek read the Shulchan Arukh there according to the simple wording, which equates the woman herself with another woman, but Kehillat Yaakov's decisive ruling is against the later authorities who do not rely leniently on the woman herself. R. Akiva Eiger siman 113 also accepts Chelkat Mechokek's interpretation, questions it from Rabbenu Yerucham, and discusses Ramban and Shiltei Gibborim at length.

<b>When she said in his name that he died.</b> See Ba'er Heitev in the name of the Chelkat Mechokek and Shevut Yaakov. At first glance, from what is before us in Knesset Yechezkel, siman 305, where the language of the Shevut Yaakov is copied, it is not at all explicit that a gentile may not contradict the woman; it says only that a gentile may not contradict a valid witness. If so, only the Chelkat Mechokek's second doubt is resolved, not his first. Still, since the Beit Shmuel and Machatzit HaShekel also write in his name that a gentile cannot contradict her, presumably it is written that way in the responsum itself, and it should be examined there. See Machatzit HaShekel, se'if katan 31, who writes about the Shevut Yaakov's words: "I do not know from where he derived this," and that by law it appears that the maidservant is believed to contradict her; see there. The Kehillat Yaakov agrees, as was cited above in se'if katan 44.

See also the responsa of my ancestor, Panim Me'irot, part 2, siman 116. He writes that the Chelkat Mechokek's doubt, where she said something in the name of a slave or maidservant and they then contradict her, may have been raised specifically for testimony permitting a woman to remarry, because in such testimony the Sages said that the witness is treated like two witnesses and relied on the woman's careful inquiry. But regarding other prohibitions one may say that he had no doubt: certainly she is established as having lied even by disqualified witnesses. Even if one says that his doubt applies everywhere, that is only where the person comes to testify and the Torah has believed him; where the obligation rests on him to clarify the matter, however, see there.

See also Responsa R. Akiva Eiger, in the addenda to siman 113. He first writes that the Chelkat Mechokek's wording, "in the name of a slave or maidservant," is not necessarily precise, and the same would apply to one who testifies in the name of a woman, for we do not find a distinction among disqualified witnesses between a woman and a slave or maidservant. He then writes that perhaps the wording is precise. The first doubt of the Chelkat Mechokek is difficult to understand: when she says it in the name of a slave or maidservant, both the woman and the maidservant are disqualified, so surely the first one should be believed. One should not entertain a doubt that perhaps the Ramban's rule applies only to a disqualified person testifying from the mouth of a valid witness, for from his phrase "and so is the law in every case of one witness from the mouth of another" it certainly follows that the rule applies even when both are equal.

If so, one must also analyze the Chelkat Mechokek's second doubt, where a valid witness testifies in the name of a slave or maidservant. Since the Ramban learned his rule from the passage in Ketubot about one who said, "So-and-so the sage purified this stain for me," and there it is a disqualified person reporting from a valid one, from where did the Ramban know that the first is believed even when both are equal? Necessarily he does not distinguish in this matter, because the reason is that the later testimony comes through the force of the first; therefore the first is believed, whether the reporting witness is valid or disqualified. If so, the same should apply to a valid witness reporting from a disqualified one. One must therefore say that the Chelkat Mechokek mentioned specifically a slave or maidservant because ordinary slaves and maidservants are morally loose and somewhat suspected of lying; about that he was unsure whether the first speaker is believed. His second doubt is also built on this: even if with a woman, who is also disqualified, she is believed to contradict the report, nevertheless with an actually valid witness one may say that a slave or maidservant is not believed. But where one testifies in the name of an ordinary woman, where there is no suspicion that she is lying and she is disqualified only by the Torah's decree, she is believed. This requires study; these are his words.

What Ba'er Heitev writes further, that if two witnesses say in the name of one person, etc., is also written by the Chelkat Mechokek and the Beit Shmuel. Below, se'if katan 123, he cites this in the name of the Ra'anach; see there further. R. Akiva Eiger writes there that this seems to apply to two valid witnesses. Although with respect to the root of the permission their testimony is not full testimony, for they testify only from the mouth of one person, and even if they testified from the mouth of two it would still be testimony from the mouth of testimony, nevertheless as to this point, namely that so-and-so said this to them, they are full witnesses that he said it to them. He is not believed to contradict them and say that he did not say it to them, for on this point it is one against two valid witnesses. Once their testimony that he said it to them has been established, we return to the rabbinic enactment that a woman may remarry on the basis of one witness from another witness. But if, on the essential foundation, they are disqualified and it is not full testimony that he said it to them, he is believed to contradict them, since they spoke only from his mouth; these are his words.

What Ba'er Heitev writes further, that if the first witness came and said that he told him falsely, etc., see the Chelkat Mechokek, whose words imply that even if they had already permitted her to remarry the law is so, for one cannot distinguish in this matter since, in the end, the first witness never testified in court. See Responsa R. Akiva Eiger there, who writes that at first glance the Chelkat Mechokek's words are unclear, for we see that the Shiltei Gibborim in chapter 15 of Yevamot cites the Riaz; however, after analysis it appears that the Chelkat Mechokek's words are correct; see there. Regarding R. Akiva Eiger's further difficulty with Maran the Beit Yosef in responsum siman 4, see what I wrote in Nachalat Tzvi.

See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 75. His view is unlike that of the Chelkat Mechokek, who took it as obvious that the first witness may say, "I fabricated it," even after the second witness has already testified in his name before the court. The Chatam Sofer writes that his own heart does not think so. Just as it is obvious that even in testimony about women, where the basic testimony is valid only rabbinically, the first statement in court is decisive and the witness may not retract and testify again, because otherwise the Torah's ways would not be pleasant, for after she had already been permitted and married the witness could say, "I fabricated it," and give some excuse, as the questioner wrote in Responsa Ran, siman 34. The very same reason applies to a witness who says something in the name of another witness. Once the second witness's words have been accepted and the woman has been permitted and married, a stumbling block would occur if the first could say, "I fabricated it." This too is therefore included in the Sages' enactment to believe one witness from another witness. The Sages have the power in this matter, so that once the second witness testified in court, even though the first one's statement was outside court, the first may not retract and say, "I fabricated it." Even if she was permitted and married, it is like the general rule in the Torah that one may not retract and testify again.

For this reason, the Chatam Sofer also addresses the Chelkat Mechokek's first doubt, where she said it in the name of a maidservant and the maidservant contradicts her. It appears that the maidservant is certainly not believed, in accordance with the Shevut Yaakov above. True, where she says it in the name of a trustworthy witness, the concern that the Torah's ways would not be pleasant does not arise, lest the first witness come and deny having said anything and be believed by the Ramban's tradition; since neither of them is suspected of lying, there is no need to fear that. But where one testifies in the name of a maidservant or a gentile speaking incidentally, whose mouths are full of falsehood, although they are believed for permitting a woman through the combination of the woman's careful inquiry and the fact that the matter is likely to become known, and this reporter is also believed that he heard it from them, nevertheless we are not immediately secure that they will not later come and deny their first words. If we believed them by the Ramban's tradition, we would thereby remove a woman from her husband whom we had initially permitted, and how could we permit her at first? That would not be the Torah's pleasant way. Necessarily the enactment was originally made that such people are not believed to contradict one who reports in their name.

See there further regarding an agunah whose husband had been taken into the army thirty years earlier. A woman testified that she heard from a poor traveler that this woman's husband had died, and the woman was permitted on the basis of responsa from rabbis. When she came to receive chalitzah, it was heard that the female witness had retracted her testimony. The court summoned her and intimidated her, and she said that because she had pity on the woman she had added something the poor man had not told her at all about her husband's death, and that the same poor man had later come to her again and denied having said anything about this woman's husband; she accepted that she deserved repentance. The question was whether she is believed to retract. The Chatam Sofer answered that had he been there he would not have summoned the female witness at all to hear her words, since she had already testified. Even in testimony about women, which is valid only rabbinically and not by Torah law, the first testimony is decisive, as explained in Responsa Ran, simanim 34 and 47.

In that case the testimony had already been examined by a court. Even if one says that being permitted through responsa is not called permission by a court until three convene together and say to her, "You are permitted to marry," as the Beit Shmuel states in se'if katan 124, here, if when she came for chalitzah a court had assembled to arrange chalitzah for her, that was certainly like permission by a court. But if there was not yet a court there and she had only asked the rabbi to arrange chalitzah, perhaps she had not yet been permitted, and if one witness came contradicting the first witness she would leave her permitted status. Nevertheless here no outside witness came to contradict the first testimony, and from the wording of the question it sounds that the testimony had been accepted by a court of three. If so, she can no longer say, "I fabricated it," neither the second witness nor the first. Even if the first witness were before us and contradicted her by saying, "You testified falsely in my name," the witness would be believed under the Ramban's tradition; but the second witness is not believed to say that the first witness contradicts his own first words. The court should not have sent for her at all or listened to her retraction, as is written in Shittah Mekubbetzet to Bava Batra 48.

Still, one must examine the woman's statement that because she had pity she added to the story, and that when the traveler came again she questioned him. Her words imply that the first time she erred in her imagination about the traveler's words, and from conjecture understood it as if he had explicitly said that the husband died; the second time she investigated him carefully and he said he had never intended this. In such a case, had she immediately come to court when the traveler returned and cried out, "I erred in my imagination," there might have been some reason to be concerned for her words. But now that she remained silent until after the traveler had slipped away, and further waited until the woman came for chalitzah, and only after the court sent for her, in such circumstances she is not believed, as is proved from Rashi to Ketubot 18b. However, since in every testimony it is proper and obligatory at the outset to investigate whether she spoke from conjecture and imagination, and only here we are lenient because we do not judge what is possible from what is impossible, therefore, if investigation is possible, we must do whatever is possible. If this woman's husband died in the army and did not desert, it may be easy to investigate with the officer under whom he was listed, for their practice is to issue a document called a Totenschein. See what I wrote below, se'if katan 53. But if this is impossible, we do not judge the possible from the impossible; and if a Torah scholar qualified to rule agrees with me, I will join in permitting this woman. These are his words; see there.

<b>Even though they contradict one another.</b> Chelkat Mechokek notes that this ruling is disputed: Shiltei Gibborim, citing Riaz, holds that if one witness says "he died" and another says "he was killed," this is a contradiction, following Rambam's view that contradiction about the substance of the event is like contradiction in formal interrogation and voids the testimony. Rosh, Tur, and the Shulchan Arukh do not follow Rambam here and treat it like contradiction in secondary inquiries. Beit Shmuel suggests, first, that even Rambam may agree here, since this resembles one witness saying "a black maneh" and the other "a white maneh"; and second, that for permitting an agunah it is enough that one witness says the husband is no longer alive, unlike monetary cases that require two witnesses.

Pitchei Teshuvah observes that Beit Shmuel's second explanation appears to conflict with the later seif, which validates contradictions in secondary inquiries but implies that contradictions in formal interrogation would not be valid. He explains that, according to this approach, even contradiction in formal interrogation would permit her under the law of one witness; the later seif means only that when contradiction is merely in secondary inquiries both witnesses remain valid as a pair, with consequences for later rules.

Noda BiYehudah siman 46 argues that the Rosh, Tur, Shulchan Arukh, and later authorities overlooked an explicit Yerushalmi in Ha-Ishah Shalom, where R. Yehudah and R. Shimon concede regarding ordinary witnesses that such contradiction matters; their leniency concerns only rival wives. Since the Bavli does not contradict this, one cannot be lenient in a matter of forbidden relations. The Rosh's actual case may still be correct because the contradiction concerned whether someone was present at burial, not the body of the testimony. The Vilna Gaon likewise writes sharply that the proof from the Mishnah is mistaken and that the Rosh permitted for a different reason, namely contradiction only in secondary inquiries. Later authorities, including Maharit's responsa, Korban HaEdah, Beit Meir, Kehillat Yaakov, Yad HaMelekh, Chemdat Shlomo, and Berit Avraham, discuss the issue.

In Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 56, a respondent attempts to defend the Rosh from the Yerushalmi and notes that Ra'avan also ruled like the Rosh. Noda BiYehudah rejects the defense, but nevertheless writes that since Ra'avan and Rosh are lenient, and Rif and Rambam are silent, one who relies on these two pillars of halakhah cannot be stopped, especially because the Yerushalmi's statement is attributed to R. Yochanan while Rav disagrees, and perhaps the rule that the law follows R. Yochanan over Rav applies only in the Bavli. Machatzit HaShekel rules at length like Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Arukh, as do Rashdam, Tzemach Tzedek, Maharam Lublin, and Maharchash, and he is inclined to be lenient even in real contradictions in formal interrogation where another ground for leniency exists.

Yeshuot Yaakov distinguishes between valid witnesses and disqualified witnesses: two biblically valid witnesses may be relied on despite contradiction, but where rabbinic belief is extended to otherwise invalid witnesses, the Sages did not institute reliability when they contradict one another. Noda BiYehudah Kamma siman 41 and Responsa R. Akiva Eiger siman 87 discuss agunah cases with contradictory details of day, place, and burial; R. Akiva Eiger finds many grounds to be lenient but hesitates to rely on them against Beit Shmuel and the explicit Yerushalmi. He also rejects a proposed majority argument where two witnesses contradict a third, since if the contradiction is in interrogation they may constitute one invalidated testimony; however, where burial details are not essential to the testimony, the contradiction may be only in secondary inquiries.

Beit Shmuel adds that a contradiction between one witness saying the husband drowned and another saying he was killed depends on this dispute. If the drowning was in waters with an end, Shiltei Gibborim would treat it as contradiction; if in endless waters, even he may agree that there is no contradiction, since the man might have emerged and later been killed. According to the Shulchan Arukh, she is permitted in all such cases. Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 57 discusses whether a witness who says the man was crucified contradicts another who says he died naturally, and notes several complicating factors, including the majority likelihood of death by crucifixion, the credibility of the witnesses, and local practices of execution.

<b>For we are concerned that perhaps it is a demon.</b> See Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, and see Machatzit HaShekel, s.k. 34-36, who wrote at length with sharp analysis to answer the difficulties of the later authorities from below, paragraph 23, and from siman 141, paragraph 19. In the end he leaned toward leniency nowadays, when the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom not to marry two wives applies; from now on there is no concern for a rival wife, and there remains only the concern of a demon. With a single concern, they were lenient in a place of danger, and if so it is not necessary that they see the shadow of a shadow. In any event, where the shadow of a shadow is visible, we properly rely on it according to everyone and act upon it.

However, from the words of Rashba and Ran in Gittin it appears that the concern for a demon is a closer concern than the concern for a rival wife, and one should be concerned for it independently. But from the words of Rif and the Rosh this is not compelled, as he wrote above. Therefore it appears that even nowadays one should not be lenient unless they saw his shadow of a shadow. But where they did see it properly, one may be lenient nowadays and need not be concerned for the words of the Gaon who says that we are not expert, for the reason we have written, especially since Bach is entirely lenient even without seeing a shadow, following Rambam and Ritva. If so, we too may rely on leniency at least where they saw his shadow of a shadow.

He further wrote that the Gaon's statement that we are not expert in the matter of the shadow of a shadow is, at first glance, difficult, for this does not seem to require expertise: who does not know whether the shadow is longer than the height of the person? Perhaps he was speaking of the rest of the day, when considerable discernment is needed to distinguish according to the thickness or faintness of the shadow. If so, it appears that in the morning and evening, when the shadow is very long, one may properly rely even nowadays on seeing the shadow.

<b>Even if it is not authenticated.</b> See Ba'er Heitev, who writes that some authorities nevertheless hold that authentication is required, but authentication by one witness is effective; ordinarily one witness does not authenticate a document because it is like testimony from the mouth of testimony, etc. This is the language of the Beit Shmuel, se'if katan 29. See Responsa R. Akiva Eiger, siman 92, who writes that his words are unclear; see there. See also what I wrote below, siman 97, se'if katan 2.

Regarding Ba'er Heitev's further statement that if a writing came from afar the Chelkat Mechokek is lenient, see Seder HaTzvi, se'if katan 38, who discusses this and concludes that in practice the main view appears to be the ruling of the Chelkat Mechokek: in a case of agunah, where no other testimony can be obtained, one may rely on the authorities who do not require authentication, since in this case there is no basis to suspect forgery. But where the woman herself is not believed, such as where there was a quarrel and the like, so that there is reason to suspect forgery, the matter requires study; see there. He means this even if two witnesses are signed on it.

See Responsa Pnei Aryeh, siman 20, who writes that a letter coming from afar through the postal service is worse and requires authentication by Torah law. It is only where someone brings a document in his hand and says that it was handed to him by a Jew in a distant place, and he found written in it that so-and-so died, that one might have thought, according to the Ramban and Rashba, that authentication is unnecessary. But where a letter comes through the post, since the postal official in the place where the letter is deposited receives sealed and closed writings and does not care who brings them, even if it is ultimately clarified that they were forged it can never be discovered from whom the evil came. Therefore we suspect forgery by Torah law until it is authenticated. If it is written in a Jew's handwriting, perhaps one may be lenient in a case of agunah, as the Chelkat Mechokek wrote; but in his case the writing was that of a gentile woman, etc.; see there. In his index he writes that the Shakh in Choshen Mishpat, siman 46, is also of this opinion.

See also Kehillat Yaakov by the gaon of Lisa, who is very stringent about a writing that comes from afar and requires authentication at least by one witness. In his opinion, even if it is written in a Jew's handwriting, one should not be lenient even in a case of agunah, unlike the Chelkat Mechokek, because this is a matter that is not at all likely to become revealed, and how can one witness be believed in a matter of forbidden relations? See there. Perhaps, however, if two people are signed on it in such a way that it is clearly recognizable that one person did not write both signatures, he too would agree.

See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 33, who discusses at length the dispute among the authorities on this law and concludes that the main view is the one that does not require authentication. This is obvious according to those who hold that one witness in testimony permitting a woman is believed by Torah law; see what I wrote above, se'if katan 13. But even if we say that one witness in testimony about a woman is believed only rabbinically, nevertheless we say that authentication is not required. He concludes that if two more rabbis agree, he too will join in permitting; see there.

Responsa Kehillat Yaakov by the gaon of Karlin, siman 11, also tends toward leniency, that authentication is not required. He brings a proof from Yevamot 115b, the case of Yitzchak the exilarch, where "they sent from there," and the language "sent" implies that they sent by writing in a letter. In Bava Batra 38 it is explained that Hispania is a year's journey from the Land of Israel, so certainly no one knew how to authenticate a letter coming from there; evidently authentication is not required. But according to Responsa Maharik, siman 30, also cited in Responsa Galia Masekhet, siman 70, letter 7, the explanation there is that they sent from Cordoba, not from Hispania, and according to that there is no proof.

In particular, the Kehillat Yaakov writes to permit in his own case, where the letter was in the handwriting of three people constituting a court that had accepted testimony. Some authorities hold that authentication by a court does not itself require authentication even regarding bills of divorce, as Rema writes at the end of siman 142; see Responsa Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 136. All the more so in testimony about a woman, to testify that her husband died, and especially where it is difficult to obtain authentication, the woman should be permitted. Still, a court of three should be seated to permit her; see there.

In Responsa Chatam Sofer, simanim 44 and 49, he too discusses this at length. It is clear there that his opinion follows the Chelkat Mechokek: in a case of agunah one may rely on an unauthenticated document, but where it is not a case of agunah it is not proper to rely on it without authentication, even if three are signed on it. But if a letter was written to a known addressee and the answer came back through the post, that is sufficient even if only one person signed it, for in such a case no authentication is needed. He had already written this in siman 43: any writing sent by post to known owners, where a reply comes from them back by post, is plainly from its owners and involves no forgery, as every understanding person sees, and it needs no authentication. See there. At the end of siman 45 he also writes this reasoning and mentions that the gaon in Responsa Beit Ephraim also wrote so, and that cases had already been decided several times on the basis of this reasoning. This is also explained further at the end of siman 94; see there and see the next se'if katan.

<b>They must know.</b> See Ba'er Heitev. See Machatzit HaShekel, se'if katan 40, who writes that the Levush gave the Maggid Mishneh's reason: this would be two leniencies, a gentile speaking incidentally and testimony by writing. According to his words, if one witness testifies in writing from the mouth of a gentile who spoke orally in the course of conversation, it too should not help, for there would be three disadvantages: one witness from another witness, from writing, and from an incidental gentile statement. The Machatzit HaShekel discusses this at length and concludes that we do not rule this way, and that we should not invent stringencies on our own in the case of an agunah that are not mentioned in the Talmud. Even with a gentile's writing in the manner of incidental speech, there is room to be lenient wherever he wrote it in the course of another matter and in connection with other details, since we rule that a gentile speaking incidentally from the mouth of another gentile speaking incidentally is effective, as stated in se'if 16. In any event, he writes, the rule appears to apply only where the gentile speaks incidentally in writing; but where one witness writes from the mouth of a gentile who spoke orally in the course of conversation, that certainly helps. These are his words; see there.

See Beit Meir, who writes that the Chelkat Mechokek had to introduce this rule according to his own view in halakhah 13, where he upholds the Rivash's view, which is the first opinion in se'if 14 in the gloss, that whenever there was no question, even without contextual connection, it is considered incidental speech. Therefore he had a difficulty here with the Rambam: why did the Rambam write that it was known to be Jewish handwriting? He therefore had to introduce this rule that incidental speech is ineffective through writing. But according to the second view, derived from the Rambam's language, that contextual connection is specifically required, there is no necessity for this rule. Here the case involved no contextual connection; it was merely written that so-and-so died and nothing more. Therefore the Rambam had to insist that it be Jewish handwriting. He would agree that if it had been written with contextual connection, it would be effective. From this it follows that if a woman has a gentile's written incidental statement with contextual connection, and also a gentile who testifies orally without a question but without contextual connection, she is certainly permitted whichever way one looks at it; see there.

See Responsa Pnei Aryeh, siman 20, whose opinion is also that a gentile speaking incidentally in writing is ineffective. He brings that this is also the view of the Terumat HaDeshen, siman 240, and Maharam Alshikh, siman 118, and he rules that this is the practical law; see there. Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 67, writes similarly.

But where he writes to another gentile in incidental fashion, for example where he comes to collect a debt from the estate of so-and-so who died, why should it not be effective? See there. The Kehillat Yaakov writes about him that he reasoned well; see there.

See Yeshuot Yaakov, se'if katan 15. A case came before him concerning a man taken into the imperial army who was killed there, and a document came from a high office, the General Command, stating that this man had been killed and mentioning his name and his father's name. The matter was permitted on the basis of the reliability of the General Command, together with further estimates and evidentiary reasons showing that he was killed; see there.

See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, simanim 43, 44, and 94. His opinion is to permit even without joining additional estimates. He writes that, needless to say, if a Jew himself saw in the military register that the man was listed among the dead, and the circumstances leave no concern for two people with the same name and father, nor for two towns of the same name, nor for mistaken inference, the woman is certainly permitted. That writing, on the day it is written, is no less than incidental speech. Although the Maggid Mishneh wrote that a gentile speaking incidentally in writing is ineffective, the reason seems to be that one should not add further to the leniency: two leniencies in one place, believing a gentile and from their writing, and also because one cannot discern from writing whether it was said incidentally. But here, in military records, their writing is better than their speech: speech is mere chance and they may lie, but they would not damage themselves so much by signing falsely. If, however, the register did not write explicitly that he died, and his name was only erased by crossing it out, one must doubt whether it was erased because he deserted, unless it is clarified that it was erased because of death.

Even if the Jew did not see that register himself, but obtained an answer from the chancery in response to an inquiry, and then from another place obtained a death certificate written in the same terms and signed with the seal of the civil authorities, although both are like testimony from the mouth of testimony, since the writer himself did not bury him, nevertheless one may be lenient. Even according to the Rosh, who did not wish to permit where the testimony did not begin and end in a state of validity, as noted above in se'if katan 38, the reason appears to be that we should not add beyond what the Sages said, since all of this is only for the enactment of agunot; perhaps that enactment sufficed to believe a gentile speaking incidentally, which is common. Nevertheless, in this last generation, when, because of our many sins, cases involving soldiers are very common every day and no testimony can be obtained except by inquiry to the authorities, there is no greater enactment for agunot than this.

At the end of siman 94 the Chatam Sofer concludes that he had already permitted this three times in cases that came before him, where Jews asked the commandant officers, they wrote to the General Command, and from there a death certificate was sent, copied from the military register, signed by their officers and sealed with the official governmental seal. Since the matter goes from one civil office to another and is also sealed with the official seal, whose forgery is punishable by death to the king, there is a strong presumption that both offices would not lie. Also, since they wrote by post and received the reply by post, it is like authentication: no one receives an answer by post from the commandant officers except the one who sent to them, and this is better than Persian documents discussed in Gittin 19. These are his words; see there.

<b>Because a rumor had gone out that he died.</b> See Beit Shmuel in the name of Responsa Beit Yosef; his wording is somewhat obscure, and Responsa Maharam Lublin siman 128 clarifies that it accords with Taz s.k. 8. Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 41 writes that Maharai's responsum might seem to imply concern even for an unspecified death rumor, but distinguishes that case as involving a gentile speaking incidentally, where all agree there is reason for concern, unlike a Jew who comes to testify.

Machatzit HaShekel further explains that this rule applies only to someone who did not come before court to testify, but merely wrote or said to another in ordinary conversation or complaint, which resembles incidental speech and was not intended as testimony. If, however, someone came before court to testify or to permit the woman and stated simply that the husband died, then all agree we do not suspect that he testified merely from rumor. If the rumor was already known where he testified, he would have added nothing; and even if the rumor circulated only in the place from which he came, everyone knows that a court does not rely on rumor and that testimony must report the facts as they occurred. The same applies where testimony is sent to the court in writing.

Responsa Masat Binyamin siman 105 is very strict, requiring even a gentile speaking incidentally to say at least that he knows clearly that the man died, lest he merely heard a vague report. Taz rejects this stringency, and Machatzit HaShekel writes that Masat Binyamin himself appears to have retreated from it in siman 109. In the addenda to Machatzit HaShekel, Seder Eliyahu distinguishes three cases: first, when a rumor says the man drowned, or the witness himself says he drowned and then died, all require explicit evidence of death, such as the sea casting him up or his leaving the water alive and later dying. Second, where it was already known that he entered a place of danger and a rumor of death then spread, a plain statement that he died may be based on general assumption; but if only the danger was known and no death rumor had spread, we do not suspect that the witness inferred death from the danger. Third, where a rumor says that a traveler from one place to another died, later authorities dispute whether a plain statement of death is suspected to rest on rumor.

Seder Eliyahu adds that the dispute concerns a witness whose main purpose is to report the death. If the speaker's main purpose is another matter and the death is mentioned only incidentally, all agree there is concern that he called the man dead merely because of the rumor, especially where the other matter only makes sense if the man is assumed dead. These are matters of sound reasoning, and he also discusses gentile incidental speech in this context.

<b>קטן. </b> עבה"ט מ"ש ובפרישה כו' וע' בב"י שכן עלה בדעתו מתחילה לפרש כן דברי הטור ואח"כ דחה פירוש זה והפרישה מקיים זה הפירוש וע' במה"צ ס"ק מ"ג מ"ש בזה והעלה שדברי הב"י עיקר. וע"ש עוד שהביא בשם יש"ש סי' י"ד שדייק מלשון הרמב"ם בפי' המשנה דבאו' התינוק מת פלוני לבד אפילו אומר ג"כ כך וכך ספדני הוי התם לא מהני כיון שלא אמר שעסוק בעצמו בענין הספדו כענין המשנה הרי אנו באין מלספוד שהענין תולה במעשה הקטן עצמו ובק"א שם הביא שגם הרח"ש ז"ל דקדק כן מדברי הרמב"ם ויש ראיה לזה מכתובות כ"ח ע"ב נאמן התינוק לומר כו' ע"י עצמו אבל לא ע"י אחר ע"ש אולם מסקנת היש"ש שם דא"נ שיהא הענין תולה במעשה הקטן עצמו והוכיח כן מלשון הא"ז בשם ראב"ן וראבי"ה. והרב מה"ז פלפל בזה וסיים דצ"ע לדינא ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A minor.</b> See Baer Heitev, who wrote in the name of Perishah, etc. See Beit Yosef, for initially it occurred to him to explain the Tur's words this way, and afterward he rejected this explanation; the Perishah upholds this explanation. See Machatzit HaShekel s.k. 43, what he wrote about this, concluding that the words of Beit Yosef are primary. See there further, where he brought in the name of Yam shel Shlomo siman 14, who inferred from the Rambam's language in his commentary to the Mishnah that when a child says only, so-and-so died, even if he also says there were such-and-such eulogizers there, it does not help, since he did not say that he himself was involved in the matter of the eulogy, like the Mishnah's case, we are coming from eulogizing. The matter depends on an act of the minor himself. In Kuntres Acharon there he brought that R. Chaim Shabtai z"l also inferred this from the Rambam's words, and there is proof for this from Ketubot 28b: a child is believed to say, etc., regarding himself but not regarding another; see there. However, the conclusion of Yam shel Shlomo there is that it is not necessary for the matter to depend on an act of the minor himself, and he proved this from the language of Or Zarua in the name of Raavan and Raavyah. Rabbi Machatzit HaShekel discusses this and concludes that the law requires study; see there.
