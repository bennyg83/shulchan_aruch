**** ENGLISH ****
<b>There are those who say, etc.; but, etc.; or, etc.</b> So wrote the Ra'avad in his glosses to chapter 4 of Hilchos Ishus. His reason is from Kesubos 23a: 'Let us say that they disagree concerning Rav Hamnuna.' This implies that even according to Rav Hamnuna, only where witnesses came after she married is she permitted, since she married permissibly because she had a migo. The Mishnah there teaches: 'But if there are witnesses,' etc.; even after the fact she must leave. The same follows according to the answer, 'No, all agree,' etc. All the more so regarding the kesubah, for she does not collect on her own statement except through the exposition of the kesubah, as written in chapter 15 of Yevamos.

<b>And there are those who further say, etc.</b> For the generations deteriorate, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of the chapter HaMekabel: Rabbi, etc., said, 'As great as the difference between sacrificial holy things and ordinary profane things, so is the difference between our generation and the generation of Rabbi Yose.' See the Rosh, chapter 2 of Yevamos, section 68, and the Rosh, chapter 5 of Kesubos, end of section 34.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>There are those who are stringent.</b> This follows from the statement there, 'Woe for the swift horseman,' etc. It is also somewhat implied by the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, who write, 'Woe, woe for so-and-so,' etc. They similarly explain the statement, 'A pity for,' etc., meaning that he did me a kindness, etc. Addendum: this is also implied in Gittin 28b, 'The established rule is,' etc. Yevamos 25b apparently supports the latter view, but the Nimukei Yosef and Shitah Mekubetzes explain that since he said, 'Tell the wife,' etc., he intended to testify. Likewise, the Mishnah's statement, 'He died; I killed him,' etc., indicates intent to testify, paralleling the first clause concerning one who brings a get. Therefore the Gemara asks, 'He himself,' etc. Kesubos 26a also somewhat implies this from the example of incidental speech, 'like this,' etc., and Rabbi Chiya, etc., for all of that is otherwise superfluous, especially Rabbi Chiya's incident. The primary ruling follows the first opinion, as the Gemara asks, 'Where is this found? In incidental speech such as,' etc.; this shows that in the first incident it stated the core rule without qualification. The same applies to the incident of Chasa and to Gittin, chapter 3, 'The established rule is,' etc.
