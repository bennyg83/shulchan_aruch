**** ENGLISH ****
Only from land, meaning even from him personally. This is also proven from the sugya in HaOmer, page 65b, and so it is stated there in the Ritva, and likewise in Nedarim 65b. See the Ran there. So too, in many places in Ketubot, in Tosafot, including in chapter HaIsha Shenaflu, page 81, they write that the dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages concerns collection even from the husband himself.

However, in the responsa of Mahari ben Lev, book 2, kelal 55, he writes that this is not correct, for even if we say that a ketubah also collects from movable property during the husband’s lifetime according to Talmudic law, in accordance with the view of most decisors, and I do not know who these decisors are. See the Rambam, chapter 16 of Hilchot Ishut. According to the text of the Rashba, even the Rambam holds this way. I also do not know how they would reconcile the above-mentioned sugyot.

However, from Rashi, of blessed memory, Ketubot 67a, s.v. purna, “to collect from orphans,” it is clearly implied that during the husband’s lifetime this is not considered nichsei melog, and this requires study.

It is true that according to the Pnei Yehoshua, of blessed memory, page 81, s.v. dilma Rabbi Meir hi, who writes that it appears obvious to him that this depends on the dispute whether a ketubah is biblical or rabbinic, and Rabbi Meir follows his own view that ketubah is biblical, one could say that the decisors hinted to by the above Mahari hold like Rabbenu Tam, that we rely on Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that ketubah is biblical. The sugyot in HaOmer and Nedarim would then be surprising, since from there also it seems proven this way.

But in my humble opinion, the words of Tosafot in Ketubot 10, s.v. ho'il vetiknu chachamim, explicitly imply unlike the Pnei Yehoshua. For they write that no Tanna anywhere states that a woman’s ketubah is collected from movable property except Rabbi Meir. It is clear that they do not hold that this depends on whether ketubah is biblical or rabbinic. This is also genuinely proven from Nedarim, for according to the Pnei Yehoshua there would be no reason to question Rabbi Akiva, since perhaps he too holds like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and Rabbi Meir.

The Pnei Yehoshua further writes there: One should not say the opposite, that since ketubah is rabbinic so that it should not be easy for him to divorce her, then if he has no land it will be easy for him to divorce her. This is not correct, because nevertheless it will not be easy, for if he later acquires land it will become subject to payment. This answer is forced, for who will compel him to buy land? Furthermore, as long as he has no land, the woman does not rely on this, and this is close to a licentious union.

One could also say that although movable property is not subject to collection, this only means that the court does not go down to movable property to collect from it. Nevertheless, he is obligated to pay, and they place him under ban until he pays. Some explain at the end of Bava Batra that lien is not biblical, even regarding free assets. See the Rambam and Ritva, Kiddushin 13. If so, the Gemara in Nedarim raises no difficulty.

It appears to me that in the Beit Yosef, siman 119, there is a responsum of the Rashba in the name of the Rif: One who wishes to divorce and does not have the ketubah payment may not divorce, and we place him under ban if he does not obey the law. He brings proof from the Mishnah, “If he took her,” and if he said, “Here is your get and here is your ketubah,” he is permitted. This implies that without the ketubah he is not permitted. According to his words, there is no difficulty, for when he has nothing, if he divorces without the ketubah, they place him under ban. In the time of the Talmud, when he had movable property, he was at least not permitted to divorce, and therefore it is not easy for him.

Although from the Rama there it appears that he disagrees based on the Rivash, nevertheless one may say that those who disagree hold like the Yerushalmi cited by the Tur in siman 93, that as long as he has not paid the ketubah, he remains obligated in her maintenance, and therefore it is also not easy for him to divorce her. According to the Yerushalmi, there is no proof from the above Mishnah to the law of the Rif, because if he does not say, “Here is your ketubah,” he will be obligated in her maintenance and medical care, and the divorce accomplishes nothing in that regard. The Rif is the one who disagrees with the Yerushalmi. This also appears from the responsum of the Rashba, who arranged the two rulings next to one another. In any case, one law is necessarily proven from the Mishnah.

The position of the Rivash cited in Beit Yosef, siman 93, namely that he holds unlike the Yerushalmi and also does not hold like the Rif, requires study as to how he explains the Mishnah. See siman 79, where I explained the language of the Rambam, according to which the Mishnah is also resolved.

However, according to the Rif, there is still a difficulty: What does the Gemara ask in Nedarim? In any case, he may not divorce until he pays the ketubah. Perhaps Rabbi Tarfon’s obligation means that they prohibit the divorce until he pays. It must be that this is not so, since he is not the one demanding the divorce; rather, she is demanding it because she made a vow. Therefore, although he caused the situation, nevertheless regarding the vow he is no worse than one who transgressed and divorced. If he transgressed and divorced, the Rif must agree that he no longer has the status of one who has nothing. The same applies in the time of the Talmud where he has no land, and therefore the Gemara properly asks.

If so, the original difficulty returns, that it should be easy for him to divorce her privately, as we say in Bava Batra 174: Does everyone who divorces do so in court? The Ramban proves from this in Sefer HaZechut on Gittin 86, against the view of the Rif that according to Rabbi Elazar, witnesses of signature also effect the divorce. However, this requires study according to the beginning of Gittin, how we rely on an ordinary scribe, that they are knowledgeable in the law. The Rashba, when quoting the Ramban, indeed omits this proof.

According to the Yerushalmi, however, it works well, because since he is obligated in her maintenance as long as he has not paid her, it is again not easy to divorce her. In Nedarim it also works well according to the decisors who hold that he cannot prohibit her through a vow unless he connects it to marital relations. After the divorce, however, he can do so, for he is truly feeding her. According to the Rambam, in any case he cannot feed her, and this should be examined.

Perhaps nevertheless, even according to the Rif, one can answer with difficulty that since the prohibition against divorce remains upon him as long as he has not paid the ketubah, it is no longer easy for him to divorce her.

הג"ה ומיהו היורשים כו' איתא בח"מ סימן ק"א סעיף א' בא לפרוע אם יש לו מעות צריך ליתן לו וא"י לדחותו אצל קרקע או מטלט' וכתב הש"ך וכן הדין לענין גביות כתו' שצריך ליתן מעות מזומנים אם יש ליתומים מעות בעין לפי מה שכתב בטור א"ע סימן ק' וכן הוא בתשובת רשד"מ וכו' וספר זה א"א. וגם הבאר היטב כתב הכא אם יש מעות בעין והיא תובעת מעות והיורשים רוצים לתת לה מטל' עיין כנה"ג ורדב"ז. וגם ספרים אלו א"א ודברי הש"ך צ"ע אי קאי לעיקר כתובה מבואר הכא דאפי' בקרקע יכולים לסלקה ומכ"ש במטל' ואפי' אי איירי בנדוניא שלרוב הפוסקים היא כחוב גמור מ"מ בחוב גופיה מבואר בח"מ סימן ק"ז סעיף א' אם ירשו קרקע ומטלטלין וכו' הדין עם היורשים ומה שכתב לפי מה שכתב בטור הכא א"י למה רומז ואם למה שבדינ' דש"ע סעיף ג' במקום שבש"ע כתוב אם נמצא בני חורין גובה כתוב בטור אם מוצאים לי קרקע בני חורין מוכרין ונותנין לה מזה לע"ד ל"מ מידי כי ר"ל שלטובתו אם רצה למכור בהכרזה כמו שעושין בלוה אולי ימצא מי שיתן יותר מהשומא אבל אם לא ימצא קונה מחויבת ליגבות בשימא כי מה"ת תתעלה מבעל חוב ולהכי כתב הב"י שהוא פשוט. וע"כ כונתו לשטרי כתו' דהאידנא שכותבין נכסים דאית להון אחריות ודלית להו וכו' ואפי' מן גלימא כו' בחיים ובמות והפירש לע"ד שמשעבד בפירש מטלטלין אף דלאחר מיתה וע"ד המבואר ברמ' שנהגו לפרש בכל כתובה מקרקע ומטלטלין (וע' במ"ל פכ"א מה"ס תוך תשובתו מביא בשם מהרי"ו ואע"ג שכתב ג"כ אותה תשובה וז"ל ועוד אני סבור לכך אנו כותבין בחיים ובמותי כדי לגבות מן הראוי וכו' ולע"ד הפשוט כנ"ל) לפי זה לדידן מטלטלין דיתמי מדינא משתעבדי ושפיר שאין יכולין לדחותה אצל קרקע כבח"מ סימן ק"ז הנ"ל שכתב ואם ירשו קרקע ומטלטלין ואין מפורש נשמע הא מפורש לא וכן מביא הב"ש ס"ק ד' בשם הש"ג:
**** ENGLISH ****
Gloss: “However, the heirs,” and so on. It is stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 101, se'if 1, that if one comes to pay, and he has money, he must give money and cannot push the creditor off to collect from land or movable property. The Shach writes that the same law applies to collecting a ketubah, that he must give ready cash if the orphans have actual money available, according to what is written in the Tur, Even HaEzer, siman 100, and so it is in the responsa of Rashdam. This book is not available to me.

The Ba'er Heitev here also writes that if there is actual cash available, and she demands money, while the heirs wish to give her movable property, see Knesset HaGedolah and Radbaz. These books too are not available to me.

The words of the Shach require study. If he is referring to the basic ketubah, it is clear here that even with land they can discharge their obligation to her, and certainly with movable property. Even if he is discussing dowry, which according to most decisors is like an ordinary debt, nevertheless regarding an ordinary debt itself it is explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 107, se'if 1: if they inherited land and movable property, the law follows the heirs.

What he wrote, “according to what is written in the Tur here,” I do not know what he is hinting to. If he means the ruling in Shulchan Aruch, se'if 3, where the Shulchan Aruch writes, “If free property is found, she collects,” while the Tur writes, “If they find free land for me, they sell it and give her from it,” in my humble opinion this proves nothing. For he means that for his benefit, if he wishes to sell it by public announcement as is done with a borrower, perhaps someone will be found who will pay more than the appraisal. But if no buyer is found, she is obligated to collect according to the appraisal, for why should she be superior to an ordinary creditor? This is why the Beit Yosef writes that this is obvious.

Rather, his intent must be to the ketubah documents of our time, in which we write that property that has responsibility and property that does not have responsibility, and even from the cloak on his shoulder, are subject to collection, during life and after death. The explanation, in my humble opinion, is that he explicitly places movable property under lien even after death, according to what is explained in the Rama, that the custom is to specify in every ketubah both land and movable property.

See Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, in the course of his responsum, where he cites Mahari Weil. Although he too wrote that same responsum, saying: “Furthermore, I think that this is why we write ‘during my lifetime and after my death,’ so as to collect from what is only fit to come later,” in my humble opinion the simple explanation is as above.

According to this, for us, the movable property of orphans is legally subject to collection, and therefore it is proper that they cannot push her off to collect from land, as in the above Choshen Mishpat, siman 107, which writes: “If they inherited land and movable property,” and it is not specified. This implies that if it is specified, the law is different. The Beit Shmuel, se'if katan 4, also brings this in the name of the Shiltei Gibborim.

“And nowadays the custom is to write in every ketubah,” and so on, “and what I will acquire in the future,” nevertheless, and so on. From this it appears that only regarding movable property, because of the market enactment, this writing does not help. Were it not for that, it certainly would help. The Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 115, se'if katan 29, writes: According to this, it emerges that da'aknei does not help for ketubah and additional ketubah. In my humble opinion, this is not so, as is implied here. This is also proven from the beginning of chapter Af Al Pi, where the Gemara does not state as a practical difference between ketubah and other debts the law of da'aknei, but mentions only enhancement. Specifically enhancement is different, because it is not truly da'aknei, but the Sages agreed regarding a creditor that it is considered like da'aknei through the seller’s writing, “I will stand it up for you,” as is well explained in Nimukei Yosef, cited by the Sema there. This is one of the leniencies of ketubah. But with true da'aknei, why should we distinguish?

The main reason is that the Shach wrote this according to his interpretation of the sugya in Bechorot. He wrote that all the great authorities struggled needlessly, and he proposed that when Rava needs the reason that a creditor collects enhancement, that applies only to expenses. But for enhancement beyond the expenses, he does not need that reason, for through da'aknei he seizes it by law. Therefore, it was obvious to him that enhancement is actual da'aknei, and he learned that just as a ketubah does not seize enhancement, so too writing da'aknei does not help for it. All of this led him to reject the words of Nimukei Yosef in se'if katan 12 and 27 with both hands.

But I have already elaborated and concluded that the words of Nimukei Yosef are necessary and clear in their reasoning. Specifically with a purchaser, through the above writing, the enhancement is considered da'aknei. But with a recipient of a gift, who has no one against whom to claim reimbursement, and all the more so with orphans, where both factors exist, namely they have no one from whom to seek reimbursement and when the enhancement came into existence the one who created the lien, namely the father, had already died, see there, se'if katan 16, it is not considered da'aknei at all. The early authorities did not struggle for no reason, for it is impossible to consider enhancement that comes through expenses in the hands of orphans as da'aknei.

In my humble opinion, with due respect to the other early authorities, the clearest explanation is that of Milchamot, which he cites there. Since this is not its place, I will not copy the lengthy discussion of his words against most of the words of the Shach there.

In any case, this is clear in my humble opinion: writing da'aknei helps for ketubah and additional ketubah. The expression “she seizes” is certainly said regarding purchasers.

The Beit Shmuel writes that he wrote this based on the responsum of the Rosh. I already wrote nearby that one should examine the Rashba on this. He means the end of se'if katan 5, where he wrote: “It is heard from these responsa,” and so on. Therefore he writes here that even without this the Rashba does not agree, and it appears according to all, and so on.

I am astonished. Does the Rashba really have a different legal view from the Rosh? The Shach already cites in Choshen Mishpat, siman 60, se'if katan 4, that several decisors, including the Rosh, wrote this custom. Nevertheless, there in se'if katan 6 he decides that even if the property came after the sale into the hands of this creditor, who has agav, he must return it, since the purchaser already acquired it according to his acquisition. See his words at the end of siman 126, where he explains his view and challenges the responsum of Maharam Alshich.

If this is the Shach’s view regarding an ordinary creditor, where in se'if katan 4 he expresses doubt because the custom is not to write this in every document, then all the more so regarding ketubah, where the custom is to write it in all ketubot, there is certainly no basis to question the custom not to seize based on the market enactment. Otherwise, no person would buy an object from someone who has a wife. Rather, the main purpose of writing this agav clause is only for the specific matters mentioned in siman 60 in the Sema. The clear conclusion is as the Shach writes, that even through an acquisition alone, even if the property came into her hands, it is taken out of her hands. This requires study. See Sefer Nachalat Shivah, and above siman 66.

“And they are not collected from enhancement.” The Beit Shmuel writes that this is not like the commentary on the Mishnah, which writes that after the enactment a woman collects from enhancement and from property that is only fit to come. See Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh.

“But not from what is only fit to come.” The Ba'er Heitev writes that “what is only fit to come” applies even from the husband himself, citing Mahari Weil, siman 8. See there. The Levush wrote well on this, for although that is indeed explained there, nevertheless in my humble opinion the Rama, of blessed memory, deliberately omitted these words, because they are astonishing. He wrote that a man who divorced his wife and did not have the means to pay the ketubah, and afterwards money came into his hands in any manner, is exempt from paying. The proof that he holds this way is from what he wrote at the end of his words, where he raises a difficulty against himself from the Rambam, chapter 12 of Hilchot Ishut, who writes that if the husband was extremely poor and unable to give her bread, they compel him to divorce, and the ketubah remains a debt upon him until he has the means to pay. Mahari Weil writes that even though Maimonides disagrees, nevertheless since he holds that one does not collect from what is only fit to come, we do not extract money.

It escaped him that the Rosh himself, in a responsum cited in Beit Yosef at the end of siman 117, rules this way as well, and it is explained in Nedarim 65b.

His words were taken from the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. Since the Rosh wrote that the Mishnah in Bechorot, “a woman does not take from enhancement,” means even during the husband’s lifetime, Mahari Weil inferred that the clause “and she does not take from what is only fit to come” also applies during the husband’s lifetime. This is nothing. Does he explain that what the Mishnah says, “she does not take from enhancement,” means even from the husband himself? That is certainly impossible, for from him personally she collects even from the cloak on his shoulder. Rather, the Rosh only means that the Mishnah speaks in every applicable case: where she comes to collect from orphans, she does not seize the enhancement that the orphans enhanced; and where she comes to collect during his lifetime from purchasers, she does not seize what the purchasers enhanced. On this the Gemara’s question from an ordinary creditor applies, and it answers that this is among the leniencies of ketubah. But the clause “and not from what is only fit to come” can obviously be explained only after his death.

Know this, for according to Mahari Weil’s view, the Mishnah’s clause “and not from what is only fit to come” must also be referring to land, for movable property would in any case not be subject to collection. Now consider: If he divorced and did not have the means to pay the ketubah, and then later inherited land, according to his view he would also be exempt. Heaven forbid to mention such a thing, for we explicitly rule in Choshen Mishpat, siman 112, that from the debtor himself a creditor collects from whatever he finds with him, even if he did not write da'aknei.

It is already explained in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 104, that there is a dispute among early and later authorities whether a creditor collects from what is only fit to come. But regarding this case no one was ever uncertain. The reason is that from him personally, as long as he is alive, the lien does not move from his body, and when what was only fit to come becomes actually held by him, the lien falls upon it from then, and she collects from him. So it is stated in Bava Batra 157. It is obvious that the law of “fit to come” applies only after one dies, when the personal lien is canceled and the lien had not taken effect on the property during his lifetime. This is clear without any doubt, and proof is unnecessary.

The main law that he derived from chapter HaIsha Shenaflu, that we do not find a Tanna, and so on, concluding that since we see that according to Rabbi Meir she does not collect from movable property that is only fit to come even during his lifetime, the same should apply to us, who hold that she does collect from movable property, namely that she does not collect from what is only fit to come, is very astonishing. Even according to his own approach, the source disproves him. The Rosh there writes that according to our view that movable property is subject to collection, it is not at all relevant to attach collection from movable property to the leniency of ketubah, since there is no dispute about it.

There is also proof from there that as long as he is alive, there is no place for “fit to come,” only for the existence of the law of Rabbi Natan. The practical result is that if he wishes to pay and does not wish to claim, “You are not my litigant,” then during his lifetime it becomes actually held and he is obligated to pay it. Were it not for Rabbi Natan’s law, the litigant would have room to say, “You are not my litigant.” Therefore, even before the Gemara descends to Rabbi Natan’s law, it does not say that the Mishnah is teaching that a loan is considered fit to come, since a yavam is like the husband during his lifetime. From the husband himself, it is obvious that “fit to come” does not apply at all.

In my humble opinion, all his words in this matter appear astonishing.

Gloss: “The father who instructed.” It is stated in Mahari Weil, siman 40, from which this gloss emerges, concerning a case where one obligated himself to give his son a dowry, to be paid two years after the wedding. In the meantime, the son became an apostate, and the apostate’s wife came to collect her ketubah from this money. The father prevailed for two reasons. One reason was that the money was only fit to come, even though the son was alive. I have already elaborated on the contradictions in this reason. But he also ruled in favor of the father because the father immediately acquired the ownerless property.

He wrote that although the woman’s lien was earlier, this is comparable to that which Rabbi Yochanan says: If one says, “Pull this cow, but do not acquire it until after thirty days,” he does not acquire it if it is standing in the public domain, even if he said “from now,” because once he said “until after thirty days,” the acquisition is not completed until after thirty days. So too here, since he specified that payment should be after two years, the obligation was not completed until after two years. Since this is so, the woman’s lien took effect only after two years, and the money had already become ownerless before then. End quote.

In my humble opinion, this reason also requires study, for it is not at all similar to that case, where he explicitly said, “and do not acquire,” and so on. That is not so here. Consider according to his view: If the father died within the two years, how would the lien be completed after his death? If it was not completed during his lifetime, from where would the assets become subject to lien, since they had already fallen before the orphans? Rather, it must be that the lien takes effect immediately, only the time of payment is set for after two years. It is exactly like one who truly borrows and sets the repayment date for a certain time.

Although in siman 66 I cited the Rosh, who wrote similarly regarding ketubah, that is different, because he wrote “when you marry another,” and one can say that the lien is completed one moment before the divorce or death. Even there, were it not for the proof from the Talmud, see there, there would be no room for his words. See siman 93, se'if 22. But here, where it is tied to a period of two years, how is it possible that the lien would be completed if he dies within the time?

In my humble opinion, there is another basis for his ruling, namely clear estimation of intent, that he did not obligate himself with this in mind. This is worse than the dowry case of the Mishnah according to Rabbenu Tam. I later saw that perhaps this is included in his words, where he writes, “Greater than this Maharam wrote,” and so on, concerning one who pledged a dowry for his daughter. See there. It is also possible that this is a very unusual matter, that he would become an apostate, and it is worse. This requires study.

What is implied simply from his words is that if the lien had taken effect immediately, she would collect, and the father could not acquire it from ownerless property because her lien was earlier. This is indeed explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 275, that wherever collection is made from heirs, collection is also made from one who acquired ownerless property. However, this requires some study, because there the Beit Yosef brings a responsum of the Rashba comparing ownerless property to monetary consecration, and therefore it is obvious to him that just as monetary consecration does not remove a lien from movable property, the same is true of ownerless property.

If so, one could say according to the view of all the decisors cited in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 255, that monetary consecration has the law of encumbered property, and an oral loan is not collected from it. Likewise, the Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 115, holds that monetary consecration removes a lien from movable property. See siman 93, se'if 20. If so, perhaps ownerless property too has the law of encumbered property, and debts and ketubah are not collected from encumbered movable property. Alternatively, one can say that ownerless property is worse than monetary consecration, and is like one who accepts a condition, no better than an heir. This requires study in the responsum of the Ramban, which the Beit Yosef hints to here and also there in Choshen Mishpat.

Later, when I merited to see that responsum, I found it in siman 69, and thank God my understanding matched his words. See there, where he concludes as halacha that movable property deposited with another person cannot be used for ketubah collection if the deposit holder acquires it from ownerless property. It sounds there that in this case the enactment of the Geonim does not help, because it is treated like movable property of orphans. But from what they took from the domain of the convert, an ordinary creditor and ketubah creditor collect properly. See there and examine.

The Chelkat Mechokek writes: Since he compares it to a worker’s wages, why does it matter that a time was specified? The Beit Shmuel answers that the Shulchan Aruch is speaking where he instructed this as a deathbed gift. Therefore, if he had not written a time, it would not be considered fit to come. This distinction is also necessary in Mahari Weil, for he wrote regarding the ruling of Maharil, which is the second law written by the Rama, “or where he instructed,” and so on, and his reason is that since the money was deposited in the hands of others for three years, it was considered fit to come. This implies that if it had been an inheritance deposited simply in the hands of others, and he had not instructed them not to give it to him, even though it had not yet come into his hands, it would not be considered fit to come. This is because immediately upon death, wherever it is, it stands in the possession of the heir. The same applies to a deathbed gift, which is considered a rabbinic inheritance.

In any case, we learn from this that a biblical inheritance is certainly considered actually held even before it reaches the heir’s hand, except where he instructed not to give it until a later time. See siman 90.

The Chelkat Mechokek writes that one who is precise in Mahari Weil’s language will see that he compares it to a worker’s wages, and that it is unlike a loan, where the money had been in his hands and his right did not disappear even when he lent it to others. This is the language of Mahari Weil. One may infer from this language that if he lent the money before he married, then married and died, she would not collect from this loan, because at the time he married her the money was no longer with him for the lien to take effect upon it. The Ba'er Heitev brings this in the name of Maharam Trani, though I do not have it, and in the name of the Rama.

I examined the matter, and it is only that he forced himself regarding the core reason why the woman collects from a loan, even though for other matters it is considered fit to come. Perhaps they did not accept the Rosh’s reason based on the law of Rabbi Natan, because a ketubah is not given for collection during his lifetime. But according to us, who rely entirely on Rabbi Natan’s law, as explained in the Beit Yosef and Chelkat Mechokek, se'if katan 19, one who examines Choshen Mishpat, siman 86, in the Beit Yosef, will see explicitly that in this manner the opposite is true, and everyone agrees that Rabbi Natan’s law applies. Since when he borrowed from Reuven, Levi was already obligated to him, when he entered into obligation to Reuven, he brought Levi’s obligation with him. This is unlike a loan made after he was already obligated to Reuven, which is similar to our case, where he lends after marriage. In that case there is a dispute whether Rabbi Natan’s law applies.

If so, from the plain language of the decisors regarding loan in this se'if, it is certainly clear that in every case she collects. The reason of the Mordechai regarding workers’ wages appears to me to be as explained in Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, that he follows the “some say” view in the Ba'al HaTerumot. The Mishneh LaMelech writes: The author of Panim Meirot also wrote regarding that Mordechai about workers’ wages that perhaps the Rambam disagrees, because both a creditor and a woman collect from workers’ wages even though they had not come into his hand, since from the time the employer became obligated, he also became obligated to his creditor through Rabbi Natan’s law. See there. This law is not agreed upon, for there is an opinion that Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply to wages, as the Ba'al HaTerumot brings in sha'ar 51. End quote. This view is also mentioned in Beit Yosef, Choshen Mishpat, siman 86, and in Shulchan Aruch the ruling is not like it.

According to this, the Rama requires study, for here he rules plainly like the Mordechai, while there he rules plainly that even regarding wages Rabbi Natan’s law applies. Perhaps regarding ketubah this is considered two leniencies, and we do not apply them: first, applying Rabbi Natan’s law, and second, saying that Rabbi Natan’s law applies to wages.

According to the wording copied there in the Beit Yosef, namely: “There is an opinion that we apply Rabbi Natan’s law only to a debt that he lent or became obligated through commerce, but regarding a debt of wages or a grant, we do not rule like Rabbi Natan, for the verse expounds ‘to him,’ and not to his creditor. This shows that Rabbi Natan’s law applies only to a debt through a loan or commerce. According to us, his view is logical regarding a grant but not regarding wages.” End quote. According to this, we do not need the distinction of Mahari Weil and the Chelkat Mechokek at all. Rather, whenever the debt resembles wages, meaning it is not through commerce but a gift that the obligating party undertook, there is no difference whether he became obligated before marriage or afterwards. In every case, Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply according to this view. Regarding ketubah we follow this view. But an actual loan or commerce is different, and again there is no distinction. It is never considered fit to come because of Rabbi Natan’s law.

However, in Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh, in his long responsum, toward the end of the passage beginning “Furthermore, I say,” he also writes: The Mordechai at the beginning of Yesh Nochalin writes that workers’ wages that never came into his hand and were not collected appear to be fit to come. The Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 104, reads “for the husband.” One should examine this: since he rules that a loan is not considered fit to come, why are workers’ wages considered fit to come? It appears that he holds like the “some say” view in Ba'al HaTerumot, that Rabbi Natan’s law applies only to a debt that he lent, or that became obligated to him because something came from his hand into the other’s hand, so that when his money reached the other, he already acquired a right in it. But with a debt of wages or a grant, the second borrower does not enter under the lien of the first borrower.

According to this language, it sounds that this “some say” opinion distinguishes between a loan because of the same reasoning as the dispute of Mahari Weil and the Chelkat Mechokek. If so, perhaps the view of the Mordechai is specifically that a debt of wages earned after he became obligated to his lender, similar to a grant, is where Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply, because two deficiencies exist: this money never came into his hand for a lien to take effect on it, and when he borrowed, the one who later owed him these wages was not yet obligated to him. See the Mordechai in chapter HaManiach. But if he earned wages before he married, then at the time of marriage he brought this lien with him, and Rabbi Natan’s law properly applies, and he agrees that the ketubah is collected from it.

Therefore, regarding a loan, the ruling is stated plainly that the ketubah is collected from it, even from what he lent before marrying her. In this case, although no lien took effect on the money itself, nevertheless at the time he married he brought this obligation with him. This is unlike wages earned after marriage, where there are two drawbacks. If so, Mahari Weil has no proof for his ruling, for one can say that the debt of his father, which was owed to him before he married, is such that at the time of marriage he brought this lien with him, and Rabbi Natan’s law properly applies.

But it is more reasonable that the Mordechai follows the “some say” view in the language copied by the Beit Yosef, and therefore regarding wages he rules plainly that one never collects. The actual text of Ba'al HaTerumot requires study, because it is practically relevant in the case of Mahari Weil, where the husband died and the woman came to collect her ketubah from the father’s dowry debt that he had obligated himself to before the wedding. See Maharil, siman 74.

Although our ketubot have the law of ordinary debts, and it is already agreed in Choshen Mishpat that Rabbi Natan’s law applies to a loan in every case, the practical difference would be where he added on his own more than the one-third addition to the dowry, which certainly has the law of ketubah. Regarding ketubah, it appears proper to rule like the Mordechai and the Rama, of blessed memory, because we do not apply two leniencies in ketubah. Perhaps even regarding the one-third addition itself there are two leniencies, since the Beit Yosef holds that it has the law of ketubah, and he wrote regarding Maharik, “his words do not appear to me.” This requires study.

“Some say that if he died,” and so on, “since they were encumbered.” See Darkei Moshe, and see Chelkat Mechokek’s explanation of the Rama, of blessed memory, according to the answer of Maharil.

What the Rama wrote, “but in a place,” and so on, “she collects from there,” means only what is actually held beyond the amount of the ketubah. In my humble opinion, if this is his intent, the main point is missing. Furthermore, although the language of Maharam somewhat implies a distinction, that if there is actually held property beyond the ketubah, the ketubah lien does not harm the remainder by making it “fit to come,” since he wrote “even if there is actually held property beyond her ketubah,” see siman 102 for the explanation, nevertheless the Gemara’s difficulty in chapter Chezkat HaBatim, page 55, “If so, you have nullified the inheritance of a firstborn son,” does not imply this. Rather, wherever the lien has the power to make property “fit to come,” as long as he did not remove it during his lifetime, all his property, even that which exceeds the debt, is considered fit to come. See there, and see the responsum of Rabbi Aharon Sasson, siman 149. See below, siman 163.

It appears from the Darkei Moshe that he did not consider the answer of Maharil to be primary, and was satisfied with his own answer alone, since he writes, “Perhaps here it speaks in a place that follows the people of Judea.” According to Maharil’s answer, this would not be necessary. Here, he is speaking of a place where they cannot remove her by paying her ketubah, and specifically because such a maintenance lien has no fixed amount it is called fit to come. The law in siman 90, however, speaks in a place where they can remove her, and the maintenance lien is no stronger than a ketubah and other debts, and the property is therefore called actually held. For this reason, in siman 90 the Rama writes “even if they were encumbered to the ketubah,” but omits “and to maintenance,” unlike the Mordechai, because regarding maintenance the matter is not fixed and depends on local custom.

However, this requires study, for the Maharam in chapter 25 brings proofs. One proof is that otherwise the inheritance of the firstborn son would be nullified. This is the language of the Gemara and Tosafot, as above. According to the Rama’s answer, this is difficult: If so, in a place that follows the people of Galilee, the inheritance of the firstborn son would be nullified.

This proof can perhaps be rejected. One may say that specifically regarding the father, who was already in possession of the property, liens that come upon the property do not remove it from his possession and make it considered fit to come with respect to him. It is properly called actually held regarding the firstborn. But regarding the heir, where there are liens on the property from the deceased, it is not called actually held in his hand because of the earlier liens from the deceased that prevent the heir’s possession from taking effect. This kind of reasoning is found in Bava Metzia 70b, in Tosafot, s.v. ve'i, regarding firstborn inheritance in relation to a non-Jew’s lien.

But he brings another proof from chapter Mi Shemet, from that which we learned: If the daughter married and died, his widow is maintained from his property. It is proven that the property remains with the husband. Why do we not say that after the daughter’s death, it should return to her heirs, since a husband does not inherit property that is only fit to come? Rather, it must be that nevertheless the property is considered actually held by the daughter. According to the answer of the Darkei Moshe, this is difficult. How can we say that the baraita is established according to the people of Judea, against the halacha?

However, there is truly reason to wonder: Since we rule in siman 93, se'if 15, that the orphans must bring proof, according to the Gemara’s simple assumption that the property is in her possession, how can it be actually held for two parties, the widow and the heirs? It is obvious and logical that for the heirs it is only fit to come. If so, see siman 93, se'if 19, and siman 95, se'if 1. According to the second answer of Tosafot, which I have proven is the main answer, one can say that what the Gemara assumes simply, that the property is in her possession, is only according to the people of Galilee, whose view is halacha. If so, we abandon a forced reading of the verse and establish it according to its straightforward meaning. Therefore, the baraita in Mi Shemet follows the people of Judea, and the property is not in the widow’s possession but in the daughter’s possession. If it followed the people of Galilee, there would be the difficulty above. Therefore Maharam ruled plainly that they are truly fit to come for the heir, because they are in the possession of the widow. The answer of the Rama fits well.

In truth, Maharil’s answer is very difficult to say about Maharam. In chapter 25 he expanded the explanation with proofs, yet in chapter Na'arah he would write a law based only on his own reasoning, with no proof, and that reasoning is weak and forced. It is difficult to know from where he derived it. However, in siman 111, se'if 8, I brought a proof for Maharil’s answer from the Rosh.

The answer of the Shiyurei Knesset HaGedolah cited in the Beit Shmuel, that here it speaks where she took an oath, also cannot settle the matter, for Maharam tied his reason to the maintenance lien, and regarding maintenance the conclusion of most decisors in siman 93 is that she does not need an oath.

Second gloss: “All of this,” and so on, “but dowry and other nichsei tzon barzel have the law of other debts.” The Beit Shmuel writes that it sounds that he holds that an ordinary creditor takes from property that is only fit to come. In my humble opinion, this is not necessary, for perhaps if he had waited to gloss until the end of the se'if, we would always say that the main distinction is because of inferior land. The Shach has already written that the Rama himself did not decide this matter in his responsum, and so it appears to the reader there.

However, one must examine what practical difference there is in this dispute whether a creditor collects from what is only fit to come. The Shach cites from Hagahot HaRosh a practical case: where property fell to him after death from his mother’s father, which his paternal brothers can inherit only through him, a creditor collects from it if a creditor collects from what is only fit to come. I have already wondered greatly at this, for we hold that a son does not inherit his mother in the grave in order to pass the inheritance to his paternal brothers.

The Bach there apparently therefore wrote the case regarding a son who inherited his father, who died during the lifetime of the son’s maternal grandfather, and the father was the borrower. Even this is astonishing, for here too the verse “in place of your fathers shall be your sons” applies. Although one could force an answer and say that the verse was stated only regarding the father’s family, this requires study, for from where do we know such a reasoning from the Talmud? The language of Hagahot HaRosh also does not tolerate his explanation and emendation.

Furthermore, since the Tur in Choshen Mishpat, siman 211, regarding cancellation of a sale, does not distinguish where “in place of your fathers” does not apply, such as where the property fell from his mother’s father, that he cannot cancel the sale, this implies that there is no distinction. If the practical difference is regarding wages for work, I already wrote in this se'if that according to us, since we hold that Rabbi Natan’s law applies even to workers’ wages, a creditor collects even from workers’ wages. It is specifically regarding ketubah, because of two leniencies, that it is considered fit to come.

It appears to me that there is a major practical difference. It is stated in siman 90, se'if 1, in the gloss, that a chatzi zachar document gives the husband inheritance rights in the power of that document through his offspring. See what I wrote there. If this son was himself indebted, in my humble opinion even during his lifetime, after he already inherited this document from his mother, there is no aspect of Rabbi Natan’s lien. First, the maternal grandfather is not obligated to pay this debt until one moment before his death, and even this he can nullify by not leaving property to be inherited by his sons. See the words of the Ritva cited by the Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 86, se'if katan 11. Here one can say that everyone agrees Rabbi Natan’s law does not apply, since he can consume and destroy everything and be exempt from his obligation.

In my poverty, I am even uncertain whether this document can be collected during his lifetime in the manner explained in Choshen Mishpat, siman 101, and it seems close to me that it cannot. All the more so where this son died and his father inherits him with property that is only fit to come, namely what his mother’s brothers give him from their father’s inheritance, and the debt is automatically discharged, it is called fit to come. One can then discuss whether, if an ordinary creditor collects from fit-to-come property, his father is obligated to pay his debts from it. In this case, certainly the phrase “through,” and so on, does not apply. If a creditor does not collect from fit-to-come property, he is exempt.

Perhaps, in order not to make Hagahot HaRosh and those who copied it into people who erred in an explicit Mishnah, one can force an explanation and say that they intended this kind of case, where if his father died, the law would concern the paternal brothers.

Recently, the book Urim VeTumim came into my hands, and I found that he cited my difficulty in the name of the responsa of Rabbi Avraham ben Chaim. Thank God, I was very pleased. What he wrote in that book to resolve it based on the words of Maharam here in this se'if, in the first gloss in the name of “some say,” that if Yaakov died during the lifetime of his son Reuven, and so on, Reuven’s widow does not collect her ketubah from this, and he turned the answer of Maharil to a different intent, namely that even regarding a creditor this fit-to-come status applies, does not appear in Maharil. The words of Maharil are because it is extracting money, and in Nachalat Shivah too Maharil’s answer is mentioned only regarding ketubah.

One who studies Maharil, siman 74, will see even more explicitly that his words were said only regarding the ketubah of the son’s wife, for he gives another reason, that it was not given for collection during the lifetime. From this he initially made a practical distinction. According to his view, it is certainly understandable, because it is obvious to me that where the son lived after his father, the grandson can no longer say, “I come through my father’s father,” for how could he be considered the heir of his grandfather when the grandfather left an heir? Only afterwards, in order to sharpenly explain the gloss, his spirit carried him elsewhere, that the heir can say, “I do not want this benefit.” See there, for these are words that the mind does not accept.

Even with all his words, the language of the gloss, “property fell to him from his mother’s father,” cannot bear his explanation at all. According to his explanation, the inheritance fell to him during his lifetime, only after his death the wife of his mother’s father died and her lien was removed after his death. Heaven forbid to say that such a sharp reading ever entered the mind of the gloss. See there and examine.

“As other debts.” It is clear that in any case she is not better than an ordinary creditor, and from orphans she certainly collects only from inferior land. This is also in the Maggid Mishneh.

In this respect, the Rashba in Gittin 50, s.v. amar Ravina, requires study. He writes that at first glance it would imply, meaning that dowry would not be limited to inferior land, because perhaps she would refrain and not marry. This is learned from our sugya, because it asks, “Let it be because the guarantor of a ketubah is not obligated.” If the Mishnah also speaks about nichsei tzon barzel, what is the question? Certainly a guarantor for dowry is obligated, because he caused her loss. This is difficult.

Although according to the conclusion, that the Mishnah speaks about collecting from him personally, it is not speaking about nichsei tzon barzel, because of the concern of closing the door, meaning that she would refrain from marrying, nevertheless from orphans it certainly should not be collected better than an ordinary creditor, as above. If so, according to the explanation that the case concerns a guarantor and because every place where he cannot collect from him, from whom does he collect? From the orphans. He too, when he collects, collects only from inferior land. The difficulty still remains, for the Gemara could have answered that it speaks of a guarantor for dowry, and nevertheless the law is inferior land for the above reason. See the beginning of siman 93.

It must be that the Rashba held that according to this approach, where the concern of refraining from marriage applies to dowry, even from orphans its law is medium-quality land, and it is stronger than other creditors. The reason can be explained: An ordinary creditor does not consider that the borrower may die, because the debt is collectable during life. But a ketubah is not given for collection during life, so she certainly considers this possibility, and there is concern that she will refrain even regarding orphans.

However, the plain implication of the decisors is not this. Rather, it appears that they hold that although it is not given for collection during life, nevertheless she does not assume that he will die before her, or else they did not differentiate. The whole sugya also implies that from orphans it is obvious that she collects only from inferior land. One could force an explanation that this is truly the reason the Gemara concludes, “Actually, from orphans, and ketubah of a woman was necessary to state.” One might have thought that because of grace the Sages were lenient for her even regarding orphans, because she does consider the possibility. Therefore it teaches that “more than,” and so on, and the concern of grace does not apply. Rabbi Meir, who holds that grace does apply, indeed holds that even from orphans she collects from medium-quality land.

However, what the Gemara says, “Come and hear, from the very beginning of the enactment,” proves in my humble opinion like the view of most decisors. Otherwise, what is the question, “If you would think it speaks about orphans, that is because they are orphans”? What difficulty is that? Regarding a woman, where she considers that he may die, even regarding orphans we need the reason of “more than,” and so on. Without that, even by orphans there would be concern that she would refrain, and one could always say that specifically from orphans she collects inferior land, but from him personally she collects medium-quality land. Rather, it must be that regarding orphans there is no logic that she should be better than an ordinary creditor, and there is no need for the reason of “more than,” and so on.

Although even according to all decisors, according to Rashi’s explanation that “because of grace” means so that men will find favor in women’s eyes and women will marry them, this refutation itself requires study. The Gemara already answered that the reason the Mishnah taught that a woman’s ketubah from orphans is collected from inferior land is so that we should not say like Rabbi Meir, that even from orphans she collects from medium-quality land, because for the sake of grace they were lenient even regarding orphans. It teaches that we do not say this. It then needed a reason why we do not say this regarding orphans, because “more than,” and so on. So perhaps it is specifically from orphans.

This works well according to Rabbenu Chananel’s explanation, s.v. mishum china, for even Rabbi Meir agrees to the reasoning of “more than,” which is strong enough to cancel this reason of grace. Only Rabbi Meir is concerned for grace so that men will hurry to marry them, and the first Tanna is not concerned for that at all. If he were concerned for that, the reasoning of “more than” would not cancel it at all. The reason of “more than” only cancels Rashi’s type of grace, as explained by Maharshal at the beginning of the chapter in Tosafot, s.v. uketubat ishah. See Sefer Kikayon DeYonah.

If so, the objection is proper: if the case is from orphans, why speak of the reason “more than”? Even if Rashi’s type of grace applied, it would be because they are orphans, and Rashi’s grace is no stronger than closing the door for the borrower, which nevertheless does not apply to orphans according to all. But according to Rashi, of blessed memory, where we see that Rabbi Meir disagrees for this very reason of grace even regarding orphans, this requires study.

However, according to Rashi one can say that even aside from this, one should examine why the Gemara does not object from Rabbi Shimon, who also said, “Why is a woman’s ketubah collected from inferior land? Because more than,” and so on. Rather, from Rabbi Shimon there is no question, as Tosafot explains in the Mishnah, in the above passage, and as I asked, that the reason “more than” is needed to exclude Rabbi Meir. This is not so in this baraita, which I explained as speaking from the beginning of the enactment. The meaning is that Rabbi Shimon says that even from him personally she collects inferior land because “more than,” and the Sages were concerned that if it were collected from medium-quality land, people would separate from them if they had to sell medium-quality land, and they were concerned so that men would hurry to marry them. So too Tosafot writes in Ketubot 10, s.v. ho'il, giving this reason regarding best-quality land and movable property according to Rabbi Meir. It emerges that this is the main reason for the enactment. But if the case were from orphans, that would be because they are orphans, which is the main enactment, and the reason of “more than” would be needed only to answer the reason of grace, so that she should not be stronger through grace than closing the door for the borrower, which Rabbi Meir is concerned for even regarding orphans more than closing the door for the borrower.

If so, even according to the Rashba, of blessed memory, one could force an answer in this way. But from the beginning of his words, where he writes, “Therefore, whether from orphans or from him personally, it is inferior land, for there is no concern of closing the door before marriage, because more than,” and so on, it implies that he does not explain the phrase “from the beginning of their enactment” this way.

One should also not say that nevertheless there is no difficulty against him, for I already forced an explanation of the sugya according to him: “Actually from orphans, and ketubah of a woman was necessary,” and it teaches that the reason of grace does not apply to her because of “more than,” and so on. This is difficult, for if so, according to Mar Zutra, according to the first Tanna, why is the law of orphans truly different from the law of collection from him personally? It must be that Mar Zutra means minor orphans, and because the Sages made an enactment for orphans. The Gemara therefore properly asks: “That is because they are orphans,” meaning, how can it say “from the beginning of their enactment because of ‘more than,’” when this is not sufficient, and the main reason is that because it is from orphans the Sages made an enactment for them? Rather, learn from it that it is from him personally, and that “more than,” and so on, does not apply to women as closing the door does, and truly there is no distinction. Even from him personally, it is inferior land.

But this is not so, because then the original difficulty returns: this does not work for dowry, where the concern that she will refrain applies and it is like closing the door. Nevertheless, the Gemara could still have answered according to Mar Zutra that it speaks of a guarantor for dowry, and since it is from orphans, the Sages made an enactment for them even where closing the door applies. This requires study.

“One who was married.” This is the language of the Rambam, and the Tur copied the language of the Mishnah: One who was married to two women and sold his field, and the first wife wrote to the buyer, “I have no claim or dealings with you,” in a manner that is effective. This means, as the Gemara answers, where they made an acquisition from her, and also where she wrote to the first buyer but did not sign for him.

As for why the Gemara did not answer that the buyer first bought from the woman, Tosafot answer that the language of the Mishnah implies that after the field was sold, she wrote to the buyer. One should not ask why the Tanna did not teach the case where he bought from the woman first, because he wished to teach incidentally the law of two buyers, where the concern of “I acted only to give satisfaction” does not apply. The law of one who bought from the woman first had already been taught in its place.

In Sefer Torat Yekutiel, in the introduction, he writes that one must examine the Gemara’s question: “When she wrote, what of it? But the condition,” and so on. That case is not comparable, for there it discusses a field owned by partners, and it is correct that he has said nothing, as Rashi writes at the beginning of HaKotev, because there is no language of gift there. There, if he did not speak in the language of sale, it is nothing. But here, if he removed himself from the buyer, why should we not say that it is like waiver? If a creditor waives to the buyer that he has no claim against him, would it not help?

One must simply say that certainly if he waived to the buyer so that he would have no claim or dealings against him, it would help like any waiver. Why should waiving to the buyer be worse than waiving to the borrower? Rather, here one must necessarily establish the case as one where he removed himself from the body of the field. Otherwise, why would the collection cycle return repeatedly? See there.

I am astonished that he did not let his ears hear what his mouth had uttered in this very discussion, for he cited the language of the Rambam in chapter 19 of Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh: One who borrowed from one person and afterwards transferred his property to two people, and the creditor wrote to the second buyer, “I have no claim or dealings with you,” and they made an acquisition from him, he cannot seize from the first buyer. These are the words of the baraita in HaKotev, page 95, except that the Rambam added “and they made an acquisition from him” in order to resolve the difficulty: when he wrote to him, what of it? There, no repeated collection cycle is mentioned.

The Mordechai at the beginning of HaKotev sensed the basic difficulty and wrote: If you ask, even with a married woman too, why can she not remove herself, since waiver does not require an acquisition? One can answer that since the produce has not yet come into the world, she cannot remove herself. End quote. In this answer, he means that it is like waiving something that has not yet come into the world.

Here too, according to that answer, since he cannot seize from the buyer as long as he finds free assets, it is called a waiver before the obligation has arisen, and is like waiving something that has not yet come into the world. Nevertheless, since the land is already in the world, and he has a lien upon it if the creditor does not find other property, it is considered as though he has acquired a right in it, and removal does not help except through an acquisition. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 209, end of se'if 4 in the gloss, and the Sema, and examine carefully.

Although presumably if the lender waives the guarantor’s obligation, it helps, even though he too cannot come against him before going to the borrower, one can say that a guarantor is different. His own person is a guarantor for whatever may be found, so it is considered more as though he is already obligated to him and the obligation is already in the world. This is unlike a buyer, upon whom there is no lien at all except on this land.

Alternatively, one may forcefully say that the concept of waiver does not apply at all to a specific object where he has no lien on the person himself. Our Sages, of blessed memory, were more expert than we are in knowing how and when the language of waiver, removal, gift, or sale applies. From the second answer of the Mordechai it sounds that he objected because of the weakness of the phrase “claim and dealings,” and one may infer from this that if he used explicit language of waiver, it would help there. One may say the same here. This requires study, for in my humble opinion it appears as I have written.
