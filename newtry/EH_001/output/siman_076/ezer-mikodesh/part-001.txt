<b>Section 2, "donkey drivers."</b> From what the Sages said, that the conjugal interval for donkey drivers, camel drivers, and sailors is fixed, even though they sometimes separate from their homes not for present livelihood, and even if they have great wealth sufficient for years without needing to leave home, nevertheless whenever he acts according to the pattern of earning a livelihood, and his wife did not object to that pattern, the conjugal obligation applies only according to that pattern. It appears that although we sometimes suspect that permission she gives at his request is not wholehearted but merely outward, regarding the establishment of a livelihood pattern we do not suspect this. Whenever she knows that he is now adopting this livelihood pattern, we say that by not objecting she certainly accepts it.

The same applies to teachers who leave home to earn their livelihood by teaching outside their city, and to merchants delayed on their routes when this follows the merchants' way because of possible profit, avoiding loss, or waiting for wagons to travel in the normal manner. Merchants of their standing do not customarily walk from city to city, and this too affects the form of livelihood; one established as a merchant in a somewhat honorable manner succeeds more easily and profitably. Business depends on the favor and grace of the transaction. All of this concerns the structure of his household livelihood, and is like the livelihood of sailors; one should not distinguish because he has substantial wealth. Since the lighter conjugal obligation was established for such a livelihood, it is thereby lightened automatically.

<b>Seif 5 in the gloss: he may not delay more than a month.</b> The measure of thirty days in Even HaEzer siman 76 appears not to distinguish whether there are no more than thirty days from one conjugal period to the next, or whether, because she is not tahor, there will be many days of delay before he leaves and, knowing the pattern of her veset, many further delays after he returns. Since Chazal stated the measure simply as thirty days, that is the rule. Nevertheless this still requires some further study, for perhaps the days of tumah are deducted from the thirty days, or at least one occurrence is deducted, like the ordinary veset in which there are always days of tumah within thirty days, while if there are more than that they are not deducted. Reason too suggests that one occurrence should be deducted. Even so, with a pregnant woman and the like the measure is thirty days. This still requires further study.
