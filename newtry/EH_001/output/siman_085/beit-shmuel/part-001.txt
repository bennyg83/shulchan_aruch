<b>נצ"ב</b>. ר"ל הנכסים אשר יש להם קיום כצאן ברזל משום דכל אחריו' מוטל עליו, ומלוג ע"ש שאוכל כעין מליג' הראש שתולשים השער הגמי"י, ויש לאשה ג' דברים כתובה ונצ"ב ונ"מ והפרש בין נצ"ב לבין הכתובה עיין סי' נ"ג מ"ש ועיין במגיד ר"פ ט"ז ה"א ואפילו נצ"ב דאינו רשאי למוכרם מפני שבח בית אביה ואין לו אלא פירות מ"מ יש לו יתרון טפי מנ"מ דפירי פירות הכל שלו ועיין בריב"ש סי' ק"נ:
**** ENGLISH ****
(א) <b>Nichsei tzon barzel.</b> This means property that has ongoing existence, like iron sheep, because all liability rests upon him. Melog is so called because he eats in the manner of plucking a head, where one removes the hair. Hagahot Maimoniyot. A woman has three things: ketubah, nichsei tzon barzel, and nichsei melog. Regarding the difference between nichsei tzon barzel and the ketubah, see what I wrote in siman 53. See the Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 16, halacha 1. Even nichsei tzon barzel, which he is not permitted to sell because of the honor of her father’s house, and he has only the produce, nevertheless he has a greater advantage in them than in nichsei melog, for the produce of the produce all belongs to him. See Rivash, siman 150.

<b>ואם פשע הבעל וכו'</b>. משמע מגניבה ואבידה פטור מטעם דלא קיבל עליו שמירה אלא מפשיעה אינו פטור מטעם זה כיון דהוא משתמש בהם אלא מטעם שמירה בבעלים הוא פטור ונצ"ב הוא חייב אף על גב דהוי שמירה בבעלים כיון דמקבל  בפירוש אחריות מתחייב את עצמו, והטור כ' דין זה בשם ר"י דהוא פטור מטעם שמירה בבעלים והקשה הב"י למה תלה הטור הדין בדברי ר"י הא מבואר כן בסוגי' פ' השואל ולמ"ש ניחא דאת' לאשמעינן בשם ר"י דאינו פטור משום דלא קיבל שמירה עליו ונ"מ הלוקח בהמה לשלשים יום דאינו מקבל שמירה מ"מ פסק ר"י דהוא ש"ח:
**** ENGLISH ****
(ג) <b>And if the husband was negligent, etc.</b> It implies that for theft and loss he is exempt because he did not accept guarding upon himself. But from negligence, he is not exempt for that reason, since he uses them. Rather, he is exempt because it is guarding while the owner is with him. Regarding nichsei tzon barzel he is liable, even though it is guarding while the owner is with him, because since he explicitly accepts liability, he obligated himself. The Tur wrote this law in the name of Ri, that he is exempt because of guarding while the owner is with him. The Beit Yosef asks why the Tur made the law depend on Ri’s words, since this is explicit in the sugya in Perek HaShoel. According to what I wrote, it is understandable, for he came to teach us in the name of Ri that he is not exempt because he did not accept guarding upon himself. The practical difference is one who takes an animal for thirty days: he does not accept guarding, nevertheless Ri ruled that he is an unpaid guardian.

<b>ואם יש פירות בקרקע</b>. עיין בת"ה סי' שי"ב שם מבואר לר"ה ורמב"ם פ"ג ה"ש וסמ"ג ס"ל קרקע שאין בה פירות צריך הרשאה ומשמע אפילו עומדת לגדל פירות צריך הרשאה ולא כסמ"ע בסי' קכ"ב שכתב כשעומדת לגדל פירות א"צ הרשאה אף לדעת ר"ה, ולהרא"ש א"צ הרשאה ומעות לכ"ע א"צ הרשאה, וכן קרקע כשאין בה פירות ורוצה למכור אותה וליקח דבר שעושה פירות א"צ הרשאה כיון שמגיע לו הפסד, וכל זה איירי בנ"מ אבל נצ"ב א"צ הרשאה אפילו במיתתה ועיין בטח"ה שם וכ"כ בש"ך שם ועיין בר"ן ס"פ השולח וכלל הוא נ"מ ויש בהם פירות דינם איש ואשתו כשותפות מ"ה כשהיא במת' והוא ירד לדין ויצא חייב מחמת עדים שהעידו לא הוי כקבלו' עדות שלא בפני בעל דבר ואם ליתא במתא א"י לקבל עדות שלא בפניה ולגבי פירות נמי הודאת פיו לא מהני אפילו על חלקו שהרי א"י למכור משום רווח ביתו ובנצ"ב מקבלים עדים שלא בפניה אפילו ליתא במתא אבל הודאתו לא מהני כיון די"ל כליי אני נוטל כ"כ הרשב"א בחדושיו ומזה נשמע נמי כשאין פירות בקרקע נ"מ א"י לדון בלא הרשאה דהא הטעם הוא דהם כשותפות, וכן משמע לכאורה אפילו במעו' א"י לירד לדין בלא הרשאה וכן מדייק בתשובת רש"ך ס"ג סי' ס"ז דהרשב"א חולק על דין של הת"ה ויש לדחות:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if there is produce on the land.</b> See Terumat HaDeshen, siman 312, where it is explained that Rabbenu Hai, the Rambam in chapter 3 of Hilchot Sheluchin, and the Semag hold that land that has no produce requires authorization. It implies that even if it stands to grow produce, authorization is required, unlike the Sema in siman 122, who wrote that when it stands to grow produce, authorization is not required even according to Rabbenu Hai. According to the Rosh, authorization is not required, and regarding money, according to all opinions authorization is not required. So too with land that has no produce, if he wants to sell it and buy something that produces fruit, authorization is not required, since he would incur a loss. All this discusses nichsei melog, but nichsei tzon barzel do not require authorization, even after her death. See Tur Choshen Mishpat there, and so wrote the Shach there. See the Ran at the end of Perek HaSholeach.

The general rule is: nichsei melog that have produce are treated, between husband and wife, like a partnership. Therefore, when she is in the town and he entered litigation and was found liable because of witnesses who testified, this is not considered accepting testimony outside the presence of the litigant. But if she is not in the town, one cannot accept testimony outside her presence. Also regarding produce, his own admission is not effective, even regarding his own share, since he cannot sell because of household profit. Regarding nichsei tzon barzel, witnesses may be accepted outside her presence even if she is not in the town, but his admission is not effective, since he can say, "I take my utensils." So wrote the Rashba in his novellae. From this we also learn that when there is no produce on nichsei melog land, he cannot litigate without authorization, for the reason is that they are like partners. So too, it appears at first glance that even with money he cannot enter litigation without authorization. So Responsa Rashach, part 3, siman 67, infers that the Rashba disagrees with the law of Terumat HaDeshen, but one can reject this.

<b>Someone gave her.</b> In Nedarim, Rav and Shmuel disagree, and there are three approaches. The Rash in the name of Rav Amram Gaon wrote that the halacha follows Shmuel, so even if he said plainly, "on condition that the husband has no right," it is effective. This is also the view of the Ramban. So too, if he only said "do with it what you want," it is also effective. The Ran wrote this according to this approach. Tosafot in the first chapter of Kiddushin, page 24, in the name of Rabbenu Tam, ruled that the halacha follows Rav, that he must specifically say, "on condition that the husband has no right except what you put in your mouth," or something similar, where he designates a particular matter. It is explained in Ba'al HaTerumot, gate 6, that if he stipulated "on condition that you do what you want" without the husband’s permission, he does not inherit her. This is also brought in Kesef Mishneh.

There is some basis to infer from Rashi and the Rosh in the above sugya that they do not hold this way, for they wrote regarding what is stated, if one accepted from the woman he returns it to the woman, and if she died he returns it to her husband. We are not concerned that perhaps they gave it to her on condition that the husband has no right, because even if they gave it to her that way, he inherits her. According to what Ba'al HaTerumot wrote, the difficulty still remains: perhaps they gave it to her with a condition that he has no right except what she wants, in which case he does not inherit her. So too I saw in the gloss of the Shach there.

(ו) <b>And she has a migo, etc.</b> She is believed in every case, and even when she conducts business in the house. Perhaps even according to the Rambam’s approach, that he holds whatever is in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog, nevertheless she is believed. Although it is a migo against a presumption, since in any case she is holding it, the issue of a migo against a presumption is an unresolved question, and we do not remove it from her hand, as written nearby. The Rav Rama wrote this law in the name of Responsa Maharam, as written in Darkei Moshe. At first glance this is difficult: how did he learn this law from Responsa Maharam? There the case was that she said, "you gave it to me," which involves brazenness. From where does he know she is believed when she did not say "you gave it to me"? One can answer that there, brazenness was not relevant, because that case involved her rebelling against him, and then she certainly is brazen before him. In the responsum of the Rashba brought by the Beit Yosef, end of seif 7, this law is also explained, but in Responsa Maharam it is explained that even when she conducts business, she is believed with a migo.

However, from Tosafot in this sugya it implies that when she conducts business she is not believed, even though she has a migo. For they wrote that when one who conducts business deposited an item, she is not believed to say it is mine or belongs to so-and-so. There she has a migo, since she could give it to so-and-so, as written in Tosafot there. See Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 62. In the gloss there it is written: a woman who wrote that the money in her hand belongs to others, if it is known that she had money, she is believed. The Sema explains that this discusses where she has no migo. But if she has a migo, she is believed even where it is not known that she has money, even if she conducts business in the house. This follows the view of Maharam. The Shach challenged him and brought these Tosafot, which hold that with one who conducts business, a migo is not effective. But he challenged the Sema for no reason, for the Sema wrote only to explain there the words of the Rav Rama, and the Rav Rama ruled here that she is believed with a migo.

**** ENGLISH ****
(ז) <b>You gave it to me as a gift.</b> The Maggid wrote that she is believed because she is not brazen enough to lie in his presence. Ba'al HaTerumot asked: why is she not believed when she says, "it was given to me as a gift on condition that he has no right," with a migo that she could have said, "you gave it to me"? He answered that we do not say a migo that weakens the claim, for according to her claim he does not inherit her, while when she says "you gave it to me," he does inherit her. The practical difference from this is that if her husband already died, she is believed. The Shach, siman 62, challenged him that his question is not a question, because we do not say migo here since it is a migo against a presumption, for everything in the woman’s hand is presumed to be nichsei melog. He discusses this at length.

This does not appear to me, for although everything is presumed to be nichsei melog, nevertheless she is holding it, and a migo against a presumption is an unresolved question. We do not remove it from her hand when she has a migo. Therefore his question was proper. One should not say that the Shach holds that even what is in her hand is considered as if it were in the husband’s hand and she comes to extract it, for that is not so. If she were considered the one extracting, then the oath would always belong to the husband, for he is the defendant. Rather, certainly, whenever she holds it, she is the defendant, and when she has a migo she is believed and we do not remove it from her hand. The whole law of the Rambam discusses where she has no migo, for when money is found with her, she can no longer give it to another, and although she could have given it earlier, that is a retroactive migo. She also can no longer say that it is hers on condition that he has no right in it. When she says, "you gave it to me," she is believed without a migo, because she cannot be brazen, as written in the name of the Maggid.

However, the Maggid’s reason is still difficult, for we hold that where there is monetary benefit, one can be brazen, as written at the beginning of siman 17. Perhaps he holds that maintaining what is in her hand is different, and in this kind of case we say she cannot be brazen. Also, one should not compare one kind of brazenness to another. It is different when she is his wife; she cannot be brazen even because of monetary benefit, unlike there, where she wants to leave him. In Gidulei Terumah he challenged Ba'al HaTerumot: this is a migo of brazenness, to say "you gave it to me," and we do not say a migo of brazenness. In the Shach’s gloss he wondered about him, for it is explicit in the Ran that we do say a migo of brazenness to exempt from money; only to exempt from an oath we do not say it. The proof is from those goats that ate shelled barley, where he is believed to claim they ate up to their value with a migo that he could have said, "I bought them," even though had he said "I bought them," he would have had to be brazen. It is possible that Gidulei Terumah holds that she is believed to say "you gave it to me" because she cannot be brazen, as the Maggid wrote. Learn from this that although it involves monetary benefit, nevertheless she cannot be brazen. Therefore he properly asked: what is Ba'al HaTerumot’s question? We do not say a migo of brazenness. Necessarily, here, "you gave it to me" is brazenness, even though it involves monetary benefit.

<b>They should not be sold.</b> So ruled the Rambam in chapter 22 according to his version. In the first clause, Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel disagrees and says he should not sell, and the halacha follows him. Regarding olives, Rabbi Yehudah disagrees, and the halacha does not follow him. Regarding whether she can compel him to sell, see the Tur. Where he is not permitted to sell, she cannot compel him to sell. But where he can sell, the Ramah holds that when he does not want to sell, she can compel him to sell. According to this, a goat and other things that he cannot sell, and he uses until the principal is exhausted, she too cannot compel him to sell. Only regarding old slaves, the Ramah holds that he can sell against her will, and she too can sell against his will. According to the Rosh, although he can sell against her will, nevertheless she cannot compel him to sell. This is not comparable to where money fell to her, because there they necessarily must buy something with the money, and then each one can prevent buying something that does not produce fruit. However, something that does not produce fruit and has no use, and is fit to be sold, she too can compel him.

This dispute is learned from the Mishnah: if slaves fell to her, according to the first Tanna he can sell against her will. The Rosh holds that the first Tanna is concerned for his benefit, as written in Tosafot. Although there is a disadvantage for her because of the honor of her father’s house, they are not concerned for her disadvantage, only for his benefit. From this we learn that everywhere we follow his benefit. Even according to the Rambam’s approach, that regarding slaves we are concerned for her benefit because of the honor of her father’s house, nevertheless regarding olives, where the honor of her father’s house is not as noticeable, as written in Perishah, he ruled that we hold like the first Tanna. It automatically follows that we are always concerned for his benefit, except where the honor of her father’s house is recognizable. According to the Ramah, what the first Tanna says, "he should sell," means that either one can compel the sale, and she too can compel him. According to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, he cannot compel her because of the honor of her father’s house. According to the Rosh, one must say that what is taught regarding a goat and other things, that he eats until the principal is exhausted, means if he wants, he eats; and if he wants, one can say that he may sell. So too it appears in Tosafot, s.v. "the rule." If an old tree fell to her, he is not permitted to cut it down, as written in Perek HaMekabel. The Ran wrote that this is because the exhaustion is visible.

(ב) <b>The offspring of nichsei melog.</b> We are not concerned for death, lest the maidservant die and the principal be exhausted. The offspring of the offspring, the Rif holds, belongs to the woman; Tosafot and the Rosh hold it belongs to the husband. Although Chazal did not enact produce of produce for the husband, here it is produce from produce of the body in which the husband has acquired rights, unlike produce of produce that comes from the outside. The Rosh further wrote that if the mother dies while the child is still alive, the child fills the place of its mother and again becomes principal.
