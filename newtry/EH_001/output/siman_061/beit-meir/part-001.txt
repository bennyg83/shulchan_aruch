**** ENGLISH ****
<b>"She is still as betrothed." — Ran's challenge; Rambam vs. Rosh on niddah's chuppah; acquisitions vs. permission for intimacy; Ravad, Maggid Mishneh, Lechem Mishneh; challenge to Rosh and Ran from forbidden marriages.</b>
Rather, for all matters she is as betrothed. The Ran raised a challenge from the rule that there is chuppah for those forbidden in marriage. The Rambam's own language is as follows: "Once the betrothed woman has entered the chuppah, she is permitted to him whenever he wishes, and she is fully his wife for all purposes; from the time she enters the chuppah she is called married, even though he has not been intimate with her — provided she is fit for intimacy. But if she was a niddah, even though she entered the chuppah and he secluded with her, the marriage is not complete and she is still as betrothed." The Maggid Mishneh wrote: "And it is written in the Halachot — therefore it does not acquire, and Rabbenu's view is that the same applies to all matters: a niddah's chuppah does not acquire — not for inheritance, not for becoming impure for her" — end of his words.

It seems to me that the Shulchan Aruch's language is not precise, and it only means that for purposes of acquisitions she is as betrothed — but it concedes that in a niddah's chuppah she has left the prohibition of a bride (kalah), and once she purifies he may be intimate without specific marital intent, since she is called "one who entered the chuppah," and so too in the Gemara she is called "one who entered the chuppah" — she is thus permitted through chuppah and kiddushin. This is why the Rambam — after writing that after the chuppah she is permitted — continues: "And from when she enters the chuppah she is called married" — meaning what the Mishnah states: "A married woman has an advantage over him, the husband, etc." — and regarding this he concludes that if she was not fit for intimacy the marriage is not completed so that she be called "married"; and similarly she is as betrothed for purposes of the acquisitions that depend on the status of marriage. This is different from the permission for intimacy, which depends only on chuppah and kiddushin.

This resolves what the Rosh wrote — and the attentive reader will understand — for it is obvious to him that a niddah's chuppah is a chuppah, and this follows from the fact that it is called "chuppah" in all events. According to what I wrote, this is clear. So too is proven from what the Rambam wrote: "She shall not marry until she is purified" — and the Maggid Mishneh explained his reason; so too the Beit Yosef in Yoreh De'ah siman 192 gives a reason. The Rosh wrote: "And it appears his proof is from what is at the beginning of our tractate (Ketubot)." And one must ask: since according to those who hold that a niddah's chuppah is still only as betrothed — obviously she cannot marry, since this marriage amounts to nothing, and once she is purified she would need another chuppah — why then mention it using the expression "she shall not marry ab initio"? And "if one did violate the rule"? It is obvious the chuppah is complete regarding the permission for intimacy — only for purposes of acquisitions does the question arise; and he cautions ab initio for the stated reasons.

This is proven by the Maggid Mishneh's comment on Rambam ch. 11 of Hilkhot Ishut, regarding the law of the seven clean days required by a woman who has been asked to marry — "if she married a Torah scholar she is permitted to marry immediately." The Ravad challenged: "And moreover, how would a forbidden chuppah be performed? For a bride who became a niddah is forbidden in seclusion." The Maggid Mishneh responded: "This is no challenge, since this woman is fully permitted by Torah law — the Sages merely imposed a stringency, and in this respect it is not a forbidden chuppah and is not analogous to a bride who became a niddah, who is a definite niddah." See the Kesef Mishneh there. The Beit Shmuel at the end wrote: "And if she had chuppah within the seven clean days, the Maggid Mishneh holds it is a chuppah even according to the Rambam, since it is only a concern" — the implication being that this chuppah is effective for all the acquisitions.

In my humble view, this is not the implication of the Maggid Mishneh at all. For would the chuppah be considered fit for intimacy merely because the prohibition is rabbinic? Rather, his intent is regarding the Ravad's challenge: "How would a forbidden chuppah be performed, since seclusion is forbidden with her?" — to this he responds that she is permitted in seclusion to a Torah scholar, since she is not certainly a niddah. But he concedes that a chuppah not fit for intimacy is still called such for purposes of acquisitions — only that in this marriage he has called her his wife through chuppah and kiddushin and seclusion — which is permitted to him — and she is "bread in his basket." But if one holds that a chuppah not fit for intimacy does not constitute chuppah even for the permission of intimacy, then obviously one must ask what these nissu'in accomplished — and if so, the Ran's challenge is not so much a challenge after all, since the chuppah is valid for the permission of intimacy; it is therefore appropriate that it should also disqualify her from terumah. So too wrote the Lechem Mishneh in his second approach.

In my humble view, the Ran should have responded better from the Rambam's own ruling based on the Tosefta: "One who marries those subject to negative prohibitions — he inherits her," even though their chuppah too is not fit for intimacy — and this is in the Shulchan Aruch, siman 90, seif 3. One who studies the Rambam (ch. 22 of Hilkhot Ishut) will see clearly that her law is like all women: through chuppah alone, without intimacy, he inherits her. So too the Tosefta's language indicates: "A widow married to a Kohen Gadol — she is as his wife for all purposes; he inherits her" etc.

The Beit Shmuel noted and wrote that since his intent is to be intimate with her through a forbidden act, she is called "fit for intimacy." So too the Lechem Mishneh dismisses the Ran's challenge in his first approach. In my humble view, the law is with the Ran, and this reasoning does not satisfy him: what does it matter that he intends to be intimate with her — for we hold that one who betrothed but did not consummate does not receive lashes until he consummates, as stated in the Laws of Forbidden Intercourse. If so, even if he married her, perhaps when they warn him about the lashes he will separate — so it follows she was never fit for intimacy. Why then should he inherit her?

The Ran could similarly have challenged from the rule in siman 116 that those subject to negative prohibitions who "found favor in her eyes" have a ketubah — implying like all women, through chuppah alone — even if she is not fit for intimacy. And according to the Rambam, a betrothed woman has no ketubah.

In my humble view there is a stronger challenge against the Rosh and the Ran, who interpreted the question as concerning only the additional ketubah — from the rule in siman 116 that "she whom he did not know" has an additional ketubah amount; and from the plain meaning of the whole chapter, it is clear that even if immediately after the chuppah he discovered and divorced her, she has an additional ketubah. If so, even according to those who interpret "specifically if he did not know she was a niddah" — the Rosh still wrote: "the question is only whether if one says the affinity of intimacy acquires — [did he] question from Rabbenu [whether a chuppah fit for intimacy acquires] — for presumably he wrote this with reference to a chuppah that is normally fit for intimacy, not to one that is unfit — even though her chuppah is a chuppah, he did not write with the intent that she was not fit for intimacy, since reason suggests the main consideration is on the emotional proximity of the affinity of intimacy." And the implication of the above — that even if he did not know and immediately after the chuppah he recognized and divorced her — she certainly has an additional ketubah; but what is the difference from a niddah's chuppah? Rather, even if he did not know until he was intimate with her, in my view there too applies the presumption that a chuppah not fit for intimacy is not written with reference to this — since it is reasonable that the main consideration is on the emotional proximity approximating intimacy, and not with reference to a forbidden accidental intimacy which is not called enjoyment.

Do not say that the reason there is that he was penalized — as Rashi explained in the chapter "Even Nursing" in the Mishnah: this is incorrect, first because penalty does not apply where he did not know; and furthermore, even Rashi's language is not precise, as stated in the responsa of Chavat Yair (siman 124), writing: "Therefore it is not an actual penalty — but since the Mishnah states that a second wife has no ketubah because she was penalized, this teaches that in the case of a widow the Sages did not wish to penalize her but to treat her like all other women." So too Rashi wrote explicitly in the chapter "There Is Benefit" (folio 85) — end of his words.

I am astonished at Chavat Yair in the cited siman, who brings proof for Ri — that if the groom knows she is a niddah she acquires the additional ketubah — from the widow of a Kohen Gadol who has a ketubah "if he recognized her." He already wrote before this: "Furthermore, Ri also holds like the Rosh that it is only regarding the additional ketubah that the question was posed." He did not note that according to the Rosh, even the case of "he did not know she was a niddah" is a challenge compared to "he did not know she was forbidden" — for the additional ketubah. In my humble view, the presumption regarding one not fit for intimacy at all is stronger than that of a niddah who will be fit when she purifies.

In my humble view it is because of this challenge that the Geonim and the Rambam departed from the Rosh's interpretation. For according to their interpretation, in my view there is no challenge from the case of those forbidden in marriage for whom "her husband inherits her" — once I have carefully noted the distinction in terminology: a niddah's chuppah the Talmud calls "not fit for intimacy," whereas kiddushin forbidden for intimacy (as in kiddushin not delivered for intimacy, or "he betrothed one of two sisters") — those are called "kiddushin not delivered for intimacy." This indicates a distinction: a chuppah of those forbidden in marriage is in itself a chuppah fit for intimacy — only for this specific husband is she forbidden; unlike a niddah where for all opinions she is not fit for intimacy. Furthermore, specifically in a niddah's case, where the deficiency is at the time of the chuppah and all agree that had they waited until she purified she would have been fit — this is called "not fit," and she does not acquire until she purifies. But in the case of those forbidden in marriage, the deficiency does not stem from this chuppah itself — for even if this husband never consummates, this woman does not have a "more appropriate" chuppah with this man; she is therefore not called "unfit" but "not delivered for intimacy." Just as kiddushin not delivered for intimacy acquires, so too chuppah acquires.

But according to the reasoning of the Rosh and the Ran — that it is a matter of presumed intent — it is certainly a challenge. And what the Ran there gives as the reason that it does not implicate her, and the Chelkat Mechokek in siman 116, subsection 10, adds that the husband too was at fault — all this, in my humble view, is insufficient if one explains the question on the basis of presumed intent.

There still remains to explain the challenge to the Rosh from Shmuel (in Yevamot), who said there is no chuppah for those forbidden in marriage. Tosafot explain that according to Rashi's interpretation, even a chuppah following kiddushin has nothing for them — and this is a challenge to Shmuel from a baraita where a tanna agrees with him — ketubah in ch. 101 — where Shmuel said: "This applies only to the base of one hundred or two hundred maneh, but for the additional amount they have." This implies that she is like all other women — through chuppah she acquires the additional amount. But according to him, there is no chuppah for the forbidden!

Perhaps therefore Rashi was careful to explain that he gives her a gift by reason of the affinity of intimacy — even though we rule that the affinity of chuppah acquires — and certainly what is stated in the baraita (such as one who refused kiddushin) "but they do have an additional ketubah" refers to chuppah alone. Nevertheless he explained it thus for Shmuel, who holds there is no chuppah for the forbidden — therefore she is different from all women and acquires only after intimacy, by the affinity of intimacy. And in truth, of necessity the baraita speaks in terms of alternatives: a woman who refused kiddushin acquires through chuppah; a woman for whom kiddushin is forbidden acquires through intimacy; and it supports itself on what is known according to Shmuel — each has an additional amount through what renders her "married."

But the decisors do not make this distinction since we rule like Rav, that there is chuppah for the forbidden — because she is considered fit for intimacy, as I wrote above — and accordingly, the baraita evidently speaks of one type, and all of them acquire through chuppah alone.

And in truth, the question about a niddah's chuppah according to the Rambam is not relevant to Shmuel, since according to him a niddah's chuppah is not fit for intimacy and she has no chuppah — all the more so a niddah's chuppah. But according to Rav, the question remains unresolved, based on the above reasoning.

<b>"Behold she is as his wife." — Resolving the Beit Shmuel's challenge; chuppah = seclusion derived from Talmud folio 56; the im timtza lomar structure.</b>
See the Beit Shmuel's challenge against the Rambam: if chuppah means seclusion, what is the Talmudic question about a niddah becoming a niddah? One should say it is a forbidden chuppah that does not acquire — the same as the Ran's challenge. See siman 55 where, in my humble view, the Ran's proof from the statement about a forbidden act is not conclusive.

Even according to this approach one may resolve: he derived that ordinary chuppah means seclusion from the Talmud's resolution (folio 56): "A chuppah too presents no difficulty, since ordinary chuppah exists for the sake of intimacy — hence it teaches us that seclusion by night is required." From this one certainly gathers that ordinary chuppah means seclusion — for otherwise, how would it be "ordinary" to be for intimacy? Furthermore, why should the ordinary practice be by night? This certainly indicates ordinary chuppah means seclusion.

This resolves the Rosh's challenge: why was this question placed here? One must have thought that an ordinary chuppah involves seclusion without intimacy, and there would be nothing to question about fitness for intimacy in this regard. But once we learn from Rav Yosef's teaching that ordinary chuppah means seclusion, the question about fitness for intimacy is quite appropriate.

One can also resolve the Chelkat Mechokek's challenge: he argued that since the first question necessarily does not concern inheritance under Torah law, and the second question concerns all matters — the Beit Shmuel wrote: "According to what I wrote in siman 56, there is no difficulty, and all the questions" etc.

In my humble view this is certainly incorrect, for then: how is it possible that chuppah does not acquire until intimacy? If so, what purpose does this chuppah serve? Through intimacy alone she is as his wife for all purposes, as in siman 55. Even if one might compare them — saying the function of chuppah is to permit intimacy without specific marital intent, and the entire question is only regarding acquisitions — nevertheless one may object: if so, one should resolve it from Rav Huna, who in tractate Kiddushin derives through a kal va-chomer (a fortiori) that chuppah acquires: "A betrothal, which does not complete the marriage, acquires; a chuppah, which completes — does it not acquire?" If chuppah only acquires together with intimacy, how is this a kal va-chomer — intimacy alone also obviously acquires? Rather, the answer must be that the first question was only regarding the additional ketubah, and the second also regarding inheritance — see the Beit Shmuel there.

According to what I wrote, one may say: the first question was always only regarding the additional ketubah, and nevertheless it was stated in the "if you say" form — because if the affinity of intimacy alone acquires, then nothing is learned from Rav Yosef's teaching, since he wrote only with reference to the affinity of the first night, which refers to chuppah — and we would not learn that ordinary chuppah means seclusion; obviously there would be nothing to question about whether a chuppah fit for intimacy is required. But if one says the affinity of chuppah acquires, then we learn from Rav Yosef that ordinary chuppah means seclusion — appropriately one may then question whether a chuppah fit for intimacy is required. This is clear.
