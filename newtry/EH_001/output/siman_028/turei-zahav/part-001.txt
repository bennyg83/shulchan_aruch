לר"ש דאמר סתם גזלה יאוש בעלי' אלא לא הוי יאוש ואינה מקודשת כלל אפי' להחמיר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>She is betrothed rabbinically.</b> This implies specifically after the owner's despair, for the Shulchan Aruch has already ruled that despair is not assumed unless it is known. Therefore, in an ordinary case before known despair there is no betrothal even rabbinically, and she does not require a get. I wrote this to exclude the words of my father-in-law, who, because of a difficulty raised by Maharshal, maintained that a get is required. I already wrote in Choshen Mishpat that the difficulty can be resolved, and that if one betrothed a woman with stolen property before it is known that the owner certainly despaired, no get is required, as all the authorities rule here.

Even if some great authorities were said to require a get, Mahari Weil records a practical ruling that no get is required, and Maharshal's teacher and others agreed. No later authority is found requiring a get in such a case. Since even according to the stringent view the concern is only rabbinic, and the authorities clearly rule leniently, there is no basis to require a get. Indeed, stringency here would cast aspersion on earlier practical rulings, much as the Gemara at the beginning of Gittin warns against stringencies that call earlier gittin into question.

The Tur cites Ramah: specifically with property stolen from another she is betrothed after despair, because there are both despair and a change of domain; but with her own stolen property, if he had not first arranged the match, even after despair she is not betrothed, because there is no change of domain. The words "and if he stole" are the Tur's continuation, not Ramah's. This resolves the Beit Yosef's question why the Tur attributed the ruling to Ramah when it is explicit in the Talmud: Ramah teaches that in her own stolen property, despair does not help.

There is, however, a broader difficulty. In Choshen Mishpat the Rosh rules that if a thief betrothed a woman with stolen property after despair, she requires a get out of doubt, because perhaps despair alone acquires biblically. If the very act of betrothal were considered a change of domain, then after despair she should certainly be betrothed, not merely doubtfully. It cannot be speaking of her own stolen property, because if he had arranged the match she would certainly be betrothed, and if not, there would not even be rabbinic kiddushin. It must concern property stolen from others, and even there the Rosh apparently does not treat the act of kiddushin itself as a change of domain, for at the time of giving the money must already be his.

It follows that there is a dispute between the Rosh and Ramah. Although the Tur cites both in different places, here, in the laws of kiddushin, his ruling is primary: the betrothal and the change of domain can occur together, just as elsewhere we find acquisition and domain arriving simultaneously. Rambam's language also implies definite kiddushin after known despair, because of the change of domain. Thus Ramah's statement is a real novelty.

One should not object from Tosafot in Kiddushin, who ask what Rav adds beyond the Mishnah. Rav was commenting on the Mishnah, which deals with a case before despair, as the sugya itself indicates. My father-in-law again wrote that rabbinically a get is required in every case and claimed that this is the gloss in the Shulchan Aruch, but that is inaccurate. The gloss concerns only a case after definite despair. In an ordinary case, we have a settled rule that ordinary theft is not presumed to involve despair by the owner, and she is not betrothed at all, even stringently.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Specifically where she does not know.</b> This is the Tur's language in the name of the Rosh. The Perishah writes that although the Tur began with both a deposit and a borrowed object, this final rule applies specifically to a deposit. With a borrowed object, he says, even if she knew the amount at first and at the time of kiddushin did not know it had been stolen, she is nevertheless betrothed if a perutah remains, because as a borrower she is obligated to pay for the stolen amount. The amount she owes is a loan, and the remaining perutah is a perutah, so this is a case of kiddushin with a loan and a perutah, where she is betrothed. My father-in-law wrote similarly.

I am astonished that these great scholars did not attend to the plain sense. The Gemara itself speaks of one who betroths with a deposit in her hand, and it is speaking of a deposit for which she bears responsibility, as Rashi explicitly says: even though she accepted liability, if nothing remains she is not betrothed. On that very case the Rosh writes that if she knows the amount of the deposit, the remaining perutah does not help. This is therefore just like a borrowed object.

It seems clear that the Tur mentions borrowing precisely to teach that the deposit in the Gemara is one for which she is liable, like a borrowed object. There is no ordinary rule of "loan and perutah" here. Since, according to the Rosh, his intent was specifically for the entire amount of the deposit, the whole amount is included in the kiddushin. Therefore, if part of it is a loan, the remaining part does not help. This is also stated in the gloss later in this siman.

If at the time of kiddushin he explicitly says, "Be betrothed to me with this loan and this perutah," we say she is betrothed with the perutah, because the ineffective loan language is disregarded and the kiddushin rests on the perutah. Here, by contrast, she thought the whole deposit was still intact, and the entire sum was the object of the kiddushin. Once part of that sum is only a debt, it is as though the whole matter is a loan. Therefore, if part was lost, even though a perutah remains, it does not help in a deposit for which she is liable, just as with a borrowed object, because the lost portion is included in the kiddushin as a loan.

The reason the final clause mentions only a deposit is that the Gemara itself mentions only a deposit; the Rosh revealed that borrowing is the same because the deposit in the Gemara bears liability. In both cases there is a distinction between whether she knew the amount of the deposit or did not know it.

<b>And if he sold it, etc.</b> This refers to a gentile's pledge. Mahari Weil wrote to the questioner that, although one might think that because this is rabbinic theft it should not effect kiddushin, like chametz in the sixth hour, he was hesitant. Here it is not theft but more like withholding repayment of the gentile's loan, which is permitted; although it may be forbidden where it causes desecration of the Name, that does not make it theft. We find many matters that are forbidden ab initio, such as the dust of interest, yet if one betroths with them the kiddushin is not thereby void. Moreover, even if she cannot sell the pledge, one can sometimes evade the gentile by saying it was lost, in which case it becomes permitted in his hand, and perhaps there is not even desecration of the Name.

This requires analysis. If the issue is that there is an issur but not theft, what would be different if it were theft from a gentile? Theft from a gentile is also prohibited, yet the prohibition is rabbinic according to Mahari Weil's own discussion. If kiddushin can take effect there, why not here? And if one says that it can indeed take effect even with actual theft from a gentile, why did Rema rule that if one betrothed with a gentile's pledge she is not betrothed, following Mordekhai, because a Jew does not acquire a gentile's pledge? At minimum it should be a doubtful kiddushin. This remains difficult.

The glossator cites Beit Shmuel: when he has a pledge, he must inform her that it is not his but a pledge, because she can say, "I do not wish to be betrothed with something that is not his," as we find regarding a borrowed ring and a rented utensil. I do not understand this comparison. In a borrowed or rented object he has no proprietary claim in the object itself; it must be returned at the end of the period without payment. A pledge, however, is acquired by him indefinitely unless it is redeemed, and even if it is redeemed, the redemption is like a sale back for money. It is therefore considered his, and there is no mistake in the kiddushin. We do not say that if a man betroths with an object and later must sell it, the kiddushin was mistaken because she assumed no such circumstance would arise.

Furthermore, if one must disclose that an item is a pledge, why did the Tur not write that rule here, where the law of a pledge appears first, from which one would have learned it all the more so for borrowing? Even regarding a borrowed object, the Darkhei Moshe cites authorities that if the woman later says, "Had I known it was not yours I would not have accepted kiddushin," her words are ineffective because unexpressed intentions are not legally operative. Although this may conflict with the responsum of the Rosh, it is possible that the Rosh spoke only of borrowing; with a pledge even the Rosh would agree that her later claim does not help, since it is treated as his. Similarly, where one betroths with a gift that must be returned, she is betrothed even though he did not tell her, and we do not say she can object that it was not truly his.

Accordingly, when Rema rules that one who betroths with a gentile's pledge does not effect kiddushin, he is speaking even where he did not tell her it was a pledge, parallel to a Jew's pledge. How, then, did he give it as kiddushin if it is not his? It must be that he intended to seize it, making it comparable to theft from a gentile. The Taz and Chelkat Mechokek's question therefore remains: why should she not be betrothed?

<b>With a loan and a perutah.</b> The Tur writes: Therefore, if she said to him, "Make me bracelets and earrings, and I will be betrothed to you for the wage of the labor," she is not betrothed, because wages accrue from beginning to end. The meaning is that since betrothal is effected only with money and not with a loan, this wage too has the status of a loan. Rashi explains in Kiddushin 48 that according to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, whenever each perutah's worth of work is completed the employer becomes obligated to the artisan for that portion of the wage. Therefore, when he returns the article to her, the wage is retrospectively a loan. According to the view that wages are owed only at the end, when he returns the article and waives the wage for the sake of betrothal, there is no prior loan at the moment of return.

The essential point, then, depends on the return of the completed work. Only then does the payment become due according to the view that wages are payable at the end. According to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, the obligation has attached already, even before she has received the object; as soon as he performs work worth a perutah, that perutah becomes a loan against her retroactively when he later gives it to her. The same applies even if the entire wage is only one perutah. But according to the view that wages are not owed until the end, no loan rests upon her until the object is returned to her, for only then can he collect the wage from her.

This is not like building a wall, where each row becomes payable as it is made, because the wall already stands in the owner's domain and is not in the worker's hand. Here the due date depends on transferring the object back into her domain, and then, specifically, the wage is treated retrospectively as a loan. Rashi likewise writes that once they have finished the work and must return it in court, it has already become a loan.

The Perishah explains that according to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, even if he gives the object immediately upon completing the work, the final hammer-blow is not worth a perutah; otherwise it would be a case of a loan together with a perutah, in which she would be betrothed. I am astonished at this. If so, why did the Gemara need to formulate the dispute about a loan and a perutah in a case where he added a jewel of his own, as the Tur also writes nearby? It could have been found in every case where the final blow was worth a perutah. Furthermore, below in siman 38 the same issue appears regarding "I will speak for you to the ruler," and there it is impossible to say that the final speech is not worth a perutah. Nor can one say that the final blow is never worth a perutah; if the whole wage were only one perutah and there were only one blow, would she then be betrothed? Certainly not, for the wage still has the name of a loan.

Rather, Rashi repeatedly makes clear that the matter depends on the return, whereas the Perishah focuses on the final blow. In truth the giving is the act of betrothal. The difference is this: according to the view that wages are owed only at the end, the loan begins only from the moment of giving onward, not retrospectively from the time of labor; according to the view that wages accrue from beginning to end, the giving causes each perutah, even a single perutah, to become a prior loan retroactively.

This is unlike the case in Avodah Zarah concerning building a structure for idolatry, where the wage is permitted because wages accrue from beginning to end and the prohibition takes effect only at the final hammer-blow, which is not worth a perutah. There the completion itself is the final blow, and there is no later act of giving. In betrothal, however, everything depends on the giving, which is the essential act of kiddushin.

Tosafot Rid writes that the baraita's phrase, "for the wage that I will do with you," states the legal case, but the man must say at the time of giving, "Be betrothed to me for the wage of this vessel." If he said at the outset, "Do you wish to be betrothed to me for the wage of what I will do for you," and she said yes, but at the time of giving he said nothing, she is not betrothed, just as with a get one must say at the time of giving, "This is your get." The Tur's cases of giving the vessel for its improvement, or adding a jewel of his own, would accordingly require such a statement at the time of giving.

I find this difficult. The Gemara says that "for the wage I have done with you" is ineffective, while "for the wage I will do with you" is effective. If a new declaration were required at the time of giving, he would necessarily have to say then, "for the wage I have done with you," and the case would collapse into the first clause. Rashi also writes simply that when he finishes and gives it to her, she is betrothed, without mentioning a new declaration. It therefore seems that no new declaration is required. Since one who was already speaking with her about betrothal and then gave without specifying can effect betrothal, all the more so where he stated explicitly at the outset that the labor would be for kiddushin. At the time he gives the vessel, no further words are needed; the giving plainly relies on the first declaration, even though time has intervened.

This is unlike the thirty days of borrowing, for during that time it is like a sale, as we say regarding rental, that throughout the rental days it is like a sale. So too with borrowing, even when unspecified it is like specifying thirty days; and where he specifies, all agree that she is betrothed. This is as the Rama wrote, for he holds that if he says, with this benefit that you derive, etc. Afterward the Tur brought the view of the Rosh, as written here in Shulchan Aruch, that if he informed the lender that he would betroth with it, and informed the woman that it is borrowed until such-and-such time, though this is not in the Tur and in truth it is not needed when the lender knows he will betroth with it, then she is betrothed in every case, for we are witnesses that the lender resolved in his mind to give it to him in whatever manner is effective for kiddushin, etc. See what I wrote about this in Orach Chayyim siman 14, for there is reason to wonder about it. Darkei Moshe wrote: since the Geonim disagree about the law of one who betroths with a borrowed ring, it is good to be careful not to betroth with it. So too it implies in Hagahot Mordechai to Kiddushin. But the custom has already spread to betroth with a borrowed ring for the purpose of betrothing with it.

**** ENGLISH ****
With respect to prohibitions of benefit, the Rosh's view is that if there is a biblical aspect, whether because of chametz itself or because of the time, she is not betrothed; but where there is no biblical aspect she is betrothed. Rambam holds that even where there is no biblical aspect she is not betrothed. The Shulchan Aruch's doubt is therefore a doubt between these two rabbinic sides. Where the Tur cites the Rosh regarding chametz in the sixth hour, it should mean rabbinic chametz in the sixth hour. This resolves the Beit Yosef's wonder that the Tur's words are obscure.

Consider one who found an object within his four cubits, and another person picked it up. We rule that a person's four cubits acquire for him by rabbinic enactment. If the one who acquired it through four cubits then betrothed a woman with it, we might have said that she is not betrothed, since biblically the finder who lifted it acquired it, for four cubits do not acquire biblically, and it would be theft in the first man's hand. Yet we do not find anywhere that such kiddushin is invalid; on the contrary, the rule is stated simply that four cubits acquire for him, and it becomes like his own money for all purposes. We must therefore say that the Sages have power to make property that is not his under Torah law into his by means of hefker beit din, divesting the one who lifted the lost object.

So too with a get, the Sages enacted that a woman's four cubits acquire for her because of the concern of agunah, as Tosafot writes. The divorce is effective even against the biblical baseline. If so, why should the Sages not likewise have power regarding rabbinic prohibitions of benefit to divest the benefit from him and treat it like a biblical prohibition of benefit? Moreover, the Rosh writes in a responsum that if a court enacted that kiddushin performed without the father and mother should not take effect, and someone violated the enactment, the kiddushin is void because the Sages divested him of the money. Although Rema cites nearby that it is only a concern of kiddushin, that is a stringency and not a leniency.

The same reasoning would apply to a rabbinic prohibition of benefit, and likewise to kiddushin with a gift on condition that it be returned at the beginning of the next siman: the Sages divested the money and made it ownerless, so the kiddushin should not take effect. A responsum of Rashba also implies from chametz that, in any rabbinic prohibition, kiddushin should not take effect at all because of hefker beit din. The matter requires practical analysis.
