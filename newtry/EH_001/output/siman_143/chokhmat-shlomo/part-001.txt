<small>סעיף א</small> <b>המגרש על תנאי וכו'</b> נ"ב קשה לי לדעת הסוברים דבתנאי על מנת א"י לבטלו או להוסיף בו תנאי ומכ"ש להני הסוברים דהיכי דזמנו מוכיח עליו דהוי כמעכשיו וא"י להוסיף בו תנאי מהו פריך הש"ס ר"פ השולח גבי אם משהגיע הגט לידה א"י לבטלו מהו פריך נימא דמיירי בכה"ג דנתן לה הגט בתנאי מעכשיו או היכי דזמנו מוכיח עליו וקמ"ל אף דלא נתגרשה מיד א"י לבטלו וצ"ע ודו"ק:
**** ENGLISH ****
(Seif 1:) <b>One who divorces on condition, etc.</b> NB: I have difficulty according to those who hold that with a condition framed as "on condition that," he cannot revoke it or add another condition to it, and all the more according to those who hold that where the date of the get proves its intent it is treated as from now, and he cannot add a condition to it. What, then, is the Gemara's question at the beginning of Perek HaSholeach regarding the rule that once the get reaches her hand he can no longer revoke it? Let the Gemara say that it speaks in such a case, where he gave her the get on a condition from now, or where its date proves its intent, and it teaches that even though she is not divorced immediately he still cannot revoke it. This requires study; examine carefully.

<small>ס"א</small> <b>המגרש על תנאי וכו'</b> נ"ב הנה בדין אם אחד רצה לילך למרחקים ובקשו ממנו שיתן גט לאשתו ולא רצה לגרש רק על תנאי שיחול הגט אחר ג"ש ולא רצה בזה ואח"כ כשהכין את עצמו לילך אמרו לו שאינו מן היושר לעגן את אשתו ולא ישא עון על זה ונתרצה ונתן גט סתם אם הוי הכוונה מהיום או על תנאי הראשון ע' בשו"ת מוהרי"ט ח"א סימן ל"ה שהעלה דהוי על תנאי הראשון ואני במהדורא ג' על אה"ע סי' ס"ג בתשובתי לק' טרנאפאל דחיתי דבריו על נכון בעזה"י והעליתי דהגט חל מיד ע"ש והוא אמת לפענ"ד ודו"ק שם:
**** ENGLISH ****
(Seif 1:) <b>One who divorces on condition, etc.</b> NB: In the case where someone wished to travel far away, and they asked him to give his wife a get, but he did not wish to divorce except on condition that the get take effect after three years, and he did not agree otherwise; afterward, when he prepared to leave, they told him that it was improper to leave his wife as an agunah and that he should not bear sin for this, and he agreed and gave an unconditional get. The question is whether his intent was that it take effect from today or according to the first condition. See Responsa Maharit, part 1, siman 35, who concluded that it follows the first condition. But in my third edition on Even HaEzer, siman 63, in my responsum to the holy community of Tarnopol, I properly rejected his words, with God's help, and concluded that the get takes effect immediately; see there. This is true in my humble opinion; examine carefully there.

**** ENGLISH ****
(Seif 6:) <b>If he said to her, "on condition that you give me such-and-such vessel or such-and-such garment," etc.</b> NB: The ruling that it is not a get requires study regarding the Mechaber and the later authorities, who wrote simply in a way that implies that it is not a get at all. In truth, one who examines Rashi on Gittin 74b, s.v. de-letza'urei ka-mekhaven, sees that Rashi writes that because of doubt we say he intended to distress her, and we do not assume leniently that he intended only to profit. It is therefore clear that Rashi holds this is only a doubt, not a certainty. Accordingly, if another man betrothed her, she at least requires a get from him, for perhaps the husband intended only profit and the condition has already been fulfilled. I later saw that Beit Shmuel above, s.k. 6, cited this in the name of the Bach, though he pressed Rashi's intent into another explanation, and Rashi's language does not bear that at all. It further appears to me to prove that this is Rashi's intent, for one may ask why Rashi waited until the end of the sugya, after the dispute of Rav Yosef and Rabbah, to explain the intent of "he means to distress her," and did not explain it earlier regarding the maneh, where the Gemara answers the Mishnah in Nedarim. It therefore appears that Rashi had a specific intent. Rashi was troubled by the Gemara's initial thought that Rabbah follows Rashbi, since the Gemara had already distinguished between vows and this case on the ground that here he intends to distress her, so it is not comparable to there. One must say that Rashi holds that the case of an aris is weaker than vows. With vows, there is no reason to attribute any intent except benefiting the son, and there is no proof or assessment that he intended some other reason; it is simply reasonable that he intended profit. But with an aris there are two possible intentions: either he meant to give him the third only if he himself irrigates, but if he does not irrigate he will not give it to him, since he did not trouble himself, and what difference is there whether the aris irrigated or the rain came; or he meant that in whatever manner the field is irrigated as he wishes, he will give him a third, and this is the Gemara's meaning that he intended profit. From the standpoint of reason, however, it certainly leans more to saying that he intended only if the aris would need to exert himself, and if he did not exert himself he would not give. It is therefore truly similar to gittin, for here too an ordinary divorce is given from animosity, and therefore we say that he means to distress her; likewise, an ordinary promise of a third is in payment for labor and not where no labor was done. Thus even after the Gemara distinguished between vows and gittin, it challenges Rabbah from the aris case, because he holds that an aris resembles gittin. According to this, one must understand the Gemara's answer there: when it says that there he means to distress her but here he means profit, the questioner also knew this possibility, but thought that here too it resembles gittin. Rashi therefore explains that the novelty of the answer is not that we know certainly that in gittin he means to distress her, such that this should also apply to the aris, but that in gittin the assumption that he means to distress her is only a matter of doubt; since gittin is a Torah prohibition, we rule stringently and attribute it to distress. But with an aris, which is monetary law, and monetary doubt is treated leniently, we attribute it to profit. Even if the aris is in possession, Rashi holds that seizure does not help for a claim of perhaps, and the produce remains in the possession of its original owner, since the aris has no right in it until the work is completed. Rashi's statement that a man generally divorces his wife from animosity is meant to distinguish vows, where even though that too may involve a Torah prohibition, we do not rule stringently, because there we say he certainly intended profit; here, where divorce is from animosity, there is some proof that he intended to distress her. Therefore we do not assume leniently. The aris, by contrast, even though there is also an assessment that the owner intended only if the aris troubled himself, is monetary, so we assume leniently. This is Rashi's explanation of the Gemara: when the Gemara says that there the Sages said only that he means to distress her, it means that we attribute such intent only by way of stringency, whereas here we attribute profit and rule leniently. Therefore Rashi did not explain this earlier, for at that stage we would have explained that in get we have certain testimony that he means to distress her, and in vows he certainly means profit. But since here it would then certainly be distress, there would be no distinction between money and prohibition, and the Gemara could challenge from aris to get. The Gemara therefore answers that it is only doubtful, and Rashi was compelled to explain it so. According to this, Rashi indeed holds that the matter is doubtful. However, from the Gemara there, which says regarding "they are waived to you" that she is not divorced, it implies that she is entirely not divorced and that there is no get at all. Although one could press that it says "not divorced" only stringently, it remains difficult: if he waives it, why should he not be believed that he did not intend to distress her but only to profit, especially according to those above in seif 5 who hold that if he says "let it be in your hand without condition," it helps. If so, even when he uses language of waiver and admits that he did not intend to distress her, why should he not be believed with a migo, since had he wished he could have said it in the language of no condition at all. Perhaps the Gemara speaks where he does not say that he did not intend to distress her, but if he says so, perhaps according to Rashi he is believed. This requires study.

<b>And some say that if she is appeased by receiving the money, it is a get.</b> NB: See Beit Shmuel, who cites the Drishah and Maharshal as raising a difficulty against the view of the Rosh, that one should prove from the case that giving against one's will is not considered giving. In my humble opinion, it is very astonishing to say that this was Maharshal's difficulty, for there is no proof here that it is not considered giving. The Gemara explicitly concludes that according to Rabbah, whose ruling we accept, the reason of the Sages is that he intends to distress her. If so, that applies well when she does not give him the object that was stipulated; but if they give him the very object stipulated, then she has truly distressed him, and the concern of distress does not apply at all. It is possible that this is considered giving, and this is obvious even to a young child who studies the Gemara. How could the great Maharshal have raised such a question and left it difficult? The correct explanation is that a scribal error fell into the Drishah before them, and the correct text is as found in the newer Berlin editions of the Turim, where it says that one may prove from here that giving against one's will is considered giving, as the Beit Shmuel himself later asks. In that form Maharshal's question is sound. Also, what Beit Shmuel wrote, asking from the Gemara's conclusion that he intends to distress her, despite having already written that giving to beit din not in his presence does not help if we do not know whether he is pleased, requires explanation. It appears that his intent is to question Rabbah's own rule there: since there the reason of distress does not apply, and the condition is properly fulfilled, even though the litigant now claims that he specifically wanted the aris to irrigate and not the rain, and the other party does not wish to give him what was stipulated, nevertheless he must give it to him, even though it is giving against his will, since the owner does not want this. Since distress does not apply, it is certainly valid giving. So too with a condition in a get: if she gives him that very object, it is proper giving, for the Gemara compares Rabbah's rule to get and only distinguishes that there it implies distress. But where there is no distress, it resembles Rabbah's rule. Even if the husband does not want this, if he wanted to accept the giving we would have no issue; rather, he claims that he does not want to accept except where the aris actually drew the water, not where rain came. Nevertheless, it is considered giving; so too by get. This seems to be how Beit Shmuel's words must be explained, for otherwise they have no explanation at all; examine carefully. What the Mechaber wrote above in this seif, "if he said to her on condition that you give me such-and-such vessel," has its source in Gittin 74. The difficulty raised by the Chiddushei HaRim, cited in his commentary and novellae, should be seen there; I resolved it with God's help in a responsum to the holy community of Konstantin, placed in siman 135 in my fourth edition, siman 1; see there. While engaged in this matter, I saw that one should examine slightly from where the poskim know that even when he stipulated concerning a particular object that was never his, we still say that he intended to distress her. This is difficult, for the Mishnah that is the source of the rule speaks of giving him his cloak, implying that it had been his originally. If so, one could say that since we generally hold that a person prefers his own kav, in that case Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that even with his own object we testify that he intended only profit and money helps, while the Sages hold that since one can say he prefers his own, and one can say he intended distress, these two considerations together make return of the money ineffective. But where it was never his, one could say that the argument that he meant to distress her is not strong enough by itself to void the get. Nor can this be derived from "on condition that you serve my father" or "on condition that you nurse my son," for if so Tosafot would not have needed to say in the sugya that they had a tradition; necessarily there is room to distinguish between them. See Penei Yehoshua in his novellae, who understood this. If so, it is difficult: perhaps the tradition applies only where he conditioned about his own object, where there is an additional assessment that he desires what is his, but in general from where do we know it? This requires study. In truth, the Tur mentions only the language of "my cloak," and one may say that he spoke specifically. But regarding Rambam and the Mechaber the difficulty remains as stated above; examine this carefully.
